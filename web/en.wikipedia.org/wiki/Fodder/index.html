Fodder

Fodder (also called provender) is any agricultural foodstuff used specifically to feed domesticated livestock, such as cattle, horses, sheep, goats, pigs and poultry. "Fodder" refers particularly to food given to animals (including plants cut and carried to them), rather than forage, which is what animals graze in fields. Most fodders are plant-based: fresh pasture grasses and legumes, hay (dried grasses and legumes such as alfalfa and clover), silage (fermented green forage), straw, root crops, and concentrate feeds made from cereal grains, oilseed meals and by-products.

Types

Roughages — pasture, hay, silage and straw — provide the fibre essential to ruminant digestion. Conserved fodder (hay and silage) allows livestock to be fed through winter or dry seasons; silage is produced by ensiling chopped maize, grass or other crops in airtight silos or wrapped bales, where lactic-acid fermentation preserves them. Energy concentrates include maize, barley, oats and sorghum; protein supplements include soybean meal, rapeseed (canola) meal, cottonseed meal and distillers' grains. Some fodders are complete manufactured feeds, pelleted or mixed as total mixed rations.

Production and trade

Fodder crops occupy a large share of world agricultural land; alfalfa (lucerne) is among the most valuable forage crops, and the United States, China, Argentina and the European Union are major producers of feed ingredients. Dedicated forage varieties of maize, sorghum, oats and ryegrass are bred for yield and digestibility. Growing water and land competition between food, feed and fuel crops is a recurring theme in agricultural policy, as is the contribution of feed production (including fertiliser use and land-use change) to livestock's environmental footprint.
