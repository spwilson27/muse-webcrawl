22° halo

A 22° halo around the Sun

A 22° halo is an atmospheric optical phenomenon that consists of a ring with an apparent radius of approximately 22° around the Sun or Moon. It is produced by the refraction of light through hexagonal ice crystals suspended in cirrostratus clouds high in the troposphere.

Formation

When light passes through the 60° apex angle of randomly oriented hexagonal ice prisms, it is deviated by a minimum of about 22°, concentrating light at that angle and producing a bright ring. Because shorter (blue) wavelengths are refracted more than longer (red) ones, the inner edge of the halo tends to appear reddish and the outer edge bluish, though the colours are usually faint. No light is refracted at angles smaller than the minimum deviation, so the sky inside the ring appears noticeably darker.

Frequency and folklore

Halos are fairly common — in many climates they can be seen on dozens of days per year, more often than rainbows. Because cirrostratus often precedes a warm front, folk weather sayings such as "ring around the Moon means rain soon" associate halos with approaching precipitation.

The lunar version, sometimes called a moon ring or winter halo, is perceived as colourless because moonlight is too dim to stimulate colour vision strongly.
