Perspective projection

Type: Graphical projection

Related: Linear perspective, projective geometry, camera obscura

Perspective projection is a type of drawing or rendering in which a three-dimensional scene is represented on a two-dimensional picture plane by projecting points of the scene along straight lines that all pass through a single point, the centre of projection (also called the eye point or viewpoint). It is the projection used, approximately, by the human eye and by the photographic camera, and it is the mathematical basis of linear perspective in art.

Principles

In a perspective projection, an object is depicted by drawing lines from each of its points to the centre of projection; where these projectors intersect the picture plane, the image of the point is formed. Because the projectors converge, objects farther from the viewpoint appear smaller, parallel lines not parallel to the picture plane appear to converge toward vanishing points, and equal intervals along a receding line appear progressively compressed — an effect known as foreshortening.

The geometry was studied systematically during the Italian Renaissance. Filippo Brunelleschi demonstrated the method around 1415, and Leon Battista Alberti described it in his treatise De pictura (1435), treating the picture as an open window (finestra aperta) through which the scene is viewed.

Classification

Perspective drawings are commonly classified by the number of principal vanishing points:

  • One-point perspective — one face of the object is parallel to the picture plane; used for interiors and roads receding directly away from the viewer.
  • Two-point perspective — the object is rotated so that two sets of horizontal edges recede to two vanishing points on the horizon line.
  • Three-point perspective — the picture plane is tilted so vertical lines also converge to a third vanishing point, above or below the horizon.

Mathematical description

In homogeneous coordinates, perspective projection is represented by a 4×4 matrix acting on points of projective three-space, followed by division by the depth coordinate. This perspective divide is what makes distant objects shrink. Modern computer graphics pipelines — from OpenGL and Direct3D to the pinhole camera model in computer vision — implement exactly this transformation, clipping geometry against a viewing frustum before rasterisation.
