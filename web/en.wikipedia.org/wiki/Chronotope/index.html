Chronotope

The chronotope (from Greek chrónos, "time", and tópos, "place") is a concept in literary theory introduced by the Russian philosopher and literary scholar Mikhail Bakhtin to describe the intrinsic connectedness of temporal and spatial relationships in narrative. Bakhtin defined it as the way in which configurations of time and space are represented in literature and serve as the organising centres for a work's narrative events.

[Image: A winding road — Bakhtin's chronotope of the road]

Origin

Bakhtin developed the concept in the essay "Forms of Time and of the Chronotope in the Novel", written in 1937–38 and published in 1975 (in English in The Dialogic Imagination, 1981). He borrowed the term from the theory of relativity, where Einstein's spacetime unifies time and space, and from the physiologist A. A. Ukhtomsky, who used it for spatiotemporal organisation in biology.

Examples in Bakhtin

Bakhtin analysed characteristic chronotopes of successive literary genres:

  • Adventure time in the Greek romance: an empty, reversible temporality in an alien, interchangeable space, leaving no mark on the hero.
  • The chronotope of the road: the road as a place of chance encounters across social strata, central to the picaresque and to novels from Rabelais onward.
  • The provincial town: cyclical, eventless everyday time in Flaubert and others.
  • The threshold in Dostoevsky: moments of crisis and break, where time is instantaneous and decisive action occurs on staircases, in doorways and corridors.
  • The castle in the Gothic novel, saturated with historical time.

Influence

The chronotope has been taken up across literary studies, anthropology, linguistics and film studies as a tool for analysing how narratives encode cultural models of time and space — for instance in accounts of the "chronotope of modernity" or of national histories. Critics note the concept's flexibility but also its vagueness, since almost any patterned time–space setting can be called a chronotope.
