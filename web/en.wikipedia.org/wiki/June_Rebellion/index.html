The June Rebellion, or the Paris Uprising of 1832 (French: Insurrection républicaine à Paris en juin 1832), was an anti-monarchist insurrection of Parisian republicans on 5 and 6 June 1832. The rebellion originated in an attempt by republicans to reverse the July Revolution of 1830, which had brought King Louis Philippe to power in the July Monarchy.

Background

Republicans had grown disillusioned with the July Monarchy, which they saw as betraying the promises of 1830: suffrage remained restricted to wealthy property owners, and economic distress and a cholera epidemic fuelled unrest in the capital. The deaths in spring 1832 of the reformist prime minister Casimir Perier and of the popular Napoleonic general Jean Maximilien Lamarque, a hero to the opposition, sharpened tensions.

The uprising

At Lamarque's funeral procession on 5 June 1832, crowds and armed republicans clashed with government troops, and fighting spread through eastern Paris. Insurgents seized weapons, raised barricades in the narrow streets around the Cloître Saint-Merry and the rue du Canal Saint-Martin, and briefly took control of parts of the eastern districts. The government responded with some 60,000 regular troops and National Guardsmen under Marshal Lobau and others.

By the evening of 6 June the last barricades had fallen after fierce fighting; the final stand was made at the Cloître Saint-Merry. Roughly 800 people were killed or wounded in total. The government declared a state of siege, tightened press restrictions, and put hundreds of insurgents on trial, though many were acquitted.

In culture

The rebellion is a central episode in Victor Hugo's 1862 novel Les Misérables, in which the character Éponine dies at the barricade and the student Enjolras leads the fictional "Friends of the ABC". Hugo, who witnessed the fighting, also based the barricade of the rue de la Chanvrerie on the real events of June 1832. The uprising features prominently in stage and film adaptations of the novel.
