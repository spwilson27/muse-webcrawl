Government of Ireland Act 1914

Citation: 4 & 5 Geo. 5 c. 90
Introduced by: H. H. Asquith
Royal assent: 18 September 1914
Status: Never implemented; repealed by the Government of Ireland Act 1920

The Government of Ireland Act 1914 (4 & 5 Geo. 5 c. 90), also known as the Home Rule Act, and before enactment as the Third Home Rule Bill, was an Act passed by the Parliament of the United Kingdom intended to provide home rule (self-government within the United Kingdom) for Ireland. It was the first such Act to be passed into law, after two earlier bills of 1886 and 1893 were defeated.

However, the Act was never implemented: its enactment was suspended for the duration of the First World War by the simultaneous Suspensory Act 1914, and the Irish political landscape was transformed by the Easter Rising of 1916 and the rise of Sinn Féin. It was ultimately repealed and replaced by the Government of Ireland Act 1920, which partitioned Ireland.

Background

Prime Minister H. H. Asquith's Liberal government depended on the votes of John Redmond's Irish Parliamentary Party, and introduced the Third Home Rule Bill in April 1912. The bill would have created a devolved Irish parliament in Dublin with control over most domestic matters, while Ireland retained 42 MPs at Westminster. The House of Lords twice rejected the bill, but under the Parliament Act 1911 the Commons could override it after two years.

The Ulster crisis

Ulster unionists, led by Edward Carson and James Craig, fiercely opposed being governed from Dublin: they signed the Ulster Covenant of 1912, formed the Ulster Volunteer Force, and imported arms at Larne in 1914. Nationalists formed the Irish Volunteers in response. The Curragh incident of March 1914 revealed that some British Army officers would refuse orders to coerce Ulster. A proposed compromise — temporarily excluding some Ulster counties from home rule — was under negotiation when war broke out in August 1914.

Provisions and fate

The Act provided for a bicameral Irish parliament (a Senate of 40 and a House of Commons of 164), with an Irish executive headed by the Lord Lieutenant. On 18 September 1914 it received royal assent, but the Suspensory Act postponed its operation until the war ended — and by then the Easter Rising, the Irish War of Independence and the Government of Ireland Act 1920 had rendered it obsolete.
