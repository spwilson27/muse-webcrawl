Sino-Japanese vocabulary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sino-Japanese vocabulary, called kango (漢語, "Han language / Chinese words") in Japanese, is the stratum of the Japanese lexicon borrowed from Chinese, together with words coined in Japan from Chinese-derived morphemes. Together with native Japanese words (wago) and later Western loanwords (gairaigo), kango forms one of the great layers of Japanese vocabulary. Estimates suggest that Sino-Japanese words constitute roughly half of the entries in Japanese dictionaries, and an even higher share of technical, academic and formal vocabulary.

History of borrowing

Chinese words entered Japanese in several waves alongside the Chinese writing system, beginning around the fifth and sixth centuries CE and intensifying with the adoption of Buddhism, Chinese law codes and Confucian learning. Because Japanese scholars read Chinese characters with Sino-Japanese pronunciations (on'yomi), successive waves of borrowing preserved different historical stages of Chinese phonology: the earlier go-on readings reflect Chinese of the Southern dynasties period transmitted via Korea, the later kan-on readings reflect the prestige pronunciation of the Tang-dynasty capital Chang'an, and still later tō-on readings came with medieval trade and Zen contact.

Japanese coinages (wasei-kango)

Especially during the Meiji period, Japanese intellectuals coined thousands of new compounds from Chinese morphemes to translate Western concepts — words such as kagaku (科學, "science"), tetsugaku (哲學, "philosophy"), denwa (電話, "telephone") and kyōwakoku (共和國, "republic"). Many of these wasei-kango were subsequently borrowed back into Chinese, Korean and Vietnamese, where they remain standard today.

Characteristics

Kango words are typically compounds of two or more kanji, favour formal, abstract and technical registers, and often form homophone-rich pairs distinguished in writing by their characters. Their on'yomi phonology is constrained to the syllable patterns of the borrowed strata, unlike the freer phonology of native words. In modern usage, kango dominates government, law, science and medicine, while everyday conversation relies more heavily on native vocabulary.
