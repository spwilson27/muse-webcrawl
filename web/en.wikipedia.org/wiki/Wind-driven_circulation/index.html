Wind-driven circulation

For deep ocean circulation driven by density differences, see Thermohaline circulation.

Wind-driven circulation is the large-scale movement of the ocean's upper layers caused primarily by wind stress acting on the sea surface. It produces the great subtropical gyres, intense western boundary currents such as the Gulf Stream and the Kuroshio, and coastal upwelling systems. Although wind directly forces mainly the upper few hundred metres, the resulting circulation organizes the transport of heat, salt, nutrients and organisms throughout the ocean basins.

Ekman transport and pumping

Wind stress drives a surface Ekman layer in which net transport is at right angles to the wind — to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. Where the wind field has curl, convergence or divergence of Ekman transport raises or depresses the sea surface and forces vertical motion known as Ekman pumping. In the subtropical oceans, anticyclonic wind curl drives downwelling (Ekman suction is negative), piling up water in the gyre interior and depressing the thermocline.

Sverdrup balance

In the ocean interior, away from boundaries, the depth-integrated circulation is described by the Sverdrup balance, formulated by Harald Sverdrup in 1947: the meridional transport is proportional to the wind stress curl divided by the planetary vorticity gradient (beta). This predicts the broad equatorward drift across subtropical gyres.

Western intensification

Because the Sverdrup flow must be returned within a closed basin, the return flow is concentrated in narrow, swift western boundary currents. Henry Stommel (1948) showed that the variation of the Coriolis parameter with latitude (the beta effect) causes this asymmetry, and Walter Munk (1950) added lateral friction to produce a realistic boundary layer structure. Western boundary currents — the Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Brazil, East Australian and Agulhas currents — are among the strongest flows in the ocean.

Upwelling

Where winds blow parallel to a coast with the coast on the appropriate side, Ekman transport moves surface water offshore and cold, nutrient-rich water rises to replace it. Such eastern boundary upwelling systems, including the California, Canary, Humboldt (Peru) and Benguela currents, support some of the world's most productive fisheries. Equatorial upwelling likewise enriches surface waters along the equator.
