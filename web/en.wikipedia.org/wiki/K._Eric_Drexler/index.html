Kim Eric Drexler (born 25 April 1955) is an American engineer best known for introducing the concept of molecular nanotechnology (MNT) — the idea of engineering with atomically precise machines able to build complex structures to atomic specification. His 1986 book Engines of Creation: The Coming Era of Nanotechnology popularised the term "nanotechnology" and laid out both the promise of molecular manufacturing and its risks, including the famous "grey goo" scenario of runaway self-replicators. He co-founded the Foresight Institute in 1986 to prepare for the technology. [Image: K. Eric Drexler, engineer and nanotechnology pioneer]

Education and early work

Drexler studied at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, earning bachelor's and master's degrees in interdisciplinary science in the 1970s and, in 1991, the first PhD in molecular nanotechnology, awarded through the MIT Media Lab. In the 1970s he was active in the space-colonisation movement, working on designs descending from Gerard K. O'Neill's studies and helping to develop concepts for high-performance solar sails and space manufacturing. His 1981 paper "Molecular engineering" in PNAS is generally considered the founding publication of the field.

Molecular nanotechnology

Drexler proposed that molecular machinery modelled on biology's ribosomes could be developed into general-purpose assemblers, and in his technical book Nanosystems (1992) he analysed molecular machines using the tools of physics and computational chemistry. The vision implies manufacturing systems of enormous productivity — making products, from computers to medical devices, by assembling them atom by atom with negligible waste — as well as existential risks from misuse or accident, which Drexler discussed under the heading of "grey goo" and later regretted as a distracting image.

Debate and later work

Mainstream chemistry reacted coolly: Nobel laureate Richard Smalley argued, in a much-cited exchange with Drexler in 2003, that chemistry in solution cannot support assembler-style mechanosynthesis (the "fat fingers" and "sticky fingers" objections). Drexler responded that his proposals relied on machine-guided positioning in controlled environments, not enzymes in solution. The field of funded nanotechnology meanwhile developed along materials-science lines rather than molecular manufacturing. Drexler later worked on broader questions of transformative technology, serving as a research fellow at Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute, and published Radical Abundance (2013), an accessible restatement of the atomically precise manufacturing thesis.
