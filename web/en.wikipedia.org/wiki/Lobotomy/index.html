Lobotomy

Other namesLeucotomy, prefrontal lobotomy

A lobotomy (from Greek λοβός lobos "lobe" and τομή tomē "cut, slice") or leucotomy is a form of psychosurgery, a neurosurgical treatment of a mental disorder that involves severing connections in the brain's prefrontal cortex. Most of the connections to and from the prefrontal cortex, the anterior part of the frontal lobes, are severed. It was used for psychiatric and occasionally other conditions as a mainstream procedure in some countries for more than two decades, despite general recognition of frequent and serious side effects.

History

The procedure was pioneered in 1935 in Lisbon by the Portuguese neurologist António Egas Moniz, who reported success in calming patients with severe anxiety and obsessional disorders; he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1949 for the development of the technique. In the United States, Walter Freeman and James Watts adapted the operation, and Freeman later developed the transorbital lobotomy, in which an instrument resembling an ice pick was driven through the thin bone of the eye socket and swept through the frontal lobe. Freeman performed or supervised thousands of procedures, popularising the operation as a remedy for illnesses from schizophrenia to depression, and even for chronic pain and "difficult" behaviour.

Use and decline

Tens of thousands of lobotomies were performed in the United States and Europe in the 1940s and early 1950s, when asylum populations were large and no effective drug treatments existed. Notable patients included Rosemary Kennedy, sister of President John F. Kennedy, who was left severely incapacitated by the procedure in 1941. Outcomes frequently included apathy, blunted affect, disinhibition, seizures, intellectual impairment and death. The arrival of chlorpromazine and other antipsychotic drugs in the 1950s, combined with growing ethical revulsion, led to the procedure's rapid decline; the Soviet Union banned it in 1950, and most countries had abandoned it by the 1970s. A small number of modern, far more precise ablative procedures (such as anterior cingulotomy) remain in use for intractable conditions, but they differ fundamentally from the historical lobotomy.

Cultural legacy

The lobotomy remains a potent cultural symbol of medical overreach, depicted in works such as Ken Kesey's novel One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest and Tennessee Williams's Suddenly Last Summer. Its history is routinely cited in debates about medical ethics, informed consent and the treatment of psychiatric patients.
