Debate is a process of formal discourse on a particular topic, in which two opposing sides present arguments and rebuttals before an audience or judge. Debates occur in legislatures, public meetings, and academic institutions, and competitive debating is a widespread school and university activity in many countries.

Methods and rules

In a formal debate, two teams or individuals argue for ("affirmative"/proposition) and against ("negative"/opposition) a motion or resolution. Speeches alternate under time limits, typically including constructive arguments, rebuttal speeches, and sometimes periods of cross-examination. Judges score speakers on the strength of evidence, quality of reasoning, organization, and delivery, and may vote for the side that best upholds its burden of proof.

Competitive formats

Major formats include British parliamentary debate (four two-person teams, used at the World Universities Debating Championship), policy debate (evidence-heavy, centered on a policy resolution, common in U.S. schools), Lincoln–Douglas debate (one-on-one, focused on values), and public forum debate (accessible discussion of current events for a general audience). Debating societies such as the Oxford and Cambridge Unions are longstanding centers of the activity.

Political debates

Televised debates between candidates are fixtures of modern election campaigns, beginning famously with the 1960 U.S. presidential debates between John F. Kennedy and Richard Nixon. Legislative debate governed by parliamentary procedure is central to democratic lawmaking.

History

Debate descends from classical rhetoric and the dialectical methods of Greek philosophy, medieval scholastic disputations, and the debating societies of the 18th and 19th centuries, which spread through schools and universities worldwide.
