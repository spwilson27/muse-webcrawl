Single-lens reflex camera

A single-lens reflex camera (SLR) is a camera that typically uses a mirror and prism system (hence "reflex" from the mirror's reflection) that permits the photographer to view through the lens and see exactly what will be captured. With most cameras of other types, the viewfinder presents a scene that differs from what is recorded on the film or sensor.

Design

Light entering the taking lens is reflected by a movable mirror set at 45 degrees up into a focusing screen and pentaprism (or pentamirror), which presents an upright, laterally correct image to the photographer's eye. When the shutter is released, the mirror flips up out of the light path, the focal-plane shutter opens, and the image is exposed on the film or sensor; the mirror then returns. Because viewing and exposure share the same lens, interchangeable lenses — from fisheye to super-telephoto — can be used with accurate framing and focusing.

History

Reflex viewing was used in early camera obscuras, and practical 35 mm SLRs emerged in the 1930s (the German Kine Exakta of 1936 is generally credited as the first). Post-war Japanese manufacturers — Asahi (Pentax), Nikon, and Canon — refined the design with instant-return mirrors and through-the-lens metering, making the SLR the dominant professional and advanced-amateur camera of the late twentieth century. Digital SLRs (DSLRs) replaced film with an image sensor from the late 1990s, before mirrorless interchangeable-lens cameras began displacing them in the 2010s.
