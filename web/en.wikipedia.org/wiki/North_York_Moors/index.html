The North York Moors are an upland area and national park in north-eastern England, in the county of North Yorkshire. Designated a National Park in 1952, the area covers 1,436 square kilometres and contains one of the largest expanses of heather moorland in the United Kingdom.

Landscape

The park stretches from the Hambleton Hills in the west to the North Sea coast near Whitby in the east. Its landscape combines high sandstone moorland plateaus, cut by steep-sided dales such as Rosedale and Farndale, with a dramatic Heritage Coast of cliffs and fishing villages including Robin Hood's Bay and Staithes. The highest point is Urra Moor at 454 metres.

History

The moors are rich in archaeology, with Bronze Age burial mounds, Iron Age hillforts and Roman remains. In the medieval period monasteries such as Rievaulx Abbey farmed the dales; later industries included alum, jet mining around Whitby, and ironstone mining in the 19th century, served by the Rosedale railway. The preserved North Yorkshire Moors Railway now runs steam trains between Pickering and Whitby through Newtondale.

Wildlife and access

Managed heather moorland supports red grouse, curlew and merlin, while the dales contain ancient woodland and famous spring displays of wild daffodils along the River Dove in Farndale. Long-distance trails crossing the park include the Cleveland Way and the Lyke Wake Walk.
