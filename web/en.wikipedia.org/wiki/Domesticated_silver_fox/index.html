Domesticated silver fox

The domesticated silver fox (Vulpes vulpes forma amicus) is a form of the silver fox that has been to some extent domesticated under laboratory conditions. The silver fox is a melanistic form of the red fox. The product of a long-running experiment in Siberia, the domesticated foxes are the result of selective breeding solely for tameness.

The experiment

The experiment began in 1959 at the Institute of Cytology and Genetics in Novosibirsk, under geneticist Dmitry Belyaev, and was continued after his death by Lyudmila Trut. Foxes from fur farms were bred only on the criterion of friendliness toward humans. Within a few dozen generations the population became eager for human contact, wagging their tails and whining like dogs.

Domestication syndrome

Selection for tameness alone produced a suite of correlated changes — piebald coats, floppy ears, shortened snouts, curly tails, altered reproductive cycles and reduced adrenal stress hormones — paralleling traits seen in domesticated dogs and other animals. The experiment is widely cited as evidence for the "domestication syndrome" hypothesis, though that hypothesis has since been debated. A parallel population bred for aggression was also maintained for comparison.
