Georg Wilhelm Steller (10 March 1709 – 14 November 1746) was a German botanist, zoologist, physician and explorer, who worked in Russia and is considered a pioneer of Alaskan natural history.

Steller was born in Windsheim, near Nuremberg in Germany, and studied medicine at the University of Wittenberg. He travelled to Russia as a physician and joined the Imperial Academy of Sciences in Saint Petersburg. He became the naturalist on Vitus Bering's Second Kamchatka Expedition (1733–1743), the great Russian exploring venture that mapped much of the Arctic coast of Siberia and reached North America.

The Great Northern Expedition

In 1741 Steller sailed with Bering aboard the ship St. Peter to America. When the expedition sighted the Alaskan mainland near Kayak Island in July 1741, Steller became the first European naturalist to set foot in Alaska. During his brief time ashore — famously, he was allowed only a few hours — he recorded plants and animals new to science, including the jay that now bears the name Steller's jay, correctly identifying it as related to the American blue jay and inferring that the land was part of America.

Shipwreck and the sea cow

On the return voyage the St. Peter was wrecked on Bering Island in November 1741. Bering died there, but Steller kept many of the crew alive through the winter with his medical knowledge and foraging. On the island he studied the great marine mammal later named Steller's sea cow (Hydrodamalis gigas), a giant relative of the manatee up to nine metres long. The species was hunted to extinction within 27 years of its discovery. The survivors built a new vessel from the wreck and returned to Kamchatka in 1742.

Later life and death

Steller spent further years exploring Kamchatka, whose natural history he described in his posthumous work Beschreibung von dem Lande Kamtschatka (1774). Recalled to Saint Petersburg, he died of fever at Tyumen in Siberia in 1746, aged 37.

Legacy

Many species are named after Steller, including Steller's sea eagle, Steller's eider, Steller's sea lion, Steller's jay and the gumboot chiton. His journal of the voyage, published in 1793, remains a foundational document of North Pacific natural history.
