Fur seals are any of nine species of pinnipeds belonging to the subfamily Arctocephalinae in the family Otariidae (the eared seals, which also includes the sea lions). They are much more closely related to sea lions than to true (earless) seals, and share with them external ear flaps (pinnae), relatively large foreflippers, and the ability to walk on all fours on land.

Characteristics

Fur seals are named for their dense underfur — up to 300,000 hairs per square inch in the northern fur seal — protected by longer guard hairs. This coat, rather than a thick blubber layer, provides much of their insulation, and they groom it constantly to maintain its water-repellent air layer. Males are much larger than females: a male northern fur seal may exceed 270 kg, while females weigh about 50 kg. Fur seals feed on krill, squid and schooling fish, and are preyed on by orcas and large sharks.

Species

  • Northern fur seal (Callorhinus ursinus) — North Pacific, Bering Sea
  • Antarctic fur seal (Arctocephalus gazella) — Southern Ocean
  • Subantarctic fur seal (A. tropicalis)
  • South American fur seal (A. australis)
  • Galápagos fur seal (A. galapagoensis)
  • New Zealand fur seal / kekeno (A. forsteri)
  • Brown fur seal (A. pusillus) — southern Africa and Australia
  • Juan Fernández fur seal (A. philippii)
  • Guadalupe fur seal (A. townsendi)

Hunting and recovery

Fur seals were hunted to near extinction in the 18th and 19th centuries for their pelts; some populations numbered in the tens when protection began. The North Pacific Fur Seal Convention of 1911 — among the first international wildlife treaties — halted pelagic sealing and allowed dramatic recoveries. Several species have rebounded into the millions, though the Guadalupe and Juan Fernández fur seals remain comparatively rare, and fur seal populations are now monitored for the effects of climate change and fishery interactions on their prey.
