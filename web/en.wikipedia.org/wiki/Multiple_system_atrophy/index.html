Multiple system atrophy

Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a rare neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a combination of autonomic failure and motor impairment — parkinsonism, cerebellar ataxia, or both. It is an alpha-synucleinopathy: abnormal aggregates of the protein alpha-synuclein accumulate mainly in oligodendrocytes, forming glial cytoplasmic inclusions, in contrast to Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies, where aggregates occur mainly in neurons.

Signs and symptoms

MSA is classified by its predominant motor features. MSA-P (parkinsonian type, formerly striatonigral degeneration) presents with rigidity, bradykinesia, tremor, and postural instability that respond poorly to levodopa. MSA-C (cerebellar type, formerly olivopontocerebellar atrophy) presents with gait and limb ataxia, dysarthria, and oculomotor problems. Autonomic failure is required for diagnosis in current criteria and includes orthostatic hypotension, urinary incontinence or retention, erectile dysfunction, and constipation; REM sleep behaviour disorder is also very common and may precede motor symptoms by years.

Cause and epidemiology

The cause is unknown; most cases are sporadic, with no clear hereditary or environmental trigger established. MSA affects roughly 3–5 people per 100,000, with onset typically in the sixth decade of life, somewhat earlier than Parkinson's disease. Progression is more rapid than in Parkinson's disease, with average survival of about 6–10 years from symptom onset.

Management

There is no cure and no disease-modifying therapy. Treatment is symptomatic: a minority of patients benefit transiently from levodopa; orthostatic hypotension is managed with fluids, salt, compression garments, and drugs such as midodrine, fludrocortisone, or droxidopa; urinary, speech, swallowing, and mobility problems require multidisciplinary supportive care, often involving neurology, physiotherapy, speech therapy, and palliative care.
