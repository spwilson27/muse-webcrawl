Canonical transformation

In Hamiltonian mechanics, a canonical transformation is a change of canonical coordinates (q, p) → (Q, P) that preserves the form of Hamilton's equations, so that the dynamics can be written with a (possibly new) Hamiltonian in the new coordinates. Canonical transformations are the coordinate changes natural to Hamiltonian mechanics: they preserve the symplectic structure of phase space, and with it quantities such as phase-space volume (Liouville's theorem).

Generating functions

Canonical transformations are commonly constructed with a generating function, which depends on a mixture of old and new coordinates and momenta. There are four basic types, conventionally denoted F₁(q, Q), F₂(q, P), F₃(p, Q) and F₄(p, P), related to one another by Legendre transformations; the transformation equations follow by differentiation of the generating function.

Conditions

Equivalent characterisations include the preservation of the fundamental Poisson brackets, {Qi, Pj} = δij, with all other brackets among the new variables vanishing, or the condition that the difference p·dq − P·dQ be an exact differential. Infinitesimal canonical transformations are generated by functions on phase space through the Poisson bracket, a fact that connects symmetries with conserved quantities.

Applications

Canonical transformations are used to simplify Hamiltonians — ideally transforming to action-angle variables in which the new momenta are constants and the coordinates advance linearly in time — and they underlie the Hamilton–Jacobi theory. In quantum mechanics the analogous role is played by unitary transformations.
