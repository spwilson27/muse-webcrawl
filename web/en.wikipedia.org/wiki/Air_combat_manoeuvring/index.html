Air combat manoeuvring

Air combat manoeuvring (ACM), also called dogfighting tactics, is the tactical art of moving, turning, and situating one's fighter aircraft in order to attain a position from which an attack can be made on another aircraft. It relies on offensive and defensive basic fighter manoeuvres (BFM) to gain an advantage over an aerial opponent. ACM differs from basic fighter manoeuvres in that it encompasses the full three-dimensional problem of relative geometry, energy management, and weapons employment between aircraft.

Fighters practise turning engagements during dissimilar air combat training.

Principles

The central problem of air combat is geometry: a pilot must control range, angles, and closure rate relative to the opponent. Key quantities include angle off tail (the angle between the attacker's flight path and the defender's fuselage line extended), aspect angle, and the position of the control zone behind the defender from which guns or missiles can be employed. Energy — the combination of altitude (potential energy) and airspeed (kinetic energy) — can be traded one for the other; the theory of energy–manoeuvrability, developed by Colonel John Boyd and mathematician Thomas Christie in the 1960s, quantifies an aircraft's ability to change its energy state (specific excess power) and profoundly influenced fighter design.

Basic manoeuvres

  • Break turn — a maximum-rate, high-g turn into an attacker to spoil his firing solution.
  • Barrel roll attack — a rolling manoeuvre used to reduce closure and stay behind a turning defender without overshooting.
  • High yo-yo — climbing out of plane to preserve energy and cut across the defender's turn circle.
  • Low yo-yo — diving to gain speed and close range inside the defender's turn.
  • Scissors — a series of reversing turns intended to force the attacker to overshoot.
  • Immelmann turn and split S — manoeuvres for reversing direction, gaining or spending altitude respectively.

One-circle and two-circle fights

When two fighters merge head-on, the ensuing engagement tends to resolve into either a two-circle fight, in which both turn in the same direction and the winner is usually the aircraft with the smaller turn radius, or a one-circle fight, in which they turn in opposite directions and turn rate matters most. Recognising which fight is developing, and whether one's own aircraft is a "rate" or "radius" fighter, is a fundamental ACM skill.

Modern practice

Although modern missiles with high off-boresight seekers and helmet-mounted sights have reduced the need for prolonged turning fights, ACM remains a core training discipline: engagements can still decay to visual range, missiles can be defeated, and the same geometry governs beyond-visual-range combat. Dissimilar air combat training (DACT), in which pilots fight against aircraft of different types — sometimes flown by dedicated adversary squadrons — is used by most major air forces.
