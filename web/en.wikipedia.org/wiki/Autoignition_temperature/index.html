Autoignition temperature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The autoignition temperature or self-ignition temperature, often called simply ignition temperature, of a substance is the lowest temperature at which it spontaneously ignites in a normal atmosphere without an external source of ignition, such as a flame or spark. This temperature is required to supply the activation energy needed for combustion.

Measurement and factors

Autoignition temperatures are measured by standardised tests (for example ASTM E659) in which a sample is introduced into a heated vessel and the lowest temperature producing ignition after a delay is recorded. Values depend on pressure, concentration, vessel geometry, and the time the substance is held at temperature: ignition is not instantaneous, and longer exposure generally lowers the apparent ignition temperature. The autoignition temperature is distinct from the flash point, below which a vapour can be ignited only with an ignition source present.

Importance

Autoignition governs hazards in chemical plants, dust explosions, the storage of oily rags (which can self-heat through oxidation), and engine knock in spark-ignition engines; conversely, diesel engines deliberately exploit compression heating beyond the fuel's autoignition temperature to achieve ignition without spark plugs.
