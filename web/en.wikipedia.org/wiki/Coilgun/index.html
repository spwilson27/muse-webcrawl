Coilgun

A coilgun accelerates a ferromagnetic projectile using a sequence of switched electromagnetic coils.

A coilgun, also known as a Gauss gun, is a type of mass driver consisting of one or more electromagnetic coils used as linear motors to accelerate a ferromagnetic or conducting projectile to high velocity. The coils, arranged along a barrel, are energised in sequence so that the projectile is continually pulled toward the next energised coil.

Operation

When a coil is energised it produces a magnetic field that attracts the projectile toward the coil's centre. The coil must be switched off (or reversed) just as the projectile reaches the centre, otherwise the field would pull it backward—a difficulty called "suckback". Multi-stage designs use optical, inductive or pre-programmed timing to switch each coil at the right instant, and capacitor banks supply the large pulses of current required.

History

The concept was patented in the early 20th century by Norwegian physicist Kristian Birkeland, who demonstrated a coilgun in 1901. Coilguns remain largely experimental and hobbyist devices; laboratory multi-stage systems have accelerated small projectiles to speeds of tens to hundreds of metres per second, but efficiencies are typically only a few percent.

Comparison with railguns

Unlike a railgun, a coilgun has no sliding electrical contact with the projectile and no barrel-wearing arc, but it requires complex switching electronics and its force falls off as the projectile leaves each coil. Coilgun principles are also used in electromagnetic aircraft launch systems and proposed space-launch assist systems.
