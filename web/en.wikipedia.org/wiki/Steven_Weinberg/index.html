Steven Weinberg (3 May 1933 – 23 July 2021) was an American theoretical physicist and Nobel laureate in Physics. His research on elementary particles and physical cosmology was honoured with numerous prizes and awards, including the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physics and the 1991 National Medal of Science.

Weinberg's 1967 paper 'A Model of Leptons' proposed the gauge theory of the electroweak interaction — unifying electromagnetism and the weak force — that became a cornerstone of the Standard Model of particle physics. He was also a noted writer on science for general audiences, author of The First Three Minutes (1977) and Dreams of a Final Theory (1992).

Early life and education

Weinberg was born in New York City and attended the Bronx High School of Science, where the future Nobel laureate Sheldon Glashow was a classmate. He studied at Cornell University (graduating 1954) and took his doctorate at Princeton in 1957 under Sam Treiman, then worked at Columbia, Berkeley and MIT before settling at the University of Texas at Austin in 1982.

Electroweak unification

In 1967 Weinberg applied the idea of spontaneous symmetry breaking via the Higgs mechanism — independently developed by Abdus Salam — to a model of leptons, constructing the theory now known as the Glashow–Salam–Weinberg model. The theory predicted the masses of the W and Z bosons and a new weak neutral current, confirmed experimentally in the 1970s and at CERN in 1983. Weinberg shared the 1979 Nobel Prize with Glashow and Salam 'for their contributions to the theory of the unified weak and electromagnetic interaction between elementary particles'.

His three-volume treatise The Quantum Theory of Fields (1995–2000) and his emphasis on quantum field theory as the inevitable framework for relativistic quantum physics deeply influenced the field. He also helped develop effective field theory methods and worked on cosmology, including the cosmological constant problem and the anthropic reasoning he controversially applied to it in 1987.

Later life and legacy

Weinberg remained scientifically active into his eighties, writing on the history and philosophy of science (To Explain the World, 2015) and speaking publicly in defence of science and secularism. He died in Austin in 2021. The Weinberg angle (weak mixing angle) of the Standard Model is named for him.
