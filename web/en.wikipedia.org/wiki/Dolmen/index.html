Dolmen

A dolmen is a type of single-chamber megalithic tomb, usually consisting of two or more upright stones supporting a large flat horizontal capstone. Most date from the early Neolithic period (4000–3000 BCE) and were sometimes covered with earth or smaller stones to form a tumulus, which may have eroded away.

Distribution

Dolmens are found across western Europe — Brittany, Ireland (such as Poulnabrone), Wales, Cornwall — Scandinavia, the Iberian Peninsula, and also in the Levant, India, and in great numbers on the Korean Peninsula, where sites at Gochang, Hwasun and Ganghwa are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Construction and use

Builders raised massive capstones using earth ramps, levers and rollers. Chambers served as collective burial places, sometimes used for centuries; grave goods included pottery, tools and ornaments. The word derives from Breton for "stone table", popularised by 18th–19th century antiquarians.

In folklore

Dolmens attracted myths as houses or tables of giants, fairies and druids; in Korea they were associated with chiefs' burials demonstrating social hierarchy.
