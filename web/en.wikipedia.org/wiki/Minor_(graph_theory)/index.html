Minor (graph theory)

In graph theory, an undirected graph H is called a minor of the graph G if H can be formed from G by deleting edges, deleting vertices, and contracting edges (merging the two endpoints of an edge into a single vertex). Graph minors generalize the idea of a subgraph and play a central role in structural graph theory.

Definitions and examples

An edge contraction replaces an edge's two endpoints with a single vertex adjacent to everything either endpoint was adjacent to. Repeated operations of deletion and contraction can produce, for example, a complete graph from a sufficiently dense graph: every planar graph is minor-free of the complete graph K5 and the complete bipartite graph K3,3. Wagner's theorem characterizes planar graphs exactly this way: a graph is planar if and only if it has neither K5 nor K3,3 as a minor.

Robertson–Seymour theorem

The celebrated Robertson–Seymour theorem (the graph minor theorem), proved by Neil Robertson and Paul Seymour in a series of twenty papers spanning 1983 to 2004, states that finite graphs are well-quasi-ordered by the minor relation: in any infinite sequence of graphs, one is a minor of a later one. A consequence is that any graph property closed under taking minors — such as planarity or embeddability on a given surface — can be characterized by a finite set of forbidden minors, and there exist polynomial-time algorithms to test any such property, though the theorem is nonconstructive about the forbidden sets.

Applications

Minors underpin the theory of graph structure, including the grid minor theorem, which relates treewidth to the presence of large grid minors, and excluded-minor characterizations of many graph classes. Hadwiger's conjecture, a major open problem, proposes that a graph with no Kt minor can be colored with t − 1 colors.
