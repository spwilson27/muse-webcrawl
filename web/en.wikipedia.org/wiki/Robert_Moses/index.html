Robert Moses


Born: December 18, 1888, New Haven, Connecticut, U.S.
Died: July 29, 1981, West Islip, New York
Occupation: Public official, urban planner

Robert Moses (December 18, 1888 – July 29, 1981) was an American public official who built much of modern New York City and its suburbs: hundreds of miles of expressways and parkways, bridges including the Triborough and Verrazzano-Narrows, Jones Beach State Park, the Lincoln Center and United Nations sites, and two world's fairs. Never elected to office, he wielded power for over four decades through public authorities whose toll revenues he controlled.

Rise and fall

Moses became New York City parks commissioner and head of the Triborough Bridge Authority under Mayor La Guardia in 1934, and accumulated a dozen posts spanning city and state government. He championed the automobile, building parkways and expressways that reshaped the region and displaced hundreds of thousands of residents, disproportionately in poor and minority neighbourhoods.

His power waned in the 1960s after defeats to community opposition — famously Jane Jacobs's campaign against his Lower Manhattan Expressway — and Governor Nelson Rockefeller stripped him of his authorities in 1968. Robert Caro's Pulitzer Prize-winning biography The Power Broker (1974) fixed his reputation as a figure of immense achievement and immense controversy.
