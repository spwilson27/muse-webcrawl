Ella Fitzgerald

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Ella Fitzgerald singing in 1947, photographed by William P. Gottlieb: smiling mid-phrase, the First Lady of Song at her peak]

Ella Jane Fitzgerald (25 April 1917 – 15 June 1996) was an American jazz singer, called the 'First Lady of Song' and the 'Queen of Jazz'. Her voice — pure, girlish, with perfect intonation over three octaves — and her virtuoso scat singing made her the defining female jazz vocalist; her Song Book albums for Verve (1956–64), devoted to Cole Porter, Gershwin, Rodgers and Hart and others, are monuments of the American songbook itself.

Discovered at an Apollo Theater amateur night in 1934, she joined Chick Webb's orchestra and hit with the novelty song 'A-Tisket, A-Tasket' (1938). After Webb's death she led the band herself, then went solo with Norman Granz's Jazz at the Philharmonic, fighting segregation on tour as a condition of her contracts.

The Song Books and later career

Granz founded Verve for her: the Song Books rescued the Broadway/Hollywood songwriters as serious repertoire, and live albums (the 1960 Berlin concert's 'Mack the Knife', with its improvised forgotten-lyrics verse) showed the wit inside the technique. She recorded with Ellington, Basie, Armstrong (the Ella and Louis albums) — virtually everyone — and kept performing into her seventies, winning 14 Grammy Awards in all.

Honours

Kennedy Center Honors (1979), the National Medal of Arts (1987) and the Presidential Medal of Freedom (1992) mark her official standing; her private standing among musicians was higher still — 'the greatest singer', in more than one star's judgement, full stop. Diabetes took her legs and then her sight; she died in Beverly Hills in 1996.
