Robert Altman

Born: February 20, 1925, Kansas City, Missouri
Died: November 20, 2006, Los Angeles, California
Occupation: Film director, producer, screenwriter
Years active: 1947–2006

Robert Bernard Altman (February 20, 1925 – November 20, 2006) was an American film director, producer, and screenwriter. A leading figure of the New Hollywood era, he developed a distinctive style marked by large ensemble casts, overlapping dialogue, multi-track sound recording, and a roving, zoom-lens camera that observes groups rather than following a single protagonist.

Career

Altman served as a bomber pilot in the Second World War and spent two decades directing industrial films and television episodes, including work on Bonanza and Combat! His breakthrough came with M*A*S*H (1970), a Korean War comedy whose irreverent tone resonated with Vietnam-era audiences; it won the Palme d'Or at Cannes. Through the 1970s he made a remarkable series of genre-revising films: the western McCabe & Mrs. Miller (1971), the noir The Long Goodbye (1973), California Split (1974), the sprawling bicentennial tapestry Nashville (1975), and 3 Women (1977).

The failure of Popeye (1980) consigned him to a decade of smaller stage adaptations, but he returned to prominence with The Player (1992), a satire of Hollywood, and Short Cuts (1993), adapted from Raymond Carver. Later works include the British country-house mystery Gosford Park (2001) and his final film, A Prairie Home Companion (2006).

Honors and legacy

Altman was nominated five times for the Academy Award for Best Director without winning, and received an Honorary Oscar in 2006 for a career that "repeatedly reinvented the art form." Critics regard him as one of the most influential American filmmakers; his multi-character, overlapping-sound approach reshaped expectations of realism in film dialogue and inspired generations of ensemble directors.
