Taconite is a low-grade, iron-bearing sedimentary rock—a variety of banded iron formation—in which iron minerals (chiefly magnetite and hematite) are interlayered with chert, quartz and carbonates. The term was coined by Minnesota state geologist Newton Horace Winchell, who adapted a name from the Taconic range of New England. Unprocessed taconite typically contains only 15–30% iron, too lean to smelt directly, but it occurs in enormous deposits, notably Minnesota's Mesabi Range, which sustained the United States steel industry after rich direct-shipping ores were depleted.

Processing

Because the iron is locked in extremely hard rock, taconite must be mined in open pits, crushed to a fine powder, and the magnetite separated magnetically from the waste silica. The concentrate is rolled with bentonite clay into marble-sized pellets and fired in kilns at about 1,260 °C (2,300 °F), producing hard taconite pellets of roughly 65% iron that can be shipped efficiently and fed to blast furnaces. The pelletising process was developed over decades by Edward Wilson Davis of the University of Minnesota's Mines Experiment Station.

History

High-grade hematite from the Mesabi Range fuelled American steel production through both World Wars, but by the 1950s the best ores were running out. Commercial taconite production began with the Reserve Mining Company at Silver Bay, Minnesota (1955) and Erie Mining at Hoyt Lakes (1957), transforming the region's economy. Taconite remains the dominant source of iron ore in the United States.

Environmental issues

Reserve Mining's practice of dumping tailings into Lake Superior led to a landmark environmental lawsuit in the 1970s after asbestos-like amphibole fibres were found in the tailings and in Duluth's drinking water; the company was ordered to switch to on-land disposal at Milepost 7. Debates over tailings, fibres and habitat effects continue around taconite mining districts.
