George Frost Kennan (February 16, 1904 – March 17, 2005) was an American diplomat and historian, best known as the architect of the U.S. Cold War strategy of containment of the Soviet Union.

Career

Kennan joined the U.S. Foreign Service in 1926 and became one of its first Soviet specialists, serving in Moscow in the 1930s and again during the Second World War as deputy to Ambassador W. Averell Harriman. In February 1946, as chargé d'affaires in Moscow, he sent the famous "Long Telegram" analysing Soviet behaviour and recommending a policy of firm resistance to Soviet expansion. In July 1947 he published, under the pseudonym "X", the article "The Sources of Soviet Conduct" in Foreign Affairs, which laid out the containment doctrine publicly.

Policy Planning and later service

As the first director of the State Department's Policy Planning Staff (1947–1949), Kennan influenced the Marshall Plan and early Cold War strategy, though he later insisted that containment as he conceived it was political and economic rather than the global military strategy it became, and he opposed the Vietnam War. He was briefly ambassador to the Soviet Union in 1952 before being declared persona non grata, and later served as ambassador to Yugoslavia (1961–1963).

Historian

At the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, Kennan wrote acclaimed works of diplomatic history, including Russia Leaves the War (1956) and The Fateful Alliance, winning two Pulitzer Prizes, and two volumes of memoirs. He died in 2005 at the age of 101.
