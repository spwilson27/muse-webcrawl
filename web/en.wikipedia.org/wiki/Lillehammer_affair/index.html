The Lillehammer affair (also known as the Lillehammer case) was the killing by the Israeli intelligence agency Mossad of Ahmed Bouchiki, a Moroccan waiter, in Lillehammer, Norway, on 21 July 1973. The Israeli agents mistook Bouchiki for Ali Hassan Salameh, the chief of operations of the Palestinian militant organisation Black September, who was believed to have planned the Munich massacre of Israeli athletes at the 1972 Summer Olympics.

Background

Following the murder of eleven members of the Israeli Olympic team in Munich in September 1972, Israel launched a covert assassination campaign, often called Operation Wrath of God, aimed at those involved in planning the attack. Ali Hassan Salameh, a leading figure in Black September and the Palestine Liberation Organization's Force 17, was among the principal targets. In the summer of 1973 Mossad received a tip that Salameh was in Norway, and a team of some fifteen agents was dispatched.

The killing

The team wrongly identified Ahmed Bouchiki, a Moroccan immigrant who worked as a waiter and lived in Lillehammer with his pregnant Norwegian wife, as Salameh. On the evening of 21 July 1973, Bouchiki was walking home from a cinema with his wife when two agents shot him repeatedly in front of her. He died shortly afterwards. Salameh, the intended target, was not in Norway at all; he was eventually killed by a Mossad car bomb in Beirut in 1979.

Arrests and trial

Norwegian police and security services rapidly investigated the killing, and six members of the Mossad team were arrested, including two of the shooters' support personnel. Interrogations exposed a wider network of Mossad safe houses and operations across Europe. Five of the arrested agents were convicted by a Norwegian court in February 1974 and received prison sentences ranging from one to five and a half years; all were released and deported to Israel by 1975. One agent was acquitted. The affair severely embarrassed Israel and exposed details of its European intelligence infrastructure, including an extensive network of passport and safe-house arrangements.

Aftermath

Norway's relations with Israel were strained for years by the affair. In 1996 the Israeli government paid compensation to Bouchiki's widow and children, although Israel did not formally accept official responsibility for the killing. The Lillehammer affair remains one of the most notorious failures of the Mossad assassination campaign, frequently cited as an instance of the dangers of mistaken identity in targeted killings.
