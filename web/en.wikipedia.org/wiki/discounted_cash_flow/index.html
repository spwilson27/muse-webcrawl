Discounted cash flow

The discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis, in finance, is a method used to value an investment, asset, company, or project by estimating its future cash flows and discounting them to their present value using a discount rate that reflects the time value of money and the risk of the cash flows. It is a fundamental technique of capital budgeting and security valuation.

Formula

The present value of expected cash flows is given by summing, over each period, CFt / (1 + r)t, where CFt is the cash flow in period t and r is the discount rate. For valuing a company, analysts typically forecast free cash flow for a number of years and add a terminal value, often estimated with the Gordon growth model or an exit multiple. The discount rate for firm valuation is commonly the weighted average cost of capital (WACC).

History

The mathematics of discounting has been used since ancient times in connection with interest; John Burr Williams formalised DCF for stock valuation in his 1938 book The Theory of Investment Value, arguing that a stock is worth the present value of its future dividends.

Limitations

DCF results are highly sensitive to assumptions about future cash flows, growth, and the discount rate, especially the terminal value, which often accounts for most of the total value. Small changes in inputs can produce large changes in the valuation.
