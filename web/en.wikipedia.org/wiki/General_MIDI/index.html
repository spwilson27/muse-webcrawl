General MIDI (GM) is a standardized specification for electronic musical instruments that respond to MIDI messages. Developed by the American MIDI Manufacturers Association (MMA) and published in 1991, General MIDI ensures that a MIDI file created on one device will play back with approximately the same instrumentation on any other GM-compatible device, solving the problem of inconsistent program (instrument) assignments in earlier MIDI equipment.

Requirements

A General MIDI instrument must provide at least 24 notes of polyphony (or 16 melodic plus 8 percussion voices), respond to velocity, support all 16 MIDI channels, and map its first 128 program numbers to a fixed list of instrument sounds, organized into 16 families of eight instruments each. Channel 10 is reserved exclusively for percussion, where each note number triggers a different drum or percussive sound rather than a pitched tone.

Program list

The 128 GM melodic instruments are arranged in families including Piano, Chromatic Percussion, Organ, Guitar, Bass, Strings, Ensemble, Brass, Reed, Pipe, Synth Lead, Synth Pad, Synth Effects, Ethnic, Percussive, and Sound Effects. Program 1 is the Acoustic Grand Piano; program 41 is the Violin; program 74 is the Flute; program 128 is the Gunshot sound effect.

General MIDI Level 2

In 1999 the specification was expanded as General MIDI Level 2 (GM2), which increased polyphony to 32 notes, added bank selection to allow multiple variations of each instrument (over 256 melodic sounds), standardized controllers such as modulation and portamento, and incorporated features pioneered by Roland's GS and Yamaha's XG extensions.

Legacy

General MIDI became the basis for the music of early 1990s personal computer games and was widely used in ring tones and web page background music. Although largely superseded in professional production by sampled virtual instruments and formats such as SoundFont, GM remains a common denominator for portable MIDI playback.
