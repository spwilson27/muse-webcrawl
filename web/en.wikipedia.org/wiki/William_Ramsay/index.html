William Ramsay

Sir William Ramsay (2 October 1852 – 23 July 1916) was a Scottish chemist who discovered the noble gases and received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1904.

Sir William Ramsay KCB FRS FRSE (2 October 1852 – 23 July 1916) was a Scottish chemist who discovered the noble gases and received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1904 "in recognition of his services in the discovery of the inert gaseous elements in air" along with his collaborator, Lord Rayleigh, who received the Nobel Prize in Physics that same year for their discovery of argon.

Early life and career

Ramsay was born in Glasgow and studied at the University of Glasgow and at Tübingen, where he took his PhD. He became professor of chemistry at University College, Bristol, in 1880 and then at University College London in 1887, where he remained until his retirement in 1913.

Discovery of the noble gases

Prompted by Lord Rayleigh's observation that nitrogen prepared from chemical compounds was slightly less dense than nitrogen from air, the two men announced in 1894 the isolation of a new, inert element, argon. Ramsay went on to isolate helium from the mineral cleveite in 1895 (previously known only from the solar spectrum), and with Morris Travers in 1898 discovered neon, krypton, and xenon by fractional distillation of liquid air. These discoveries completed a whole new group (Group 18) of the periodic table.

Later work

With Frederick Soddy, Ramsay showed in 1903 that helium is produced during the radioactive decay of radium; with Robert Whytlaw-Gray he isolated radon ("niton") in 1910 and measured its atomic weight. He was knighted in 1902 and died at High Wycombe on 23 July 1916.
