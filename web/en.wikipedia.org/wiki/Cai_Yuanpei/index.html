Cai Yuanpei (Chinese: 蔡元培; 11 January 1868 – 5 March 1940) was a Chinese philosopher and politician who served as the principal of Peking University and founder of the Academia Sinica. He is known for his critical evaluation of Chinese culture and synthesis of Chinese and Western thinking. Cai was influential in the development of modern Chinese education and helped shape the intellectual environment that gave rise to the New Culture Movement and the May Fourth Movement.

Early life and education

Cai was born in Shanyin County, Shaoxing, Zhejiang province, to a banker's family. Educated in the classical Chinese tradition, he passed the imperial examinations with distinction, earning the jinshi degree at the unusually young age of 24 and taking a post at the prestigious Hanlin Academy in Beijing. Following China's defeat in the First Sino-Japanese War in 1895, Cai became convinced that traditional scholarship could not save the country and turned his attention to modern, Western-style education.

He taught at and administered schools in Shaoxing, became involved in revolutionary circles opposed to the Qing dynasty, and in 1907 travelled to Germany, where he studied philosophy, psychology, art history and anthropology at the University of Leipzig.

Career

Minister of Education

After the Xinhai Revolution and the founding of the Republic of China in 1912, Cai served briefly as the republic's first Minister of Education under Sun Yat-sen. He promulgated a modern national education system, abolished the reading of Confucian classics as the core of primary schooling, promoted co-education and emphasised aesthetic education. He resigned later that year in protest at Yuan Shikai's authoritarian tendencies and returned to Europe for further study in Germany and France, where he helped organise work-study programmes for Chinese students.

President of Peking University

In December 1916 Cai was appointed president (principal) of Peking University, taking office in January 1917. He transformed the institution — then known as a finishing school for would-be officials — into China's leading modern university. His reforms included:

  • Academic freedom and intellectual pluralism, summarised in his principle of "freedom of thought and inclusiveness" (思想自由、兼容并包). He hired scholars of sharply opposed views, from the New Culture radicals Chen Duxiu, Hu Shih and Li Dazhao to the conservative classicist Ku Hung-ming.
  • Replacing sinecure posts with genuine scholars and establishing research institutes.
  • Admitting female students — Peking University became the first Chinese university to do so, in 1920.
  • Student self-governance and faculty participation in university administration.

Under Cai, Peking University became the centre of the New Culture Movement. Cai defended students arrested during the May Fourth Movement of 1919 and repeatedly resigned in protest when the warlord government interfered with the university, finally leaving the presidency in the mid-1920s.

Later career

In 1927 Cai became head of the University Council of the Nationalist government and in 1928 he founded and became the first president of Academia Sinica, China's national research academy. He also served as president of the Control Yuan. In the 1930s he co-founded the China League for Civil Rights with Soong Ching-ling and Lu Xun, and campaigned for the release of political prisoners. He spent his final years in Hong Kong, where he died on 5 March 1940.

Philosophy and thought

Cai advocated an education resting on five principles drawn from both Chinese and Western sources: utilitarian (military-citizen) education, intellectual education, moral education, education for a worldview, and aesthetic education. He argued that aesthetic education could perform some of the functions of religion in modern society, and he famously proposed "replacing religion with aesthetic education." His thought combined Confucian ethics with Kantian philosophy and anarchist and liberal ideas absorbed during his years in Europe.

Legacy

Cai Yuanpei is revered in China as the founding father of modern Chinese education. His stewardship of Peking University is still regarded as the model of the independent, liberal university, and his mausoleum stands at Aberdeen Cemetery in Hong Kong. Numerous schools, streets and institutions in China and Taiwan bear his name.
