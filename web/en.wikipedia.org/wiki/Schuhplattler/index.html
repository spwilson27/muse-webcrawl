Schuhplattler


Origin: Bavaria and the Alpine regions of Germany and Austria
Type: Traditional folk dance

The Schuhplattler is a traditional style of folk dance popular in Bavaria, Tyrol, and the Alpine regions of southern Germany and Austria. In this dance, the performers stomp, clap, and strike the soles of their shoes (Schuhe), thighs, and knees with their hands held flat (Plattler). The name derives from the German words Schuh (shoe) and platteln (to strike flat).

History and form

Origins trace to courtship dances of Alpine peasants, described as early as the nineteenth century, in which a young man displayed acrobatic feats to impress a partner. Traditionally performed by men and boys wearing lederhosen, the dance evolved from the Ländler and is accompanied by zither or, later, accordion music and a distinctive 3/4 rhythm.

There are more than 150 basic variants of the Schuhplattler, each often named after its village of origin — for example the Haushamer, the Reit im Winkl, and the "Rehbacher." Many dances incorporate props such as wood-chopping blocks or benches, and figures in which dancers are lifted or spun. Since the nineteenth century, folk-costume preservation societies (Trachtenvereine) in Bavaria and among German and Austrian diaspora communities worldwide have maintained the dance as a living tradition.
