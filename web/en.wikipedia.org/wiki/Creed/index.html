Creed

Creeds are formal statements of religious belief

A creed (from Latin credo, "I believe") is a statement of the shared beliefs of a religious community, generally in the form of a fixed formula summarising core tenets of the faith. The term is particularly associated with Christianity, where creeds developed in the early Church as baptismal confessions and as authoritative definitions of doctrine, but comparable statements exist in other religions, such as the Jewish Shema and the Islamic Shahada.

Christian creeds

The best-known Christian creeds are the Apostles' Creed, a baptismal statement from the early Western Church, and the Nicene Creed, formulated at the First Council of Nicaea in 325 and expanded at Constantinople in 381, which affirms the Trinity and the incarnation of Christ. The Athanasian Creed and the Definition of Chalcedon (451) are other influential statements. Creeds are recited in worship in Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, and many other churches, and serve as tests of doctrinal orthodoxy.

Function

Creeds function to teach the faith, to unite worshippers in a common confession, and to mark the boundaries of accepted belief against teachings judged heretical. In many traditions, confessing the creed is part of baptism and of regular liturgy. Broader Protestant confessions of faith — such as the Augsburg Confession and the Westminster Confession — are longer doctrinal documents serving a similar purpose.

Use of the term

By extension, "creed" is used for any set of guiding principles or beliefs, such as a political creed or a personal creed. In this general sense it is close in meaning to "credo" or "motto".
