Mount Lebanon

Mount Lebanon (Arabic: جبل لبنان, Jabal Lubnān) is a mountain range in Lebanon, extending about 170 km parallel to the Mediterranean coast. Its highest peak, Qurnat as Sawda, rises to 3,088 metres (10,131 ft), the highest point in the Levant outside Turkey. The range gave the country of Lebanon its name.

Geography

The range runs roughly north–south, separated from the Anti-Lebanon mountains by the Beqaa Valley. Its western slopes face the sea and receive heavy winter snowfall, supporting Lebanon's ski resorts; its eastern slopes descend more steeply to the Beqaa. Historically the mountains were covered in cedar forests — the famous Cedars of Lebanon — of which only remnant groves survive, now protected in reserves such as the Horsh Arz el-Rab.

History

Mount Lebanon served for centuries as a refuge for religious minorities, notably Maronite Christians and Druze. Under the Ottomans, the Mount Lebanon Emirate and later the Mutasarrifate of Mount Lebanon (1861–1918) enjoyed a degree of autonomy. The name "Mount Lebanon" is also used for one of Lebanon's governorates (Mohafazat Jabal Lubnan), which surrounds Beirut.
