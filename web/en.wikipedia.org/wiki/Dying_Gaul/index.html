Dying Gaul

YearRoman copy, c. 1st–2nd century AD
TypeMarble sculpture
Dimensions93 cm × 186 cm
LocationCapitoline Museums, Rome

The Dying Gaul (Italian: Galata morente), also called the Dying Galatian, is an ancient Roman marble copy of a Hellenistic bronze work, perhaps from the victory monuments erected by Attalus I of Pergamon around 230–220 BC to commemorate his victory over the Galatians in Anatolia. It depicts a mortally wounded Celtic warrior, sunk to the ground on his shield, supporting himself with one arm as life ebbs away.

Description

The figure is rendered with great naturalism and pathos. Details identify him as a Gaul or Galatian: the torc around his neck, his thick, matted hair and moustache, and the war trumpet (carnyx) and unsheathed sword lying beside him. His nudity, standard for heroic statuary, heightens both his dignity and his vulnerability. The work is celebrated for expressing the nobility of the defeated enemy rather than triumph over him.

History

The marble was recorded in the Ludovisi collection in Rome in 1623 and later passed to the Capitoline Museums. Napoleon took it to Paris after the Treaty of Tolentino (1797), where it was displayed in the Louvre; it was returned in 1816. Lord Byron praised it in Childe Harold's Pilgrimage, mistaking the Gaul for a gladiator. The statue remains one of the most famous works of ancient sculpture and a defining image of the Pergamene "baroque" style.
