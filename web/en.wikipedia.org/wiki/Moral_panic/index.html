Moral panic

A moral panic is the feeling of fear spread among many people that some evil threatens the well-being of society. It is "the process of arousing social concern over an issue – usually the work of moral entrepreneurs and the mass media".

The concept was first developed by the sociologists Jock Young and Stanley Cohen. Cohen's 1972 study Folk Devils and Moral Panics examined the media reaction to the Mods and Rockers disturbances in 1960s Britain, arguing that the media exaggerated the scale of the events and cast the participants as "folk devils" — visible reminders of what society should not be.

Cohen's model

Cohen identified stages in a moral panic: a condition or group emerges and is defined as a threat; the media present it in a stylised and stereotypical fashion; moral barricades are manned by editors, politicians and other "right-thinking" people; experts pronounce diagnoses and solutions; and the condition then disappears, submerges, or deteriorates.

Criteria

Sociologists Erich Goode and Nachman Ben-Yehuda proposed five criteria for a moral panic:

  1. Concern — heightened concern over a group's behaviour;
  2. Hostility — the group is cast as an enemy of respectable society;
  3. Consensus — fairly widespread agreement that the threat is real;
  4. Disproportionality — public concern exceeds the objective threat;
  5. Volatility — panics erupt and subside suddenly.

Examples

Frequently cited examples include the Satanic ritual abuse panic of the 1980s, fears over comic books in the 1950s, the "video nasties" controversy in Britain, panics over rave culture and ecstasy, the Salem witch trials, and recurrent panics about youth subcultures, violent video games, and strangers abducting children. The term "moral entrepreneur", coined by Howard Becker, describes individuals or groups who campaign to have their norms enforced and thereby generate or sustain a panic.

Criticism

Critics argue the concept can be applied too loosely, dismissing genuine public concerns; that disproportionality is difficult to measure; and that in fragmented modern media environments consensus is harder to establish than Cohen's mass-media model assumed.
