Minka

Minka (Japanese: 民家, literally "house of the people") are vernacular houses built in traditional Japanese architectural styles for commoners — farmers, artisans, and merchants — as opposed to the shōin-style residences of the samurai elite. Minka developed during the Edo period and were built into the early 20th century.

Characteristics

Minka are built with heavy timber post-and-beam frames, earthen or wooden floors, sliding partitions, and roofs of thatch, tile, or shingles. Rural farmhouses (nōka) often have steep thatched roofs; the dramatic multi-storey gasshō-zukuri houses of Shirakawa-gō and Gokayama, built to shed heavy snow, are UNESCO World Heritage Sites. Interiors center on an earthen-floored workspace (doma) and a sunken hearth (irori), whose smoke preserved the timbers and thatch.

Preservation

Because traditional houses were taxed by size and were costly to maintain, many minka were demolished in the postwar decades. Open-air museums such as Nihon Minka-en near Tokyo preserve relocated examples, and a preservation movement values minka for their craftsmanship and sustainable materials, related to the broader mingei appreciation of folk craft.
