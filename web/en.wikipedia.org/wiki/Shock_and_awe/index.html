Shock and awe

Shock and awe — technically known as rapid dominance — is a military doctrine based on the use of overwhelming power, dominant battlefield awareness and spectacular displays of force to paralyse the enemy's perception of the battlefield and destroy their will to fight.

Doctrine

The doctrine was articulated in a 1996 study by Harlan K. Ullman and James P. Wade of the US National Defense University, Shock and Awe: Achieving Rapid Dominance. Drawing on examples from the Roman legions to the Hiroshima bombing and the "Blitzkrieg", the authors argued that rapidly applied, precisely targeted force could achieve "rapid dominance" over an adversary's mind and will, shortening wars. The concept rests on near-total knowledge of the enemy and environment, rapidity and timeliness, and operational brilliance.

Iraq War

The term entered popular usage during the opening of the 2003 invasion of Iraq, when US officials promised a campaign of aerial bombardment of Baghdad designed to induce shock and awe. Senior correspondent accounts of the first night's strikes on 21–22 March 2003 — hundreds of cruise missiles and precision bombs — popularised the phrase worldwide. Critics debated whether the actual bombing matched the rhetoric and whether airpower alone achieved the doctrine's psychological aims, especially as the conflict evolved into a prolonged insurgency.
