A ninja (忍者) or shinobi (忍び) was a covert agent, mercenary or guerrilla fighter in feudal Japan. The functions of a ninja included siege infiltration, espionage, sabotage, deception and assassination. Their covert methods of waging irregular warfare were deemed dishonourable by the samurai caste, who observed strict rules about honour and combat.

History

Precursors appear in the Nanboku-chō period, but the shinobi of Iga and Kōga — two mountainous regions whose self-governing communities trained in stealth arts — became famous during the Sengoku period of civil war. Daimyo such as Takeda Shingen and Uesugi Kenshin employed shinobi as spies; Oda Nobunaga's brutal conquest of Iga in 1581 (the Tenshō Iga War) destroyed their independence. Under the Tokugawa shogunate, ninja activity declined, though the shogunate retained Iga and Kōga men as palace guards and police.

Training and equipment

Surviving manuals such as the Bansenshūkai (1676) describe training in disguise, explosives, water crossing, climbing and survival. Iconic equipment in popular culture includes shuriken (throwing stars), kunai, caltrops, smoke bombs and the straight-bladed ninjatō — several of which owe more to later imagination than to historical record. In reality, ninja typically disguised themselves as peasants, monks or merchants; the black costume derives from kabuki theatre, where stagehands (kuroko) dressed in black to signify invisibility.

In popular culture

Edo-period fiction and woodblock prints romanticised the ninja with supernatural powers. In the 20th century, ninja became global pop-culture icons through films, manga and anime (e.g. Naruto), martial-arts booms around ninjutsu schools, and franchises such as Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles. The cities of Iga and Kōka in Japan now promote ninja heritage with museums and festivals.
