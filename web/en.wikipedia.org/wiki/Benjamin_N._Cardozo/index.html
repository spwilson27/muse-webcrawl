Benjamin Nathan Cardozo (24 May 1870 – 9 July 1938) was an American lawyer and jurist who served as an Associate Justice of the Supreme Court of the United States from 1932 until his death in 1938. Before that he served on the New York Court of Appeals, becoming its Chief Judge, and wrote a series of opinions that reshaped American common law, especially in tort and contract.

New York Court of Appeals

Cardozo was elected to the New York Supreme Court in 1913 and almost immediately designated to the Court of Appeals, where he sat from 1914 to 1932. His opinions include MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co. (1916), which abolished the privity requirement in products cases and laid the foundations of modern products liability; Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad Co. (1928), the classic opinion on duty and proximate cause in negligence; and Jacob & Youngs v. Kent (1921) on substantial performance in contract. His Storrs Lectures at Yale, published as The Nature of the Judicial Process (1921), remain a classic account of how judges decide cases.

Supreme Court

Appointed by President Herbert Hoover to succeed Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., Cardozo joined Justices Brandeis and Stone in the liberal "Three Musketeers". He wrote for the Court in important New Deal-era cases including Helvering v. Davis (1937), upholding the Social Security Act, and Palko v. Connecticut (1937), whose "ordered liberty" analysis of selective incorporation influenced later due-process jurisprudence.

Legacy

Cardozo's prose style and progressive common-law method made him one of the most admired American judges of the 20th century. The Benjamin N. Cardozo School of Law of Yeshiva University is named for him.
