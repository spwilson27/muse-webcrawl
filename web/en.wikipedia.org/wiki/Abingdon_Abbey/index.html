Abingdon Abbey was a Benedictine monastery at Abingdon in the Thames valley, traditionally founded in the seventh century and refounded in the tenth as a spearhead of the English Benedictine reform. For nearly six centuries it was one of the wealthiest and most important abbeys of southern England, until its surrender in the Dissolution of the Monasteries in 1538.

History

Monastic tradition traced the foundation to about 675, under the West Saxon kings, with the first abbot named as Hæha or Cissa. The community was destroyed or dispersed during the Danish wars and refounded around 954 by King Eadred, who installed Æthelwold — later bishop of Winchester and a leader of the monastic revival — as abbot. After the Norman Conquest the abbey retained its wealth; its twelfth-century abbot Faritius, a Lombard physician, began a great new church. The abbey's estates and the town market made it a power in Berkshire, resented by the townspeople, who rioted against the monks in 1327.

Dissolution and remains

Abbot Thomas Pentecost (Rowland) surrendered the abbey to Henry VIII's commissioners on 9 February 1538, one of the earliest of the great houses to fall; the site and estates passed to Sir Thomas Seymour and then to Christ's Hospital. The great church was demolished, but several monastic buildings survive in the Abbey Gardens area — the Abbey Exchequer, the timber-framed Long Gallery, the bakehouse and gateway — among the finest surviving domestic monastic buildings in England, now in civic and theatre use.
