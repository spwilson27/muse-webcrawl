The siemens (symbol: S) is the SI unit of electrical conductance, electric susceptance and electric admittance. It is the reciprocal of resistance: one siemens equals one ampere per volt, or one inverse ohm (Ω−1). Conductance G, susceptance B and admittance Y are all measured in siemens. The unit is named after the German inventor and industrialist Werner von Siemens.

Definition

For an element conducting direct current, conductance is defined by G = 1/R = I/V, where R is electrical resistance, I is the current through the element and V is the voltage across it. Thus 1 S = 1 A/V = 1 Ω−1. For alternating current, admittance is the complex ratio of current to voltage, and its imaginary part is the susceptance; both are expressed in siemens.

History

The name was adopted by the 14th General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1971. Before that, the reciprocal ohm was widely called the mho (symbol ℧, an inverted omega), a name coined by Lord Kelvin — "ohm" spelled backwards. Werner von Siemens had himself proposed a unit of resistance in 1860 based on a column of mercury; the modern unit honours him for conductance instead. In English the same form serves as singular and plural: one siemens, two siemens.

Multiples

Very small conductances are commonly expressed in microsiemens (μS) and millisiemens (mS), for example in measurements of water conductivity. For a material, conductivity σ (siemens per metre, S/m) relates to conductance through the geometry of the sample.
