Oneida language

The Oneida language (Oneida: Onʌyotaʔa:ka, "People of the Standing Stone") is an Iroquoian language spoken by the Oneida people, one of the Five Nations of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy). It is spoken today in communities in New York, Wisconsin, and Ontario, Canada.

Status

Oneida is critically endangered. Fluent first-language speakers number only in the dozens to a few hundred, most of them elders, concentrated in the Oneida Nation of the Thames in Ontario and in Wisconsin. Language revitalization programs, immersion classes, and dictionaries have been developed in each community to teach new generations of speakers.

Features

Like other Northern Iroquoian languages, Oneida is polysynthetic: complex words are built from many morphemes, and a single word can express what English requires a whole sentence to say. The language has a small consonant inventory, nasalized vowels, and distinctive whispered or devoiced syllables at the ends of words. Orthographies differ slightly between the New York, Wisconsin, and Ontario communities.
