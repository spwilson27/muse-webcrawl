Community-supported agriculture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Community-supported agriculture (CSA) is a model of agriculture and food distribution in which consumers ("members" or "shareholders") buy a subscription share of a farm's harvest in advance of the growing season and receive regular deliveries or pickups of seasonal produce throughout it. By paying up front, members share both the risks (poor harvests) and the rewards (bumper crops) of farming with the grower, and give the farm working capital when it is most needed.

History

The model has roots in Japan's teikei ("partnership") movement of the 1960s–70s, in which mothers concerned about food safety contracted directly with farmers, and in Swiss and German biodynamic farming circles. It took hold in North America in the mid-1980s — early farms included Indian Line Farm in Massachusetts and Temple-Wilton Community Farm in New Hampshire — and grew to thousands of programmes. Related schemes exist worldwide, including France's AMAP and Britain's community-supported agriculture network under the Soil Association.

Structure

A typical CSA provides a weekly box of vegetables (full or half shares), sometimes with add-ons of fruit, eggs, flowers, meat, or dairy. Farms may ask members to work a few hours per season, invite them to farm events, and communicate through newsletters with recipes for unfamiliar vegetables. Variations include market-style CSAs, in which members choose produce at a farm stand against a prepaid credit, multi-farm cooperatives, and workplace or institutional CSAs.

Criticisms and challenges

CSA has been praised for reconnecting eaters with farms, keeping food spending local, and supporting diversified organic production. Criticisms include the burden on members of seasonal gluts and unfamiliar produce, member attrition ("churn"), the model's limited reach among low-income households, and financial precarity for farmers despite upfront payments.
