Science and Civilisation in China

Author: Joseph Needham (and collaborators)

Publisher: Cambridge University Press

First volume: 1954

Planned scope: 7 volumes in 27 parts

Science and Civilisation in China (1954– ) is a monumental multi-volume history of science and technology in China, initiated by the British biochemist and historian Joseph Needham (1900–1995) and published by Cambridge University Press. It transformed Western understanding of China's scientific past, documenting in exhaustive detail the achievements of Chinese scholars and engineers in fields from astronomy and mathematics to medicine, engineering and alchemy.

Origins

Needham became fascinated by Chinese civilisation while teaching biochemistry at Cambridge, where Chinese research students — among them Lu Gwei-djen, who became a lifelong collaborator — sparked his interest in the history of Chinese science. During the Second World War he served in Chongqing as director of the Sino-British Science Co-operation Office, travelling widely in China and collecting books and material. After the war he secured the support of Cambridge University Press for a comprehensive history; the first volume appeared in 1954.

Structure

The work was planned in seven volumes:

  1. Introductory Orientations (1954)
  2. History of Scientific Thought (1956)
  3. Mathematics and the Sciences of the Heavens and the Earth (1959)
  4. Physics and Physical Technology — issued in parts on physics, mechanical engineering, and civil engineering and nautics
  5. Chemistry and Chemical Technology — many parts, covering alchemy, gunpowder and explosives, paper and printing, textiles, agriculture-related industries, and more
  6. Biology and Biological Technology — botany, agriculture, medicine (including a volume by Lu Gwei-djen on acupuncture and moxibustion)
  7. The Social Background — language, education and the concluding reflections; a general index completes the series

Because of the project's scale, most later parts were written by specialist collaborators — including Wang Ling, Kenneth Robinson, Nathan Sivin, Francesca Bray and Christopher Cullen — under Needham's editorship, and publication continued after his death under the auspices of the Needham Research Institute in Cambridge.

The "Needham Question"

Running through the work is the famous Needham Question (or "grand question"): why did modern science — the mathematisation of hypotheses about nature — develop in Europe rather than in China, given that China had been far ahead of the West in scientific and technical knowledge for most of the preceding two millennia? Needham's own answers emphasised social and economic structures, particularly China's bureaucratic feudalism and the absence of a mercantile class able to challenge it. The question has been debated — and criticised as Eurocentric in framing — ever since.

Reception and influence

The series is regarded as one of the greatest scholarly undertakings of the twentieth century. It permanently changed the history of science by demonstrating the depth and priority of Chinese achievements — such as printing, the compass, gunpowder, cast iron, seismographs and early chemical technology — and by tracing many transmissions of technique from China to the West. An abridged version by Colin Ronan made the work accessible to general readers.
