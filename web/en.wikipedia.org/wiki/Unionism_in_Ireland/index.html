Unionism in Ireland

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Unionism in Ireland is a political tradition and ideology that supports the continued political union between Ireland — today, Northern Ireland — and Great Britain within the United Kingdom. Historically rooted in the Acts of Union 1800, which merged the Kingdom of Ireland with Great Britain, Irish unionism drew its strength chiefly from Protestants, particularly Presbyterians and Anglicans in Ulster, though it once had significant support across the island. Unionists emphasised the economic benefits of the Union, loyalty to the Crown, and — during the Home Rule crises — the danger that a Dublin parliament would subordinate Protestants: "Home Rule is Rome Rule."

History

Unionism organised in opposition to the Home Rule Bills of 1886, 1893, and 1912. Under Edward Carson, Ulster unionists signed the Ulster Covenant (1912), formed the Ulster Volunteer Force, and imported arms at Larne, threatening armed resistance. After the First World War and the Irish War of Independence, the Government of Ireland Act 1920 partitioned Ireland; six north-eastern counties formed Northern Ireland, remaining in the UK with its own devolved parliament at Stormont, dominated by the Ulster Unionist Party until its prorogation in 1972. During the Troubles (c. 1968–1998), unionist politics split between the UUP and Ian Paisley's more hardline Democratic Unionist Party (DUP), while loyalist paramilitaries waged their own campaigns. The 1998 Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement enshrined the principle of consent: Northern Ireland remains in the UK unless majorities on both sides of the border vote otherwise.

Contemporary unionism

Since 2003 the DUP has been the largest unionist party, governing in power-sharing with nationalists, though the cross-community Alliance Party's growth and demographic change have unsettled the unionist/nationalist duopoly. Brexit reopened constitutional questions: Northern Ireland voted Remain, and unionists fiercely opposed the Irish Sea border arrangements of the Northern Ireland Protocol and its successor, the Windsor Framework. Unionist parties today include the DUP, the Ulster Unionist Party, and Traditional Unionist Voice; cultural institutions such as the Orange Order and the marching-band tradition remain prominent expressions of unionist identity.
