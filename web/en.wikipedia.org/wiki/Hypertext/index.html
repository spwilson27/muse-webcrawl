Hypertext

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of hypertext documents connected by highlighted links in a web-like graph]

Hypertext is text displayed on a computer display or other electronic device with references (hyperlinks) to other text that the reader can immediately access. Hypertext documents are interconnected in a structure that need not be linear like a book: readers choose their own paths through a network of information.

The idea has a long intellectual lineage, from Vannevar Bush's hypothetical Memex machine (1945) — a desk of linked microfilm — to Ted Nelson, who coined the words 'hypertext' and 'hypermedia' in the 1960s and pursued the vision for decades in his Project Xanadu, with its ambitions of two-way links, transclusion and micropayments for quoted content. Douglas Engelbart's NLS system demonstrated working hypertext in the famous 1968 'Mother of All Demos', and Apple's HyperCard (1987) brought a popular form of it to personal computers.

Hypertext became a planetary medium when Tim Berners-Lee implemented a simple, one-way, decentralised version of it at CERN in 1989–1991: the World Wide Web, with its documents in HTML, addresses in URLs and transport in HTTP. Nelson's richer design was never finished; Berners-Lee's simpler one conquered the world — a recurring lesson in the history of technology.
