Interpreting (or interpretation) is the facilitation of oral or sign-language communication between speakers of different languages, performed by an interpreter. Unlike translation, which works on written text, interpreting happens in real time and involves not only rendering meaning but conveying register, tone and intent. Professional standards — notably those of AIIC, the International Association of Conference Interpreters — require that an interpreter reproduce the speaker's message faithfully in the first person.

Modes

In simultaneous interpreting the interpreter speaks while the source speech continues, typically from a soundproof booth with headphones, as at the United Nations. In consecutive interpreting the speaker pauses every few minutes and the interpreter renders the segment, often from structured notes. Whispered interpreting (chuchotage) serves one or two listeners. Sign-language interpreting bridges deaf and hearing communities.

History

Interpreting is as old as multilingual contact; dragomans mediated for centuries in the Ottoman world. The profession crystallised after the First World War at the Paris Peace Conference, and simultaneous interpreting achieved its breakthrough at the Nuremberg trials (1945–46), where the four-language system made a lengthy trial practicable.

Settings

Beyond diplomacy and conferences, interpreters work in courts, hospitals, asylum hearings, business negotiations and media broadcasts. Community and medical interpreting have developed their own codes of ethics, stressing confidentiality and impartiality.
