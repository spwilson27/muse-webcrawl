Geology of Indonesia

The geology of Indonesia is among the most complex and active on Earth. The archipelago sits at the junction of several tectonic plates — the Eurasian, Indo-Australian, Pacific, and Philippine Sea plates — whose convergence drives intense volcanism, earthquakes, and mountain building along the Pacific Ring of Fire.

Tectonic setting

Two great volcanic arcs cross the country: the Sunda Arc, running from Sumatra through Java to the Banda Sea, and the Banda Arc in the east. The western islands rest on the relatively stable Sunda Shelf, while New Guinea in the east lies on the Australian continental margin. Deep oceanic trenches, such as the Java Trench, mark subduction zones offshore.

Volcanism and resources

Indonesia has more than 130 active volcanoes, including Merapi, Krakatoa, and Tambora. The Toba caldera in Sumatra records a super-eruption about 74,000 years ago. The country's geology also yields major resources: oil and gas in Sumatra and Kalimantan, tin on Bangka, copper and gold in Papua, and extensive coal deposits.
