British government bond

A British government bond, commonly known as a gilt (or gilt-edged security), is a bond issued by HM Treasury on behalf of the government of the United Kingdom. The term "gilt" derives from the gilded edges once used on the certificates, and gilts are regarded as among the lowest-risk sterling investments because they are backed by the full faith and credit of the UK government.

Types of gilt

Conventional gilts

Conventional gilts pay a fixed coupon every six months and repay their principal (par value of £100) at maturity. They are identified by their coupon and maturity date, for example "4¼% Treasury Gilt 2050". Maturities range from a few months to more than 50 years; in 2020 the United Kingdom issued a gilt maturing in 2071.

Index-linked gilts

Introduced in 1981, index-linked gilts have coupons and principal adjusted in line with inflation, measured by the Retail Prices Index (RPI). They are widely held by pension funds seeking to match inflation-linked liabilities.

Undated gilts

A small number of undated stocks, such as War Loan, were issued without a fixed redemption date. The government redeemed the last of these, including the 3½% War Loan, in 2015.

Issuance and market

Since 1998 gilts have been issued by the UK Debt Management Office (DMO), an executive agency of HM Treasury, primarily by auction to a group of market makers known as gilt-edged market makers (GEMMs). Gilts are listed on the London Stock Exchange and can be bought by private investors. The Bank of England accumulated a large share of the gilt market through its quantitative easing programme after 2009.
