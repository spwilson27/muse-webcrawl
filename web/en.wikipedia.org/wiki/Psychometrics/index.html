Psychometrics is a field of study within psychology concerned with the theory and technique of measurement. Psychometrics generally covers specialised fields within psychology and education devoted to testing, measurement, assessment, and related activities. It is concerned with the objective measurement of latent constructs that cannot be directly observed, such as intelligence, personality traits, abilities, and attitudes.

History

The field traces its origins to work by Charles Darwin's cousin Francis Galton, who attempted to measure individual differences in sensory and motor capacities, and to the psychophysical methods of Ernst Weber and Gustav Fechner. Charles Spearman introduced factor analysis and the concept of a general intelligence factor (g), while Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon produced the first practical intelligence test in 1905. In the twentieth century the field was shaped by Louis Leon Thurstone's multiple-factor models, by classical test theory, and later by Georg Rasch's measurement models and item response theory.

Key concepts

Reliability is the consistency of a measure — whether the same result can be reproduced under similar conditions — commonly summarised by coefficients such as Cronbach's alpha and test–retest correlations. Validity concerns whether a test measures what it claims to measure, encompassing content, criterion, and construct validity. Modern approaches model responses to individual items: item response theory (IRT) estimates the probability of a given response as a function of a person's latent trait level and item parameters such as difficulty and discrimination, enabling adaptive testing in which items are selected in real time to match the examinee.

Applications

Psychometric methods underpin educational testing, clinical and neuropsychological assessment, personnel selection, and large-scale survey measurement. Professional standards, such as the Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing, govern the development, validation, and fair use of tests.
