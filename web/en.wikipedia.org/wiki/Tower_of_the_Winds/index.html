The Tower of the Winds, also called the Horologion of Andronikos Kyrrhestes, is an octagonal Pentelic marble tower in the Roman Agora in Athens, named after the eight wind deities (the Anemoi) whose reliefs adorn its sides. It functioned as a horologion or timepiece, combining sundials, a water clock (clepsydra), and a wind vane, and is considered the world's first meteorological station.

Description

The tower stands 12.1 metres (40 ft) tall with a diameter of about 8 metres (26 ft). It has the form of an octagon, with a cylindrical annex on the south side housing the mechanism of the water clock and a small porch on the northeast and northwest faces. On top of the conical roof stood a bronze Triton acting as a wind vane, holding a rod that pointed to the wind's direction; the vane has not survived.

Each of the eight sides faces a point of the compass and carries a frieze with a relief figure of the corresponding wind god: Boreas (north), Kaikias (northeast), Apeliotes (east), Eurus (southeast), Notus (south), Lips (southwest), Zephyrus (west) and Skiron (northwest). Below each figure were the lines of a sundial, so that the tower told the time on all its faces. Inside, a water clock was driven by water piped from a spring on the Acropolis.

History

The tower is traditionally attributed to the astronomer Andronicus of Cyrrhus and dated to the second century BC, though some scholars place it in the first century BC, around 50 BC, shortly before the Roman Agora itself was completed. Varro and Vitruvius both refer to the structure.

In early Christian times the tower was used as a church belfry. During the Ottoman period it became a tekke used by whirling dervishes of the Mevlevi order, and the building was partly buried in accumulated earth. It was fully excavated by the Greek Archaeological Society in the nineteenth century.

Influence

The Tower of the Winds inspired several later buildings, including the eighteenth-century Tower of the Winds at Shugborough Hall and the observatory tower of the Radcliffe Observatory in Oxford, modelled directly on it. The wind gods depicted on the tower became the classical source for depictions of the eight winds.
