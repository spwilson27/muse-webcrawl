Lusatia (German: Lausitz; Upper Sorbian: Łužica; Lower Sorbian: Łužyca; Polish: Łużyce) is a historical region in Central Europe, stretching from the Bóbr and Kwisa rivers in the east to the Pulsnitz and Black Elster in the west, today split between Germany (the states of Saxony and Brandenburg) and Poland (Lower Silesian and Lubusz voivodeships). It is traditionally divided into Lower Lusatia in the flat north, centred on Cottbus, and Upper Lusatia in the hillier south, centred on Bautzen and Görlitz. Lusatia is the homeland of the Sorbs, a West Slavic minority recognised in Germany, whose languages — Upper and Lower Sorbian — are still spoken and officially protected there.

History

The region takes its name from the Slavic tribe of the Lusici, settled in the north; the south was the land of the Milceni, ancestors of the Upper Sorbs. From the 10th century the margraves of Meissen and the Holy Roman Empire extended German rule and settlement (the Ostsiedlung). Upper Lusatia's six principal towns formed the Lusatian League of Six Cities in 1346, which endured until 1815. Both Lusatias passed between the margraves of Brandenburg, the Bohemian Crown (from the 14th–15th centuries) and the Electorate of Saxony (1635), until the Congress of Vienna in 1815 gave Lower Lusatia and north-eastern Upper Lusatia to Prussia. In 1945 the Oder–Neisse line made the Neisse the German-Polish border, and the part of Lusatia east of the river became Polish, its German population expelled.

Economy and landscape

Lower Lusatia is one of Germany's main lignite (brown coal) mining regions, with huge open-cast mines and power stations such as Jänschwalde and Schwarze Pumpe; the post-mining landscape is being flooded to create the Lusatian Lake District (Lausitzer Seenland), one of Europe's largest artificial lake landscapes. The Spreewald, a UNESCO biosphere reserve of canals and meadows southeast of Berlin, lies in Lower Lusatia. Upper Lusatia is known for its textile tradition, the Zittau Mountains and the Rakotzbrücke (Devil's Bridge) at Kromlau.

Sorbian culture

Bilingual German–Sorbian signage is standard in the Sorbian settlement area. Institutions such as the Domowina (the Sorbs' umbrella organisation), Sorbian schools, and the Wendish Museum in Cottbus maintain the language and customs, including the painted Easter eggs and the Bird Wedding children's festival.
