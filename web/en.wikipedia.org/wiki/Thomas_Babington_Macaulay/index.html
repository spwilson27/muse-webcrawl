Thomas Babington Macaulay, 1st Baron Macaulay (25 October 1800 – 28 December 1859) was a British historian, essayist and Whig politician. His five-volume History of England was a phenomenal bestseller that entrenched the "Whig interpretation of history" — the story of English progress toward liberty and constitutional government — in the Victorian mind. As Law Member in India he drafted the Indian Penal Code and authored the famous 1835 "Minute on Education" promoting English-language instruction.

Early life and Parliament

Macaulay was the son of Zachary Macaulay, a leading abolitionist of the Clapham Sect, and a child prodigy educated at Trinity College, Cambridge. Called to the bar, he made his name with brilliant essays for the Edinburgh Review beginning with his 1825 essay on Milton. Entering Parliament in 1830, he spoke powerfully for the Reform Bill and served on the Supreme Council of India (1834–1838), where his Minute championed English education to create "a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste," and where he presided over the commission that produced the draft Indian Penal Code, enacted in 1860.

Historian

Back in Britain, Macaulay served as Secretary at War and Paymaster General, was raised to the peerage in 1857, and devoted his later years to the History of England from the Accession of James the Second (1848–1855, last volume posthumous). Its vivid narrative of the Glorious Revolution and William III sold in tens of thousands and was read across the English-speaking world. His poems, the Lays of Ancient Rome (1842) — including "Horatius at the Bridge" — were memorised by generations of schoolchildren. Macaulay died in 1859 and was buried in Westminster Abbey.

Assessment

Twentieth-century historians, notably Herbert Butterfield, criticised Macaulay's Whig teleology and partisanship, yet his literary power and command of detail continue to be admired, and his influence on British historical consciousness remains immense.
