Nissl body

A Nissl body, also known as Nissl substance or tigroid substance, is a large granular structure found in neurons. It consists of rough endoplasmic reticulum studded with ribosomes together with free ribosomes, and is the principal site of protein synthesis in the nerve cell. Nissl bodies are named after the German neuropathologist and psychiatrist Franz Nissl (1860–1919), who developed the staining method that reveals them.

Staining and appearance

Nissl substance is strongly basophilic: it takes up basic aniline dyes such as cresyl violet, toluidine blue, and thionin, a technique known as Nissl staining. Under the light microscope, Nissl bodies appear as coarse, dark granules or "tigroid" clumps filling the perikaryon (cell body) and extending into the dendrites, but they are conspicuously absent from the axon hillock and the axon itself. Their abundance reflects the high rate of protein synthesis neurons require to maintain their large cytoplasmic volume.

Function

As rough endoplasmic reticulum, Nissl substance synthesises proteins including enzymes for neurotransmitter production, membrane receptors, and ion channels. The prominence of the material in motor neurons and other large neurons corresponds to their extensive axonal arbors.

Chromatolysis

Following injury to an axon, the Nissl bodies of the parent cell disperse and the cytoplasm loses its basophilic staining — a reaction called chromatolysis (or the axon reaction). The cell body swells, the nucleus moves to an eccentric position, and protein synthesis is redirected toward repair. Chromatolysis is thus a classic histological sign of neuronal injury and regeneration.
