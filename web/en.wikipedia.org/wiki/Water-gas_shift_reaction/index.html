The water–gas shift reaction (WGSR) describes the reaction of carbon monoxide and water vapor to form carbon dioxide and hydrogen:

H2O + CO → H2 + CO2    ΔH = −41.1 kJ/mol

It is a key industrial step for increasing hydrogen yield from syngas and for adjusting the H2/CO ratio in downstream synthesis processes.

History

The reaction was reported by Italian physicist Felice Fontana in 1780. Its industrial value was realized only in the early twentieth century, when Carl Bosch and colleagues at BASF developed catalysts making it practical for the hydrogen feed of the Haber process for ammonia synthesis.

Applications

  • Production of high-purity hydrogen for refineries and fuel cells
  • Adjusting syngas composition for methanol synthesis and the Fischer–Tropsch process
  • Reducing toxic carbon monoxide in gas streams, e.g. in fuel processing for proton-exchange-membrane fuel cells

Industrial practice

Industry runs the shift in two stages to balance kinetics against equilibrium. A high-temperature shift (HTS, roughly 310–450 °C over an iron oxide–chromium oxide catalyst) converts most CO quickly. A low-temperature shift (LTS, roughly 200–250 °C over copper–zinc oxide on alumina) then exploits the more favourable equilibrium at lower temperature to reduce CO to a few tenths of a percent. The copper catalyst is sensitive to sulfur poisoning and sintering, so feed purification is critical.

Thermodynamics and kinetics

The reaction is mildly exothermic and equimolar, so pressure has no effect on its equilibrium, but conversion is favoured by low temperature and excess steam. These opposing kinetic and thermodynamic demands motivate the two-bed design. A related biological version of the reaction is exploited by carboxydotrophic bacteria using carbon monoxide dehydrogenase enzymes.
