Pope Nicholas V (Latin: Nicolaus V; 13 November 1397 – 24 March 1455), born Tommaso Parentucelli, was head of the Catholic Church from 6 March 1447 until his death in 1455. A humanist scholar, he is regarded as the first Renaissance pope: he founded the Vatican Library and conceived the rebuilding of St. Peter's Basilica and the renewal of Rome.

Early life

Parentucelli was born in Sarzana and studied at Bologna and Florence, earning a reputation as a brilliant teacher of the humanities. He served Niccolò Albergati and Eugenius IV, attended the Council of Florence, and was created bishop of Bologna and then cardinal shortly before his election.

Pontificate

Nicholas ended the last remnants of the conciliar schism by securing the resignation of Antipope Felix V in 1449, restoring the unity of the Western Church. The Jubilee of 1450 brought vast crowds — and revenue — to Rome. His plans for the city, developed with Leon Battista Alberti, included new streets, restored aqueducts and churches, and a new St. Peter's, though only the beginnings were executed.

He assembled thousands of Latin and Greek manuscripts, founding the Vatican Library, and patronised translators and scholars such as Lorenzo Valla, Poggio Bracciolini and George of Trebizond. Nicholas issued the bull Dum Diversas (1452) and Romanus Pontifex (1455), which granted Portugal rights to conquer and enslave non-Christian peoples in newly reached lands — documents later cited in the doctrine of discovery. His final years were darkened by the fall of Constantinople to the Ottomans in 1453, whose recapture he vainly sought to organise.
