Boykos

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Boykos in the Ukrainian Carpathians, early 20th century.

The Boykos (Ukrainian: бойки, romanized: boiky; Polish: Bojkowie) are an ethnographic group of Ukrainian highlanders inhabiting the Carpathian Mountains in south-western Ukraine and adjacent areas of Poland and Slovakia. Together with the Hutsuls to the east and the Lemkos to the west, the Boykos are one of the three principal groups of Ukrainian Carpathian highlanders, historically known collectively as part of the Rusyn/Ukr­ainian mountain population.

Territory

The Boyko region, called Boikivshchyna, stretches along the northern and southern slopes of the Carpathians roughly between the San and Uzh rivers in the west and the Limnytsia and Teresva in the east. In Ukraine it covers mountain districts of Lviv, Ivano-Frankivsk, and Zakarpattia oblasts; smaller portions historically extended into what is now south-eastern Poland (the Bieszczady) and north-eastern Slovakia. The name's origin is debated—it may come from the dialect word boi/boje, an interjection characteristic of local speech, or from an older tribal name.

Culture

Boyko culture was shaped by a pastoral mountain economy of cattle and sheep herding, haymaking on steep meadows, and forestry. The Boykos are renowned for their wooden church architecture: the classic Boyko church is a tripartite log structure with three tent-like or stepped tiers of shingled roofs, several of which survive in villages and open-air museums such as the one at Lviv. Traditional dress featured homespun linen shirts with distinctive red-and-black or multicoloured embroidery, sheepskin waistcoats, and woollen trousers. The Boyko dialect belongs to the Carpathian group of Ukrainian dialects and preserves archaic vocabulary and local words.

History

The Boyko lands were divided by the partitions of Poland between Austria and later Poland, and the highlanders' distinct identity persisted through both empires. After the Second World War the border changes between Poland and the Soviet Union, together with population transfers and Poland's Operation Vistula (1947), largely emptied the Polish side of its Boyko population, many of whom were resettled in western Poland or in Soviet Ukraine. In independent Ukraine, Boyko identity is recognised as a regional-ethnographic identity within the Ukrainian nation, celebrated in festivals, museums, and the preservation of wooden churches, some of which form part of the UNESCO-listed Wooden Tserkvas of the Carpathian Region.
