Drug tolerance

Drug tolerance is a pharmacological concept describing subjects' reduced reaction to a drug following its repeated use. Increasing its dosage may re-amplify the drug's effects; however, this may accelerate tolerance, further reducing the drug's effects. Drug tolerance is indicative of drug use and is a contributing factor to drug dependence and drug-related behavioural disorders. Tolerance develops unevenly: with drugs such as opioids, tolerance to respiratory depression develops more slowly than tolerance to analgesia and euphoria, which contributes to overdose risk.

Types

Pharmacokinetic tolerance

Pharmacokinetic (metabolic) tolerance occurs when repeated exposure increases the rate at which the body eliminates the drug, for example by induction of metabolising enzymes such as cytochrome P450 enzymes. The classic example is barbiturate and alcohol induction of hepatic enzymes, which shortens the drug's half-life.

Pharmacodynamic tolerance

Pharmacodynamic (functional) tolerance arises from adaptive changes in the target tissue: receptor downregulation or desensitisation, changes in signal-transduction pathways, and compensatory homeostatic responses that oppose the drug's effect. The dose–response curve shifts to the right, so a higher dose is required for the same response.

Behavioural tolerance

Behavioural or learned tolerance results from practice and environmental conditioning. Organisms may learn to compensate for drug-induced impairment, and tolerance can be context-specific: cues associated with drug administration trigger conditioned compensatory responses, which is one explanation for overdoses occurring when a drug is taken in a novel environment.

Related phenomena

Tachyphylaxis is a rapid, acute form of tolerance that can appear after a single dose, as with some sympathomimetic decongestants. Cross-tolerance occurs when tolerance to one drug confers tolerance to another, usually of the same class — for example between alcohol and benzodiazepines. Reverse tolerance (sensitisation) is the opposite effect, in which repeated use increases response, seen with stimulants.

Clinical significance

Tolerance is a normal pharmacological response and does not by itself imply addiction, which requires compulsive drug-seeking behaviour. Clinically, tolerance may necessitate dose escalation (as in chronic pain management with opioids) or drug rotation, and loss of tolerance after a period of abstinence is a major risk factor for overdose on relapse.
