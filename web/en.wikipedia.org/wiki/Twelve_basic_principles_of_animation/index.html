Twelve basic principles of animation

The twelve basic principles of animation were introduced by the Disney animators Ollie Johnston and Frank Thomas in their 1981 book The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation. The principles, distilled from the working methods of Disney's animators from the 1930s onward, aim to create characters that seem to obey the basic laws of physics while allowing for exaggeration and emotional expression. They remain foundational to traditional, computer, and stop-motion animation.

The principles

  1. Squash and stretch — the deformation of an object to convey weight, flexibility, and impact; the most important principle, exemplified by a bouncing ball.
  2. Anticipation — preparing the audience for an action with a small contrary movement, such as a crouch before a jump.
  3. Staging — presenting an idea so it is unmistakably clear, through pose, framing, and composition.
  4. Straight ahead action and pose to pose — the two approaches to drawing a scene: improvising frame by frame, or planning key poses first.
  5. Follow through and overlapping action — the tendency of appendages and loose parts to keep moving after the main body stops.
  6. Slow in and slow out — easing: objects accelerate and decelerate rather than moving at constant speed.
  7. Arc — natural motion follows arched trajectories rather than straight lines.
  8. Secondary action — supporting actions that enrich the main action, such as facial expressions while walking.
  9. Timing — the number of drawings used for an action, which determines its apparent speed and weight.
  10. Exaggeration — pushing movements and expressions beyond realism for clarity and effect without breaking believability.
  11. Solid drawing — the mastery of form, volume, weight, and anatomy in drawing three-dimensional-looking figures.
  12. Appeal — the charisma of a character: a clear, pleasing design that holds the audience's interest.

Legacy

Johnston and Thomas's book is often called the "bible of animation". The principles were carried over wholesale into 3D computer animation — John Lasseter's 1987 SIGGRAPH paper applied them to CGI — and they continue to be taught in animation schools and used in video games, motion graphics, and user-interface animation.
