High-altitude cerebral edema


MRI signs of HACE

High-altitude cerebral edema (HACE) is a medical condition in which the brain swells with fluid because of the physiological effects of traveling to a high altitude. It generally appears in individuals who have acute mountain sickness (AMS) and involves disorientation, lethargy, and nausea among other symptoms. It occurs when the body fails to acclimatize while ascending to a high altitude.

Signs and symptoms

Early symptoms commonly include severe headache, confusion, loss of coordination (truncal ataxia — often considered the cardinal sign), slurred speech, and altered consciousness. Without treatment, HACE can progress to coma and death from brain herniation, sometimes within 24 hours. It is regarded as the end-stage of the AMS spectrum and often occurs together with high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE).

Cause and prevention

HACE is thought to result from vasodilation of cerebral blood vessels in response to hypoxia, increasing cerebral blood volume and pressure, which leads to vasogenic edema and raised intracranial pressure. Risk factors include rapid ascent, altitude above roughly 4,000 m, and prior susceptibility. Prevention relies on gradual ascent ("climb high, sleep low") and prophylactic acetazolamide or dexamethasone in selected cases.

Treatment

The definitive treatment is immediate descent to a lower altitude. Supplemental oxygen, dexamethasone, and a portable hyperbaric chamber (such as a Gamow bag) can be lifesaving when descent is delayed.
