Virtual private network

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of an encrypted VPN tunnel between a laptop and a remote network across the public Internet]

A virtual private network (VPN) extends a private network across a public network, enabling users to send and receive data across shared or public networks as if their computing devices were directly connected to the private network. Traffic travels inside an encrypted tunnel, protected from observers on the intervening networks.

VPNs began as enterprise technology: in the 1990s, protocols such as PPTP, L2TP/IPsec and later OpenVPN let employees reach the office network securely from home or the road, replacing expensive leased lines. A second, consumer market then grew around privacy and geography: commercial VPN services route a subscriber's traffic through their servers, hiding it from the local network operator and Internet service provider, and making it appear to originate in another country — useful against surveillance, public Wi-Fi snoops, and region-locked streaming alike, at the cost of trusting the VPN provider instead.

Modern protocols such as WireGuard (merged into the Linux kernel in 2020) made VPNs faster and simpler to audit. Meanwhile employers have begun replacing blanket VPN access with 'zero trust' per-application controls, even as consumer VPN advertising — sometimes with exaggerated claims — made the term a household word.
