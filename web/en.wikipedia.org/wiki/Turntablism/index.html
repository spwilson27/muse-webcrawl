Turntablism is the art of manipulating sounds and creating new music, sound effects, mixes and other creative sounds and beats, typically by using two or more turntables and a cross fader-equipped DJ mixer. The turntable, designed as a playback device, is instead used as a musical instrument: the DJ manipulates vinyl records by hand — moving them back and forth under the stylus, stopping, starting and cutting between decks — to produce rhythms and tones that were never recorded as such.

History

The practice grew out of the Bronx hip hop scene of the 1970s. DJ Kool Herc pioneered the "breakbeat" technique, using two copies of the same record on twin turntables to extend the percussive breaks of funk records indefinitely — a setup he called the "Merry-Go-Round". Grand Wizard Theodore is credited with accidentally inventing scratching around 1975, discovering that moving a record back and forth while holding it produced a rhythmic sound. Grandmaster Flash systematised techniques such as backspinning, punch phrasing and beat juggling, and showcased them on the 1981 recording "The Adventures of Grandmaster Flash on the Wheels of Steel".

Through the 1980s and 1990s, competitive "battle" culture — above all the DMC World DJ Championships — pushed technique rapidly. Crews such as the Invisibl Skratch Piklz (DJ Qbert, Mix Master Mike and Shortkut) and the X-Ecutioners elevated beat juggling and scratch composition into ensemble performance, and turntablism spread into jazz, rock and experimental music.

Techniques

Scratching

Scratching moves a vinyl record back and forth under the stylus while the mixer crossfader opens and closes the channel, carving the sound into rhythmic fragments. Named variants include the baby scratch, chirp, flare, crab and transform scratches, distinguished by their fader patterns.

Beat juggling

Beat juggling uses two copies of the same record to rearrange a drum break live — doubling hits, rearranging the pattern and composing new rhythms from the fragments of the original.

Other techniques

Backspinning rewinds a record rapidly by hand; needle dropping places the stylus precisely on a chosen groove; body tricks and under-the-leg moves add showmanship in battle routines. Turntablists also use "drumming" — assembling a full drum kit pattern from individual sounds located on battle records.

Equipment

The classic setup centres on direct-drive turntables — the Technics SL-1200 series became the standard for its torque and durability — a battle mixer with a sharp-cut crossfader, and rugged cartridges designed to hold the groove under vigorous hand movement. Since the 2000s, digital vinyl systems (such as Serato Scratch Live) allow identical manipulation of digital audio files from timecode records, while controllerism and CDJs have broadened the toolkit; purists continue to prize all-vinyl performance.

Recognition

Turntablism's status as musicianship gained institutional recognition as DJs were invited into orchestral and jazz settings, composition with turntables entered conservatoire curricula, and in 2016 DJing practitioners were acknowledged in mainstream awards categories. The 2001 documentary Scratch chronicled the culture and its leading figures.
