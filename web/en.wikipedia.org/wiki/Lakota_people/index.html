The Lakota (pronounced [laˈkˣota]; Lakota: Očhéthi Šakówiŋ, "Seven Council Fires") are a Native American people and the westernmost of the three major groups of the Sioux, the others being the Dakota and Nakota. Their historic territory included the northern Great Plains, centred on what is now South Dakota, including the sacred Black Hills (Paha Sapa).

History

Originally woodland peoples of the upper Mississippi valley, the Lakota migrated westward onto the Plains in the 17th and 18th centuries, adopting an equestrian culture centred on the hunting of American bison. Organised into seven major divisions — including the Oglala, Brulé (Sičháŋǧu), Hunkpapa, Miniconjou and Santee — they became the dominant power of the northern Plains.

Conflict with the United States culminated in the Great Sioux War of 1876, in which Lakota and Cheyenne forces under leaders including Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse defeated Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer at the Battle of the Little Bighorn. The subsequent loss of the Black Hills, seized after gold was discovered despite the Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868), remains a grievance; the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in 1980 that the taking was illegal, but the Lakota have refused the monetary settlement, insisting on the land's return. The era of armed resistance ended with the Wounded Knee Massacre of 1890.

Culture

Lakota society was organised around the tiyospaye, extended family groups, and guided by virtues including generosity, fortitude, kinship and wisdom. Ceremonies such as the Sun Dance (Wi Wanyang Wacipi), the vision quest (Hanbleceya) and the sweat lodge (Inipi) remain central to Lakota spirituality. The Lakota language is part of the Siouan family; immersion schools work to revitalise it.

Contemporary

Today most Lakota live on reservations in South Dakota, including Pine Ridge (home of the Oglala), Rosebud, Standing Rock, Cheyenne River and Lower Brule. Pine Ridge is among the poorest areas in the United States. The 1973 occupation of Wounded Knee by the American Indian Movement drew national attention to treaty rights and reservation conditions.
