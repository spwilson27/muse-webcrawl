Columnar jointing

Columnar jointing is a geological structure in which sets of intersecting closely spaced fractures (joints) produce a regular array of polygonal prisms, or columns. The columns are most often hexagonal in cross-section, though polygons with three to twelve sides occur. Columnar jointing forms most famously in thick lava flows, especially basalt, as the rock cools and contracts, but it also occurs in welded ignimbrites and in shallow intrusions such as sills and dikes.

Basalt columns of the Giant's Causeway, Northern Ireland.

Formation

As a thick body of lava cools, it contracts. Contraction generates tensile stress, and when the stress exceeds the tensile strength of the rock, cracks form perpendicular to the cooling surface and propagate inward. The fracture network organises itself into roughly polygonal columns because this geometry relieves stress most efficiently, analogous to mud cracks in drying mud (though on a much larger scale, and driven by thermal rather than moisture loss). Column diameter varies inversely with cooling rate: slowly cooled interiors produce wide, regular columns, while rapidly chilled margins yield narrower, more irregular ones.

Structure of a flow

Classic columnar-jointed flows show a tiered structure: a lower colonnade of straight, regular columns; a middle entablature of thinner, curved, and more chaotic columns where cooling was disrupted (often by water); and an upper colonnade. This architecture is displayed spectacularly in the Columbia River Basalt Group of the northwestern United States.

Notable examples

  • Giant's Causeway, County Antrim, Northern Ireland — some 40,000 interlocking columns formed by a Palaeogene lava flow.
  • Fingal's Cave and the island of Staffa, Scotland.
  • Devils Tower, Wyoming, USA — an eroded intrusive body with prominent columns.
  • Devils Postpile, California, USA.
  • Svartifoss, Iceland.
  • Organ Pipes formations in Namibia and Tasmania.

Columnar jointing has also been identified beyond Earth, including in basaltic outcrops on Mars imaged by orbiting spacecraft.
