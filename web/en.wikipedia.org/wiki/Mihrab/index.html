Mihrab

A mihrab (Arabic: مِحْرَاب) is a niche in the wall of a mosque that indicates the qibla, the direction of prayer toward the Kaaba in Mecca. The imam stands in front of the mihrab when leading prayer, and the niche often helps project his voice to the congregation. The mihrab is usually the most richly decorated element of the mosque interior.

History

The first mihrabs were simple markers; the concave niche form is generally credited to the rebuilding of the Prophet's Mosque in Medina under Caliph al-Walid I (early 8th century), reportedly influenced by the apses of Christian churches and possibly executed with Coptic craftsmen. The mihrab in the Great Mosque of Córdoba, with its golden mosaics and horseshoe arch, is among the most famous examples.

Design

Mihrabs may be flat or deeply concave, framed by carved stucco, inlaid marble, tile mosaic (as in Iranian mosques, often with muqarnas hoods), or carved stone. Above or within the niche, calligraphy frequently quotes the Qur'an, notably the Light Verse or the verse of Zachariah in the mihrab (Q 3:37), the source of the Arabic word. The area around the mihrab is the maqsura in large congregational mosques, near the minbar.

Prayer rugs

"Mihrab" designs also appear on prayer rugs (sajjada), where an arch motif points the direction of prayer; such rugs imitate the architectural niche.
