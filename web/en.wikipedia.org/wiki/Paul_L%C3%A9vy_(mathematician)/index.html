Paul Lévy (mathematician)

Paul Pierre Lévy (15 September 1886 – 15 December 1971) was a French mathematician who worked especially in probability theory, making fundamental contributions to the theory of stochastic processes. He introduced concepts including Lévy processes, Lévy flights, Lévy measures, and the Lévy distribution, and he developed the theory of stable distributions and local time.

Life and career

Born in Paris, Lévy entered the École Polytechnique in 1904 and published his first paper the following year. He became a professor at the École des Mines in 1913 and at the École Polytechnique in 1920. During the Second World War, being Jewish, he was forced into hiding under the Vichy regime. He was a member of the French Academy of Sciences.

Work

Lévy's research covered functional analysis, partial differential equations, and probability. His 1937 book Théorie de l'addition des variables aléatoires laid foundations for the modern theory of sums of independent random variables. He characterized Brownian motion (Lévy's characterization theorem), studied the arcsine laws, and introduced martingale ideas before their formal development by Doob. The Lévy–Cramér decomposition theorem and the Lévy continuity theorem for characteristic functions bear his name.
