Thomas Robert Malthus (13/14 February 1766 – 29 December 1834) was an English clergyman, demographer and political economist, best known for An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798, much expanded in later editions), which argued that population grows geometrically while food supply grows arithmetically, so that living standards are held near subsistence by "checks" — misery and vice, or the preventive check of moral restraint.

Influence

The Essay influenced the 1834 Poor Law reform, evolutionary biology (both Darwin and Wallace credited it as a spur to natural selection) and two centuries of debate about population, resources and growth. In economics, Malthus defended the Corn Laws against his friend David Ricardo, anticipated arguments about deficient aggregate demand and general gluts later associated with John Maynard Keynes, and gave his name to Malthusian predictions of scarcity — whose repeated failure, as technology outran population in the industrial era, is itself a central case in the study of growth and Poverty. Modern neo-Malthusianism reframes the argument around environmental limits.
