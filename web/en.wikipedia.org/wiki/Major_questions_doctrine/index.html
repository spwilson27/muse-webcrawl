Major questions doctrine

The major questions doctrine is a principle of United States administrative law holding that courts will not presume that Congress has delegated to an administrative agency the authority to decide questions of major economic and political significance unless Congress has spoken clearly. Articulated most prominently by the Supreme Court in West Virginia v. EPA (2022), the doctrine requires "clear congressional authorization" before an agency may claim an expansive or transformative interpretation of its statutory powers.

Development

The doctrine's roots lie in cases such as FDA v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp. (2000), where the Court rejected the FDA's claim to regulate tobacco, and Utility Air Regulatory Group v. EPA (2014), which spoke of expecting Congress to speak clearly before assigning decisions of "vast economic and political significance" to an agency. The phrase "major questions doctrine" was used in West Virginia v. EPA, striking down the Clean Power Plan because the EPA had claimed a transformative power — restructuring the nation's power sector — from a rarely used ancillary provision of the Clean Air Act.

Applications and debate

The Court applied the doctrine in 2023 in Biden v. Nebraska to invalidate the administration's mass student-loan cancellation programme. Supporters describe the doctrine as a safeguard for democratic accountability and the separation of powers; critics call it a vague, results-oriented judicial tool that constrains expert agencies and transfers policymaking power to courts. It operates alongside (and has partly eclipsed) the now-overruled Chevron deference framework, ended by Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (2024).
