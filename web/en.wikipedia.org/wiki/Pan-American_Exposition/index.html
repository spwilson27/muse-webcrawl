The Pan-American Exposition was a world's fair held by Buffalo, New York, United States, from May 1 to November 2, 1901. The fair occupied 350 acres (140 ha) of parkland on the western edge of what is now Delaware Park, extending from Delaware Avenue to Elmwood Avenue and northward to Great Arrow Avenue. It is remembered today mainly as the site where United States President William McKinley was assassinated.

The fair

The exposition celebrated commercial and cultural ties among the nations of the Americas. Its centrepiece was the 375-foot Electric Tower, festooned with some 200,000 incandescent bulbs powered by alternating current generated at Niagara Falls—a spectacular advertisement for electric light. Buildings around the Court of Fountains were rendered in a colourful Spanish Renaissance revival style, lit at night in a graded sequence of coloured illumination. Exhibits included X-ray demonstrations (one machine was later used, too late and improperly, on the wounded president), an early motion-picture record of the fair shot by Edison's company, and ethnological "villages" typical of the era's fairs.

Assassination of McKinley

On 6 September 1901, while greeting the public in the Temple of Music, President McKinley was shot twice by anarchist Leon Czolgosz, whose revolver was concealed in a handkerchief. McKinley died of gangrene eight days later, on 14 September; Vice President Theodore Roosevelt was sworn in as president in Buffalo. Czolgosz was convicted and executed in the electric chair within weeks.

Legacy

Almost all fair buildings were temporary and demolished; the New York State Building survived as the Buffalo History Museum. The site is marked in a residential neighbourhood, and the spot of the shooting is commemorated by a plaque in a median of Fordham Drive.
