Polytechnic (United Kingdom)

In the United Kingdom, a polytechnic was a tertiary education institution offering higher education, particularly in science, technology, engineering, and applied subjects. Between the late 1960s and 1992, polytechnics formed the "public sector" half of a binary system of higher education, alongside the universities. Under the Further and Higher Education Act 1992, the polytechnics were granted university status and degree-awarding powers; institutions such as Manchester Polytechnic (now Manchester Metropolitan University), Birmingham Polytechnic (now Birmingham City University), and the Polytechnic of North London (now part of London Metropolitan University) became the "new universities" or post-1992 universities.

History

The idea of the polytechnic in Britain dates to the Regent Street Polytechnic in London (founded 1838 as the Royal Polytechnic Institution). The modern system, however, derives from the 1966 White Paper implementing the policy of Secretary of State Anthony Crosland, who argued for a public sector of higher education distinct from the universities, responsive to local and industrial needs, and committed to access for part-time and mature students. Some thirty polytechnics in England and Wales were designated from 1969–70, often by merging technical colleges; Scotland had analogous Central Institutions, and Northern Ireland had the Ulster Polytechnic.

Character

Polytechnics were controlled by local education authorities and their degrees were validated nationally by the Council for National Academic Awards (CNAA). They emphasized vocational and professional education — engineering, business, art and design, health, and teacher training — alongside growing provision in humanities and social sciences, and typically carried a heavier teaching load and a stronger widening-participation mission than the older universities.

After 1992

The abolition of the binary line unified funding and titles, but distinctions of prestige, research income, and recruitment between pre- and post-1992 universities have persisted in public discussion of British higher education.
