Half-timbering (German: Fachwerk) is a method of constructing buildings in which a load-bearing frame of heavy timber is left exposed on the exterior, with the spaces between the timbers filled with materials such as wattle and daub, brick, or plaster. It is characteristic of much medieval and early-modern vernacular architecture in northern Europe.

Construction

The frame consists of vertical posts, horizontal rails, and diagonal braces joined by mortise-and-tenon joints secured with wooden pegs. Upper storeys often jut out over the street on projecting beams called jetties. Infill panels could be decorated with carved braces and painted patterns; in England, elaborate black-and-white patterns became fashionable, while German Fachwerk often features ornate carved figures and inscriptions.

History and distribution

Timber-framed building dates to antiquity, but half-timbering flourished from the 12th century in Germany, France (notably Normandy and Alsace), England, Denmark, and Poland. Whole historic towns, such as Rothenburg ob der Tauber and Colmar, preserve dense concentrations of such houses. The style was later revived decoratively in the 19th and 20th centuries — for instance in Tudor Revival architecture, where thin applied boards imitate structural timbers without performing their function.
