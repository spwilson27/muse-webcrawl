Porro prism

A Porro prism, named for its inventor Ignazio Porro, is a type of reflection prism used in optical instruments to alter the orientation of an image. It consists of a block of glass in the shape of a truncated right-angle prism, with right-angled isosceles triangles as ends and rectangles as faces. Light entering one face is reflected twice by total internal reflection from the two inclined faces and emerges parallel to its original direction but displaced, with the image both inverted and reversed — that is, rotated 180°.

Applications

Porro prisms are most commonly used in pairs in prism binoculars. Because a refracting telescope forms an upside-down image, the prism pair serves as an image-erecting system: the first prism reverses the image in one axis and the second, mounted at right angles to the first, reverses it in the other, so the observer sees a correctly oriented view. The folded light path also allows a long focal length to fit into a compact instrument, and offsetting the objective lenses farther apart than the eyepieces enhances stereoscopic depth perception. Porro-prism binoculars are recognisable by their characteristic zig-zag, offset-barrel shape.

Characteristics

Unlike mirrors, the prisms rely on total internal reflection, so they need no reflective coating and introduce very little light loss, though cheaper designs may use silvered surfaces on faces where the angle of incidence is insufficient for total reflection. Porro systems generally deliver bright, high-contrast images at a lower manufacturing cost than the roof-prism designs that dominate compact modern binoculars, but they are bulkier and can be knocked out of collimation more easily by impact. Variants include the Porro–Abbe (Abbe–Koenig-related) prism and the double Porro arrangements used in some periscopes and image-erecting telescope diagonals.
