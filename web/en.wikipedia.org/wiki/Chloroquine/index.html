Chloroquine

Class: 4-aminoquinoline antimalarial

Synthesised: 1934 (Hans Andersag, Bayer)

Uses: Malaria treatment/prevention; amoebic liver abscess

Chloroquine is a medication used to prevent and treat malaria in areas where the parasite remains sensitive to it, and for certain other conditions such as extraintestinal amoebiasis. It belongs to the 4-aminoquinoline class and is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines. The related drug hydroxychloroquine is more widely used for autoimmune diseases such as lupus and rheumatoid arthritis.

History

Chloroquine was synthesised in 1934 by Hans Andersag at Bayer in Germany (initially under the name Resochin) but was set aside as too toxic. Re-evaluated during the Second World War by Allied researchers, it proved highly effective against malaria and became the standard antimalarial of the post-war era, playing a central role in malaria-control campaigns.

Mechanism and resistance

The drug concentrates in the malaria parasite's food vacuole, where it interferes with the detoxification of haem released from digested haemoglobin, killing the parasite. Widespread resistance in Plasmodium falciparum, emerging from the 1950s onwards and now near-universal in many regions, has greatly limited chloroquine's role in falciparum malaria; it remains effective for most P. vivax, P. ovale and P. malariae infections in many areas, and artemisinin-based combination therapies are now first-line for falciparum malaria.

Side effects

Common adverse effects include nausea, itching (particularly in people of African descent), headache and blurred vision. Serious toxicity with long-term high-dose use includes retinopathy, which requires ophthalmic monitoring. Overdose is dangerous, causing cardiovascular collapse and seizures.

COVID-19

During the COVID-19 pandemic, chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine were promoted as treatments, but large randomised trials found no clinical benefit, and regulatory authorisations for that use were revoked.
