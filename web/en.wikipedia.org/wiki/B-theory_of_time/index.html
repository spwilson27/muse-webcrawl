B-theory of time

The B-theory of time is the name given to one of two positions regarding the temporal ordering of events in the philosophy of time. B-theorists argue that the flow of time is an illusion, that past, present and future events are equally real, and that time is tenseless: events are ordered by the relations earlier than, simultaneous with and later than, but none is objectively "now". The contrasting position is the A-theory, which treats the distinction between past, present and future as objective.

Origins

The terminology comes from J. M. E. McTaggart's 1908 argument for the unreality of time, in which he distinguished the A-series (events ordered as past, present or future) from the B-series (events ordered merely as earlier or later). Later philosophers such as D. H. Mellor and J. J. C. Smart defended versions of the B-theory, arguing it fits naturally with the four-dimensional "block universe" picture suggested by relativity.

Relation to physics

B-theorists often appeal to the theory of relativity: because simultaneity is frame-dependent, there is no single privileged present moment, which suggests that all times exist on an equal footing — a view known as eternalism.

Criticism

Critics argue the B-theory cannot account for the subjective experience of time passing, and that tense is indispensable to thought and language. B-theorists reply that tensed beliefs can be explained by token-reflexive or indexical facts about when a belief is held.
