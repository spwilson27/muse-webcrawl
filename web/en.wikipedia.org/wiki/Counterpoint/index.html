Counterpoint

[Image: opening bars of a two-voice contrapuntal composition in staff notation]
EtymologyLatin punctus contra punctum, "point against point"
Related conceptsPolyphony, fugue, canon, voice leading

In music, counterpoint is the relationship between two or more simultaneous melodic lines (voices) that are harmonically interdependent yet independent in rhythm and contour. The term derives from the Latin punctus contra punctum ("note against note"), reflecting the medieval practice of writing one note of a melody against each note of another. Music written according to contrapuntal principles is called polyphonic.

Principles

Counterpoint balances two dimensions: each voice must make sense as a melody by itself (the horizontal dimension), while the combinations of voices must produce acceptable harmonies (the vertical dimension). Traditional rules govern melodic intervals, consonance and dissonance, the treatment of dissonances by preparation and resolution, and the avoidance of parallel perfect fifths and octaves between voices.

Species counterpoint

The standard pedagogical method, codified by Johann Joseph Fux in his Gradus ad Parnassum (1725), teaches counterpoint in five "species" of increasing rhythmic freedom against a given cantus firmus, from note-against-note writing up to florid counterpoint. Fux's model was the style of Palestrina, and his treatise trained generations of composers including Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven.

History

Counterpoint developed from medieval organum and discant, flowered in the Franco-Flemish polyphony of the Renaissance (Josquin, Palestrina), and reached a celebrated peak in the Baroque era with the fugues and canons of J. S. Bach, such as The Art of Fugue and The Musical Offering. Contrapuntal techniques — imitation, inversion, augmentation, diminution and stretto — remain central to composition and music theory.
