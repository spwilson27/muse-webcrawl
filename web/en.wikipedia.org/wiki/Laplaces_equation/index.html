Laplaces equation

Laplace's equation is a second-order partial differential equation named after Pierre-Simon Laplace, who first studied its properties in work on gravitational and fluid potentials in the 1780s. It is written as:

∇²φ = 0

where ∇² is the Laplace operator (or Laplacian) and φ is a scalar potential function. In Cartesian coordinates in three dimensions it takes the form ∂²φ/∂x² + ∂²φ/∂y² + ∂²φ/∂z² = 0. Functions that satisfy Laplace's equation are called harmonic functions and are of central importance in complex analysis and potential theory.

Properties

Harmonic functions are infinitely differentiable within their domain and obey the mean value property: the value at any point equals the average of values over any sphere centred at that point. They also satisfy the maximum principle — a non-constant harmonic function cannot attain a maximum or minimum in the interior of its domain. Solutions are determined by boundary conditions: with Dirichlet conditions (values fixed on the boundary) or Neumann conditions (derivatives fixed) the solution is unique under suitable assumptions.

Applications

Laplace's equation arises wherever a potential is studied in a source-free region: gravitational and electrostatic potentials in empty space, steady-state temperature distributions, incompressible and irrotational fluid flow, and the velocity potential of ideal fluids. When sources are present, it generalizes to Poisson's equation, ∇²φ = f. In electrostatics, solutions with appropriate boundary conditions give the electric potential; in gravitation, they describe the Newtonian potential outside mass distributions.

Two dimensions and complex analysis

In two dimensions, Laplace's equation is intimately connected with analytic functions: the real and imaginary parts of any holomorphic function each satisfy it, and conversely any harmonic function on a simply connected domain is the real part of some holomorphic function. Solutions can be found by separation of variables, Green's functions, conformal mapping, and numerical methods such as finite differences.
