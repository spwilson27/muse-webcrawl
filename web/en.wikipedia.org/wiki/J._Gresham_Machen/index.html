John Gresham Machen (July 28, 1881 – January 1, 1937) was an American Presbyterian theologian and New Testament scholar in the early 20th century. He was the Professor of New Testament at Princeton Seminary between 1915 and 1929, and led a revolt against the modernist theology at Princeton and formed the Westminster Theological Seminary as a more orthodox alternative. As the Northern Presbyterian Church continued to reject conservative departures from its official doctrines, Machen led an exodus of conservatives that formed the Orthodox Presbyterian Church (OPC).

Machen is regarded as the last of the great Princeton theologians in the line of Archibald Alexander, Charles Hodge, and B. B. Warfield, and his 1923 book Christianity and Liberalism remains a classic statement of the fundamentalist side of the Fundamentalist–Modernist Controversy.

Early life and education

Machen was born in Baltimore to a wealthy family; his mother came from Georgia and influenced him with a strong Southern Presbyterian piety. He studied classics at Johns Hopkins University, then theology at Princeton Seminary under Warfield, and pursued further study at Marburg and Göttingen in Germany, where exposure to Wilhelm Herrmann's liberal theology occasioned a personal crisis of faith that he resolved in favour of historic orthodoxy.

Career and controversy

At Princeton, Machen gained a reputation as a first-rate scholar of the Greek New Testament; his New Testament Greek for Beginners (1923) became a standard textbook. His The Origin of Paul's Religion (1921) and The Virgin Birth of Christ (1930) defended historical orthodoxy with academic rigour. In Christianity and Liberalism he argued that theological liberalism was not a variety of Christianity but a different religion altogether.

As Princeton Seminary was reorganized in 1929 to make room for more liberal views, Machen resigned and founded Westminster Theological Seminary in Philadelphia. In 1933 he founded the Independent Board for Presbyterian Foreign Missions, for which the Presbyterian Church tried, convicted, and suspended him from the ministry. In June 1936 he led the founding of a new denomination, initially called the Presbyterian Church of America, renamed the Orthodox Presbyterian Church in 1939 after a trademark suit. He died of pneumonia on 1 January 1937 in Bismarck, North Dakota, while on a speaking tour; his last telegram read: "So thankful for active obedience of Christ. No hope without it."

Legacy

Machen's institutions outlasted him: Westminster Seminary became a centre of Reformed scholarship, and the OPC a small but durable church. His writings continue to influence conservative evangelicalism, and H. L. Mencken's admiring obituary of his fellow Baltimorean remains a notable piece of the era's journalism.
