National service

National service is a system in which a government requires some or all of its citizens, usually young adults, to serve for a period in the armed forces or in an approved form of civilian service. Where the service is military it overlaps with conscription; many systems allow conscientious objectors to perform alternative civilian duties instead.

United Kingdom

In the United Kingdom, "National Service" specifically refers to the peacetime conscription introduced by the National Service Act 1947, which took effect in 1949. Young men aged 18 were called up for service in the armed forces, initially for 18 months and later for two years during the Korean War period. Over two million men were conscripted before the last call-ups in 1960; the final national servicemen were demobilised in 1963. British national servicemen served in conflicts including the Malayan Emergency, the Korean War, the Suez Crisis and operations in Cyprus and Kenya.

Other countries

Many states retain some form of compulsory service. Singapore's National Service, introduced in 1967, requires male citizens to serve about two years in the armed forces, police or civil defence. Israel, South Korea, Switzerland, Finland, Norway and Sweden maintain conscription systems of varying scope, while Germany suspended its Wehrpflicht in 2011. France abolished peacetime conscription in 2001 and later introduced a short universal national service programme for teenagers.

Debates

Supporters argue national service promotes social cohesion, shared sacrifice and useful skills; critics argue it is an unjustified restriction of liberty, economically inefficient, and often falls unequally on different groups. Proposals to revive national service periodically recur in countries that have abolished it.
