Carl Linnaeus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Carl Linnaeus (1707–1778) was a Swedish botanist, zoologist and physician who formalised binomial nomenclature, the modern system of naming organisms, and laid the foundations of biological taxonomy. He is known as the "father of modern taxonomy" and was one of the most influential scientists of the 18th century.

Born Carl von Linné in Råshult, Småland, he studied at Lund and Uppsala, travelled in Lapland, and became professor of medicine and botany at Uppsala. His Systema Naturae (first edition 1735) grew through many editions into a classification of the natural world, and his Species Plantarum (1753) and the 10th edition of Systema Naturae (1758) are the official starting points of botanical and zoological nomenclature. He named our own species Homo sapiens.

System

Linnaeus classified plants by their sexual organs (number and arrangement of stamens and pistils) — an artificial but practical system he knew was provisional — and animals by anatomy. His enduring legacy is the binomial name (genus + epithet) and the ranked hierarchy, which Darwin's theory later reinterpreted as a family tree.

Legacy and criticism

Linnaeus sent students ('apostles') worldwide to collect specimens and seeded a global network of classification. His classification of human varieties, typical of his era, attached temperaments and moral characters to continental groups and is now recognised as an influential early instance of scientific racism; his biological scheme itself rested on species being fixed, an assumption evolution later overturned.
