In usage in the Southern United States, a bayou (/ˈbaɪ.uː/, /ˈbaɪ.oʊ/) is a body of water typically found in a flat, low-lying area, and can refer to an extremely slow-moving stream or river (often with a poorly defined shoreline), a marshy lake, or a wetland. Bayous are most closely associated with the Gulf Coast of Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, Florida and Texas, and in particular with Acadiana, the Cajun country of southern Louisiana.

Etymology

The word entered Louisiana French from the Choctaw bayuk, meaning "small stream". It came into common American English usage during the nineteenth century as settlers spread through the Mississippi River Delta region.

Geography and ecology

Bayous form in abandoned river channels (oxbows), distributary networks of deltas, and low coastal plains where the water table is high and drainage is slow. Typical bayou scenery includes bald cypress and water tupelo trees hung with Spanish moss, backwater swamps, and abundant wildlife: alligators, herons, egrets, turtles, crawfish and catfish. Because water moves so slowly, bayous function as natural flood buffers and nurseries for fish and shellfish, but they are vulnerable to saltwater intrusion and land subsidence, which threaten coastal Louisiana.

Historically bayous were the highways of the region: Indigenous peoples, then French colonists, Acadian (Cajun) settlers and steamboats used them for transport and trade, and settlements grew along the natural levees of bayous such as Bayou Teche and Bayou Lafourche, the "longest Main Street in the world". In Houston, Texas, a network of bayous — including Buffalo Bayou, where the city was founded in 1836 — drains the city to Galveston Bay and now serves as greenway parkland, though it is prone to flooding in hurricanes.

In culture

The bayou is a fixture of American popular culture, evoked in blues, country and swamp rock (Hank Williams's "Jambalaya (On the Bayou)"), in Creole and Cajun cuisine, and in the imagery of Louisiana festivals, voodoo lore and ghost stories.
