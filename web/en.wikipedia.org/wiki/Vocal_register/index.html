A vocal register is a range of tones in the human voice produced by a particular vibratory pattern of the vocal folds. These registers include modal voice (or normal voice), vocal fry, falsetto, and the whistle register. Registers originate in laryngeal function: they occur because the vocal folds are capable of producing several different vibratory patterns, each of which produces sounds within a particular pitch range and with a certain characteristic sound.

Speech pathologists and vocal pedagogues have long struggled to agree on how many registers exist. Some authorities recognise four registers, while others identify as many as seven, counting transitional zones known as passaggi (singular passaggio) between the main registers.

The registers

Vocal fry register

The vocal fry register (also known as pulse register or glottal fry) is the lowest vocal register. During its production the arytenoid cartilages come together so that the vocal folds vibrate in a loose, compact pattern with a very low frequency, producing the characteristic popping or rattling sound of creaky voice.

Modal register

The modal register is the register used most frequently in speech and singing in most languages. It is the term used in phonetics to denote the most common pattern of vibration of the vocal folds. Both the superior and inferior portions of the vocal folds vibrate, and the sound produced is rich in harmonics.

Falsetto register

The falsetto register lies above the modal register and overlaps with it by roughly one octave. In falsetto only the thin, ligamentous edges of the vocal folds vibrate, and the resulting sound is breathier and flute-like, lacking the richer overtones of modal voice.

Whistle register

The whistle register (also called the flute register) is the highest register of the human voice. Production is poorly understood compared with the other registers; it is generally agreed that only a small segment of the vocal folds vibrates, allowing pitches above the soprano high C (C6).

Registers in singing pedagogy

In singing, teachers commonly speak of "chest voice" and "head voice" as practical labels for lower and upper portions of the range. The transition point between registers is called the passaggio, and much of classical vocal training aims at smoothing this transition so that a singer can move through the whole range with an even tone.
