Channel capacity, in electrical engineering, computer science, and information theory, is the tight upper bound on the rate at which information can be reliably transmitted over a communication channel. By the noisy-channel coding theorem, for any rate below the capacity there exist encoding and decoding schemes that achieve an arbitrarily low probability of error; for rates above capacity, the probability of error is bounded away from zero.

Definition

For a discrete memoryless channel with input alphabet X and output alphabet Y, the capacity is the maximum mutual information between input and output, maximised over all input distributions:

C = maxp(x) I(X; Y)

When the channel is used once per unit time, the capacity is expressed in bits per second.

Shannon–Hartley theorem

For a bandlimited channel corrupted by additive white Gaussian noise, Claude Shannon and Ralph Hartley derived the capacity in bits per second as:

C = B log2(1 + S/N)

where B is the bandwidth in hertz, S is the average received signal power, and N is the average noise power. This formula shows that capacity grows linearly with bandwidth but only logarithmically with signal-to-noise ratio.

Noisy-channel coding theorem

Shannon's 1948 theorem states that if the transmission rate R is less than the capacity C, there exists a sequence of codes with block length tending to infinity whose maximal probability of error tends to zero. Conversely, if R > C, the probability of error is bounded away from zero for any code. The theorem is an existence result proved by random coding; practical codes such as turbo codes and LDPC codes approach capacity closely.

Examples

  • A binary symmetric channel with crossover probability p has capacity 1 − H2(p) bits per use.
  • A binary erasure channel with erasure probability ε has capacity 1 − ε bits per use.
  • A telephone channel with 3 kHz bandwidth and 30 dB signal-to-noise ratio has a capacity of roughly 30 kbit/s.
