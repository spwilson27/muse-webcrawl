Mancur Olson

Mancur Lloyd Olson Jr. (22 January 1932 – 19 February 1998) was an American economist and political scientist at the University of Maryland, College Park. His work on collective action and institutions made him one of the most influential social scientists of the 20th century and a founder of the modern study of interest groups.

Major works

In The Logic of Collective Action (1965), Olson argued that rational individuals will not voluntarily act to achieve common group interests when they can free-ride on the efforts of others — a problem that grows worse as groups get larger. Small, concentrated groups therefore organise effectively, while large diffuse groups (consumers, taxpayers) usually fail to organise unless compelled or offered selective incentives. This overturned the pluralist assumption that shared interests automatically produce political organisation.

In The Rise and Decline of Nations (1982), he extended the argument to economic history: stable societies accumulate entrenched distributional coalitions — lobby groups, cartels, unions — that slow growth by diverting resources to rent-seeking, an "institutional sclerosis" he used to explain why defeated and occupied nations such as post-war Germany and Japan grew faster than victorious Britain.

His posthumous Power and Prosperity (2000) analysed government itself, contrasting "roving bandits" who plunder with "stationary bandits" who, having an encompassing interest in their territory's prosperity, provide public goods and limit their theft — a model of the origins of state capacity and democracy.
