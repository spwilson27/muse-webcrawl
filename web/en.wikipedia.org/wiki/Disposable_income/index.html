Disposable income

Disposable income is total personal income minus current income taxes and other mandatory deductions. In national accounts definitions it may also be defined more broadly as total income minus personal current taxes and other mandatory transfers paid by households. It is the amount of money that an individual or household has to spend or save after income taxes have been deducted; payments roughly mandatory for meeting basic needs, such as housing, are nevertheless paid from disposable income, a point that distinguishes it from discretionary income.

Definitions and measurement

In the United States, the Bureau of Economic Analysis publishes disposable personal income monthly as part of the personal income statistics; it equals personal income less personal current taxes. The OECD publishes household disposable income per capita, adjusted for social transfers in kind, as a headline welfare indicator in its Better Life Index and national accounts comparisons. Aggregate disposable income minus personal consumption expenditures equals personal saving in the national accounts identity, and economists track the saving rate and the marginal propensity to consume out of changes in disposable income.

Disposable versus discretionary income

Discretionary income is disposable income minus all payments that are necessary to meet current bills, including housing, food, transport, insurance, and minimum debt payments. It is the income available for investing, saving beyond necessities, and non-essential spending, and is therefore the figure of greatest interest to marketers of consumer goods and leisure services. Lenders sometimes use discretionary income in student-loan repayment formulas, such as income-driven repayment in the United States.

Uses

Disposable income figures are used to compare living standards between countries and over time (usually after adjustment for purchasing power and household size, or "equivalisation"), to model consumer spending in macroeconomic forecasts, and to set policies such as tax credits and benefit thresholds.
