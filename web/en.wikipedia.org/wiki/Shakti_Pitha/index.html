The Shakti Pithas (Sanskrit: शक्ति पीठ, "seats of Shakti") are significant shrines and pilgrimage destinations in Shaktism, the goddess-centred tradition of Hinduism. According to tradition, these are the sites where parts of the body of the goddess Sati, the first consort of the god Shiva, fell to earth after her self-immolation and the subsequent dismemberment of her corpse by Vishnu. Each Pitha is associated with a particular body part, a form of the goddess, and a form of Shiva as Bhairava.

Legend

According to the mythology, Sati, daughter of the patriarch Daksha, married Shiva against her father's wishes. When Daksha organised a great yajna (sacrificial rite) and deliberately excluded Shiva, Sati attended uninvited, was humiliated by her father's insults toward her husband, and immolated herself in the sacrificial fire. The enraged Shiva, carrying her corpse, performed the Tandava, the dance of destruction. To end the catastrophe, Vishnu used his Sudarshana Chakra to cut Sati's body into pieces, which fell at various places on earth. Each place where a part landed became a Shakti Pitha, a seat of the goddess's power.

The lists of Pithas

Different texts give different enumerations. The most widely cited list numbers 51 Pithas, corresponding to the 51 letters of the Sanskrit alphabet. Other enumerations include 52, 64 and 108 sites. The Devi Bhagavata Purana and the Kalika Purana are among the principal sources. Tradition also distinguishes four Adi Shakti Pithas of greatest antiquity and a set of eighteen Maha Shakti Pithas mentioned in a hymn attributed to Adi Shankaracharya.

Selected major Pithas

  • Kamakhya (Assam) — where the yoni (womb) fell; one of the most important tantric centres.
  • Kalighat Kali (Kolkata, West Bengal) — toes of the right foot.
  • Hinglaj Mata (Balochistan, Pakistan) — the crown/brahmarandhra.
  • Jwalamukhi (Himachal Pradesh) — the tongue.
  • Tarapith (West Bengal) — the eye.
  • Kanchipuram (Kamakkottam) and Madurai (Meenakshi) in Tamil Nadu.
  • Varanasi (Vishalakshi) in Uttar Pradesh.
  • Guhyeshwari (Kathmandu, Nepal) — the knees.
  • Jaffna Nainativu Nagapooshani (Sri Lanka) — associated with the goddess's ornaments.

Significance

The Pithas form a sacred geography of goddess worship extending across the whole Indian subcontinent, and pilgrimage to them remains a central devotional practice. Many are also important centres of Tantra. The sites collectively map the body of the goddess onto the land itself, an idea sometimes interpreted as a religious vision of the subcontinent as a single divine body.
