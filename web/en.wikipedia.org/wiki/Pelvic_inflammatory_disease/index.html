Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) is an infection of the upper part of the female reproductive system, including the uterus, fallopian tubes and ovaries. It most commonly occurs when sexually transmitted bacteria spread from the vagina and cervix upward; the most frequent causes are Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae, though normal vaginal flora may also be involved. More than a million women in the United States are estimated to experience an episode of PID each year.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms range from none (subclinical infection is common) to severe illness: lower abdominal or pelvic pain, fever, abnormal or foul-smelling vaginal discharge, bleeding after intercourse or between periods, pain during intercourse or urination, and nausea. On examination there may be cervical motion tenderness and adnexal tenderness. A serious complication is Fitz-Hugh–Curtis syndrome, inflammation of the liver capsule producing right upper abdominal pain.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical, supported by tests for chlamydia and gonorrhoea, inflammatory markers, ultrasound, and in uncertain cases laparoscopy. Because delayed treatment worsens outcomes, guidelines recommend empirical antibiotics — typically a cephalosporin plus doxycycline, with metronidazole — started as soon as PID is reasonably suspected. Sexual partners should be evaluated and treated, and patients advised to abstain from intercourse until treatment is complete.

Complications and prevention

Repeated or severe episodes can scar the fallopian tubes, causing infertility (affecting roughly one in eight women after one episode, more after recurrences), increased risk of ectopic pregnancy, and chronic pelvic pain. A tubo-ovarian abscess may require hospitalisation and drainage or surgery. Prevention relies on safer sex and on screening sexually active young women for chlamydia, which reduces PID incidence.
