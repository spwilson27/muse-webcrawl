Natural wine refers to a generalized movement among winemakers for production of wine using simple or traditional methods. Although there is no unified theory of natural winemaking, the term is generally used to describe wine made from organically or biodynamically grown grapes, fermented with native yeasts, and produced with minimal technological intervention and little or no added sulfur dioxide.

History

The natural wine movement grew out of post-war reactions against the industrialization of winemaking. In France, producers in the Beaujolais region, influenced by the négociant and scientist Jules Chauvet in the 1950s–1980s, began advocating for wines made without additives. The so-called "Gang of Four" of Beaujolais producers — Marcel Lapierre, Jean-Paul Thévenet, Charly Thévenet and Guy Breton — helped popularize the approach. From the 1990s onward, natural wine bars and fairs spread through Paris, London, New York, Tokyo and elsewhere, and styles such as pétillant naturel (pét-nat) and skin-contact ("orange") wines became signatures of the movement.

Production

Typical natural winemaking practices include hand-harvesting, organic or biodynamic viticulture without synthetic pesticides or fertilizers, spontaneous fermentation with indigenous yeasts, no chaptalization or acid adjustment, minimal fining and filtration, and little or no added sulfur at bottling. Critics note that the absence of a legal definition allows wide variation in quality and stability, and natural wines can be prone to faults such as mousiness or volatile acidity; supporters argue the wines express terroir more vividly.

Regulation

There is no official certification for natural wine in most countries. In 2020, France recognized a voluntary charter, "Vin Méthode Nature", administered by a syndicate of producers, allowing labelled wines made from organic grapes with no added sulfites or with minimal sulfites at bottling.
