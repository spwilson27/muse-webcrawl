John Wycliffe (also spelled Wyclif, Wickliffe and Wicliffe; c. 1328 – 31 December 1384) was an English scholastic philosopher, theologian, biblical translator and reformer. A professor at the University of Oxford, he became one of the earliest and most vigorous critics of the privileges of the medieval Church, attacking papal authority, clerical wealth and the doctrine of transubstantiation. His followers, the Lollards, spread his ideas across England, and he is often called "the Morning Star of the Reformation".

Life and teaching

Wycliffe rose to prominence at Oxford, becoming master of Balliol College and a doctor of theology. Writing in defence of the Crown's resistance to papal taxation, he argued in works such as De dominio divino (c. 1373) that authority depends on grace and that a sinful pope or priest forfeits true dominion. His attacks on "possessioner" monks and friars, and his insistence that the Bible — not the Church — is the supreme authority in religion, brought him before a synod at St Paul's and eventually to condemnation by Pope Gregory XI in 1377, though powerful patrons including John of Gaunt shielded him.

The Eucharist and later years

In De eucharistia (1379) Wycliffe denied transubstantiation, holding that the substance of bread remains after consecration. Oxford condemned his conclusions in 1381, his academic supporters were purged, and in 1382 the "Earthquake Synod" condemned 24 of his propositions. Wycliffe retired to his parish at Lutterworth, where he continued writing until his death from a stroke on 31 December 1384.

The Wycliffe Bible

Wycliffe championed Scripture in the language of the people. The first complete translation of the Bible into English — the Wycliffite Bible, produced by his followers such as Nicholas of Hereford and John Purvey from the Latin Vulgate — circulated widely in manuscript despite official suppression after 1408.

Posthumous condemnation and legacy

In 1415 the Council of Constance declared Wycliffe a heretic; in 1428 his bones were exhumed, burned and cast into the River Swift. His ideas nonetheless influenced Jan Hus in Bohemia and, through the Lollards, anticipated themes of the English Reformation a century and a half later.
