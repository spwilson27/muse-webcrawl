San Francisco cable car system

OperatorSFMTA (Muni)

The San Francisco cable car system is the world's last manually operated cable car network, an icon of San Francisco and a US National Historic Landmark. Cable cars are pulled along by a continuously moving steel cable beneath the street, which the car's gripman clamps with a lever-operated grip; a second operator, the conductor, works the brakes and collects fares.

History

Andrew Smith Hallidie launched the first cable railway, the Clay Street Hill Railroad, in 1873, inspired by the difficulty horses faced on the city's steep grades. At their peak, eight companies operated dozens of lines across the city. The 1906 earthquake and fire destroyed much of the system, and electric streetcars and buses replaced most lines in the following decades. Campaigns led by Friedel Klussmann and others saved the remaining lines from a 1947 closure proposal, and voters mandated their preservation in the city charter.

Lines and operation

Three lines survive: the Powell–Hyde and Powell–Mason lines, which share the Powell Street corridor and famous turntables, and the California Street line. All cables run at a constant 9.5 mph from a single powerhouse and carbarn at Washington and Mason, which also houses the Cable Car Museum.

Tourism

Carrying millions of riders a year, the cars are primarily a tourist attraction as well as transport, with long queues at the Powell and Hyde turnarounds. The system's bells — rung by gripmen in an annual bell-ringing contest — are part of the city's soundscape.
