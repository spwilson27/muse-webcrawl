The Taconic Mountains are a mountain range of the Appalachian system, extending for roughly 150 miles (240 km) along the borders of New York, Massachusetts, Vermont and Connecticut. The range includes Mount Greylock, the highest point in Massachusetts at 3,491 feet (1,064 m), and Mount Equinox in Vermont, the range's highest summit. To the east the Taconics are separated from the Green Mountains by the Vermont Valley; to the west they descend to the Hudson River valley.

Geology

The Taconics are geologically celebrated as the product of the Taconic orogeny, a mountain-building episode of the Ordovician period (about 440–460 million years ago) in which an island arc collided with the eastern margin of North America. Enormous sheets of deep-water sedimentary rock were thrust westward over younger shallow-water formations—the "Taconic allochthon"—producing the paradox of older rocks lying atop younger ones that puzzled 19th-century geologists and helped found American structural geology. The range's bedrock is dominated by slate, phyllite, schist and marble, extensively folded and faulted.

Ecology and conservation

The range supports northern hardwood and spruce–fir forests and is an important corridor for wide-ranging wildlife between the Adirondacks, Greens and Berkshires. Large protected tracts include Mount Greylock State Reservation, Taconic State Park in New York, and sections of the Appalachian Trail, which follows the ridge through Massachusetts and Connecticut. The Taconic Crest Trail runs some 37 miles along the New York–Massachusetts–Vermont ridge line.

Name

"Taconic" derives from a Native American term, rendered variously as taghkanic, associated with the area around Copake, New York, and has been interpreted as "in the trees" or "great forest".
