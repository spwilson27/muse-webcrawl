Akiva ben Joseph (c. 50 – 135 CE), also known as Rabbi Akiva, was a leading Jewish scholar and sage, a tanna of the latter part of the first century and the beginning of the second century. He was one of the most important contributors to the Mishnah and to rabbinic Judaism, and is regarded as one of the foundational figures of the rabbinic tradition.

Life

According to rabbinic tradition, Akiva began life as an uneducated shepherd in the service of the wealthy Jerusalemite Kalba Savua. Encouraged by Kalba Savua's daughter Rachel, whom he married, he began studying Torah only at the age of forty, eventually studying under Rabbi Eliezer ben Hyrcanus and Joshua ben Hananiah and founding an academy at Bnei Brak, where tradition credits him with 24,000 students.

Teaching and work

Akiva systematised the growing body of oral law, organising the halakhot topically in a way that became the basis for the Mishnah as later redacted by Judah ha-Nasi. His hermeneutic method derived legal conclusions even from ornamental features of the biblical text, such as the crowns on letters. He is also credited with a role in the organisation of the Midrash and with contributions associated with the translation of Aquila of Sinope. Among his famous sayings recorded in Pirkei Avot: "Beloved is man, for he was created in the image [of God]".

Death

Akiva publicly supported the Bar Kokhba revolt against Rome (132–136 CE), reportedly hailing Simon bar Kokhba as the Messiah. After the revolt's suppression, the Romans arrested him for continuing to teach Torah in defiance of the Hadrianic decrees. He was executed at Caesarea in 135 CE; rabbinic accounts describe him being flayed with iron combs while reciting the Shema, dying with the word "Echad" ("One") on his lips. He is counted among the Ten Martyrs of Jewish tradition.
