The electric rays are a group of rays, flattened cartilaginous fish with enlarged pectoral fins, constituting the order Torpediniformes. They are known for being capable of producing an electric discharge, ranging from as little as 8 volts up to 220 volts depending on species, used to stun prey and for defence. There are about 69 species in four families.

Anatomy and electric organs

Electric rays have a rounded, flabby disc, a stout tail with one or two dorsal fins and a caudal fin, and smooth skin without denticles or thorns. Their paired electric organs, derived from modified muscle tissue, lie on either side of the head. Each organ consists of stacks of electrocytes — flattened cells wired in series like the plates of a battery — which fire simultaneously under nervous control to generate the discharge.

Behaviour and habitat

Most species are sluggish bottom-dwellers of sandy or muddy coastal waters, burying themselves by day and hunting fish and invertebrates at night; a few, such as the Atlantic torpedo, can swim in the water column. The shock of a large torpedo ray is powerful enough to knock down an adult human, though it is used mainly on prey and is rarely dangerous beyond a painful jolt.

Human history

Electric rays were known in antiquity: the Greek word narkē (numbness) underlies "narcosis". The Roman physician Scribonius Largus prescribed shocks from a live black torpedo for headaches and gout — an early form of electrotherapy. The genus name Torpedo derives from Latin torpere, "to be numb or stiff", and the fish later lent its name to the naval weapon.
