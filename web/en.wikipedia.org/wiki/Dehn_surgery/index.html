Dehn surgery

In topology, a branch of mathematics, a Dehn surgery, named after Max Dehn, is a construction used to modify 3-manifolds. The process takes as input a 3-manifold together with a link, and produces a new 3-manifold. Dehn introduced the construction in 1910, and it became one of the central tools of low-dimensional topology: the Lickorish–Wallace theorem states that every closed, orientable, connected 3-manifold can be obtained by performing Dehn surgery on a framed link in the 3-sphere.

Construction

Given a 3-manifold M and a link L in it, one drills out a tubular neighborhood of the link, producing a manifold with torus boundary components. A new manifold is then formed by gluing solid tori back onto these boundary tori, but with a twist: the meridian curve of each solid torus is attached along a chosen slope (essential simple closed curve) on the boundary torus. The slope is conventionally specified by a rational number p/q — the surgery coefficient — relative to the knot's meridian and longitude, in a procedure called p/q-surgery. Integer coefficients correspond to integral surgery. Surgery with coefficient ∞ restores the original manifold.

Examples and results

Surgery on knots in the 3-sphere produces a vast range of manifolds: 0-surgery on a knot gives the knot's mapping torus-like "0-trace" manifold, and ±1-surgery on the unknot yields lens spaces and spheres. A fundamental theorem of Cameron Gordon and John Luecke (1989) shows that knots are determined by their complements — famously proved using surgery techniques. Hyperbolic Dehn surgery, developed by William Thurston, shows that all but finitely many surgeries on a hyperbolic knot yield hyperbolic manifolds, a key step toward the geometrization of 3-manifolds. Some surgeries are exceptional (producing non-hyperbolic or reducible manifolds); the classification of exceptional surgeries remains an active research area connected to the cosmetic surgery conjecture.
