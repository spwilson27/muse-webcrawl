Aqueducts of Rome

The Aqua Claudia near Rome, one of the great aqueducts of the ancient city.

The aqueducts of Rome were a system of eleven aqueducts built between 312 BCE and 226 CE that supplied the ancient city of Rome with water for drinking, baths, fountains, gardens, and industry. At their peak the aqueducts carried an estimated 1 million cubic metres of water per day over a combined length of more than 800 kilometres, most of it running underground.

The eleven aqueducts

The first, the Aqua Appia (312 BCE), was built by the censor Appius Claudius Caecus. It was followed by the Anio Vetus (272–269 BCE), Aqua Marcia (144–140 BCE), Aqua Tepula (125 BCE), Aqua Julia (33 BCE), Aqua Virgo (19 BCE), Aqua Alsietina (2 BCE), Aqua Claudia and Anio Novus (both completed 52 CE), Aqua Traiana (109 CE), and Aqua Alexandrina (226 CE). The Aqua Marcia was famous for the quality of its water; the Aqua Virgo still feeds the Trevi Fountain today.

Administration

Our principal source is De aquaeductu by Sextus Julius Frontinus, appointed water commissioner (curator aquarum) in 97 CE. The system relied on gravity alone, with channels following a very gentle gradient, carried on arched bridges (arcades) across valleys and maintained through settling tanks and distribution castella. The aqueducts were cut during the Gothic siege of Rome in 537 CE, and most fell into disuse in the early Middle Ages.
