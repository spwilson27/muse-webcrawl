Origin of the domestic dog

The origin of the domestic dog concerns the evolutionary divergence of the dog (Canis familiaris or Canis lupus familiaris) from the wolf, its domestication, and the development of the earliest dogs. Genetic studies show that dogs descend from an extinct population of Pleistocene wolves — not directly from any living wolf population — and that the dog was the first species to be domesticated by humans, well before the advent of agriculture.

Dogs diverged from an extinct lineage of Pleistocene wolves.

Timing

Estimates from genomic data place the divergence of dogs from wolves between roughly 20,000 and 40,000 years ago, during the Last Glacial Maximum era. The earliest widely accepted dog remains are those of the Bonn–Oberkassel dog, buried with humans in Germany about 14,200 years ago. More ancient candidate specimens — such as the Goyet Cave canid from Belgium (~31,700 years) and the Razboinichya Cave canid from Siberia (~33,000 years) — are debated and may represent early, ultimately unsuccessful domestication episodes or morphologically unusual wolves.

Place and process

Where domestication occurred remains contested: studies have proposed Siberia, East Asia, Central Asia, the Middle East and Europe, and a 2016 study suggested dual domestication from separate wolf populations in East and West Eurasia, though later work favoured a single origin followed by admixture. One influential scenario is commensal domestication: less fearful wolves scavenged around hunter-gatherer camps, and selection for tameness gradually produced a self-domesticating camp-follower population. Another proposes deliberate adoption and raising of wolf pups by Palaeolithic hunters.

Changes under domestication

Compared with wolves, dogs show reduced skull and tooth size, floppy ears and piebald coats in many breeds, retention of juvenile behaviours into adulthood (neoteny), and — in most populations — extra copies of the amylase gene AMY2B aiding starch digestion, though this adaptation may postdate early domestication. Studies of ancient DNA show that by about 11,000 years ago several distinct dog lineages already existed across Eurasia, and that dogs accompanied humans into the Americas and Oceania.
