The Cromwellian conquest of Ireland (1649–1653) was the re-conquest of Ireland by the forces of the English Parliament, led by Oliver Cromwell, during the Wars of the Three Kingdoms. It culminated in the defeat of the Irish Catholic Confederates and their Royalist allies, and in a sweeping confiscation of Catholic-owned land.

Background

The Irish Rebellion of 1641 and the subsequent Confederate Wars had left much of Ireland under the Irish Catholic Confederation, which in 1649 allied with the exiled Charles II's Royalists under the Marquess of Ormond. Parliament, having executed Charles I, resolved to reconquer Ireland both to secure England and to repay loans raised on the security of Irish land under the Adventurers' Act of 1642.

The campaign

Cromwell landed at Dublin in August 1649 with some 12,000 men of the New Model Army. His storming of Drogheda (September 1649) and Wexford (October 1649) involved massacres of garrisons and civilians that remain notorious. In 1650 the Parliamentarians took Kilkenny and won at Scarrifholis; Waterford and Derry resisted plague more than assault. Cromwell departed in May 1650, leaving Henry Ireton, and later Charles Fleetwood, to complete the conquest. Clonmel inflicted heavy losses on the attackers under Hugh Dubh O'Neill before falling. The last major strongholds — Limerick (1651) and Galway (1652) — surrendered, and the final organised resistance ended with the capitulation at Cavan in 1653.

Settlement and consequences

The Act for the Settlement of Ireland (1652) confiscated the lands of Catholics involved in the wars, transplanting many to Connacht — the origin of the phrase "to hell or to Connacht". Thousands of Irish soldiers and prisoners were transported to the West Indies and North America. War, famine, and plague during the Confederate and Cromwellian wars are estimated to have reduced Ireland's population drastically, perhaps by 15–25% or more, and Catholic land ownership fell from about 60% in 1641 to under 10% by the end of the century.

Legacy

Cromwell's campaign is remembered in Ireland as a byword for brutality — "the curse of Cromwell" — and remains one of the most contentious episodes in Anglo-Irish history.
