Treasure ship

A treasure ship is a vessel, typically of the early modern period, that transported precious cargoes — above all gold and silver bullion, coin, jewels and luxury goods — from colonies to the home country of a colonial power. The term is most closely associated with the Spanish treasure fleets that carried the wealth of the Americas to Spain between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries.

Spanish treasure fleets

Silver mined at Potosí (in present-day Bolivia) and Zacatecas (Mexico) was carried by mule train to the coasts and loaded onto galleons. Two fleets sailed annually: the flota to Veracruz in New Spain and the galeones to Cartagena and Portobelo in South America. They rendezvoused at Havana before crossing the Atlantic together to Seville (later Cádiz), braving hurricanes, shoals and attack by pirates, privateers and rival navies. The convoys were remarkably successful: only a handful of fleets were captured, notably by the Dutch admiral Piet Hein in 1628 off Cuba.

Manila galleons

A related trade was the Manila galleon route (1565–1815), in which silver from Acapulco crossed the Pacific to the Philippines and was exchanged for Chinese silk, porcelain and spices — making the galleons prized targets; several were captured by British and other raiders.

Wrecks and salvage

Storms sank many treasure ships in the Caribbean and off Florida, and their wrecks have been the object of famous modern salvage operations, including the Nuestra Señora de Atocha (sunk 1622, found by Mel Fisher in 1985) and the 1715 Treasure Fleet wrecked off Florida. Such recoveries have raised legal and archaeological controversies over ownership and the preservation of underwater cultural heritage.

Chinese treasure ships

The term is also applied to the great junks (baochuan) of the Ming-dynasty admiral Zheng He, whose fleets sailed the Indian Ocean between 1405 and 1433 carrying treasure and tribute goods.
