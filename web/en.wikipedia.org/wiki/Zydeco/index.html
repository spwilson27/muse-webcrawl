Zydeco

Zydeco is a music genre created by Black Creoles of southwest Louisiana and southeast Texas, blending French Creole songs, Cajun music, African-American blues and rhythm and blues. It is centred on the accordion and the frottoir, a rub-board worn as a vest. The name is usually traced to the French phrase les haricots sont pas salés — "the snap beans aren't salty", an expression of hard times — rendered in Creole as "zydeco".

History

Zydeco grew out of the "la-la" music played at house dances in Creole communities, itself rooted in juré singing — rhythmic group singing with hand-clapping and foot-stomping. Amédé Ardoin, a Black Creole accordionist who recorded from 1929, laid foundations together with Cajun fiddler Dennis McGee. After the Second World War the music absorbed electric instruments and R&B; Clifton Chenier, billed as the "King of Zydeco", popularised the piano accordion and the vest frottoir (designed with jeweller's help in the 1940s) and won a Grammy in 1983. Boozoo Chavis's 1955 recording "Paper in My Shoe" was an early regional hit.

Style and culture

Zydeco bands typically feature accordion, frottoir, electric guitar, bass and drums, with songs in Louisiana French, Creole or English. The music serves social dancing — two-steps and waltzes mixed with freer styles — at clubs, church halls and trail rides, the Creole horseback processions that remain central to the culture. Stanley Dural Jr., known as Buckwheat Zydeco, carried the music to national television and major-label audiences, and the genre is celebrated at festivals in Lafayette and Opelousas.

Relationship to Cajun music

Zydeco and Cajun music share geography, language and instruments, and are sometimes grouped together, but they arise from different communities — Black Creole and white French-descended Cajun respectively — and zydeco places greater weight on blues, R&B and syncopated rhythm.
