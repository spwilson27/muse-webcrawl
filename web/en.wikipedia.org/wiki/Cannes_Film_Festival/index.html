Cannes Film Festival

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Palais des Festivals steps in May: red carpet, photographers' ladders, and the golden palm crest above the doors]

The Cannes Film Festival (Festival de Cannes) is an annual film festival held in Cannes, France, on the French Riviera, which previews new films of all genres from all around the world. Founded in 1946 (first conceived 1939 as a democratic rival to Mussolini-influenced Venice) it is the most prestigious and influential film festival in the world; its top prize, the Palme d'Or (Golden Palm, introduced 1955), is cinema's highest honour alongside the Oscar.

Each May, for twelve days, world cinema decamps to the Croisette: some twenty films compete in the Official Selection, with Un Certain Regard, the Directors' Fortnight and Critics' Week alongside, and the Marché du Film — the world's largest film market — doing the industry's year's business in the Palais basement.

The ritual

Cannes's liturgy is fixed and sacred: the jury's deliberations, the photo calls, the red-carpet dress codes (the flat-shoes controversy of 2015 produced permanent embarrassment and rule-flexing), the standing ovations timed to the minute by trade papers, and the booing — equally traditional — at press screenings. Its discoveries and coronations (from Rossellini and Fellini to Pulp Fiction's 1994 Palme to Parasite's unanimous 2019 win) write film history in real time.

Cannes and power

The festival's selections map geopolitics as much as taste: Iranian, Korean, Romanian and Filipino cinemas announced themselves here; Netflix's exclusion from competition (after the 2017 Okja row, French exhibitors forced a theatrical-window rule) showed Cannes defending the cinema temple against streaming's tide. Accreditations run to tens of thousands; the waiting list for journalists is a profession's pilgrimage.
