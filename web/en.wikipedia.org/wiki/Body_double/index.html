Body double

In filmmaking, a body double is a person who substitutes for an actor in scenes where the actor's face is not shown, such as nude scenes, specific physical actions or long shots. Body doubles are selected for their physical resemblance to the principal actor in build, height, skin tone and other visible characteristics. The use of body doubles allows stars to avoid scenes they are unwilling or contractually unable to perform, and gives productions flexibility in scheduling.

Types

A photo double appears on camera in place of the actor for over-the-shoulder shots, inserts (such as close-ups of hands) or distant shots. A stunt double performs dangerous action sequences and may also serve as a body double. A stand-in, by contrast, does not usually appear on camera but takes the actor's place while lighting and focus are set. "Nude doubles" substitute in scenes involving nudity that the actor declines; contracts and union rules govern consent and the scope of such work.

Practice

Body doubles are credited variably and were historically uncredited; actors are not always publicly identified with their doubles, and some high-profile performers have objected when producers relied on doubles or, in the digital era, on computer-generated imagery to replace their appearance or performance. Digital doubling — scanning an actor's likeness to create a virtual substitute — has raised ethical and labour questions, addressed in part by union agreements requiring consent and compensation.

Outside film

The term is also used for political decoys: doubles employed by heads of state or military figures, a practice widely attributed to leaders such as Joseph Stalin and Saddam Hussein, though individual claims are often difficult to verify.
