Flight to Varennes
Date20–21 June 1791
LocationParis to Varennes-en-Argonne, France
ParticipantsLouis XVI, Marie Antoinette, the royal family, Axel von Fersen
OutcomeRoyal family recognised, arrested and returned to Paris under guard

The Flight to Varennes (French: Fuite à Varennes) during the night of 20–21 June 1791 was a significant episode in the French Revolution in which King Louis XVI, Queen Marie Antoinette, and their immediate family unsuccessfully attempted to escape from Paris in order to initiate a counter-revolution at the head of loyal troops under royalist officers concentrated at Montmédy near the frontier.

They escaped only as far as the small town of Varennes-en-Argonne, where they were arrested after having been recognised at their previous stop in Sainte-Menehould by a postmaster named Jean-Baptiste Drouet. Their failed escape had a profound impact on public opinion: it demonstrated the king's rejection of the revolutionary settlement, destroyed remaining popular faith in the monarchy, and pushed France towards republicanism and war.

The arrest of the royal family at Varennes.

Background

Since the October Days of 1789 the royal family had resided, under close surveillance, at the Tuileries Palace in Paris. Louis deeply resented the Civil Constitution of the Clergy and his effective captivity. The escape was planned with the help of the Swedish count Axel von Fersen, a confidant of the queen. The family travelled disguised as servants of a fictitious Russian baroness, in a heavy berline coach, towards the fortress town of Montmédy, where the Marquis de Bouillé commanded loyal troops.

The flight and arrest

Delays accumulated through the night: the coach left Paris late, relay horses were not ready at several stops, and protective detachments of hussars, expecting the party earlier, withdrew. At Sainte-Menehould the postmaster Drouet recognised the king — according to tradition, from his portrait on an assignat banknote — and raced ahead to Varennes, where the coach was stopped and the family detained in an upper room of a grocer's house until revolutionary commissioners arrived from Paris.

Aftermath

The royal family was escorted back to Paris in humiliating silence before enormous crowds. The Assembly at first maintained the fiction that the king had been "kidnapped", and Louis was reinstated under the Constitution of 1791 after swearing an oath to it. However, the flight shattered the image of the king as father of the nation. Republican agitation grew, culminating in the Champ de Mars massacre of July 1791. The episode also deepened foreign courts' hostility, and it foreshadowed the insurrection of 10 August 1792 that ended the monarchy.
