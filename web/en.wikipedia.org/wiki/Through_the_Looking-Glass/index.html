Through the Looking-Glass

Through the Looking-Glass, and What Alice Found There
Author: Lewis Carroll
Illustrator: John Tenniel
Published: 1871 (dated 1872)
Publisher: Macmillan
Preceded by: Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865)

Through the Looking-Glass, and What Alice Found There is an 1871 novel by Lewis Carroll, the pen name of Charles Lutwidge Dodgson, and the sequel to Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865). Alice again enters a fantastical world, this time by climbing through a mirror into the world she can see reflected beyond it. There, everything is reversed, including logic and time, and the landscape is divided into squares like a vast chessboard; Alice herself begins as a pawn and journeys across the board to become a queen.

Plot and characters

In the course of her journey Alice encounters the Red Queen, Tweedledum and Tweedledee, Humpty Dumpty, the Lion and the Unicorn, the White Knight, and the brothers' recited poems, among them "The Walrus and the Carpenter". The book contains the celebrated nonsense poem "Jabberwocky" and the song "The Walrus and the Carpenter", as well as Carroll's philosophical dialogues on language, names, and meaning — notably Humpty Dumpty's discussion of semantics and the White Knight's song with its shifting titles.

Themes

Where Wonderland drew its logic from a pack of playing cards and a garden, Through the Looking-Glass is structured by chess and mirror reversal: time can run backwards, as with the White Queen who lives backwards and remembers the future; cause and effect invert; and Alice must move forward to stay in place in the Red Queen's famous "Red Queen's race". The novel closes with the poem "A boat beneath a sunny sky" and the lingering question of who dreamed the adventure — Alice, or the Red King.

Legacy

The book introduced phrases that have entered English, such as "jam every other day", "unbirthday", and the concept of the Red Queen's race, later borrowed in evolutionary biology as the Red Queen hypothesis. Along with its predecessor it has never been out of print and remains one of the most widely read works of Victorian children's literature.
