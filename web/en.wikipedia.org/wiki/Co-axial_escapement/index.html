Co-axial escapement

The co-axial escapement is a type of modern escapement used in mechanical wristwatches. It was invented in 1974 by the English master watchmaker George Daniels and patented in 1980. Unlike the traditional Swiss lever escapement, which had remained essentially unchanged for more than two centuries, the co-axial escapement gives impulse to the balance in a largely tangential direction, reducing sliding friction between the pallet stones and the escape wheel teeth.

Design

The escapement takes its name from its double co-axial wheel: a smaller escape pinion/wheel is mounted on the same axis as the main escape wheel. Impulse is delivered partly through the traditional pallet stones and partly through an additional impulse stone acting directly with the co-axial wheel. Because the impulses are delivered nearly radially rather than through sliding contact, the mechanism needs little or no lubrication on the impulse faces, and the geometry promises longer service intervals and more stable rate over time.

History

Daniels, seeking a practical escapement that could free the mechanical watch from the lever escapement's dependence on oil, developed the design in the 1970s and spent years trying to interest Swiss manufacturers in it. The Swiss watch company Omega eventually adopted the design, introducing the first mass-produced co-axial movement, the Omega calibre 2500, in 1999. Omega subsequently developed in-house co-axial calibres, including the 8500 series introduced in 2007, and made the escapement a centrepiece of its marketing and of its Master Chronometer movements.

The early calibre 2500, adapted from an ETA base movement and running at 28,800 vibrations per hour, experienced some reliability problems; Omega reduced the beat rate to 25,200 vph and refined the design in later versions. Daniels received recognition late in life, and his handmade watches fitted with the escapement are among the most sought-after by collectors.
