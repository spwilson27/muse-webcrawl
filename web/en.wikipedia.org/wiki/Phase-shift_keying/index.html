Phase-shift keying

Phase-shift keying (PSK) is a digital modulation scheme that conveys data by changing (modulating) the phase of a constant-frequency reference signal, the carrier wave. Each symbol is represented by a distinct phase angle; the number of phases determines how many bits each symbol carries.

Variants

Binary phase-shift keying (BPSK) uses two phases, 0° and 180°, carrying one bit per symbol. It is the most robust of the PSK family against noise but the least spectrally efficient.

Quadrature phase-shift keying (QPSK) uses four phases (45°, 135°, 225°, 315°), carrying two bits per symbol while retaining the same bit-error performance as BPSK at equal energy per bit. QPSK can be viewed as two independent BPSK signals on in-phase and quadrature carriers.

Higher-order schemes — 8-PSK, 16-PSK and beyond — carry more bits per symbol at the cost of greater sensitivity to noise and phase distortion. Differential PSK (DPSK) encodes information in the phase change between successive symbols, avoiding the need for a phase-coherent reference at the receiver.

Applications

PSK and its combination with amplitude modulation (quadrature amplitude modulation, QAM) underpin most modern digital communications: Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11) uses BPSK and QPSK at its lower data rates, satellite television and DVB standards use QPSK and 8-PSK, and PSK variants appear in RFID, Bluetooth and deep-space telemetry links.

Performance is commonly described by constellation diagrams and by bit-error-rate curves: for a given symbol energy, higher-order PSK needs a higher signal-to-noise ratio to maintain the same error rate.
