Distributed control system

A distributed control system (DCS) is a computerised control system for a process or plant usually with many control loops, in which autonomous controllers are distributed throughout the system, but there is no central operator supervisory control. This is in contrast to systems that use centralized control controllers.

Structure

A DCS typically consists of field devices (sensors and actuators), controllers that execute regulatory control close to the process, a supervisory level of operator workstations and engineering stations, and historians that record process data. Redundancy at every level — controllers, networks, and servers — is a hallmark of DCS design, because the systems run continuous processes in oil refining, petrochemicals, power generation, pharmaceuticals, and water treatment where downtime is costly.

History

The first DCS products, such as Honeywell's TDC 2000 and Yokogawa's Centum, appeared in 1975, made possible by microprocessors. They replaced panels of single-loop analog controllers and centralized mainframe control with networked digital control.

Relation to other systems

DCSs overlap with SCADA systems and programmable logic controllers (PLCs). DCSs are oriented toward continuous process control, PLCs toward discrete manufacturing, and SCADA toward geographically dispersed assets, though the technologies have converged.
