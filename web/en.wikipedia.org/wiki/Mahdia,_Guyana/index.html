Mahdia is a town in the Potaro-Siparuni region (Region 8) of Guyana, in the country's interior. It serves as the regional capital and as the principal commercial centre for the gold and diamond mining districts of the middle Potaro and Siparuni areas. The town lies near the Potaro River, not far downstream from the famous Kaieteur Falls, and is connected to the coast by an unpaved road to Linden and Georgetown, as well as by Mahdia Airport.

History

Mahdia grew up as a mining settlement serving the interior goldfields; its very name is associated in local tradition with the early mining era. Prospectors known locally as "pork-knockers" have worked the creeks and rivers around Mahdia for gold and diamonds since the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The town's importance increased as it became the administrative and supply centre for Region 8. Mahdia was officially declared a town in 2018, becoming one of Guyana's newer municipalities, a status intended to bring improved governance and services to the mining community.

Economy

The local economy is dominated by gold and diamond mining, together with the retail trade, transportation, and services that support the mining camps of the surrounding districts. Shops in Mahdia supply fuel, food, equipment, and mercury-free processing services to miners, and the town functions as a transshipment point between river and road transport. Logging and small-scale agriculture are secondary activities.

Geography and transport

Mahdia sits in forested, hilly country of the Guiana Shield, drained by tributaries of the Potaro River, itself a tributary of the Essequibo. The climate is tropical rainforest, with heavy rainfall. The town is reached by a laterite road from Linden — a journey of several hours, seasonally difficult — and by light aircraft from Georgetown. Trails and rivers connect Mahdia to mining areas such as Konawaruk and to communities along the Potaro.

2023 dormitory fire

In May 2023, a fire at the Mahdia Secondary School dormitory killed 19 students, most of them girls from Indigenous communities of the interior, and injured many others. The disaster was one of the deadliest in Guyana's history, prompted national mourning, and led to reviews of dormitory safety standards across the country.
