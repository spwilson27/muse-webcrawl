Channel model

A channel model, in communications engineering, is a mathematical description of how a signal propagates through a communication channel between a transmitter and a receiver. Channel models predict effects such as path loss, shadowing, multipath fading, delay spread, and Doppler spread, and they are used to design and evaluate modulation, coding, and multiple-antenna (MIMO) techniques before deployment. Models range from simple analytical formulas to detailed site-specific ray-tracing simulations and standardized statistical models used by bodies such as the ITU and 3GPP.

Multipath propagation, a central phenomenon captured by channel models

Propagation effects

  • Path loss – the average attenuation of signal power with distance, often modelled with a log-distance law.
  • Shadowing – slow variations caused by large obstacles, commonly modelled as log-normal.
  • Multipath fading – rapid fluctuations as reflected copies of the signal add constructively and destructively; modelled with Rayleigh or Rician distributions.
  • Delay and Doppler spread – the time dispersion of multipath components and the frequency dispersion caused by motion.

Common models

The additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) channel is the simplest model, adding only thermal noise. Wireless models include the free-space path loss model, the Okumura–Hata and COST-231 models for cellular planning, tapped-delay-line fading models, and the geometry-based stochastic channel models (such as the 3GPP spatial channel model) used for MIMO system evaluation. Wired and optical channels have their own models describing attenuation, dispersion, and crosstalk.

Uses

Channel models are embedded in link-level and system-level simulators, are used to generate test conditions for standard compliance, and drive channel emulators in hardware testbeds. Shannon's channel capacity theory provides the fundamental limits against which practical systems modelled by these channels are judged.
