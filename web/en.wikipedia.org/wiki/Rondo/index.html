Rondo

A rondo is a musical form characterised by the periodic return of a principal theme (the refrain) alternating with contrasting episodes. The simplest scheme is ABACA, extended in longer works to ABACABA or more. The name derives from the French rondeau, a medieval poetic and musical form built on a refrain. In the Classical era the rondo became the standard form for the final movements of sonatas, concertos and symphonies, where its lively, recurring tune provided a memorable conclusion.

History

The Baroque rondeau was cultivated by French composers such as François Couperin and Jean-Philippe Rameau in harpsichord pieces, where a refrain alternated with couplets. In the later 18th century Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven made the rondo the typical finale of instrumental cycles; Beethoven's "Rondo alla ingharese quasi un capriccio" (the so-called Rage Over a Lost Penny) is a famous independent example. A hybrid, the sonata rondo, blends rondo recurrence with sonata-form development and recapitulation and is common in concerto finales, including those of Mozart.

Characteristics

The refrain normally appears in the tonic key at each return, often varied or abbreviated, while episodes introduce new themes and contrasting keys. Rondos are typically in a fast tempo and a light or brilliant character, though slow-movement rondos exist, such as the Adagio rondo of Beethoven's Pathétique sonata (which is better described as a modified rondo). In theory, a strict rondo requires at least three statements of the refrain; forms with two are classed as rondino or analysed as five-part rondo.

Examples

  • Mozart, Piano Concerto No. 23 in A major, K. 488, finale
  • Beethoven, Piano Sonata Op. 13 (Pathétique), third movement
  • Haydn, "Gypsy Rondo" from Piano Trio No. 39 in G major
  • Mendelssohn, Rondo capriccioso, Op. 14
