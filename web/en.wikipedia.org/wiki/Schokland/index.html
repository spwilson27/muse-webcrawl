Schokland is a former island in the Dutch Zuiderzee, now surrounded by the reclaimed land of the Noordoostpolder in the province of Flevoland, Netherlands. Inhabited since prehistoric times, it symbolises the Dutch struggle against the sea: its population battled flooding for centuries before the government evacuated the island in 1859, and the draining of the surrounding polder in 1942 left it as a low ridge of land in the middle of farmland.

History

Schokland was originally a peninsula that became an island as the sea eroded the peatlands around it during the Middle Ages. Its inhabitants lived from fishing and trade, concentrated in the settlements of Emmeloord, Molenbuurt, and Ens. Repeated storm floods, shrinking land, and poverty led the government to order the island's complete evacuation in 1859; its roughly 650 inhabitants were resettled on the mainland, many in Kampen, Volendam, and Brunnepe. The characteristic long, narrow shape of the island and its raised dwelling mounds (terps) remain visible in the landscape.

Heritage

In 1995 Schokland and its surroundings became the first Dutch site inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, recognised as an outstanding example of a prehistoric and historic settlement adapted to a changing marine environment. The Museum Schokland, housed around the former church at Middelbuurt, presents the island's archaeology and history.
