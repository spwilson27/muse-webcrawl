Lap steel guitar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The lap steel guitar, also known as a Hawaiian guitar, is a type of steel guitar played in a horizontal position across the player's lap. Unlike a conventional guitar, the strings are not pressed against the frets; instead the player slides a polished steel bar (the "steel" or tone bar) along the strings to change pitch, producing the gliding, singing tone characteristic of Hawaiian music, country music, Western swing, blues and sacred steel traditions.

History

The technique originated in Hawaii in the late nineteenth century; Joseph Kekuku is widely credited, around the 1890s, with inventing the method of sliding a steel object along guitar strings raised off the fingerboard. Hawaiian music became a craze in the United States in the 1910s and 1920s, and the Hawaiian guitar was briefly the best-selling style of guitar in America. In the 1930s the lap steel was among the very first instruments to be electrically amplified: the Rickenbacker A-22 "Frying Pan" (1931–32) is generally considered the first commercial electric guitar.

Construction and technique

Lap steels may be acoustic (often converted Hawaiian guitars with raised nuts, or squareneck resonator guitars) or solid-body electric instruments with one or two pickups and six, seven, eight or more strings. The strings sit high above a fretboard marked (but not fretted) for reference. Players use fingerpicks and a bullet or Stevens-style bar, and manipulate volume pedals and bar slants to voice chords. Open tunings are standard: C6, A6, E7 and E9 among them in Western music; Hawaiian players use a variety of "slack-key"-related steel tunings.

Styles and players

The lap steel was central to Western swing (Bob Wills's band, with Leon McAuliffe) and honky-tonk country, and it was the direct ancestor of the pedal steel guitar, which added pedals and knee levers to change tunings mid-note. In blues and rock, players such as David Lindley and Ben Harper (on Weissenborn-style instruments) revived it; in African-American Pentecostal churches, the "sacred steel" tradition produced virtuosos such as Robert Randolph (who plays pedal steel). Noted Hawaiian masters include Sol Hoʻopiʻi and Jerry Byrd, often called the "Master of Touch and Tone".
