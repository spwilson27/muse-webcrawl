Petit-Clamart attack

Date: 22 August 1962

Location: Petit-Clamart, near Paris, France

Target: Charles de Gaulle, President of France

Perpetrators: OAS commando led by Jean Bastien-Thiry

Outcome: Attack failed; de Gaulle unharmed

The Petit-Clamart attack was an assassination attempt on French President Charles de Gaulle that took place on the evening of 22 August 1962 in the Paris suburb of Petit-Clamart. It was organised by the far-right Organisation armée secrète (OAS), which opposed de Gaulle's acceptance of Algerian independence.

The ambush

A commando of about a dozen men, commanded by French Air Force lieutenant-colonel Jean Bastien-Thiry, ambushed the presidential motorcade as it drove from the Élysée Palace to Villacoublay airbase. As de Gaulle's unarmoured Citroën DS 19 — carrying the president, his wife Yvonne, their son-in-law Colonel Alain de Boissieu and driver Francis Marroux — passed the crossroads, the gunmen opened fire with automatic weapons, discharging roughly 180 rounds and bursting the car's tyres.

Miraculously, no one in the car was hit. Marroux kept control of the skidding DS — the car's hydropneumatic suspension allowed it to run on its rims — and accelerated out of the ambush. De Gaulle famously brushed glass off his clothes and remarked that the attackers "shot like pigs" (Ils ont tiré comme des cochons). A bullet had passed centimetres from his head.

Aftermath

The attackers were arrested within weeks. Bastien-Thiry was tried by a military court, sentenced to death, and executed by firing squad at Fort d'Ivry on 11 March 1963 — the last person executed by firing squad in France. The attack strengthened de Gaulle's resolve to establish direct election of the president, approved by referendum that October, and it was dramatised in Frederick Forsyth's novel The Day of the Jackal (1971), whose plot opens as a follow-up to the Petit-Clamart failure.
