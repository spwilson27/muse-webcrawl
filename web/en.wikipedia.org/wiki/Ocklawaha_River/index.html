Ocklawaha River

The Ocklawaha River is a river in central Florida, United States. Flowing north for about 74 miles (119 km), it is the principal tributary of the St. Johns River. The river's name derives from a Native American term often rendered as "crooked water" or "dark water," referring to its winding course and tannin-stained waters.

Course

The Ocklawaha begins at Lake Griffin, part of the Harris chain of lakes in Lake County, and flows north through Marion County past the Ocala National Forest. Its major tributary is the Silver River, fed by Silver Springs, one of the largest first-magnitude spring groups in Florida. Below the confluence, the river continues north and east to join the St. Johns River south of Palatka in Putnam County.

History

In the nineteenth century, steamboats plied the river, carrying tourists to Silver Springs and freight between the St. Johns and central Florida. In the twentieth century, the river was dramatically altered by the Cross Florida Barge Canal project: the Rodman Dam (later renamed the George Kirkpatrick Dam), completed in 1968, impounded the river to form Rodman Reservoir (Lake Ocklawaha). President Richard Nixon halted the canal project in 1971, partly in response to environmental campaigning led by Marjorie Harris Carr, but the dam and reservoir remain, and restoration of the free-flowing river has been debated ever since.

Ecology

The Ocklawaha supports floodplain forests of cypress and gum, abundant alligators, wading birds, and manatees that gather at its spring runs. The impoundment has flooded thousands of acres of floodplain and blocked migratory fish, issues central to the long-running restoration controversy.
