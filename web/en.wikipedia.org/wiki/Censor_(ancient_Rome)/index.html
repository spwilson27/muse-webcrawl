Censor (ancient Rome)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The censor was a magistrate in ancient Rome who was responsible for maintaining the census, supervising public morality, and overseeing certain aspects of the government's finances. The censors' regulation of public morality is the origin of the modern meaning of the words "censor" and "censorship".

Institution

The office was established in 443 BC, when the task of taking the census was removed from the consuls. Two censors were elected by the Centuriate Assembly, originally every five years; they held office for a maximum of eighteen months while they completed the census and the associated ceremonial purification of the people, the lustrum. Censors were usually former consuls, and the office was regarded as the capstone of a senatorial career, the cursus honorum. Unlike other magistrates, censors did not possess imperium and were not attended by lictors.

Duties

The censors conducted the census of citizens and their property, assigning each citizen to a tribe and a property class, and thereby to a voting century. They revised the roll of the Senate (the lectio senatus), enrolling new members and expelling those judged unworthy. Under the regimen morum they supervised public morals and could affix the nota censoria, a mark of disgrace, to citizens guilty of misconduct, with consequences for their status. The censors also let public contracts — for tax farming, construction, and maintenance of temples, roads and aqueducts — and administered certain public revenues.

History

Famous holders of the office include Appius Claudius Caecus (censor 312 BC), who built the Appian Way and the Aqua Appia, and Cato the Elder (censor 184 BC), whose severe enforcement of morals earned him the title "the Censor". Sulla curtailed the office's powers, and under the Empire the census and moral supervision passed to the emperor, the office of censor lapsing as a regular magistracy.
