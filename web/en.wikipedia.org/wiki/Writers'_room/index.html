Writers' room

A writers' room is a collaborative workspace — physical, virtual, or both — where a team of television (and increasingly streaming) writers work together to develop and write the scripts for a series. The model is most characteristic of American television production, where episodes are produced to a demanding weekly schedule and a showrunner leads a staff of writers who break stories collectively before individual scripts are assigned.

How a writers' room works

The showrunner — the executive producer in charge of the series' creative direction — heads the room. Below the showrunner, writers hold ranks ranging from executive producer and co-executive producer down through producers, story editors, and staff writers. The room's central task is "breaking story": working out the plot of each episode beat by beat, often on whiteboards or index cards pinned to a corkboard, before one writer is assigned to draft the script. Drafts are then rewritten collectively ("gang-banged" in industry slang) to keep a consistent voice.

History

Collaborative writing staffs developed in early American television and radio comedy, where variety shows required large volumes of jokes and sketches. The system became the industry standard for drama as well during the network era. In the United Kingdom, by contrast, series were more often written by a single author or a small partnership, though American-style rooms have become more common in British production in the streaming era.

Mini rooms and recent changes

In the 2010s and 2020s, streaming services popularized "mini rooms": smaller groups of writers hired for a short period to develop and script a season before a series is fully greenlit. The practice became a major point of contention in the 2023 Writers Guild of America strike, with writers arguing that mini rooms reduced employment stability and pay; the resulting agreement set minimum staffing and duration requirements for many productions.
