Moving Day

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Moving day in Montreal, Quebec, where thousands of leases traditionally change hands on July 1.

Moving Day is a tradition in the Canadian province of Quebec in which a very large proportion of residential leases begin and end on the same date, causing a mass, simultaneous change of housing each year. In modern Quebec the day falls on July 1, which is also Canada Day, so the moving frenzy takes place on a national holiday. On a typical July 1, hundreds of thousands of Quebecers — by some estimates some 10 percent of the province's households — pack up and move.

History

The tradition has several roots. Under the old French colonial law of New France, and later under the Civil Code of Lower Canada, urban leases customarily ran from May 1 to April 30, and landlords were forbidden to evict tenants during the winter months, when eviction into the cold could be life-threatening. The fixed spring moving date was a long-standing custom in Montreal and Quebec City.

In 1973 the government of Quebec passed legislation standardizing the residential lease year, moving the end date from April 30 to June 30 in order to give families with school-age children a less disruptive time to relocate, since the school year had ended. From then on, July 1 became the province's de facto moving day, a date reinforced in 1980 when July 1 and June 30 lease terms were further entrenched by housing legislation.

Consequences

Because so many moves are concentrated on a single day, moving companies in Quebec charge premium rates around July 1 and are often booked months in advance; rental trucks can be nearly impossible to obtain. Municipalities schedule extra garbage collection for the furniture and appliances left on curbs, and the day is notorious for traffic congestion, illegally parked moving vans, and disputes over whether departing tenants have left their units clean. It is also a common occasion for finding free furniture on sidewalks.

Moving Day in New York

A similar custom existed in New York City from colonial times until about the Second World War. Under Dutch and then English practice, most city leases expired on February 1 (later fixed by an act of 1820 to May 1), and on "Moving Day" — May 1 — a large fraction of the city's population changed homes simultaneously. Contemporary observers such as Davy Crockett and John Pintard described streets choked with carts, furniture piled on sidewalks, and general chaos. The custom faded as housing patterns changed in the 20th century.
