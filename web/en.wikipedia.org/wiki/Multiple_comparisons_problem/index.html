Multiple comparisons problem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In statistics, the multiple comparisons problem (also called the multiple testing problem) occurs when one considers a set of statistical inferences simultaneously or selects a subset of them for attention. The more inferences are made, the more likely erroneous conclusions — especially false positives — become: if 20 independent tests are run at the 5% significance level, on average one will appear "significant" by chance alone even when all null hypotheses are true.

The problem

If m independent comparisons are each tested at significance level α, the probability of at least one false positive — the family-wise error rate (FWER) — rises toward 1 − (1 − α)m, approaching certainty as m grows. A vivid example is genetic association studies, where hundreds of thousands of genetic variants are tested at once, and the "jelly bean" thought experiment popularised by an xkcd cartoon, in which testing twenty jelly-bean colours for a link to acne guarantees a spurious "discovery". Related concerns include data dredging, p-hacking and the look-elsewhere effect in particle physics.

Correction methods

Procedures controlling the FWER include the simple but conservative Bonferroni correction (testing each hypothesis at α/m), the step-down Holm–Bonferroni method, and Tukey's range test for pairwise comparisons. An alternative target is the false discovery rate (FDR), the expected proportion of false positives among rejected hypotheses, controlled by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure — now standard in genomics because it retains more statistical power. Resampling methods such as permutation tests offer another route.

Controversy

Whether and how strictly to adjust depends on context: exploratory analyses may report unadjusted results with appropriate caution, while confirmatory studies and large-scale screening demand correction. Critics argue that rigid correction inflates false negatives and that the relevant "family" of tests is often ill-defined; debates intensified during the replication crisis in psychology and other fields.
