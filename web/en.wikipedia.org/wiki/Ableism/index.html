Ableism

Ableism (from "able" + "-ism") is discrimination and social prejudice against people with disabilities, characterised by the assumption that typical abilities are superior and that disabled people are defective or in need of "fixing". The term is common in North American scholarship and activism; British usage often prefers disablism for discriminatory behaviour, reserving ableism for the underlying ideology — scholars such as Fiona Kumari Campbell use "ableism" for the network of beliefs that corporeality with able-bodiedness as essential and perfect.

Forms

Ableism operates at several levels: personal prejudice and stereotypes (for example, treating disabled people as inspirational merely for ordinary activities — what the late activist Stella Young called "inspiration porn" — or as objects of pity); institutional practices, such as inaccessible buildings, transport and websites, segregated education, and employment discrimination; and internalised ableism among disabled people themselves. Historically it underlay eugenics programmes, forced sterilisation, and institutionalisation. Disability-studies scholars contrast the medical model, which locates disability in individual impairment, with the social model, which locates it in barriers created by an ableist environment.

Legal responses

Antidiscrimination law addresses aspects of ableism through instruments such as the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990), the UK Equality Act (2010), and the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006), which require reasonable accommodation and accessibility. Disability rights advocates continue to campaign on issues including accessible housing, inclusive education, supported employment, and opposition to policies presuming disabled lives are of lesser value — debates that resurfaced sharply during COVID-19 triage controversies.
