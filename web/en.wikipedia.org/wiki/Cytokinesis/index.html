Cytokinesis

Cytokinesis is the part of the cell division process during which the cytoplasm of a single eukaryotic cell divides into two daughter cells. Cytoplasmic division begins during or after the late stages of nuclear division in mitosis and meiosis. During cytokinesis the spindle apparatus partitions and transports duplicated chromatids into the cytoplasm of the two separate daughter cells.

In animal cells

In animal cells, cytokinesis is accomplished by a contractile ring of actin and myosin filaments at the cell equator. The ring contracts, pinching the cell membrane inward to form a cleavage furrow. The furrow deepens until the cell is divided in two, a final step called abscission, which is mediated by the midbody, a transient structure containing microtubules and proteins derived from the spindle.

In plant cells

Because plant cells are surrounded by a rigid cell wall, they cannot divide by a cleavage furrow. Instead, vesicles derived from the Golgi apparatus accumulate at the equator of the spindle and fuse to form a structure called the cell plate. The cell plate grows outward until it reaches the cell surface, where it fuses with the plasma membrane and deposits the materials of a new cell wall between the two daughter cells.

Differences and exceptions

Some cells divide their nuclei without cytokinesis, becoming multinucleate; this process, called coenocytosis, occurs in certain fungi and in tissues such as mammalian osteoclasts and the syncytiotrophoblast of the placenta. Faithful cytokinesis ensures that each daughter cell receives one nucleus and a share of the cytoplasmic organelles.
