Sauropoda

Sauropoda is a clade of saurischian dinosaurs characterised by very long necks and tails, small heads, massive barrel-shaped bodies, and four columnar legs. Sauropods include the largest animals ever to have lived on land: forms such as Argentinosaurus, Patagotitan, and Diplodocus exceeded 30 metres in length, and the biggest may have weighed 50–80 tonnes. Familiar genera include Brachiosaurus, Apatosaurus (long known as "Brontosaurus"), Camarasaurus, and Saltasaurus.

Sauropoda
Temporal range: Late Triassic – Late Cretaceous
Named by: Othniel Charles Marsh, 1878
Groups: Diplodocoidea, Macronaria, Titanosauria, and others

Evolution and diversity

Sauropods evolved from small bipedal sauropodomorph ancestors in the Late Triassic and by the Middle Jurassic had spread to every continent. They remained the dominant large terrestrial herbivores through the Jurassic and Cretaceous, diversifying into diplodocoids with whip-like tails, macronarians with large nostrils atop the skull, and the titanosaurs, which were the last surviving group and included armoured and exceptionally gigantic species. They perished in the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event 66 million years ago.

Biology

Sauropods were herbivores that cropped vegetation with simple peg- or spoon-shaped teeth and, lacking chewing, processed food in a huge fermenting gut; they likely swallowed stones (gastroliths) to aid digestion, though the importance of this is debated. Their vertebrae were lightened by air sacs connected to an avian-style respiratory system, helping to support both the enormous body and the long neck. Trackways show that sauropods walked quadrupedally and travelled in herds. They laid eggs in nests, and their young grew extremely fast, reaching adult size in a few decades. Long-standing questions — whether they held their necks vertically or horizontally, how their hearts pumped blood to the head, and whether they were warm-blooded — continue to be investigated through biomechanics and bone histology.
