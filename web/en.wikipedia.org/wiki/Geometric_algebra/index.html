Geometric algebra

In mathematics, a geometric algebra (also known as a real Clifford algebra) is an extension of elementary algebra that incorporates geometric notions such as lines, planes and volumes as algebraic elements. It is constructed from a vector space equipped with a quadratic form, and was introduced in the 19th century by William Kingdon Clifford, building on the work of Hermann Grassmann and William Rowan Hamilton.

Definition

The geometric algebra of a vector space is generated by vectors subject to the rule that the square of any vector equals the value of the quadratic form on it. The resulting geometric product of two vectors decomposes into a symmetric part (related to the inner product) and an antisymmetric part (the exterior or wedge product). Elements of the algebra, called multivectors, are graded sums of scalars (grade 0), vectors (grade 1), bivectors (grade 2, representing oriented plane segments), and higher-grade blades up to the pseudoscalar of the whole space.

Rotations and rotors

Even-grade elements called rotors act on vectors by a double-sided product to produce rotations, providing a formulation closely related to quaternions (which form the even subalgebra in three dimensions) and to spinors in physics. This makes geometric algebra a compact language for rotation geometry and rigid-body mechanics.

Applications

Geometric algebra is used in computer graphics and robotics (especially in its conformal extension, conformal geometric algebra, which represents points, spheres and circles uniformly), in physics (where spacetime algebra reformulates electromagnetism and Dirac theory), and in computer vision. Leading modern proponents include David Hestenes, who revived the subject from the 1960s onward and promoted it as a universal mathematical language for physics.
