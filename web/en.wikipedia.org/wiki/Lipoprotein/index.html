Lipoprotein

[Image: Structure of a lipoprotein particle: phospholipid shell around cholesterol esters and triglycerides, with apolipoproteins]

Overview

A lipoprotein is a biochemical assembly of proteins and lipids whose purpose is to transport hydrophobic fat molecules (triglycerides, cholesterol and cholesterol esters) through the watery blood plasma and other extracellular fluids. The particle has a core of triglycerides and cholesteryl esters surrounded by a shell of phospholipids, free cholesterol and apolipoproteins, which act as ligands and enzyme cofactors directing the particle's fate.

Classes

Lipoproteins are classified by density/size: chylomicrons (largest, dietary), VLDL, IDL, LDL ("bad cholesterol" — delivers cholesterol to tissues and is central to atherosclerosis) and HDL ("good cholesterol" — mediates reverse cholesterol transport to the liver). Disorders of lipoproteins cause or modify cardiovascular risk (familial hypercholesterolaemia from LDL-receptor defects) and, when triglyceride-rich particles accumulate extremely, pancreatitis. Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), is a genetically determined LDL-like particle and an independent risk factor now targeted by drugs in development.
