Hiawatha (also known as Ayenwatha or Aiionwatha) was a Native American leader and statesman traditionally credited, together with the prophet Deganawida (the Great Peacemaker) and the clan mother Jigonhsasee, with founding the Iroquois Confederacy — the Haudenosaunee — a league of originally five nations: the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca (later joined by the Tuscarora). He is remembered as a skilled orator who persuaded the nations to end their cycles of blood feuding and unite under the Great Law of Peace.

Legend and history

According to oral tradition, Hiawatha was an Onondaga (in some versions Mohawk) leader consumed by grief after the deaths of his daughters at the hands of the feared Onondaga chief Tadodaho. The Peacemaker consoled him with strings of wampum — the origin of the condolence ceremony central to Haudenosaunee ritual — and Hiawatha became his spokesman, travelling among the nations to preach peace. Their greatest challenge was winning over Tadodaho himself, whose consent was necessary; he was eventually persuaded and became the presiding chief of the Confederacy's central council at Onondaga.

The date of the founding is debated: proposed dates range from the mid-12th century (a 1142 solar eclipse is sometimes cited) to the 15th or early 16th century.

In literature

Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's enormously popular epic poem The Song of Hiawatha (1855) bears little relation to the historical figure: Longfellow drew on Ojibwe legends of the culture hero Nanabozho collected by Henry Rowe Schoolcraft and transferred the name Hiawatha to them. The poem nevertheless fixed the name in the popular imagination and inspired many place names, trains, and cultural references across North America.
