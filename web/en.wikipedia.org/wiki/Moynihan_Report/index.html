Moynihan Report

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Overview

The Moynihan Report, officially titled The Negro Family: The Case for National Action, was a 1965 report written by Daniel Patrick Moynihan, then Assistant Secretary of Labor in the administration of President Lyndon B. Johnson. Prepared as an internal Department of Labor document and leaked to the press, it became one of the most debated policy documents in American history.

Content

Drawing on statistics about family structure, the report argued that the rise of single-mother households among Black Americans — about 25 per cent of Black families were then headed by women — was both a product of the legacy of slavery and Jim Crow and a cause of continuing disadvantage. Moynihan described a "tangle of pathology" in which family breakdown was linked to unemployment, poverty, crime and poor educational outcomes, and he argued that the issue of racial equality now depended on strengthening the Black family. Though sympathetic to government action, the report made few concrete policy proposals.

Reception and legacy

Civil rights leaders and many liberals attacked the report for "blaming the victim", arguing it shifted attention from racism and jobs programmes to Black culture. Conservatives later embraced its emphasis on family structure; Moynihan himself, who went on to serve as a United States Senator from New York, insisted the document had been meant to spur national action on employment and family policy. Historians continue to debate its analysis and influence: it shaped discussions of the "culture of poverty", welfare policy and fatherhood for decades, and the family-structure trends it identified later appeared, to substantial degrees, across many American demographic groups.
