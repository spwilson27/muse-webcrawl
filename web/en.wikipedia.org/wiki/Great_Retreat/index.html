Great Retreat

The Great Retreat, also known as the Retreat from Mons, was the long withdrawal to the River Marne in August and September 1914 by the British Expeditionary Force (BEF) and the French Fifth Army. The retreat took place during the first month of the First World War, after the Allied defeat at the Battles of the Frontiers and the German advance through Belgium and northern France.

Background

Under the Schlieffen Plan, German armies swept through Belgium and into France in August 1914. The BEF fought its first action at the Battle of Mons on 23 August, and, exposed by the retreat of the French Fifth Army on its flank after the Battle of Charleroi, began to fall back the following day.

The retreat

Over the following two weeks the Allies retreated some 200 miles (320 km) southward, fighting rearguard actions at Le Cateau (26 August), Étreux, Néry, and Crépy-en-Valois. British and French troops marched in extreme heat, short of food and sleep, pursued closely by the German First Army under Alexander von Kluck. On 26 August, British II Corps under Horace Smith-Dorrien turned and fought at Le Cateau against orders, inflicting heavy casualties and checking the pursuit, at the cost of some 7,800 men.

Outcome

The retreat ended south of the River Marne, near Paris. When the German First Army turned south-east, exposing its flank, the French commander Joseph Joffre launched a counteroffensive, the First Battle of the Marne (6–12 September 1914), which halted the German advance and ended hopes of a quick victory in the west. The retreat is remembered for the endurance of the regular soldiers of the "Old Contemptibles" and remains one of the longest retreats in British military history.
