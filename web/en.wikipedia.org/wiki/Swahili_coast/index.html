The Swahili coast is the coastal area of East Africa inhabited by the Swahili people, stretching from southern Somalia through Kenya and Tanzania to northern Mozambique, together with offshore islands including Lamu, Mombasa, Zanzibar, Kilwa and the Comoros. From roughly the 11th to the 16th century its stone-built city-states formed one of the great trading civilisations of the medieval world, linking the African interior to Arabia, Persia, India and China through the monsoon winds of the Indian Ocean.

History

Bantu-speaking farming and fishing communities settled the coast in the first millennium CE and traded with the wider Indian Ocean world — the 1st-century Periplus of the Erythraean Sea already describes commerce at Rhapta. Islam spread through trade from about the 8th century, and by the 12th–15th centuries prosperous, largely independent city-states raised mosques, palaces and houses of coral stone and lime mortar. Kilwa, which controlled the gold trade of Sofala from the Zimbabwe plateau, struck its own coins; the Chinese admiral Zheng He's fleets reached Malindi in the 1410s, and a giraffe from Malindi famously reached the Ming court.

The arrival of the Portuguese after Vasco da Gama's 1498 voyage disrupted the system: ports were sacked or tributised, Fort Jesus at Mombasa was built (1593), and Omani Arabs gradually expelled the Portuguese north of Mozambique by 1698. In the 19th century Omani sultans moved their capital to Zanzibar, whose clove plantations and caravan trade in ivory and enslaved people made it the coast's dominant power until European partition in the 1880s–90s.

Culture

Swahili culture is a synthesis of African, Arab, Persian and later Indian and European influences. Its language, Kiswahili, is Bantu in grammar with extensive Arabic loanwords, written historically in Arabic script and later in Latin letters; it is now a lingua franca of East Africa and an official language of the African Union. Swahili architecture — carved doors, courtyard houses, and mosques such as the Great Mosque of Kilwa — and its seafaring traditions of the lateen-rigged dhow remain hallmarks of the region. UNESCO World Heritage Sites on the coast include Lamu Old Town, the ruins of Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara, Gedi, and Stone Town of Zanzibar.
