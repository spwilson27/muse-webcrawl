Test (biology)

In biology, a test is the hard protective shell or skeleton of certain organisms, especially invertebrates. The word derives from the Latin testa ("tile", "shell", "earthen pot"). Tests serve protection, support and, in microscopic organisms, contribute enormously to marine sediments and the fossil record.

Foraminifera

The best-known tests are those of the Foraminifera, single-celled protists that secrete shells of calcium carbonate, agglutinated sediment grains, or (rarely) silica or organic material. Foraminiferan tests are typically chambered: the cell adds new chambers as it grows, producing coiled, linear or complex forms pierced by pores (foramina) through which pseudopodia extend. Because they are abundant, rapidly evolving and easily fossilised, foraminiferan tests are key index fossils and are used in biostratigraphy, petroleum exploration and reconstructing past ocean temperatures and ice volumes from isotope ratios in their calcite.

Echinoderms

Sea urchins and sand dollars possess an internal skeleton of fused calcite plates called a test, covered in life by skin and movable spines. After death the rigid, often beautifully patterned test is what commonly washes ashore. In sea urchins the test bears the Aristotle's lantern feeding apparatus internally and shows five-fold symmetry.

Other organisms

Testate amoebae (e.g. Arcella, Difflugia) build single-chambered tests from secreted plates or gathered particles, with one opening for pseudopodia. Radiolarians secrete ornate siliceous tests (more strictly called skeletons) whose accumulation forms radiolarian ooze. Certain ascidians (tunicates) have a leathery outer "test" or tunic containing cellulose-like tunicin. In botany, "testa" denotes the protective seed coat — a different word usage from the same Latin root.
