Wole Soyinka

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Wole Soyinka in his white-maned later years at a lectern, the lion of Nigerian letters addressing the world]

Akinwande Oluwole Babatunde 'Wole' Soyinka (born 13 July 1934) is a Nigerian playwright, novelist, poet and essayist, the first African to win the Nobel Prize in Literature (1986), awarded for writing that, in the Academy's paraphrase, fashions the drama of existence in a wide cultural perspective and with poetic overtones.

His plays — A Dance of the Forests (1960, for Nigeria's independence), The Lion and the Jewel (1959), Death and the King's Horseman (1975), Kongi's Harvest — fuse Yoruba cosmology and ritual with European dramatic forms; his prison memoir The Man Died (1972) records the 22 months he spent in solitary confinement during Nigeria's civil war for trying to broker peace.

The writer and the state

Born in Aké, Abeokuta (his memoir Aké: The Years of Childhood, 1981, is a classic of the form), Soyinka studied at Ibadan and Leeds, worked at the Royal Court, and founded Nigeria's first professional theatre companies. His life is a duel with power: pirate-radio broadcasts against election rigging (1965), imprisonment under Gowon, exile under Abacha (who sentenced him to death in absentia), and unceasing polemic against tyranny and religious fundamentalism — Nigeria's conscience with a lion's mane and a hunter's licence (he founded the road-safety corps, too).

The work

Soyinka's dense, ritual theatre argues that modern Africa must reckon with its gods, not discard them: Death and the King's Horseman — from a real 1946 incident, in which a king's horseman must ritually follow his king in death — is the century's great African tragedy, its author's note insisting it is about metaphysics, not a 'clash of cultures' (in his famous warning to directors). His poetry, novels (The Interpreters, 1965; Chronicles from the Land of the Happiest People on Earth, 2021) and essays complete a corpus of continental scope.
