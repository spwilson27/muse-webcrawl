Secondary modern school

A secondary modern school was a type of state secondary school in England, Wales and Northern Ireland from 1944 into the 1970s. Under the tripartite system established by the Education Act 1944, children were allocated at age eleven, largely on the basis of the eleven-plus examination, to grammar schools, technical schools, or secondary modern schools. In practice the secondary moderns educated the large majority — around three-quarters — of secondary pupils.

Design and curriculum

Secondary moderns were intended for pupils considered less academically inclined, offering a practical, general education oriented toward work and everyday life, initially with a leaving age of 15 (raised to 16 in 1972). Early schools had no national examinations and limited resources, and the schools carried a stigma of failure because admission followed non-selection for grammar school. Over time many developed strong examination courses, and from the 1960s pupils could take CSE (Certificate of Secondary Education) qualifications, and later O-levels.

Criticism and replacement

Critics argued that the system entrenched class divisions, wrote off children at eleven, and that technical schools — the third leg of the tripartite system — were never built in significant numbers. From 1965 the government encouraged local authorities to reorganise along comprehensive lines, and most secondary moderns were merged into comprehensive schools during the 1960s and 1970s. A small number of secondary moderns survive in areas that retained selection, such as parts of Kent and Buckinghamshire.
