CYP2D6

Cytochrome P450 2D6 (CYP2D6) is an enzyme that in humans is encoded by the CYP2D6 gene. CYP2D6 is primarily expressed in the liver. It is also highly expressed in areas of the central nervous system, including the substantia nigra. CYP2D6, a member of the cytochrome P450 mixed-function oxidase system, is one of the most important enzymes involved in the metabolism of xenobiotics in the body, metabolizing roughly 20–25% of clinically used drugs despite making up only a small fraction of total hepatic P450 content.

Genetic variability

The CYP2D6 gene is highly polymorphic, with more than 100 named star (*) alleles catalogued by the Pharmacogene Variation Consortium (PharmVar), including nonfunctional alleles (e.g. *4, *5 gene deletion), reduced-function alleles (e.g. *10, *17, *41), and copy-number variations producing gene duplications. Combining both alleles yields an "activity score" that classifies people as:

  • Poor metabolizers — little or no enzyme activity (about 5–10% of Europeans);
  • Intermediate metabolizers — reduced activity;
  • Normal (extensive) metabolizers — full activity;
  • Ultrarapid metabolizers — gene duplications, common in some North-East African and Middle Eastern populations.

Clinical significance

CYP2D6 activates several prodrugs: codeine is converted to morphine (dangerously fast in ultrarapid metabolizers, ineffectively in poor metabolizers), tramadol to its active metabolite, and tamoxifen to endoxifen. It inactivates many antidepressants, antipsychotics, beta-blockers (metoprolol), and atomoxetine. Strong inhibitors such as fluoxetine, paroxetine, and bupropion can convert a normal metabolizer into a functional poor metabolizer ("phenoconversion"). Pharmacogenetic guidelines from CPIC and the Dutch DPWG give genotype-based dosing recommendations for many CYP2D6 substrates, and the enzyme is one of the most commonly tested in clinical pharmacogenomics.
