Filippo Brunelleschi

Filippo Brunelleschi
Born: Filippo di ser Brunellesco di Lippo, 1377, Florence, Republic of Florence
Died: 15 April 1446 (aged 68–69), Florence
Occupation: Architect, designer, engineer, sculptor, mathematician, goldsmith
Known for: Dome of Florence Cathedral; rediscovery of linear perspective

Filippo Brunelleschi (1377 – 15 April 1446) was an Italian architect, designer, and sculptor, recognised as one of the founding pioneers of the Italian Renaissance and especially of Renaissance architecture. Trained as a goldsmith, he is famous above all for designing the dome of Florence Cathedral, an engineering feat without precedent since antiquity, and for developing the mathematical technique of linear perspective in art, which transformed Western painting.

Early life and the Baptistery competition

Brunelleschi was born in Florence, the son of a notary, and was apprenticed as a goldsmith and sculptor. In 1401 he competed to design the bronze doors of the Florence Baptistery; his trial panel of the Sacrifice of Isaac survives, but the commission went to Lorenzo Ghiberti. According to his early biographer Antonio Manetti, Brunelleschi then travelled to Rome with the young Donatello to study ancient Roman ruins, measuring cornices and drawing ground plans — research that underpinned his revival of classical architecture.

Perspective

Around 1415–1420 Brunelleschi demonstrated the principles of linear (geometric) perspective with two famous demonstration paintings of Florentine buildings, viewed through a peephole and mirror so that the painted image coincided exactly with the real view. The method — a single vanishing point, horizon line, and geometrically consistent diminution — was codified by Leon Battista Alberti in De pictura (1435) and adopted by Masaccio and generations of artists after him.

The dome of Florence Cathedral

The cathedral's crossing had stood open since the 14th century because no one knew how to vault its 45-metre span at such a height. In 1418 the Opera del Duomo held a competition; Brunelleschi proposed building a double-shelled dome without the customary wooden centering, using herringbone brickwork, stone and wooden tension chains, and machines of his own invention — including a reversible ox-driven hoist and a crane for positioning loads. He was appointed capomaestro in 1420 (jointly, at first, with Ghiberti), and the dome was completed by 1436, crowned later by the lantern, whose construction Brunelleschi also designed. It remains the largest brick dome ever built.

Other works

  • Ospedale degli Innocenti (Foundling Hospital, begun 1419) — often called the first Renaissance building, with its graceful arcaded loggia.
  • The Old Sacristy of San Lorenzo and the Pazzi Chapel at Santa Croce — exemplars of his measured, geometric classicism.
  • The churches of San Lorenzo and Santo Spirito in Florence.
  • The Palazzo Pitti (attributed) and fortifications and hydraulic works across Tuscany.

Legacy

Brunelleschi died in Florence in 1446 and was buried in the cathedral beneath his dome, an honour reflecting the city's esteem. Giorgio Vasari's Lives made him the archetype of the Renaissance artist-engineer. His integration of art, mathematics, and machine design, and his first-known patent for an invention (a river transport vessel, 1421), mark him as a prototype of the Renaissance man.
