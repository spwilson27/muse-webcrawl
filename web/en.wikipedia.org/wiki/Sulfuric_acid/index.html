Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) is a dense, oily, strongly corrosive mineral acid — historically 'oil of vitriol' — and by volume the most produced industrial chemical in the world, used so widely that its consumption is an index of a nation's industrial strength.

Most sulfuric acid goes to fertiliser manufacture (converting phosphate rock to soluble phosphates); it is also the electrolyte in lead–acid car batteries, and is used in refining, metal pickling, detergents and explosives. Concentrated acid is a powerful dehydrating agent that chars sugar and skin.

[Image: A glass bottle of concentrated sulfuric acid and a lead-acid battery]

Manufacture

The contact process burns sulfur to SO₂, oxidises it over a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst to SO₃, and absorbs the gas into concentrated acid to form oleum, which is diluted to the product. Directly adding water to SO₃ would create a hazardous mist.

Acid behaviour

Sulfuric acid is diprotic: its first proton is lost completely (strong acid) and its second partially (pKa₂ about 2). Dilution must be done by adding acid to water, never the reverse, because of the heat released.
