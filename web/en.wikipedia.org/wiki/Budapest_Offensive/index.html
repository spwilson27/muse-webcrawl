Budapest Offensive

The Budapest Offensive was the general attack carried out by Soviet and Romanian forces against Nazi Germany and its Hungarian allies in Hungary at the end of the Second World War. The offensive lasted from 29 October 1944 until the fall of Budapest on 13 February 1945, and culminated in the Siege of Budapest, one of the longest and bloodiest urban sieges of the war.

Background

After the successful summer offensives of 1944, the Red Army had liberated Romania and Bulgaria and advanced to Hungary's borders. Hungary, under Regent Miklós Horthy, attempted to negotiate an armistice with the Soviet Union in October 1944, but Germany responded with Operation Panzerfaust, deposing Horthy and installing the Arrow Cross regime of Ferenc Szálasi, which kept Hungary in the war. The 2nd and 3rd Ukrainian Fronts, under Marshals Rodion Malinovsky and Fyodor Tolbukhin, were ordered to take Budapest.

The offensive

The offensive began on 29 October 1944. Soviet forces advanced across the Hungarian plain from the south and east, fighting through Debrecen and approaching Budapest by early November, though initial attempts to take the city by a coup de main failed. By late December 1944, Soviet and Romanian troops had completely encircled Budapest, trapping some 188,000 German and Hungarian soldiers as well as a large civilian population within the city.

Hitler declared Budapest a fortress (Festung) and ordered it held at all costs. Three relief attempts by German armoured forces (Operations Konrad I–III) in January 1945 came close to the city but failed to break the encirclement. The garrison's attempt to break out on 11 February 1945 was almost completely destroyed; only a few hundred soldiers reached German lines. Pest fell on 18 January and Buda on 13 February 1945, ending the siege.

Consequences

The battle devastated Budapest: most of its buildings were damaged and all of its Danube bridges were blown up by the defenders. Civilian deaths are estimated in the tens of thousands, and the siege was accompanied by great suffering, famine and atrocities, including the Arrow Cross murders of Budapest's Jews. The fall of Budapest opened the way for the Soviet Vienna Offensive in the spring of 1945.
