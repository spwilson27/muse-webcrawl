Paperwork Reduction Act

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Paperwork Reduction Act (PRA) of 1980 (Pub. L. 96-511, 44 U.S.C. §§ 3501–3521) is a United States federal law enacted in 1980 designed to reduce the total amount of paperwork burden the federal government imposes on private businesses and citizens. It established the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (OIRA) within the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) and gave it authority over the collection of information by federal agencies.

Provisions

Under the Act, before a federal agency may collect information from ten or more members of the public — through forms, surveys, reporting, or recordkeeping requirements — it must publish notice in the Federal Register, seek public comment, and obtain approval and a control number from OIRA. Approved collections are generally valid for no more than three years. If an agency imposes a collection without a valid control number, the Act provides that no person may be penalized for failing to comply with it.

The statute declares its purposes to include minimizing the paperwork burden, ensuring the greatest possible public benefit from information collected, improving the quality and use of federal information, and coordinating federal information policies. The Act was amended by the Paperwork Reduction Act of 1995, which strengthened OIRA's role and set government-wide paperwork reduction goals.

History

Concern over federal paperwork predated the Act: the Federal Reports Act of 1942 had given the Bureau of the Budget limited clearance authority. Growing complaints from business in the 1970s, and the work of the Commission on Federal Paperwork, led to the more comprehensive 1980 statute. The PRA also created the statutory framework for federal information resources management and, together with later amendments, for the position of chief information officer in federal agencies.

Criticism

Commentators have noted that the Act's control-number requirement is rarely successfully invoked by members of the public, since agencies routinely obtain approvals, and that the process can delay beneficial data collection in areas such as public health and environmental monitoring.
