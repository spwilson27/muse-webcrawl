Second Report on Public Credit

Hamilton's report proposing a national bank, submitted 13 December 1790.

The Second Report on the Public Credit is the common name for the Report on a National Bank, submitted to the United States House of Representatives on 13 December 1790 by Alexander Hamilton, the first Secretary of the Treasury. It proposed the creation of a national bank — the First Bank of the United States — as the centrepiece of Hamilton's financial programme for the young republic.

Author: Alexander Hamilton

Date: 13 December 1790

Submitted to: U.S. House of Representatives

Subject: Proposal for a national bank

Outcome: First Bank of the United States chartered, 25 February 1791

Contents

Following his First Report on the Public Credit (January 1790), which dealt with funding the federal debt and assuming state debts, Hamilton argued that a national bank would augment the active capital of the country, facilitate tax collection and government borrowing, provide a safe place of deposit for public funds, and issue a stable paper currency convertible into specie. He proposed a bank capitalised at $10 million, with the federal government subscribing one-fifth and appointing a minority of directors, modelled partly on the Bank of England and the Bank of North America.

Constitutional debate

The bank bill passed Congress in early 1791 but provoked the first great constitutional controversy of the federal government. James Madison argued in the House that Congress had no enumerated power to charter a bank. Within Washington's cabinet, Thomas Jefferson and Edmund Randolph advised a veto on strict-construction grounds, while Hamilton's famous opinion on constitutionality defended the bank under the Necessary and Proper Clause — an early statement of the doctrine of implied powers. President Washington signed the bill on 25 February 1791, granting a twenty-year charter.

Legacy

The First Bank operated until its charter lapsed in 1811; its successor, the Second Bank of the United States (1816), stood at the centre of later battles over federal power, and Hamilton's reasoning was vindicated by the Supreme Court in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819).
