A string quartet is both a musical ensemble of four string players — two violinists, a violist, and a cellist — and a genre of chamber music written for that combination. Since the 18th century it has been regarded as one of the most prestigious forms of chamber music, often compared to a conversation among four sensible people.

History

Joseph Haydn essentially created the genre in the 1760s and 1770s, writing 68 quartets that established the four-movement form (fast movement in sonata form, slow movement, minuet, and lively finale) and the equality of the four voices. Mozart dedicated six quartets to Haydn; Beethoven's sixteen quartets — especially the experimental late quartets, including the Grosse Fuge — vastly expanded the form's expressive range. Schubert, Brahms, and Dvořák continued the tradition in the 19th century, and major 20th-century cycles came from Bartók (six quartets) and Shostakovich (fifteen).

Ensembles

Unlike orchestral musicians, quartet players typically form permanent ensembles with their own identity. Famous quartets include the Amadeus, Borodin, Emerson, Kronos, and Guarneri quartets. The repertoire demands exceptional intonation and ensemble precision, as each player carries an independent line with no conductor.
