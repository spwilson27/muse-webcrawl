Situated knowledge

Situated knowledge is a concept in feminist epistemology and science studies, introduced by Donna Haraway in her 1988 essay "Situated Knowledges: The Science Question in Feminism and the Privilege of Partial Perspective", published in Feminist Studies. It holds that all knowledge is produced from embodied, historically and socially located positions, and that claims to a disembodied, universal objectivity — what Haraway calls the "god trick of seeing everything from nowhere" — are illusions.

Argument

Haraway argues for "politics and epistemologies of location, positioning, and situating, where partiality and not universality is the condition of being heard to make rational knowledge claims". Far from endorsing relativism, she proposes that acknowledging the partial, situated character of vision — and joining partial views together — yields a better account of the world: "feminist objectivity" grounded in mobile, embodied positioning rather than a view from nowhere. The essay uses the metaphor of vision extensively, arguing that the eyes are active instruments that construct the objects they see.

Relation to other ideas

Situated knowledge is closely related to standpoint theory (associated with Sandra Harding and Nancy Hartsock), which holds that marginalised positions can afford epistemic advantages, but Haraway resists treating any standpoint as innocent or automatic. The concept should not be confused with situated cognition, a theory of learning centred on practice and context, though both share an emphasis on the locatedness of knowing. Situated knowledge has been highly influential in feminist theory, science and technology studies, anthropology, and postcolonial studies.
