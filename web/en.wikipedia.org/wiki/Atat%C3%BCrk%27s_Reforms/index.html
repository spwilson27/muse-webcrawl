Atatürk's reforms

Atatürk's reforms were a series of political, legal, cultural, social and economic changes implemented under Mustafa Kemal Atatürk between 1923 and 1938, designed to transform the Ottoman Empire's successor state, the Republic of Turkey, into a modern, secular nation-state.

Political and legal reforms

The sultanate was abolished in 1922 and the caliphate in 1924. The Republic was proclaimed on 29 October 1923. A new constitution (1924, replaced in its secular character by amendments in 1928 and 1937) removed Islam as the state religion. Swiss-style civil code (1926), an Italian-style penal code and a German-style commercial code replaced sharia law in civil matters.

Social and cultural reforms

Reforms included the Hat Law (1925), closure of dervish lodges, adoption of the Gregorian calendar and international numerals, the Surname Law (1934), and the adoption in 1928 of a new Turkish alphabet based on Latin letters replacing the Arabic script. Women gained the right to vote in municipal elections in 1930 and in national elections in 1934; education was unified under the state and made secular (Tevhid-i Tedrisat, 1924).

Economic reforms

The state pursued etatism: nationalisation of railways, creation of state banks such as Sümerbank and Etibank, and the First Five-Year Industrial Plan (1934), alongside abolition of the âşâr tithe tax burdening peasants.

Legacy

The reforms, collectively associated with Kemalism's "six arrows", remain central — and sometimes contested — pillars of the Turkish Republic.
