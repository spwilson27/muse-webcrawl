Italianization of South Tyrol

The Italianization of South Tyrol was the process of forced assimilation of the German- and Ladin-speaking population of South Tyrol, annexed by Italy from Austria after the First World War, carried out most intensively under the fascist regime of Benito Mussolini in the 1920s and 1930s.

Measures

Beginning in 1923, German-language schooling was abolished, prompting clandestine "catacomb schools"; German place names were replaced with Italian ones devised by Ettore Tolomei, and even family names and gravestone inscriptions were Italianised. German newspapers were suppressed, public employment was reserved for Italian speakers, and large-scale immigration of Italian workers — notably to a new industrial zone in Bolzano — was promoted to change the province's ethnic balance.

The Option and its aftermath

Under the 1939 South Tyrol Option Agreement between Mussolini and Hitler, German speakers were forced to choose between emigrating to the German Reich or remaining and accepting complete Italianization; a large majority voted to leave, though the war prevented most departures. After 1945 the 1946 Gruber–De Gasperi Agreement promised autonomy and protection of the German language, eventually realised in the 1972 autonomy statute, which made South Tyrol a largely self-governing, officially trilingual province.
