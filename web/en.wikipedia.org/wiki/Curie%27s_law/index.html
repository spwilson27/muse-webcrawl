Curie's law

Curie's law is an empirical law in physics describing the magnetic susceptibility of a paramagnetic material at temperatures well above any ordering transition. It states that the magnetization of a paramagnet is directly proportional to the applied magnetic field and inversely proportional to the absolute temperature.

M = C · B / T

where M is the magnetization, B is the magnetic field, T is the temperature in kelvins, and C is the material-specific Curie constant. Equivalently, the magnetic susceptibility χ varies inversely with temperature: χ = C / T. The law is named after the French physicist Pierre Curie, who formulated it from his experimental work in 1895.

Derivation

The law can be derived from statistical mechanics by considering a collection of non-interacting magnetic moments in thermal equilibrium with an applied field. Each moment has two orientations (parallel or antiparallel to the field for spin-1/2), and the average magnetization follows from the Boltzmann distribution. In the high-temperature limit, where the thermal energy kBT is much larger than the energy splitting μB, the hyperbolic tangent describing the magnetization can be linearised, giving the inverse-temperature dependence.

Limitations and the Curie–Weiss law

Curie's law fails at low temperatures, where the magnetization saturates, and in materials where the magnetic moments interact with one another. For ferromagnets and many antiferromagnets, the modified Curie–Weiss law, χ = C / (T − θ), gives a better description, where θ is the Weiss constant related to the ordering temperature.
