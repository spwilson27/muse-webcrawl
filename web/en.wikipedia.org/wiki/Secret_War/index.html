The Secret War refers to the United States Central Intelligence Agency's covert military campaign in the Kingdom of Laos during the Vietnam War. From 1964 to 1973 the United States carried out one of the largest bombing campaigns in history over Laos — some 580,000 bombing missions and more than two million tons of ordnance — while officially denying involvement, because Laos had been declared neutral by the 1962 Geneva Accords.

Background

After the First Indochina War, Laos was divided between a Western-backed royal government and the communist Pathet Lao, supplied by North Vietnam, whose forces also used eastern Laos for the Ho Chi Minh trail into South Vietnam. Beginning in 1961, the CIA, working through its proprietary airline Air America and officers such as Bill Lair, built a clandestine army among the Hmong people under General Vang Pao, headquartered at Long Tieng — at its peak one of the busiest airports in the world and the centre of the covert war.

The bombing

Under Operation Barrel Roll and related campaigns, U.S. aircraft struck the Ho Chi Minh trail and Pathet Lao positions, especially on the Plain of Jars. Laos became, per capita, the most heavily bombed country in history. An estimated 30 percent of the cluster munitions dropped failed to explode, leaving tens of millions of unexploded bomblets that continued to kill and maim Laotians for decades; U.S.-funded clearance programmes continue today.

Aftermath

The 1973 ceasefire ended the bombing, but in 1975 the Pathet Lao took power and proclaimed the Lao People's Democratic Republic. Tens of thousands of Hmong, fearing persecution, fled to Thailand and many were eventually resettled in the United States, forming large Hmong American communities in Minnesota, Wisconsin, and California. The war's existence, long denied publicly, was progressively declassified from the 1970s onward.
