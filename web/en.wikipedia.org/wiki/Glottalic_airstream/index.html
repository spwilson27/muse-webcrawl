Glottalic airstream

The glottalic airstream is one of the airstream mechanisms used in human speech. In a glottalic articulation, the closed glottis moves up or down like a piston, compressing or rarefying the air in the mouth so that sound is produced by air moving out of or into the mouth rather than by the lungs.

Types

An upward movement of the closed glottis compresses the air, producing egressive sounds called ejectives, found in languages of the Caucasus, the Americas and Africa, and written in the International Phonetic Alphabet with an apostrophe (e.g. [pʼ]). A downward movement rarefies the air, producing ingressive implosives, common in languages of Africa and Southeast Asia and written with a hooktop symbol (e.g. [ɓ], [ɗ]).

Glottalic theory

The mechanism is central to the glottalic theory of Proto-Indo-European, proposed in the 1970s by Thomas Gamkrelidze, Vyacheslav Ivanov and Paul Hopper, which reconstructed the traditional voiced stops of Proto-Indo-European as ejectives.
