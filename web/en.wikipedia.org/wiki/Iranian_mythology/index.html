Iranian mythology

Iranian mythology, or Persian mythology, is the body of the myths originally told by ancient Persians and other Iranian peoples. These stories concern the origin and nature of the world, the lives and activities of deities, heroes, and mythological creatures, and the origins and significance of the ancient Persian people's own cult and ritual practices. Modern scholars study the myths to shed light on the religious and political institutions of not only modern Iran but of the Greater Iran, which includes regions of West Asia, Central Asia, South Asia, and Transcaucasia where Iranian culture has had significant influence.

Religious background

Much of Iranian mythology is rooted in the ancient Iranian religion that preceded and informed Zoroastrianism. In the Zoroastrian tradition, the world is the stage of a cosmic struggle between Ahura Mazda, the wise lord and creator, and Angra Mainyu (Ahriman), the destructive spirit. Humanity plays a role in this struggle through good thoughts, good words and good deeds.

Key figures

  • Ahura Mazda — the supreme god of Zoroastrianism, creator of the world and upholder of asha (truth and order).
  • Angra Mainyu (Ahriman) — the hostile spirit, source of druj (the lie) and of evil in the world.
  • Mithra — the divinity of covenant, light and oath, widely worshipped across the Iranian world.
  • Anahita — the goddess of waters, fertility and healing.
  • Gayomart — the first human being in the creation account.
  • Jamshid — a legendary king of the Pishdadian dynasty, credited with many inventions of civilization, who fell through pride.
  • Zahhak — the tyrannical serpent-shouldered king overthrown by the hero Fereydun and the blacksmith Kaveh.
  • Rostam — the greatest hero of the Shahnameh, famed for his seven labours and his tragic combat with his son Sohrab.

Sources

The principal sources are the Avesta, the sacred texts of Zoroastrianism, including the Yashts hymns to individual divinities; the Middle Persian (Pahlavi) books of the 9th and 10th centuries, such as the Bundahishn and the Denkard; and above all the Shahnameh (Book of Kings), the epic poem completed by Ferdowsi around 1010 CE, which preserved the heroic legends of pre-Islamic Iran in some 50,000 couplets.

Cosmology and eschatology

Iranian tradition divides world history into periods of creation, mixture (the present age, in which good and evil are mingled), and separation. At the end of time comes Frashokereti, the renovation of the world, when evil is defeated, the dead are resurrected, and a saviour figure, the Saoshyant, brings about the final restoration.
