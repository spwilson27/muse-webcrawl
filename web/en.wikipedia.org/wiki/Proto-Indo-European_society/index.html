Proto-Indo-European society is the culture of the speakers of Proto-Indo-European (PIE), the reconstructed ancestor of the Indo-European languages, posited for the late Neolithic and early Bronze Age. Because no texts survive, the society is reconstructed by triangulating the comparative method in linguistics with archaeology — the "words and things" approach — and, more recently, with ancient DNA.

The reconstructed world

The PIE lexicon reconstructs a pastoral people who herded cattle, sheep and goats, kept the domesticated horse (*h₁éḱwos), knew the wheel and wagon (*kʷékʷlos, *weǵʰ-) — a fact that dates the language's later stages after c. 4000 BC — ploughed, wove wool, and drank mead. Kinship vocabulary points to a patrilineal, patrilocal clan society graded into nobles, commoners and a warrior class; hospitality (*ghostis, whence "guest" and "host") was a sacred bond.

Religion and myth

Comparative mythology reconstructs a sky father, *Dyēus ph₂tēr (Zeus, Jupiter, Dyaus), a dawn goddess, divine twins, and a storm god who slays a serpent — motifs echoed from the Rigveda to Norse myth. Georges Dumézil argued for a "trifunctional" ideology of priests, warriors and producers, visible in the caste and estate systems of daughter cultures.

Dispersal

On the Kurgan hypothesis, associated with Marija Gimbutas and refined by David Anthony, steppe pastoralists related to the Yamnaya culture expanded from c. 3300 BC with horse, wagon and herds, carrying their speech toward Europe and Asia — a migration ancient DNA has shown to be real and demographically massive. Their descendants' languages now stretch from Ireland to India.
