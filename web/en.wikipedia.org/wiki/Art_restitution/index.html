Art restitution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Artworks under provenance review for possible restitution

Art restitution is the return of artworks or cultural property to their original owners, or to the heirs of the original owners, when the works were looted, stolen, or otherwise wrongfully taken. The term is most often used in connection with art looted by the Nazi regime before and during the Second World War, but it also encompasses colonial-era removals and wartime plunder in other conflicts.

Nazi-looted art

Between 1933 and 1945, the Nazi regime seized hundreds of thousands of artworks from Jewish collectors and occupied countries. After the war, the Allied Monuments, Fine Arts, and Archives program recovered many works, but large numbers remained unaccounted for or ended up in museums and private collections with obscured provenance.

In 1998, 44 governments endorsed the Washington Principles on Nazi-Confiscated Art, committing to identify confiscated art, publicize records, and encourage "just and fair solutions" for pre-war owners and their heirs.

Legal and ethical issues

Restitution claims often turn on provenance research: the documented chain of ownership of a work. Claimants face obstacles including statutes of limitation, good-faith purchaser defenses, and sovereign immunity for state museums. Some countries, such as Austria, Germany, and the Netherlands, have established advisory panels or commissions to recommend outcomes in disputed cases.

Notable cases

The 2006 restitution of Gustav Klimt's Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I to Maria Altmann, following arbitration in Austria after the United States Supreme Court case Republic of Austria v. Altmann, is one of the most prominent examples of successful Nazi-era art restitution.
