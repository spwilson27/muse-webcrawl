Four Corners Monument

The Four Corners Monument marks the quadripoint in the Southwestern United States where the states of Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico, and Utah meet. It is the only point in the United States shared by four states, leading to the area being named the Four Corners region. The monument also marks the boundary between two semi-autonomous Native American governments, the Navajo Nation, which maintains the monument as a tourist attraction, and the Ute Mountain Ute Tribe Reservation.

History

The boundaries of the four states were defined in the 19th century: Congress defined the border between the Colorado and Utah Territories in 1861, and surveyors marked the point over the following decades. The first marker was placed in 1875, and a permanent monument was erected in 1912. The monument was completely rebuilt in 1992 and again renovated in 2010, with a granite and bronze marker set in a plaza bearing the seals and flags of the four states.

Tourism

Maintained by the Navajo Nation Parks and Recreation Department, the site is a popular tourist attraction despite its remote location. Visitors commonly pose for photographs with one limb in each of the four states. Navajo and Ute artisans sell crafts and food at stalls around the plaza.

Accuracy of the location

In 2009, media reports suggested the monument was misplaced by as much as 2.5 miles (4 km) from the legally intended location because of errors in early surveys. The U.S. National Geodetic Survey clarified that the monument marks the legal corner as established by the original surveys; under the doctrine of original survey, the marked point is legally correct even if it differs from the coordinates originally intended.
