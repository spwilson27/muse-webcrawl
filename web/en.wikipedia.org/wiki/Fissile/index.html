Fissile

In nuclear engineering, fissile material is material that can sustain a nuclear fission chain reaction with neutrons of thermal (slow) energy. All known fissile materials are capable of sustaining a chain reaction in this state without needing a neutron moderator. Under this definition, the only fissile nuclides in nature are uranium-235 and, in a reactor, plutonium-239 and plutonium-241 bred from fertile material, along with uranium-233 bred from thorium-232.

Fissile versus fissionable

Fissile material is distinct from "fissionable" material. A fissionable nuclide is any nuclide that can undergo fission when struck by a neutron, typically requiring a high-energy (fast) neutron. Uranium-238, for example, is fissionable but not fissile: it can fission with fast neutrons but cannot sustain a chain reaction with thermal neutrons. Virtually all fissile material is also fissionable, but not all fissionable material is fissile.

Fertile material, such as uranium-238 and thorium-232, can be converted into fissile material by neutron capture and subsequent radioactive decay — uranium-238 becomes plutonium-239, and thorium-232 becomes uranium-233. This process is exploited in breeder reactors.

Principal fissile nuclides

  • Uranium-235 — the only naturally occurring fissile nuclide in appreciable quantity.
  • Plutonium-239 — produced in reactors from uranium-238.
  • Uranium-233 — bred from thorium-232.
  • Plutonium-241 — also fissile, produced in reactors.
