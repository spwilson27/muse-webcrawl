Marsh

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A marsh is a type of wetland dominated by herbaceous rather than woody plant species. Marshes are characterised by mineral soils with poor drainage, frequent or continuous inundation, and vegetation adapted to saturated conditions, such as grasses, sedges, reeds and rushes. They are often found at the edges of lakes and streams, in floodplains, and along coasts, where they form a transition between aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems.

Types

  • Freshwater marshes occur around lakes, ponds, rivers and in low-lying depressions. They include wet meadows, prairie potholes, vernal pools and the margins of floodplains, and are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth.
  • Salt marshes develop along sheltered coasts in temperate regions, flooded daily by tides and dominated by salt-tolerant grasses such as Spartina (cordgrass). They protect shorelines from erosion, filter runoff, and serve as nurseries for fish and shellfish.
  • Tidal marshes include both salt and brackish marshes influenced by tides; freshwater tidal marshes occur near river mouths.

Ecology

Marshes support dense populations of invertebrates, fish, amphibians, waterfowl and mammals. Their saturated, oxygen-poor soils slow decomposition, allowing marshes to store significant amounts of carbon. They also buffer floods, trap sediments and improve water quality by absorbing nutrients and pollutants.

Conservation

Historically marshes were drained for agriculture and development — in many regions more than half of original marsh area has been lost — but their ecological value is now widely recognised, and marsh restoration and creation are common conservation measures. International protection for wetlands is coordinated under the Ramsar Convention.
