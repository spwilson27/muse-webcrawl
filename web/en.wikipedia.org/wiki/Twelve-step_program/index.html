Twelve-step program

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A twelve-step program is a set of recovery programs for addiction and behavioural problems based on the principles of Alcoholics Anonymous (AA). The defining feature of the approach is its set of guiding principles, known as the Twelve Steps, originally published in the book Alcoholics Anonymous (1939).

Twelve-step methods have been adapted to address a wide range of alcoholism, substance abuse and dependency problems. Over 200 mutual aid organisations — often known as fellowships — employ twelve-step principles, including Narcotics Anonymous, Cocaine Anonymous, Gamblers Anonymous, Overeaters Anonymous and Debtors Anonymous.

The Twelve Steps

As summarised by the American Psychological Association, the process involves admitting that one cannot control one's alcoholism, addiction or compulsion; coming to believe in a higher power that can give strength; examining past errors with the help of an experienced member (a sponsor); making amends for those errors; learning to live a new life with a new code of behaviour; and helping others who suffer from the same addiction or compulsion.

The steps begin with: "We admitted we were powerless over alcohol — that our lives had become unmanageable," and proceed through a moral inventory, confession of wrongs, restitution, prayer and meditation, concluding with the twelfth step: "Having had a spiritual awakening as the result of these steps, we tried to carry this message to alcoholics, and to practice these principles in all our affairs."

History

Alcoholics Anonymous was founded in 1935 in Akron, Ohio, by Bill Wilson and Dr. Bob Smith. The Twelve Steps were set out in the 1939 "Big Book", drawing on the practices of the Oxford Group, a Christian movement, and on the ideas of Carl Jung and William James. The Twelve Traditions, governing the organisation of the fellowships, were adopted in 1950.

Criticism and effectiveness

Twelve-step programs have been the subject of debate. Criticisms include their spiritual orientation, which some courts in the United States have held makes mandatory attendance a breach of the Establishment Clause, and questions about dropout rates. Conversely, a 2020 Cochrane review found that AA and twelve-step facilitation interventions performed at least as well as other treatments for alcohol use disorder, and better on measures of continuous abstinence.
