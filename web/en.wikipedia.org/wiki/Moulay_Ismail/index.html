Moulay Ismail Ibn Sharif (c. 1645 – 22 March 1727), sometimes called Ismail the Bloodthirsty, was Sultan of Morocco from 1672 to 1727, the second ruler of the Alaouite dynasty and one of the longest-reigning monarchs in Moroccan history. His 55-year reign established the Alaouite state in durable form: he subdued fractious tribes, built a powerful army, expelled European powers from most Moroccan coastal enclaves, and made Meknes his imperial capital.

Reign

Coming to the throne amid civil war after the death of his brother Moulay Rashid, Ismail spent his first years imposing authority over Fez, Marrakesh, and the mountain and desert tribes. His central instrument was the Black Guard ('Abid al-Bukhari), an army recruited from sub-Saharan slaves and their descendants, which grew to number tens of thousands and owed loyalty to the sultan alone. Against Europe, he retook Larache and Asilah from Spain and Tangier after its English evacuation (1684), and besieged Ceuta for decades, though it remained Spanish. He maintained diplomatic relations with France under Louis XIV, seeking artillery and engineers, and famously requested the hand of a French princess.

Meknes and legacy

At Meknes he raised a vast palace-city on a scale intended to rival Versailles, built by armies of labourers and Christian captives; much of it was ruined by the 1755 earthquake, but structures such as the Bab Mansour gate and the great granaries and stables survive. Ismail's personal fertility became legendary — he is credited with several hundred children, and has been cited by population scientists as possibly the most prolific father in recorded history. His death in 1727 triggered decades of succession strife among his sons.
