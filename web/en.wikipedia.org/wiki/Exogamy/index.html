Exogamy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Exogamy is the social norm of marrying outside one's social group — clan, lineage, village, caste segment or other defined category. The group defines the scope and extent of exogamy, and the rules and enforcement mechanisms that ensure its continuity. The opposite norm, requiring marriage within the group, is endogamy. Nearly all societies combine both: a person must marry outside close kin but inside some wider circle of religion, class or ethnicity.

Forms and examples

Clan exogamy is widespread: members of a clan, clan segment or Australian Aboriginal moiety must find spouses in other clans. Village exogamy has been the rule in much of rural China and India, where brides move between villages; in northern India, village exogamy combines with gotra (clan) exogamy and prohibitions on marrying within neighbouring villages. Religious and royal examples also occur — some dynasties, such as the Habsburgs, famously practised the opposite strategy of repeated cousin marriage, while many kingdoms required queens to be brought from abroad to cement alliances.

Explanations

Anthropologists have explained exogamy in several ways. E. B. Tylor argued that it let groups choose between "marrying out or being killed out", creating webs of alliance. Claude Lévi-Strauss made the exchange of spouses the centrepiece of his alliance theory in The Elementary Structures of Kinship (1949): the incest taboo, by forcing marriage outward, transforms natural kinship into social alliance and is, for him, the founding rule of culture itself. Other explanations stress the reduction of conflict within the group, the avoidance of inbreeding depression (and, in biology generally, outbreeding), and the economic advantages of wider networks. In biology and linguistics the term is also used analogically — for mating systems that promote cross-fertilisation and for the borrowing of words or spouses' languages between communities.
