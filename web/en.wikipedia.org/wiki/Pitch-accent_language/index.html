Pitch-accent language

A pitch-accent language is a language in which the pitch (fundamental frequency) of a word's syllables or morae determines lexical distinctions, but in a more restricted way than in a full tone language: typically only one syllable in a word can bear an accent, and the contrast is limited to a small number of patterns, such as high versus low.

Examples

Japanese is the best-known pitch-accent language: in the Tokyo dialect, words are distinguished by the location of a pitch fall (for example, hashi "chopsticks" versus "bridge" versus "edge"). Other examples include Korean (in some dialects), Swedish and Norwegian, whose two word accents distinguish many word pairs; Serbo-Croatian and Slovene; Lithuanian; and Ancient Greek, whose accent system was melodic.

Pitch accent versus tone and stress

Scholars debate the boundaries of the category: unlike true tone languages such as Mandarin, pitch-accent systems restrict tonal contrasts to one position per word; unlike stress-accent languages such as English, the accent is realised primarily by pitch rather than loudness or duration. Many linguists treat the three as points on a continuum of word-prosodic systems.
