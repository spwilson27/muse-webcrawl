Pontiac or Obwandiyag (c. 1720 – 20 April 1769) was an Odawa war chief known for his role in Pontiac's War (1763–1766), one of the most successful Native American resistances to European colonisation. He led a loose confederation of Great Lakes and Ohio Country tribes against the British military occupation of the region following the French and Indian War.

Background

Pontiac was born around 1720, probably along the Maumee River in present-day Ohio, to an Odawa father and an Ojibwe mother by some accounts. He became a war leader of the Odawa near Detroit, a key French trading post, and fought alongside the French in the French and Indian War, reportedly at the Battle of the Monongahela in 1755.

Pontiac's War

After Britain took over New France in 1760, British commander Jeffery Amherst cut off the gift-giving and trade practices that had sustained French–Native alliances, and settlers encroached on Native lands. Inspired in part by the Delaware prophet Neolin's nativist teachings, Pontiac organised a coalition. On 7 May 1763 he attempted to seize Fort Detroit by surprise; when the plan was exposed, he laid siege to it for months. Other tribes captured eight British forts, including Fort Sandusky, Fort Miami, and Fort Michilimackinac (taken by a stratagem during a game of baggataway). The siege of Detroit was lifted in October 1763 after France declined to send help.

Peace and death

The Royal Proclamation of 1763 reserved lands west of the Appalachians for Native peoples, a partial concession to the war's grievances. Pontiac negotiated peace with Sir William Johnson at Oswego in July 1766, though his authority waned as he was seen as accommodating the British. On 20 April 1769 he was murdered at Cahokia, Illinois, by a Peoria warrior, an act that triggered further intertribal conflict.

Legacy

Pontiac is remembered as a symbol of Indigenous resistance; the city of Pontiac, Michigan, and the Pontiac automobile brand were named for him. Historians debate how far he personally directed the wider war that bears his name.
