Hearing loss

Hearing loss is a partial or total inability to hear. It may occur in one or both ears and may be temporary or permanent. Hearing loss is classified as conductive (problems of the outer or middle ear), sensorineural (inner ear, cochlea, or auditory nerve), or mixed. The World Health Organization estimates that over 1.5 billion people worldwide have some degree of hearing loss, and about 430 million have disabling hearing loss requiring rehabilitation.

Causes

Presbycusis — age-related degeneration of cochlear hair cells — is the most common cause in adults. Chronic exposure to loud noise damages hair cells irreversibly, making noise-induced hearing loss a major occupational hazard. Other causes include earwax impaction and otosclerosis (conductive), Ménière's disease, acoustic neuroma, and infections such as meningitis, measles, mumps, and chronic otitis media (sensorineural). More than half of congenital hearing loss is genetic; the most common single gene implicated is GJB2 (connexin 26). Certain antibiotics (aminoglycosides) and chemotherapy agents (cisplatin) are ototoxic.

Prevention and treatment

WHO estimates that around 60% of childhood hearing loss is preventable through immunization, treatment of ear infections, and avoidance of ototoxic drugs and loud noise. Hearing aids amplify sound for most sensorineural loss; cochlear implants bypass damaged hair cells to stimulate the auditory nerve directly and are standard care for profound loss, including in young children, where early intervention is critical for language development. Many Deaf communities regard deafness as a cultural and linguistic identity rather than a disability, centred on sign languages.

Societal impact

Untreated hearing loss is associated with social isolation, depression, accelerated cognitive decline, and increased dementia risk, and it imposes large global economic costs. Access to hearing technology remains very limited in low- and middle-income countries.
