Traditionalist Catholicism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Traditionalist Catholicism is a movement of Catholics who hold that the changes introduced in the Roman Catholic Church after the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965) — particularly the reform of the liturgy, ecumenism, and the teaching on religious liberty — constitute a departure from authentic Catholic tradition. Traditionalists characteristically favour the pre-conciliar form of the Roman Rite, known as the Tridentine Mass or Traditional Latin Mass, and a restoration of older devotional and doctrinal emphases.

Origins

The movement emerged in reaction to the liturgical reforms of Pope Paul VI, whose new order of Mass (Novus Ordo Missae) was promulgated in 1969. Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre founded the Society of Saint Pius X (SSPX) in 1970 to train priests in the traditional manner. In 1988 Lefebvre consecrated four bishops without papal mandate, an act for which he and the bishops were declared excommunicated; the excommunications of the surviving bishops were lifted by Pope Benedict XVI in 2009, though the SSPX's canonical status remains irregular.

Spectrum

Positions within the movement range from groups in full communion with Rome that celebrate the older liturgy under provisions such as Benedict XVI's 2007 motu proprio Summorum Pontificum (whose wider permissions were restricted by Pope Francis's Traditionis custodes in 2021), through the SSPX, to sedevacantists, who hold that the post-conciliar popes are not valid popes, and conclavists, who have elected their own claimants.

Practices and beliefs

Traditionalist communities typically retain Latin, Gregorian chant, ad orientem celebration, communion on the tongue, traditional fasting and dress norms, and catechesis based on pre-conciliar manuals such as the Roman Catechism and the Baltimore Catechism.
