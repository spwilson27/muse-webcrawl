Sonata form

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sonata form (also sonata-allegro form or first movement form) is a musical structure generally consisting of three main sections: an exposition, a development, and a recapitulation. It has been the standard form for the first movement of sonatas, symphonies, string quartets and concertos since the mid-18th century, and remains one of the most influential organisational principles in Western classical music.

Sections

Exposition. The principal themes are presented, typically a first theme group in the tonic key and a contrasting second theme group in a related key (the dominant in major-key movements, the relative major in minor). The exposition is often repeated.

Development. The material of the exposition is fragmented, recombined and taken through remote keys, building tension and instability before a retransition leads back to the tonic.

Recapitulation. The exposition's material returns, now with the second theme group restated in the tonic so that the movement's tonal conflict is resolved. A coda may follow to round off the movement.

History

The form crystallised in the works of Haydn and Mozart out of Baroque binary forms, and was greatly expanded by Beethoven, who enlarged the development and coda and heightened thematic contrast. Nineteenth-century composers such as Schubert, Brahms and Mahler stretched the model with three-key expositions and vast developments, while 20th-century theorists—notably Heinrich Schenker, and later Charles Rosen in The Classical Style and Sonata Forms—sought to define its norms. Modern scholarship (e.g., Hepokoski and Darcy's "Sonata Theory") treats sonata form less as a fixed mould than as a set of dialogic conventions that composers could follow, deform or reinterpret.

Usage

Sonata form is most associated with first movements, but variants appear in slow movements, finales (including sonata-rondo), overtures and single-movement works.
