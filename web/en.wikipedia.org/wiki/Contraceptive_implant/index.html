Contraceptive implant

A contraceptive implant is an implantable medical device used for the purpose of birth control. The device is a small, flexible plastic rod, about the size of a matchstick, that is inserted under the skin of the upper arm. It slowly releases a low dose of a progestogen hormone — most commonly etonogestrel — over a period of several years.

Effectiveness

Contraceptive implants are among the most effective forms of reversible contraception, with failure rates below 0.1% per year, comparable to intrauterine devices and sterilization. Because they do not depend on user action after insertion, typical-use and perfect-use rates are nearly identical.

Mechanism of action

The released hormone works primarily by suppressing ovulation. It also thickens cervical mucus, which blocks sperm from reaching the uterus, and thins the lining of the uterus.

Insertion and removal

A trained healthcare provider inserts the implant just under the skin on the inner side of the non-dominant upper arm using a special applicator, usually under local anesthesia. Removal involves a small incision and must also be performed by a trained provider. Etonogestrel implants are typically approved for three years of use, while older levonorgestrel systems (such as Norplant and Jadelle) use two or more rods and last up to five years.

Side effects

The most common side effect is irregular menstrual bleeding, including lighter periods, spotting, or absence of periods. Other reported effects include headaches, acne, breast tenderness, and mood changes. Serious complications are rare.

History

The first implant system, Norplant, was developed by the Population Council and approved in Finland in 1983 and in the United States in 1990. Later single-rod systems such as Implanon and its successor Nexplanon (Implanon NXT) simplified insertion and added a radiopaque marker so the rod can be located by X-ray.
