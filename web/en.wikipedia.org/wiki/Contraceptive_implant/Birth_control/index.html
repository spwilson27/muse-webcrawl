Birth control

Birth control, also known as contraception, anticonception, and fertility control, is the use of methods or devices to prevent pregnancy. Birth control has been used since ancient times, but effective and safe methods only became readily available in the 20th century. Planning, making available, and using human birth control is called family planning.

Methods

The most effective methods are sterilisation by means of vasectomy in males and tubal ligation in females, intrauterine devices (IUDs), and implantable birth control, including the progestogen-releasing etonogestrel implant. These are followed by a number of hormone-based methods, including oral pills, patches, vaginal rings, and injectables. Less effective methods include physical barriers such as condoms and diaphragms, and fertility awareness methods. Emergency contraception can prevent pregnancy after unprotected sex.

Effectiveness

Effectiveness is generally expressed as the percentage of women who become pregnant using a given method during the first year of use, distinguishing perfect use from typical use. Long-acting reversible contraception (LARC), such as IUDs and implants, has typical-use failure rates below 1% per year because it does not depend on user action.

Health and society

Access to contraception reduces unintended pregnancy, maternal mortality and unsafe abortion, and is recognised by the World Health Organization and the United Nations as supporting health and human rights. Teenage pregnancies are especially risky; birth control use among adolescents has increased over time. Some methods, notably condoms, also protect against sexually transmitted infections.

History

Ancient methods included withdrawal, barriers and herbal preparations. The modern birth control movement began in the early 20th century with campaigners such as Margaret Sanger and Marie Stopes. The first oral contraceptive pill was approved in the United States in 1960, transforming the availability and social acceptance of contraception.
