Intrauterine device

An intrauterine device (IUD) is a small, T-shaped device that is inserted into the uterus to prevent pregnancy. It is a form of long-acting reversible contraception (LARC). Two main types exist: copper IUDs, which contain no hormones, and hormonal IUDs, which release the progestogen levonorgestrel.

Effectiveness

IUDs are among the most effective reversible contraceptives, with first-year failure rates of about 0.8% for copper IUDs and 0.1–0.2% for hormonal IUDs. They work immediately or shortly after insertion and remain effective for 3 to 12 years depending on the type, and fertility returns promptly after removal.

Mechanism

Copper IUDs act mainly by a cytotoxic inflammatory reaction that is spermicidal, preventing fertilisation. Hormonal IUDs thicken cervical mucus, thin the endometrium and may partially suppress ovulation.

Use and safety

IUDs are recommended by bodies including the World Health Organization and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists for most women, including adolescents and nulliparous women. Copper IUDs may increase menstrual bleeding and cramps, whereas hormonal IUDs usually reduce bleeding. Risks include expulsion, and rarely uterine perforation at insertion or pelvic inflammatory disease in the first weeks after insertion. Copper IUDs are also the most effective form of emergency contraception.
