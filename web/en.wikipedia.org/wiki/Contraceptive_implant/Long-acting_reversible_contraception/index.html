Long-acting reversible contraception

Long-acting reversible contraception (LARC) is a term for highly effective contraceptive methods that can be used for an extended period without requiring user action. The main LARC methods are intrauterine devices (IUDs)—both copper and hormonal—and the subdermal contraceptive implant, such as the etonogestrel implant.

Effectiveness

LARC methods have failure rates below 1% per year in both typical and perfect use, because their effectiveness does not depend on the user remembering to take a pill or use a barrier. In the Contraceptive CHOICE Project in St. Louis, providing no-cost contraception with LARC promotion was associated with large reductions in teenage pregnancy and abortion rates.

Duration and reversibility

Depending on the device, LARC methods last from 3 years (implants) to 10–12 years (copper IUDs). They can be removed at any time by a clinician, and fertility returns quickly, making them fully reversible. Insertion and removal require a clinical procedure.

Guidance

Professional bodies including the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and the American Academy of Paediatrics recommend LARC as a first-line option, including for adolescents. Uptake has grown but remains limited in many countries by cost, availability of trained providers, and misconceptions about safety.
