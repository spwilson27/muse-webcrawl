Hormonal contraception

Hormonal contraception refers to birth control methods that act on the endocrine system. Almost all methods are composed of steroid hormones, although in India one selective oestrogen receptor modulator is marketed as a contraceptive. The original hormonal method—the combined oral contraceptive pill—was first marketed as a contraceptive in 1960. In the ensuing decades many other delivery methods have been developed.

Methods

  • Combined methods, containing an oestrogen and a progestogen: the combined oral contraceptive pill, the contraceptive patch and the vaginal ring.
  • Progestogen-only methods: progestogen-only pills, injectables such as depot medroxyprogesterone acetate, and subdermal implants such as the etonogestrel implant.
  • Intrauterine methods: hormonal IUDs releasing levonorgestrel.

Mechanism

Hormonal contraceptives primarily prevent ovulation by suppressing the mid-cycle surge of luteinising hormone. They also thicken cervical mucus, hindering sperm passage, and alter the endometrium. Combined methods have typical-use failure rates around 7% per year, while long-acting methods such as implants and hormonal IUDs are below 1%.

Effects

Benefits beyond contraception can include regulation of menstruation, reduced dysmenorrhoea, and reduced risk of ovarian and endometrial cancers. Combined hormonal contraceptives carry a small increased risk of venous thromboembolism. Hormonal methods do not protect against sexually transmitted infections.
