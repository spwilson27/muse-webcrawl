Green's function

In mathematics, a Green's function (or fundamental solution) is the impulse response of an inhomogeneous linear differential operator defined on a domain with specified initial or boundary conditions. The concept is named after the British mathematician George Green, who first developed it in the 1820s.

Definition

Given a linear differential operator L, the Green's function G(x, s) satisfies

L G(x, s) = δ(x − s),

where δ is the Dirac delta function. The solution of the inhomogeneous equation L u(x) = f(x) can then be written as a superposition (an integral) over the responses to point sources:

u(x) = ∫ G(x, s) f(s) ds.

Physically, Green's functions describe how a system responds at a point to a unit impulse applied at another point or time — for example the electrostatic potential due to a point charge (G ∝ 1/|x − s|, the solution of Poisson's equation) or the retarded potentials of electromagnetism.

Applications

Green's functions are a central tool across mathematical physics: they solve boundary value problems for the Laplace, Helmholtz and wave equations, underpin the method of images, and provide the formalism of propagators in quantum field theory, where the Green's functions (correlation functions) of a field encode its dynamics. In many-body physics and solid-state theory, Green's functions generalise to operator and matrix forms used in perturbation theory and the Dyson equation.

Finding Green's functions

General methods of construction include eigenfunction expansions, Fourier and Laplace transforms, the method of images (for simple geometries), and conformal mapping in two dimensions. The existence and properties of Green's functions are studied within the theory of distributions and partial differential equations.
