Repugnant conclusion

The repugnant conclusion is a problem in population ethics identified by Derek Parfit in his book Reasons and Persons (1984). For any population of people with very high quality of life, total utilitarianism implies there is a better population consisting of a much larger number of people whose lives are barely worth living — a result Parfit and many others find intuitively repugnant, hence the name.

The argument

The conclusion follows from comparing populations by total welfare. Start with population A: ten billion people, all with excellent lives. Consider A+: the same people, plus another ten billion with slightly lower but still good lives and some inequality removed. By a series of steps — each apparently an improvement (adding people with lives worth living, then equalising welfare at a marginally lower level) — one reaches population Z: an enormous number of people whose lives are barely worth living, yet whose total welfare exceeds A's. If each step is at least as good as the last, Z must be judged better than A.

Responses

Proposed escapes include average utilitarianism (compare populations by average rather than total welfare — though this generates its own paradoxes, such as preferring a tiny blissful population), critical-level utilitarianism (lives contribute only the welfare above a positive threshold), perfectionist and person-affecting views, and simply accepting the conclusion as an intuition to be revised. The problem is closely related to Parfit's mere addition paradox and the non-identity problem, and remains a central topic in population ethics, debated by philosophers including Larry Temkin, Gustaf Arrhenius and Hilary Greaves.
