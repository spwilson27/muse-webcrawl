Substantive due process

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Substantive due process is the principle, developed by the Supreme Court of the United States, that the Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments protect certain fundamental liberty interests from government interference, regardless of the fairness of the procedures used. It is distinguished from procedural due process, which concerns notice and hearing.

The doctrine's history is contested. In the Lochner era (c. 1897–1937) the Court used it to strike down economic regulation in the name of freedom of contract, a jurisprudence repudiated after 1937 and long treated as a cautionary tale. From the 1960s the Court revived substantive due process for personal rights: contraception (Griswold, 1965), abortion (Roe, 1973, overruled by Dobbs, 2022), intimate conduct (Lawrence, 2003) and marriage (Obergefell, 2015). Critics across the spectrum call the doctrine oxymoronic or undemocratic; defenders answer that “liberty” must have content and that the Ninth Amendment and the Privileges or Immunities Clause point the same way. Its present scope is unsettled after Dobbs.
