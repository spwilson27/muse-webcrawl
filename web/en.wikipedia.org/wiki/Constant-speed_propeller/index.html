Constant-speed propeller

A constant-speed propeller automatically varies blade pitch to hold the engine rpm selected by the pilot.

A constant-speed propeller is a variable-pitch propeller that automatically changes its blade angle to maintain a chosen rotational speed (rpm), regardless of throttle setting or flight condition. By keeping the propeller at its most efficient angle of attack across take-off, climb and cruise, it substantially improves performance over fixed-pitch designs. The pilot controls engine rpm with a propeller lever while the throttle controls manifold pressure (power).

How it works

The heart of the system is a governor, driven by the engine, containing flyweights and a speeder spring whose compression is set by the pilot's rpm lever. If rpm rises above the selected value, the flyweights move outward and direct pressurised engine oil to the hub mechanism so as to increase blade pitch ("coarsen"), loading the engine and pulling rpm back down; if rpm falls, oil pressure is relieved (often assisted by counterweights or springs) to decrease pitch ("fine") and restore speed. On many multi-engine aircraft the same oil system can drive the blades to a fully feathered position — aligned with the airflow — to minimise drag and windmilling after an engine failure.

History

Practical constant-speed propellers were developed in the 1930s, notably by Hamilton Standard, whose two-position and then fully-governed hydromatic designs were adopted on most advanced military and airline aircraft of the Second World War era — including Liberator-derived transports. Modern turboprops and piston aircraft use electronic or hydromechanical governors with similar principles, and beta range control allows direct pitch management for ground handling and reverse thrust.
