Ascaris suum, also known as the large roundworm of pigs, is a parasitic nematode that causes ascariasis in pigs. It is one of the most common and economically important helminth parasites of swine worldwide, closely related to the human parasite Ascaris lumbricoides — so closely that the two are sometimes regarded as a single species, and cross-infections between pigs and humans have been documented.

Life cycle

Adult worms live in the small intestine of pigs; females can reach 40 cm in length and produce enormous numbers of eggs (up to hundreds of thousands per day), which pass out in the faeces. Eggs embryonate in soil over several weeks and can survive for years. When a pig ingests an infective egg, the larva hatches, penetrates the gut wall, and migrates through the liver and lungs before being coughed up, swallowed, and returning to the intestine to mature. The migration causes much of the pathology: white "milk spots" on the liver, and respiratory signs ("thumps") in young pigs.

Effects and control

Heavy infections reduce growth rate and feed conversion, damage the liver (leading to condemnation at slaughter), and can obstruct the intestine or bile duct. Control relies on anthelmintic treatment, hygiene, and management systems that interrupt egg accumulation, such as all-in/all-out production and moving sows to clean farrowing accommodation. Because eggs are extremely resistant in the environment, eradication from contaminated ground is difficult.

Research

A. suum is an important laboratory model for human ascariasis, which infects hundreds of millions of people. Its genome has been sequenced, and the pig–Ascaris system is used to study helminth immunology and vaccine development.
