Hormone replacement therapy

Hormone replacement therapy (HRT), also known as menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) or postmenopausal hormone therapy, is a form of hormone therapy used to treat symptoms associated with female menopause. These symptoms can include hot flashes, vaginal atrophy, accelerated skin aging, and stress incontinence; HRT also reduces the incidence of fractures from osteoporosis.

Forms

The main hormone involved is estrogen, which may be given alone (in women who have had a hysterectomy) or combined with a progestogen, which protects the endometrium from estrogen-induced hyperplasia and cancer. Preparations include oral tablets, transdermal patches, gels and sprays, vaginal creams and rings, and implants. Conjugated equine estrogens (Premarin) and estradiol are among the most commonly used estrogens. Androgens such as testosterone are sometimes added, and HRT principles also apply to hormone therapy for transgender people and for hypogonadism in men, though those uses are usually treated separately.

History and the Women's Health Initiative

Estrogen therapy for menopause was popularized in the 1960s, notably by Robert Wilson's book Feminine Forever. Use rose through the 1990s, supported by observational studies suggesting cardiovascular benefit. In 2002, the Women's Health Initiative (WHI) trial of combined estrogen–progestin therapy was stopped early because of increased risks of breast cancer, coronary heart disease, stroke, and pulmonary embolism in the study population of older postmenopausal women. Prescribing fell sharply worldwide. Subsequent analyses suggested the balance of risks and benefits is more favorable for women who begin therapy near menopause — the "timing hypothesis" — and current guidelines generally support HRT for symptomatic women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause, at the lowest effective dose.

Effects

HRT is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes and night sweats) and prevents bone loss. Identified risks include venous thromboembolism (greater with oral than transdermal estrogen), stroke, and, with combined therapy used for more than about five years, a small increase in breast cancer incidence.
