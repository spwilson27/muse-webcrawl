University press

A university press is an academic publishing house specialising in producing scholarly titles, including monographs in the humanities and social sciences, professional reference works, and journals. University presses are typically non-profit components of their parent universities, though some, such as Oxford University Press and Cambridge University Press, are large enough to compete in scale with major commercial publishers. Their output is characterised by rigorous peer review, modest print runs, and a commitment to publishing research of scholarly significance regardless of its commercial potential.

History

The oldest university presses are those of Oxford (printing since 1478, with rights to print granted in 1586) and Cambridge (founded 1534, publishing continuously since 1584). In North America, university presses expanded rapidly after the Second World War, supported by the growth of higher education and by foundation and government funding. The Association of University Presses, founded in 1937 as the Association of American University Presses, today represents more than 150 members worldwide.

Functions and publishing model

University presses publish primarily for scholars, libraries, and students rather than for the general trade market. Manuscripts are normally subjected to anonymous peer review by specialists, and a faculty editorial board typically gives final approval. Because scholarly monographs often sell only a few hundred copies, presses have historically relied on subsidies from their parent institutions, though many have diversified into regional titles, textbooks, and digital publishing to remain solvent. Open-access initiatives have become increasingly important since the 2010s.
