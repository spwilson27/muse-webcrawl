Acacia kempeana

Acacia kempeana, commonly known as wanderrie wattle, wandery wattle or silky wattle, is a shrub or small tree of the genus Acacia in the family Fabaceae, endemic to arid parts of central Australia. It is widespread across Western Australia, South Australia, the Northern Territory and western Queensland and New South Wales. The species epithet honours the Lutheran missionary Johann Gottlieb Kempe of the Hermannsburg Mission in central Australia.

Description

Acacia kempeana typically grows as a rounded, spreading shrub or tree about 1 to 6 metres tall (occasionally taller), with rough, fissured bark and silky-hairy branchlets. Its phyllodes are narrow, roughly 4–11 cm long, grey-green and densely covered in silky hairs when young. The bright yellow, globular flower heads appear mainly in winter and spring (roughly April to October) and are followed by flat, woody seed pods.

Habitat and uses

The species grows in red sand, spinifex plains and rocky ranges of the central Australian deserts, often in mulga and mallee communities. Aboriginal peoples of the region traditionally used its seeds as a food source (ground into flour for damper) and the plant is also valued as a hardy windbreak and ornamental in arid-zone planting, and as host plant for the larvae of some butterflies. It fixes nitrogen, improving poor desert soils.

Taxonomy

The species was first formally described by the botanist Ferdinand von Mueller in 1882. Like most Australian acacias, it was transferred for a time under the reclassification of the genus, and it sits within the informal "mulga group" of related arid-zone wattles.
