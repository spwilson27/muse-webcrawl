Axle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An axle or axletree is a central shaft for a rotating wheel or gear. On wheeled vehicles, the axle may be fixed to the wheels, rotating with them, or fixed to the vehicle, with the wheels rotating around the axle. In the former case, bearings or bushings are provided at the mounting points where the axle is supported. In the latter case, a bearing or bushing sits inside a central hole in the wheel to allow the wheel or gear to rotate around the axle.

A driven axle with differential.

Vehicles

In cars and trucks, axles are key components of the suspension and drivetrain. A drive axle transmits power to the wheels and is split into two half axles joined by a differential, allowing the wheels to rotate at different speeds in corners. A dead axle (lazy axle) carries load but transmits no power. Axle configurations are described by counts such as the 6×4 arrangement. Important design variables include axle load ratings and the gear ratio of the final drive.

Railways

On railways, axles are joined to wheels as wheelsets, normally as solid forged steel axles with the wheels press-fitted. Axle failures have historically caused serious accidents, and axle fatigue — studied since August Wöhler's nineteenth-century experiments — drove the development of fatigue science in engineering. Axle counters are widely used to detect trains in signalling.
