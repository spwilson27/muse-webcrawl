Mortgage

A mortgage is a legal instrument of the common law which is used to create a security interest in real property held by a lender as a security for a debt, usually a mortgage loan. The term also refers to the loan itself. The borrower (the mortgagor) grants the lender (the mortgagee) an interest in the property; if the borrower defaults, the lender may foreclose and sell the property to satisfy the debt.

History

The word derives from the Old French mort gage, "dead pledge": the pledge was considered dead either when the debt was repaid or when the property was taken. Mortgages have medieval English origins and were reshaped by the courts of equity, which developed the borrower's "equity of redemption". In the 20th century, long-term self-amortising mortgages — particularly the 30-year fixed-rate loan in the United States — made mass home ownership possible.

Key features

A mortgage transaction involves the principal amount, an interest rate (fixed or adjustable), a term (commonly 15 or 30 years), and scheduled repayments, usually monthly, that amortise the loan. Lenders assess the loan-to-value ratio and the borrower's creditworthiness. Many residential mortgages are sold into the secondary market and securitised as mortgage-backed securities, a practice central to the 2007–2008 financial crisis.

Foreclosure

If the borrower fails to pay, the lender may initiate foreclosure: judicial foreclosure through the courts in some jurisdictions, or non-judicial sale under a deed of trust in others. Related concepts include deeds of trust, which in many US states function in place of mortgages.
