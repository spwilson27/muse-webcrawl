Cypriot cuisine

Cypriot cuisine is the cuisine of Cyprus and is closely related to Greek and Turkish cuisines, with influences from the island's long history of Greek, Byzantine, Ottoman, Levantine, Italian, French, and British rule. It is a Mediterranean cuisine built around fresh vegetables, legumes, grains, olive oil, herbs, grilled meats, and seafood.

Meze

The most characteristic Cypriot meal is meze — a succession of many small dishes served as a shared feast. A full meze may include olives, village salad, tahini, taramasalata, hummus, tzatziki (talatouri), grilled halloumi, lountza (smoked pork loin), loukaniko sausages, sheftalia, koupepia (stuffed vine leaves), moussaka, afelia (pork in red wine and coriander), and grilled fish or octopus, finishing with fruit and sweets.

Signature foods

Halloumi cheese, made from sheep's and goat's milk (often with some cow's milk), is Cyprus's most famous food export and holds protected status; it is eaten raw, grilled, or fried. Others include souvla — large chunks of meat slow-roasted on long skewers over charcoal, distinct from smaller souvlaki — and kleftiko, lamb slow-baked in sealed clay ovens, whose name ("stolen style") recalls meat cooked by mountain brigands. Flaounes (cheese-filled Easter pastries), kolokasi (taro), and pourgouri (bulgur pilaf) are also typical.

Drinks and sweets

Cyprus has an ancient wine tradition; Commandaria, a sweet dessert wine from sun-dried grapes, is among the world's oldest named wines still in production. Zivania is a strong pomace spirit. Sweets include loukoumades (honey-soaked dough puffs), soutzoukos (grape-must and nut strings), and Cyprus delight. Strong Cypriot coffee, prepared like Greek and Turkish coffee, is central to hospitality.

Influences

Both Greek Cypriot and Turkish Cypriot communities share most dishes, sometimes under differing names, and fasting traditions of the Orthodox Church shape a large repertoire of Lenten, olive-oil-based vegetable dishes.
