Fixation (visual)

Fixation, in visual perception, is the act of maintaining the gaze on a single location. Humans and other animals with a fovea — the high-resolution centre of the retina — do not look at a scene steadily; instead the eyes make rapid movements called saccades, alternating with fixations during which visual information is actually taken in. A typical fixation during reading lasts about 200–300 milliseconds, and during scene viewing about 300 milliseconds, though durations vary widely with the task.

Components of fixation

Even while the gaze is held on a target, the eyes are never perfectly still. Fixation is maintained by three classes of small involuntary movements: tremor (a tiny high-frequency oscillation), drift (slow wandering of the eye), and microsaccades (small rapid corrections). These movements are essential to vision: if an image is optically stabilised on the retina so that it does not move at all, it fades from perception within seconds, a phenomenon demonstrated in stabilised-image experiments in the 1950s.

Fixation and attention

Fixations are closely, but not perfectly, coupled with visual attention. Readers fixate most content words but skip many short, predictable words; in scene perception, the first fixations are drawn to salient or informative regions. Researchers use fixation patterns — recorded with eye trackers — to study reading, usability, advertising, driving, and clinical conditions. Scanpath theory proposes that the sequence of fixations and saccades reflects the viewer's internal cognitive model of the scene.

Clinical aspects

Abnormal fixation can indicate neurological or ophthalmic problems. In amblyopia the weaker eye may develop eccentric fixation, using a non-foveal retinal point to look at targets. Nystagmus, an involuntary oscillation of the eyes, can make stable fixation impossible and degrade acuity.
