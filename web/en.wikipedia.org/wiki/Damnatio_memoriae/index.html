Damnatio memoriae is a modern Latin phrase meaning "condemnation of memory", denoting a form of posthumous dishonour in ancient Rome in which a person — usually an emperor or other powerful figure — was effectively erased from public record. Statues were destroyed or recarved, names were chiselled from inscriptions and monuments, coins were recalled, and legal acts of the condemned were rescinded.

Practice

The Roman Senate could decree that a deceased emperor's memory be condemned. The emperor Nero was declared a public enemy, and after the brief reign of Geta, his brother and co-emperor Caracalla had Geta murdered and his images and name systematically removed — portraits of Geta were even painted over in family group images such as the Severan Tondo. The praetorian prefect Sejanus suffered similar erasure after his fall in AD 31.

Effectiveness and paradox

Scholars note that complete erasure was rarely achieved or even truly intended: the very act of erasure left visible scars — blank spaces in inscriptions and mutilated portraits — that called attention to the condemned. Historians such as Harriet Flower have argued that the practice functioned less as genuine oblivion than as a public, memorable act of dishonour. Similar practices existed elsewhere, including the erasure of the pharaohs Hatshepsut and Akhenaten in ancient Egypt.

Modern usage

The term is now used more broadly for deliberate official erasure of persons from history, and is often invoked in comparisons with Soviet-era photographic doctoring and other modern practices, though historians caution that the ancient phenomenon was distinctive in its formal, senatorial character.
