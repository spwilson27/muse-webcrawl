Jesse Walter Fewkes

Born: November 14, 1850, Newton, Massachusetts, U.S.
Died: May 31, 1930, Forest Glen, Maryland, U.S.
Fields: Ethnology, archaeology, zoology
Institutions: Smithsonian Institution, Bureau of American Ethnology

Jesse Walter Fewkes (November 14, 1850 – May 31, 1930) was an American anthropologist, archaeologist, and ethnologist, best known for his pioneering studies of the Pueblo peoples of the American Southwest and his leadership of the Bureau of American Ethnology (BAE) of the Smithsonian Institution.

Life and career

Fewkes was born in Newton, Massachusetts, and studied at Harvard University, where he earned a doctorate in zoology in 1877. He began his career as a marine zoologist, studying jellyfish and other invertebrates, before turning to anthropology in the late 1880s when his health led him to the Southwest.

Southwestern ethnology and archaeology

Working among the Hopi and other Pueblo groups from 1889 onward, Fewkes made some of the earliest phonographic recordings of Native American music and ritual, and published foundational studies of Hopi ceremonies such as the Snake Dance and of katsina religion. As an archaeologist he excavated and documented ruins including Casa Grande in Arizona and sites at Mesa Verde, Colorado — excavating and repairing Spruce Tree House and Cliff Palace — and lobbied for the preservation embodied in the Antiquities Act of 1906. In 1918 he succeeded William Henry Holmes as chief of the Bureau of American Ethnology, serving until 1928. His later work extended to the prehistory of the West Indies and Florida.

Legacy

Fewkes published hundreds of reports and monographs. The archaeological site Fewkes Canyon at Mesa Verde is named for him, and his wax-cylinder recordings remain among the most significant early documents of Indigenous North American music.
