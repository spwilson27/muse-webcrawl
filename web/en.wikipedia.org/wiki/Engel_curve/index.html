Engel curve

In microeconomics, an Engel curve describes how household expenditure on a particular good or service varies with household income, holding prices constant. Named after the German statistician Ernst Engel (1821–1896), the curve is derived from the income expansion path of consumer choice theory: as income changes, the consumer's optimal bundle traces out quantities demanded of each good, and plotting quantity (or expenditure) against income gives the Engel curve for that good.

Engel curves slope upward for normal goods and downward for inferior goods.

Shape by type of good

The shape of an Engel curve reveals how a good is classified. For a normal good the curve slopes upward: demand rises with income. Among normal goods, a necessity (such as basic foodstuffs) has a concave Engel curve — expenditure rises less than proportionally, so its budget share falls as income grows — while a luxury good has a convex curve, with expenditure rising more than proportionally. For an inferior good (such as low-quality staple foods or bus travel for some households) the Engel curve slopes downward over the relevant range: richer households buy less of it. At a single income point, the slope of the Engel curve relates directly to the income elasticity of demand.

Engel's law

The best-known empirical application is food. Engel's law, derived from Engel's 1857 study of Belgian working-class budgets, states that the proportion of income spent on food falls as income rises, even though absolute food spending increases. The food Engel curve is thus concave, and the falling food share of budgets as economies grow — visible in long-run statistics for country after country — remains one of the most robust regularities in economics.

Applications

Engel curves are estimated from household budget surveys and are used to measure welfare and inequality, to model demand systems (such as the Almost Ideal Demand System of Deaton and Muellbauer), to compute cost-of-living indices, and in development economics, where the food budget share serves as a proxy for living standards. Working–Leser Engel curves, which plot budget shares against the logarithm of total expenditure, are a standard econometric specification.
