William Marvin Bass III (born August 30, 1928) is an American forensic anthropologist, best known for founding the Anthropology Research Facility at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville — popularly known as the "Body Farm" — the first facility devoted to studying the decomposition of human remains.

Early life and education

Bass was born in Staunton, Virginia. He earned his bachelor's degree from the University of Virginia in 1951, a master's degree in anthropology from the University of Kentucky, and his PhD from the University of Pennsylvania in 1961, studying under William R. Coe and Wilton M. Krogman. He taught at the University of Kansas before moving to Tennessee in 1971 to head the anthropology department at the University of Tennessee.

The Body Farm

As Tennessee's state forensic anthropologist, Bass was frequently consulted by law enforcement on decomposed and skeletal remains. A 1977 case, in which he overestimated the time since death of a Civil War–era corpse misidentified as a recent murder victim, highlighted the lack of scientific data on human decomposition. In 1981 he established the Anthropology Research Facility, a fenced outdoor laboratory where donated bodies decompose under varied conditions so that researchers can document the stages of decay, insect activity, and environmental effects. The facility's research transformed the estimation of time since death and trained generations of forensic scientists; similar facilities now exist elsewhere in the United States and abroad.

Writing

With journalist Jon Jefferson, Bass wrote the memoir Death's Acre (2003) and the popular "Body Farm" series of crime novels under the pen name Jefferson Bass. He also authored the widely used osteology textbook Human Osteology: A Laboratory and Field Manual.
