Panspermia is the hypothesis that life exists throughout the universe and is distributed by space dust, meteoroids, asteroids, comets, planetoids and — deliberately or accidentally — by spacecraft. In its classic form it proposes that microscopic life can survive the effects of space travel and atmospheric entry, seeding habitable planets.

History

The idea dates to the 5th-century BC philosopher Anaxagoras, who spoke of "seeds" of life everywhere. It took scientific form in the late 19th and early 20th centuries with Svante Arrhenius, who calculated that radiation pressure could propel bacterial spores between star systems. Fred Hoyle and Chandra Wickramasinghe later championed a cometary version, controversially suggesting that organic material — and even diseases — arrive from space.

Evidence and plausibility

Experiments have shown that bacterial spores, lichens and tardigrades can survive exposure to the vacuum and radiation of low Earth orbit for extended periods, and that microbes can endure the shock pressures of impact ejection from a planet. Interplanetary transfer within the Solar System ("lithopanspermia") is considered plausible, particularly between Earth and Mars, since Martian meteorites are found on Earth. However, there is no confirmed evidence that terrestrial life originated elsewhere; panspermia relocates, rather than solves, the problem of abiogenesis, and its more ambitious interstellar claims remain speculative.

Directed panspermia

Francis Crick and Leslie Orgel proposed in 1973 that an advanced civilisation might deliberately seed other worlds. Separately, planetary-protection scientists note that human spacecraft may already be engaging in accidental panspermia within the Solar System.
