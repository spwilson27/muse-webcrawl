The Frederick C. Robie House is a U.S. National Historic Landmark in the Hyde Park neighbourhood of Chicago, Illinois. Designed by architect Frank Lloyd Wright in 1908 and built in 1909–1910 for bicycle and automobile parts manufacturer Frederick Carlton Robie, it is considered the masterpiece of Wright's Prairie School period and one of the most important works of American residential architecture. Its sweeping horizontal lines, dramatically cantilevered roofs and open interior plan were intended to evoke the flat landscape of the Midwestern prairie.

Design

The house is organised around a long, narrow corner lot. Two great roof planes, cantilevered on steel beams as much as twenty feet beyond their supports, shelter bands of art-glass windows and doors whose abstract designs were executed by the Linden Glass Company. Roman brick laid with raked horizontal mortar joints emphasises the horizontal. Inside, the living and dining rooms form a single open space divided only by the central chimney mass, with built-in furniture, a prow-like terrace at each end, and — unusual for 1910 — an attached three-car garage; Robie, an automobile enthusiast, also had a pit for mechanic's work. Bedrooms occupy the upper floor, with servants' rooms and a billiard room and playroom elsewhere in the plan.

History

The Robie family lived in the house only briefly; financial difficulties and marital breakdown led to its sale in 1911, and it passed through several owners. The Chicago Theological Seminary acquired it in 1926 for dormitory use and twice planned its demolition (in 1941 and 1957), provoking protests in which the elderly Wright himself participated; in 1957 he called it "a cornerstone of modern architecture". Developer William Zeckendorf acquired and donated it to the University of Chicago in 1963. It was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1963 and placed on the National Register in 1966. The Frank Lloyd Wright Trust has operated it as a museum under lease since 1997 and completed a comprehensive restoration in 2019, when the house was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of the serial listing of Wright's twentieth-century architecture.
