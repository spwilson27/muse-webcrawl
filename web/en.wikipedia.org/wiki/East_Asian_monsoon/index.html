East Asian monsoon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The summer monsoon rain belt migrates north across East Asia through the season

The East Asian monsoon is a monsoon system affecting large parts of East and Southeast Asia, carrying moist air from the Indian Ocean and Pacific Ocean to China, Korea and Japan. It is driven by the seasonal temperature contrast between the Asian landmass and the surrounding oceans, combined with the heating of the Tibetan Plateau.

Summer and winter monsoons

In summer, warm moist southerly and southeasterly flow brings the rainy season, which advances northward in distinct stages. The stationary front associated with the rains is known as meiyu in China, baiu in Japan and jangma in Korea. Roughly a third to half of annual precipitation in the region falls during this phase, and its timing strongly affects rice agriculture as well as flooding. In winter the system reverses: cold, dry northwesterly winds pour out of the Siberian High, bringing sharp cold spells and heavy snowfall on the windward coasts of Japan (its "snow country") where the air picks up moisture crossing the Sea of Japan.

Variability

The strength and position of the monsoon vary from year to year, influenced by El Niño–Southern Oscillation, snow cover over Eurasia, and sea-surface temperatures. Weak or stalled fronts are associated with drought in northern China, while slow-moving fronts produce destructive floods in the Yangtze basin, as in 1998 and 2020. Palaeoclimate records from cave speleothems show the monsoon weakening during cold periods such as Heinrich events.
