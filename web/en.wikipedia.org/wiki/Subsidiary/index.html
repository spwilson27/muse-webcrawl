Subsidiary

A subsidiary, subsidiary company, or daughter company is a company owned or controlled by another company, known as the parent company or holding company. Control generally arises when the parent owns more than 50 percent of the subsidiary's voting stock, though effective control can exist with a smaller stake. A subsidiary is a separate legal entity from its parent, with its own management, assets, liabilities, and tax identity.

Control and ownership

A subsidiary in which the parent owns all of the equity is a wholly owned subsidiary. Subsidiaries may themselves own subsidiaries, forming tiers or chains of control within a corporate group; a company controlled through such a chain is sometimes distinguished as a "sub-subsidiary." Control can also be established contractually or through board representation, and accounting standards such as IFRS 10 define control as the power to direct the relevant activities of the investee together with exposure to variable returns.

Reasons for subsidiaries

Companies organize operations into subsidiaries to isolate risk and liability, to satisfy local legal or regulatory requirements (such as rules requiring locally incorporated banks or insurers), for tax planning, to keep brands and businesses separable for sale or spin-off, and to hold particular assets such as intellectual property or real estate. Multinational groups commonly create subsidiaries in each country of operation.

Accounting and liability

Parents ordinarily prepare consolidated financial statements combining subsidiaries' accounts, eliminating intra-group transactions. Although subsidiaries are legally distinct — creditors of a subsidiary generally cannot reach the parent's assets — courts may in exceptional cases "pierce the corporate veil," and parents may voluntarily guarantee subsidiary obligations. A company in which another holds a substantial but non-controlling stake (typically 20–50 percent) is instead an associate or affiliate.
