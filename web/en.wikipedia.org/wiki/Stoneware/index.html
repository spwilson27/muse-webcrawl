Stoneware

Stoneware is a broad class of pottery fired at high temperatures — typically between about 1,100 °C and 1,300 °C — so that the clay body vitrifies: it becomes hard, dense and non-porous like stone, whether or not it is glazed. Stoneware stands between earthenware, fired lower and left porous, and porcelain, which is fired to translucency from refined white clays.

Properties

A vitrified stoneware body absorbs little or no water, is strong and chip-resistant, and rings when struck. The ware is usually grey, brown or buff depending on the clay and kiln atmosphere; reduction firing produces darker, iron-rich colours. Common surface treatments include salt glazing (in which salt thrown into the hot kiln reacts with the clay to form a pitted, orange-peel glaze), ash glazes, slips and conventional lead-free glazes.

History

High-fired glazed wares developed early in China, where proto-porcelain stonewares were produced by the Shang dynasty and refined under the Han and Song dynasties — celadon and Jian wares are celebrated examples. In Europe, Germany's Rhineland became the great centre of salt-glazed stoneware in the late Middle Ages; grey-brown Rhenish jugs and Bartmann ("bellarmine") bottles were traded across northern Europe and exported to colonial America. In Britain, Staffordshire potters developed white salt-glazed stoneware and later refined stonewares such as jasperware. In Japan, kilns at Bizen, Shigaraki and Echizen produced unglazed wood-fired stoneware prized in the tea ceremony.

Modern use

Because it is durable, impermeable and resistant to thermal shock, stoneware is widely used for tableware, ovenware, chemical and drainage ware, floor tiles and studio pottery. Contemporary studio potters value its robust, earthy character and its response to wood, soda and salt firing.
