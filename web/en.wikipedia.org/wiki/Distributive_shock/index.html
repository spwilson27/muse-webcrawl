Distributive shock

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Pathophysiology of distributive shock

Distributive shock is a medical condition in which abnormal distribution of blood flow in the smallest blood vessels results in inadequate supply of blood to the body's tissues and organs. It is one of four categories of shock, the others being cardiogenic shock, obstructive shock, and hypovolemic shock. Distributive shock is different from the others in the sense that it occurs even though the heart's output may be normal or even higher than normal.

The most common cause is sepsis, which can cause a specific type of distributive shock called septic shock. Distributive shock can also be caused by anaphylaxis, adrenal insufficiency, drug reactions, or damage to the nervous system (neurogenic shock).

Signs and symptoms

Unlike other forms of shock, which typically present with cold, clammy peripheries, distributive shock often presents with warm, flushed skin due to peripheral vasodilation, along with bounding pulses, low blood pressure, and a widened pulse pressure. As the condition progresses, inadequate tissue perfusion leads to lactic acidosis, organ dysfunction, and altered mental status.

Treatment

Treatment focuses on identifying and reversing the underlying cause, aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation, and vasopressor medications such as norepinephrine to restore vascular tone. In septic shock, early broad-spectrum antibiotics and source control are essential; in anaphylaxis, intramuscular epinephrine is the first-line treatment.
