Mutual intelligibility

In linguistics, mutual intelligibility is a relationship between languages or dialects in which speakers of different but related varieties can readily understand each other without prior study or special effort. It is sometimes used as a criterion for distinguishing languages from dialects, although sociolinguistic factors are often decisive.

FieldLinguistics, sociolinguistics

Intelligibility and dialect continua

Mutual intelligibility exists on a spectrum. In a dialect continuum, neighbouring varieties are intelligible but distant ones may not be. It can be asymmetric: Portuguese speakers often understand Spanish better than vice versa, attributed to differences in phonology. Danish, Norwegian and Swedish are largely mutually intelligible in writing, less so in speech.

Examples

  • Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian and Montenegrin — based on Shtokavian, highly mutually intelligible, treated as separate standard languages largely for political reasons.
  • Hindi and Urdu — colloquially very close (Hindustani), diverging in script and formal vocabulary.
  • Czech and Slovak; Ukrainian, Belarusian and Russian to varying degrees.
  • Malay and Indonesian; Bulgarian and Macedonian.

Problems with the criterion

Intelligibility depends on exposure, education, attitude and topic, and is not binary. Chinese varieties such as Mandarin and Cantonese are called dialects despite low spoken intelligibility, while Scandinavian varieties are called languages despite high intelligibility — showing politics and culture shape the language/dialect distinction. Max Weinreich popularised the quip that 'a language is a dialect with an army and navy'.
