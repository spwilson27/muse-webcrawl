Parliamentary sovereignty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Parliamentary sovereignty is a principle of the British constitution holding that Parliament — the Crown-in-Parliament — is the supreme legal authority, able to make or unmake any law, and that no court or other body may override its legislation. A. V. Dicey's formulation (1885) adds the negative limb: no Parliament may bind its successors. The doctrine underlies the United Kingdom's uncodified constitution and was exported, in varying forms, to Commonwealth systems: New Zealand retains a strongly sovereign parliament, while Australia and Canada qualify sovereignty by federal written constitutions.

The doctrine has been qualified in practice without being formally abandoned: membership of the European Union (1973–2020) meant EU law prevailed over inconsistent statutes (Factortame); the Human Rights Act 1998 requires courts to interpret legislation compatibly with Convention rights and empowers declarations of incompatibility (though not invalidation); devolution distributes legislative power politically while Westminster legally retains it. Judges in Jackson v Attorney General (2005) speculated in obiter that there might be limits to what Parliament could lawfully do — a suggestion orthodox Diceyans reject.
