Outliers: The Story of Success is a non-fiction book by Malcolm Gladwell, first published by Little, Brown and Company on 18 November 2008. In Outliers, Gladwell examines the factors that contribute to high levels of success. He argues that success arises less from innate talent alone than from a combination of opportunity, cultural background, timing, and sustained effort — that exceptional people are "outliers" produced by exceptional circumstances.

Contents

The book opens with the "Matthew effect" (from the sociologist Robert K. Merton): advantages accumulate, illustrated by the skewed birth months of elite Canadian ice hockey players, whose January–March birthdays made them the oldest in youth leagues. Gladwell popularised the "10,000-hour rule", drawing on K. Anders Ericsson's research on deliberate practice, to explain the achievements of Bill Joy, Bill Gates, and the Beatles. Later chapters attribute the success of software entrepreneurs to being born at the right moment (1954–55) and of Jewish lawyers in New York to their parents' garment-trade background; discuss how "cultural legacies" — from honour cultures to power-distance in cockpit communication (Korean Air crashes) — shape outcomes; and use the KIPP academy and the Roseto effect to show how environment and community affect achievement and health.

Reception

Outliers debuted at number one on the New York Times bestseller list and was a major commercial success, but it drew criticism from academics for oversimplification and selective evidence. Ericsson himself objected that the "10,000-hour rule" distorts his research: the figure was an average from one violin study, and deliberate practice alone does not determine expertise. Reviewers also questioned the hockey birth-date effect's generality and the anecdotal treatment of cultural legacy. The book nonetheless had a wide influence on popular discussion of meritocracy and opportunity.
