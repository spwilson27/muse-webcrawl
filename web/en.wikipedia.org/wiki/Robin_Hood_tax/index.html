Robin Hood tax

The Robin Hood tax is a package of financial transaction taxes (FTTs) proposed as a way of raising revenue from the financial sector for public spending, poverty reduction and action on climate change. Named after the legendary outlaw Robin Hood, who was said to rob the rich to give to the poor, the idea is a modern version of the Tobin tax on currency trading.

Proposals

Typical proposals levy a very small rate — for example, around 0.05% — on trades in shares, bonds, currencies and derivatives. Supporters argue that because trading volumes are enormous, even tiny rates could raise tens or hundreds of billions per year, while discouraging short-term speculation. A major campaign, launched in the United Kingdom in 2010 by a coalition of charities, unions and faith groups, promoted the tax internationally.

Debate

The European Union proposed an FTT in 2011; after failing to win unanimous support, a group of member states pursued "enhanced cooperation" from 2013, though implementation repeatedly stalled. Critics, including many in the financial industry, argue the tax would raise costs for ordinary savers, push trading to other jurisdictions, and may not curb volatility. Some countries, such as France and Italy, have introduced narrower national transaction taxes.
