Five-prime cap

In molecular biology, the five-prime cap (5′ cap) is a specially altered nucleotide on the 5′ end of some primary transcripts such as precursor messenger RNA. In eukaryotes the 5′ cap consists of a guanine nucleotide connected to mRNA via an unusual 5′ to 5′ triphosphate linkage, further methylated to form 7-methylguanylate.

Functions

The cap protects the mRNA from degradation by 5′ exonucleases, promotes translation by recruiting the cap-binding complex and the eIF4F initiation machinery, aids in nuclear export, and helps the cell distinguish self mRNA from foreign RNA in innate immunity. Capping occurs co-transcriptionally in the nucleus through the sequential action of RNA triphosphatase, guanylyltransferase, and methyltransferase enzymes.

Decapping and removal

Removal of the cap by decapping enzymes such as Dcp2 is a key step in mRNA turnover. Some viruses encode their own capping machinery, and influenza virus famously "snatches" caps from host mRNAs to prime its own transcription.
