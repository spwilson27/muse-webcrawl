Psoas major muscle

Psoas major
Origin: Transverse processes and bodies of T12–L5 vertebrae
Insertion: Lesser trochanter of femur
Nerve: Lumbar plexus (L1–L3)
Actions: Flexion of the hip and trunk

The psoas major (from Greek psóā, "muscles of the loins") is a long fusiform muscle located in the lower lumbar region of the trunk, lateral to the lumbar vertebrae. It is the larger of the two psoas muscles and is a primary connector between the spine and the lower limb.

Structure

The psoas major arises from the transverse processes, vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs of the twelfth thoracic through fifth lumbar vertebrae. It descends along the brim of the pelvis, passes beneath the inguinal ligament, and joins the tendon of the iliacus muscle to insert on the lesser trochanter of the femur. The combined muscle is commonly called the iliopsoas, the body's strongest hip flexor. A smaller companion muscle, the psoas minor, is present in only about half of people. In quadruped butchery, the corresponding cut is the tenderloin (filet mignon in beef).

Function

Acting from its origin, the psoas major flexes the thigh at the hip; acting from its insertion, it flexes the trunk — as in sitting up — and helps stabilise the lumbar spine and maintain posture during walking and standing.

Clinical significance

The muscle's sheath can harbour a psoas abscess, classically a complication of spinal tuberculosis (Pott's disease) tracking down from the vertebrae, and today more often associated with gastrointestinal or urinary infections. Psoas irritation is tested by the psoas sign in suspected appendicitis. Tightness or spasm of the iliopsoas is implicated in some cases of lower-back and hip pain.
