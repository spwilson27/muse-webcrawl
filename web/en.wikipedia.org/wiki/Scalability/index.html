Scalability

Scalability is the property of a system to handle a growing amount of work, or its potential to be enlarged to accommodate that growth. In computing, a scalable system can increase its total throughput under an increased load when resources (typically hardware) are added. The term also applies in business, economics, networks and other domains where a model or process must keep working as size or volume changes.

Vertical and horizontal scaling

Vertical scaling ("scaling up") adds more resources — a faster CPU, more memory — to a single node. Horizontal scaling ("scaling out") adds more nodes to a system, distributing the work among them, as in a cluster of servers behind a load balancer. Horizontal scaling avoids single-node limits but introduces problems of data consistency, coordination and communication overhead. Amdahl's law bounds the speedup obtainable from parallelism by the fraction of work that must remain serial.

Dimensions

Scalability is discussed along several dimensions: load scalability (handling more concurrent users or transactions), geographic scalability (keeping performance acceptable as distance grows), administrative scalability (remaining manageable as the number of organizations involved grows), and functional scalability (adding new features without disruption). Bottlenecks in a system's design determine where scaling first fails, and designing for scalability from the outset is a central concern of distributed systems engineering.
