H-index

H-index
The h-index is the largest h such that h publications have at least h citations each
Proposed byJorge E. Hirsch, 2005
FieldBibliometrics, scientometrics

The h-index is an author-level metric that measures both the productivity and citation impact of the publications of a scientist or scholar. It was suggested in 2005 by Jorge E. Hirsch, a physicist at the University of California, San Diego, as a tool to determine the relative quality of theoretical physicists' work.

Definition

An author has an h-index of h if h of their papers have at least h citations each, and the other papers have no more than h citations each. In other words, the index is the maximum value of h such that the author has published at least h papers, each of which has been cited at least h times. The index is designed to balance the effects of highly cited papers and large numbers of papers, so that neither a single influential paper nor a long list of rarely cited papers can dominate it.

Calculation

The h-index can be calculated manually by ranking an author's publications by their citation counts and finding the largest rank at which the citation count meets or exceeds the rank. Citation databases such as Web of Science, Scopus and Google Scholar compute the index automatically, though values differ between databases because their coverage of publications and citations differs.

Criticism

The h-index has been criticised on several grounds. It depends on career length, favouring senior researchers; it is not field-normalised, so typical values differ greatly between disciplines; it ignores the number of authors on a paper and their order; and it never decreases, even if a researcher stops publishing. Hirsch himself noted the index should be only one measure among many in evaluating researchers. Variants such as the g-index, which gives more weight to highly cited papers, have been proposed to address some of these shortcomings.
