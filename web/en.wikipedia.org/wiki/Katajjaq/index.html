Katajjaq (Inuktitut: ᑲᑕᔾᔭᖅ), also known as Inuit throat singing, is a traditional form of vocal music of the Inuit of the Canadian Arctic. It is usually performed by two women standing face to face, holding each other's arms, who produce rhythmic, intertwined sounds using inhaled and exhaled breath, in a friendly contest that ends when one singer runs out of breath or laughs.

Performance

The singers use each other's mouth cavity as a resonator and match motifs built from voiced and unvoiced sounds, sometimes imitating natural sounds such as wind, water, geese or a saw cutting wood. Performances have a strong percussive, breathing rhythm. Although often described as a game, katajjaq is also entertainment and social bonding, historically performed by women while men were away hunting.

History and revival

Throat singing was discouraged or banned in many communities by Christian missionaries in the 20th century, and the practice declined. From the 1970s a cultural revival restored it, and it is now taught to young Inuit performers and heard at festivals and ceremonies. In 2014 Quebec formally recognised katajjaq as part of the province's intangible cultural heritage — the first such designation. Related but distinct throat-singing traditions exist among the Inuit of Greenland and Chukotka, and must not be confused with the overtone singing (khöömei) of Tuva and Mongolia.

Contemporary artists such as Tanya Tagaq have drawn on katajjaq techniques while developing their own experimental styles.
