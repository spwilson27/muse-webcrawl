Home Rule Crisis

Date1912–1914
LocationIreland and Great Britain
CauseThird Home Rule Bill for Ireland
OutcomeBill enacted but suspended on outbreak of the First World War; Irish partition followed

The Home Rule Crisis was a political and military crisis in the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland that followed the introduction of the Third Home Rule Bill in 1912. The bill proposed a devolved Irish parliament in Dublin. It provoked fierce resistance from Irish unionists, concentrated in the northern province of Ulster, and raised the prospect of civil war in Ireland on the eve of the First World War.

Background

Irish nationalists had sought Home Rule since the 1870s. Gladstone's bills of 1886 and 1893 failed, but the Parliament Act 1911 removed the House of Lords' power of absolute veto, clearing the way for H. H. Asquith's Liberal government — dependent on John Redmond's Irish Parliamentary Party — to introduce a third bill in April 1912. It was expected to pass into law by 1914.

Ulster resistance

Led by Sir Edward Carson and James Craig, Ulster unionists organized mass opposition. On Ulster Day, 28 September 1912, nearly half a million men and women signed the Ulster Covenant and Declaration pledging to resist Home Rule. In January 1913 the Ulster Volunteer Force (UVF) was formed, eventually numbering around 100,000 men. Nationalists responded by founding the Irish Volunteers in November 1913.

Curragh incident and Larne gun-running

In March 1914, in the Curragh incident, British Army officers indicated they would resign rather than move against the Ulster unionists, alarming the government. The following month the UVF landed some 25,000 rifles and millions of rounds of ammunition at Larne in a major gun-running operation that went unimpeded, while a smaller nationalist gun-running at Howth in July was met with force. Ireland appeared to be drifting toward civil war, and talks at the Buckingham Palace Conference in July 1914 failed to settle the question of excluding Ulster counties.

Outcome

The outbreak of the First World War in August 1914 averted the immediate crisis. The Government of Ireland Act 1914 received royal assent in September but was suspended for the duration of the war. The political landscape was then transformed by the Easter Rising of 1916 and the rise of Sinn Féin, and Ireland was ultimately partitioned under the Government of Ireland Act 1920.
