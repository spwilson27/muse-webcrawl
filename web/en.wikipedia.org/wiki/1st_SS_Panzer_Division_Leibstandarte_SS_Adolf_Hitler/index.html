The 1st SS Panzer Division "Leibstandarte SS Adolf Hitler" (often abbreviated LSSAH) began as Adolf Hitler's personal bodyguard unit in 1933 and grew into an elite Waffen-SS armoured division that fought on both the Eastern and Western Fronts during the Second World War. Members of the division committed numerous war crimes, and the unit's personnel were implicated in massacres of prisoners of war and civilians.

Formation and pre-war years

The unit originated in March 1933 as the SS-Stabswache Berlin, commanded by Josef "Sepp" Dietrich, and was expanded and renamed the Leibstandarte — a term recalling the bodyguard regiments of the old Prussian army. It provided ceremonial guards at the Reich Chancellery, took part in the Night of the Long Knives purge in 1934, and participated in the remilitarisation of the Rhineland, the Anschluss of Austria, and the occupation of the Sudetenland and Czechoslovakia.

Second World War

Motorised for the invasion of Poland in 1939, the regiment fought in the Netherlands and France in 1940 — where its troops murdered British prisoners at Wormhoudt — and in the Balkans and the Soviet Union in 1941. Expanded to a panzergrenadier and then a panzer division, it fought in the Third Battle of Kharkov in 1943 (where its troops massacred wounded Soviet soldiers and civilians at Yefremovka), at Kursk, in Italy, and after refitting, in Normandy in 1944. During the Ardennes offensive of December 1944, its Kampfgruppe Peiper murdered American prisoners at Malmedy and Belgian civilians along its route. The division ended the war in Austria, surrendering to US forces in May 1945.

Criminal record and legacy

At the Nuremberg trials the Waffen-SS was declared a criminal organisation, and members of the division were tried for the Wormhoudt and Malmedy massacres, among other crimes. The LSSAH is frequently cited in historical scholarship as exemplifying the fusion of military, ideological, and criminal functions in the Waffen-SS.
