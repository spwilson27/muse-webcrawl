Karl Waldemar Ziegler (26 November 1898 – 11 August 1973) was a German chemist who won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1963, with Giulio Natta, "for their discoveries in the field of the chemistry and technology of high polymers." His discovery of organoaluminium–titanium catalysts for polymerising ethylene and propylene at low pressure transformed the plastics industry.

Early life and career

Ziegler was born in Helsa, near Kassel, and studied chemistry at the University of Marburg, receiving his doctorate in 1923. He held posts at Frankfurt, Heidelberg, and Halle before becoming director of the Kaiser Wilhelm (later Max Planck) Institute for Coal Research at Mülheim an der Ruhr in 1943, a position he held until 1969.

Work

Ziegler's research covered free radicals, organoaluminium compounds, and the synthesis of ring compounds. His study of aluminium alkyls led to processes for long-chain alcohols used in detergents. In 1953 his group found that mixtures of titanium compounds with aluminium alkyls polymerised ethylene at atmospheric pressure to give high-density polyethylene with unprecedented regularity — the Ziegler catalysts. Giulio Natta extended the system to propylene, producing isotactic polypropylene.

Legacy

Ziegler–Natta catalysis underpins the production of tens of millions of tonnes of polyolefins annually. Ziegler used his substantial patent income to endow research funding; the Karl Ziegler Foundation administers prizes in his name.
