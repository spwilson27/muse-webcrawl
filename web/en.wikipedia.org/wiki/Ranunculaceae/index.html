Ranunculaceae (the buttercup or crowfoot family) is a family of flowering plants comprising about 62 genera and roughly 2,500 known species. It is one of the most widespread families of the order Ranunculales, occurring on every continent except Antarctica, and is especially diverse in temperate and montane regions of the Northern Hemisphere. Members include familiar garden and wild plants such as buttercups (Ranunculus), anemones, columbines (Aquilegia), larkspurs and delphiniums, clematis, hellebores, and monkshood (Aconitum).

Characteristics

Most ranunculaceae are herbaceous perennials, though the family also includes annuals, biennials, and woody climbers such as Clematis. Leaves are typically alternate and often divided or lobed, without stipules. The flowers are usually radially symmetrical (actinomorphic), although genera such as Aconitum and Delphinium have strongly bilateral (zygomorphic) flowers adapted to specialised pollinators. A hallmark of the family is the indefinite number of free floral parts: sepals, petals, stamens and carpels are commonly numerous and spirally arranged on an elongated receptacle, and the many separate carpels develop into a cluster of achenes or follicles.

Distribution and ecology

The family is cosmopolitan but reaches its greatest diversity in temperate Eurasia and North America. Species occupy habitats from alpine tundra and wet meadows to woodlands and marshes. Many are early-spring ephemerals of deciduous forests, flowering before the canopy closes. Several genera, including Ranunculus, have aquatic members with finely dissected submerged leaves.

Chemistry and toxicity

Ranunculaceae are notable for potent secondary compounds. Many species produce ranunculin, which breaks down to the blistering toxin protoanemonin when tissue is damaged, making fresh buttercups acrid and poisonous to livestock. Larkspurs and monkshoods contain diterpenoid alkaloids such as aconitine, among the most powerful plant toxins known, and have a long history both as arrow poisons and in traditional medicine.

Genera

Well-known genera include Ranunculus (buttercups), Anemone, Aquilegia (columbines), Clematis, Delphinium, Aconitum (monkshood), Helleborus, Nigella, Thalictrum (meadow-rues), Caltha (marsh marigolds) and Actaea (baneberries and black cohosh).

Uses

Ornamental horticulture draws heavily on the family: delphiniums, clematis, anemones and hellebores are staples of temperate gardens. Nigella sativa seeds (black cumin) are used as a spice, and several species feature in herbal traditions, although their toxicity demands caution.
