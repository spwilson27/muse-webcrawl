Abyssal plain

An abyssal plain is an underwater plain on the deep ocean floor, usually found at depths between 3,000 and 6,000 metres (10,000–20,000 ft). Lying beyond the continental rise, abyssal plains are among the flattest, smoothest and least explored regions on Earth, and together they cover roughly half of the planet's surface — more than all the continents' land area above sea level in extent of flat terrain.

Formation

Abyssal plains form where turbidity currents and continuous pelagic sedimentation drape and bury the originally rugged topography of oceanic crust created at mid-ocean ridges. Fine sediments — clays, silts and the shells of plankton — accumulate at rates of millimetres to centimetres per thousand years, smoothing abyssal hills into plains hundreds to thousands of kilometres across. The Sohm Abyssal Plain in the North Atlantic and the plains of the Indian Ocean are typical examples; the Pacific, ringed by trenches that trap sediment, has fewer and smaller plains.

Life and resources

Despite darkness, near-freezing temperatures and immense pressure, abyssal plains support specialised fauna: deposit-feeding holothurians, brittle stars, xenophyophores and microbial communities, fed by a slow rain of detritus ("marine snow") from surface waters. The plains are scattered with polymetallic (manganese) nodules — concretions of manganese, nickel, copper and cobalt — whose potential deep-sea mining is the subject of scientific study and environmental controversy, notably in the Pacific's Clarion–Clipperton Zone.
