Abraha

Abraha (Ge'ez: አብርሃ; Arabic: أبرهة), also known as Abraha al-Ashram, was a ruler of the Aksumite kingdom in South Arabia (present-day Yemen) in the mid-6th century CE. Originally a general — in some accounts a former slave — sent with the Aksumite expedition that conquered the Himyarite kingdom around 525, he seized power for himself and ruled Yemen as a largely independent Christian king until about 553–570.

Rise to power

After the Aksumite king Kaleb's forces overthrew the Himyarite king Dhu Nuwas, who had persecuted Christians, a governor named Sumyafa Ashwa was installed. Abraha, commanding Aksumite troops in the region, rebelled and defeated Sumyafa. Kaleb sent further expeditions against him, but they failed or defected, and Kaleb (or his successor) eventually recognised Abraha's rule in exchange for tribute.

Rule

Abraha is known from several inscriptions, including a long text at the Marib Dam recording repairs to the dam around 548–549, receptions of embassies from Aksum, Byzantium, Persia and Arab principalities, and campaigns against rebellious tribes. He built a celebrated cathedral, al-Qalis, in Sana'a, which Islamic tradition says was intended to rival the Kaaba as a pilgrimage destination.

The Year of the Elephant

In Islamic tradition, Abraha marched on Mecca with an army that included war elephants to destroy the Kaaba, but the expedition failed — an event associated with the "Year of the Elephant," conventionally dated to about 570, the year of Muhammad's birth, and commemorated in Surah Al-Fil of the Quran. Historians debate the date and details of the campaign; Abraha's inscriptions record a major expedition into central Arabia (Murayghan inscription, 552) but do not mention Mecca.
