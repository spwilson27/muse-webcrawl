Public key infrastructure

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A public key infrastructure (PKI) is a set of roles, policies, hardware, software and procedures needed to create, manage, distribute, use, store and revoke digital certificates and manage public-key encryption. Its purpose is to facilitate the secure electronic transfer of information by binding public keys to identities (such as people, devices, or services) through a certificate authority (CA).

Components

  • Certificate authority (CA) — issues and signs digital certificates attesting that a public key belongs to a named subject.
  • Registration authority (RA) — verifies the identity of entities requesting certificates.
  • Certificate repository — stores certificates and certificate revocation lists (CRLs); revocation status may also be queried via OCSP.
  • End entities — the users, servers, and devices that hold key pairs and certificates, most commonly in X.509 format.

Trust models

In the hierarchical model used for TLS on the web, trust flows from pre-installed root CAs through intermediate CAs to end-entity certificates. Alternative models include the web of trust popularized by PGP, bridge CAs, and distributed/trust-list approaches. Browser and operating-system vendors maintain root programs that decide which CAs are trusted.

Uses

PKI underlies HTTPS/TLS server authentication, S/MIME email signing and encryption, code signing, smart-card logon, document signing, and device identity in enterprise and IoT networks. Mismanagement or compromise of a CA — such as the 2011 DigiNotar breach — can undermine trust for large numbers of relying parties, which has motivated transparency mechanisms such as Certificate Transparency logs.
