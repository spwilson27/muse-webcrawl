Salt lake

A salt lake or saline lake is a landlocked body of water that has a concentration of salts (typically sodium chloride) and other dissolved minerals significantly higher than most lakes (often defined as at least three grams of salt per litre). In many cases, salt lakes have a higher concentration of salt than sea water; such lakes can also be termed hypersaline lakes, and may be pink lakes due to the presence of pigmented algae and bacteria.

Formation

Salt lakes form when the water flowing into a lake, carrying dissolved salts leached from rocks and soils, is unable to leave the basin through an outlet. Water escapes only by evaporation, which concentrates the salts over time. They therefore occur mainly in endorheic (closed) basins in arid or semi-arid climates.

Notable examples

By volume, the largest salt lake in the world is the Caspian Sea. The Dead Sea, between Jordan and Israel, is among the most saline bodies of water on Earth and lies at the lowest elevation of any lake. The Great Salt Lake of Utah is the largest salt lake in the Western Hemisphere. Other well-known examples include Lake Assal in Djibouti, the Salar de Uyuni in Bolivia (a salt flat flooded seasonally), and Lake Eyre in Australia.

Ecology and uses

High salinity excludes most fish, but hypersaline waters support specialised life: brine shrimp (Artemia), which feed vast flocks of flamingos, and extremophile microorganisms such as Dunaliella algae and haloarchaea that tint water pink or red. Salt lakes are mined for salt, potash, lithium, and other minerals, and several are important tourist and therapeutic sites because the dense brine makes swimmers unusually buoyant.
