St. Bartholomew's Day massacre

The St. Bartholomew's Day massacre (French: Massacre de la Saint-Barthélemy) was a targeted series of assassinations and Catholic mob violence against the Huguenots (French Calvinist Protestants) that began in Paris on the night of 23–24 August 1572, the eve of the feast of Saint Bartholomew. The killings spread to the provinces and lasted for weeks. Estimates of the dead range from about 5,000 to 30,000 across France, including some 2,000–3,000 in Paris alone.

Background

The massacre occurred during the French Wars of Religion, a series of civil conflicts between Catholics and Huguenots. The Peace of Saint-Germain-en-Laye (1570) had ended the third war on terms relatively favorable to the Protestants. In August 1572, thousands of Huguenot nobles gathered in Paris for the wedding of the Protestant Henry of Navarre (the future Henry IV) to Margaret of Valois, sister of King Charles IX — a marriage intended to reconcile the factions.

The assassination of Coligny and escalation

On 22 August, an assassin shot and wounded Admiral Gaspard de Coligny, the most prominent Huguenot leader, who had great influence over the young king. Fearing a Huguenot coup in retaliation, Charles IX and his mother, Catherine de' Medici, ordered the pre-emptive killing of Huguenot leaders. On the night of 23–24 August, royal soldiers murdered Coligny and other Protestant nobles. The targeted killings then ignited popular violence: Catholic mobs, inflamed by years of religious tension and economic grievance, slaughtered Protestant men, women, and children throughout Paris, looting and mutilating bodies, many of which were thrown into the Seine.

Spread to the provinces

News of the Paris killings triggered massacres in provincial cities including Orléans, Lyon, Rouen, Bordeaux, Toulouse, and Bourges over the following weeks, despite royal letters ordering the peace to be kept. Many Protestants fled France for England, the Dutch Republic, Switzerland, and German states, contributing to the growth of Huguenot diaspora communities.

Reactions and legacy

Protestant Europe reacted with horror; the massacre became a symbol of Catholic fanaticism and tyranny, and it shaped political theory — monarchomach writers used it to argue for a right of resistance against unjust rulers. Pope Gregory XIII, by contrast, celebrated the event, ordering a Te Deum and having a medal struck, although the papacy had not ordered the killings. The massacre destroyed the policy of reconciliation, drove the Huguenots toward a more radical and organized resistance, and prolonged the Wars of Religion. Henry of Navarre survived by converting to Catholicism (he later abjured and converted again), and ultimately issued the Edict of Nantes in 1598.
