Turkish alphabet

The Turkish alphabet (Turkish: Türk alfabesi) is a Latin-script alphabet used for writing the Turkish language, consisting of 29 letters. It was adopted in 1928 as part of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk's reforms, replacing the Ottoman Turkish alphabet based on Arabic script. The alphabet is notable for its close one-to-one correspondence between letters and sounds.

Letters

Lowercaseabcçdefgğhıijklmnoöprsştuüvyz

The letters Q, W, and X are absent. Distinctive features include the dotted İ / i and dotless I / ı, which are separate letters representing different vowels — a feature that famously complicates case conversion in computing (the "Turkish I problem"). The letter Ğ (soft g) lengthens the preceding vowel rather than being pronounced itself. Ç, Ş, Ö, and Ü represent sounds similar to their German or French counterparts.

History

The Law on the Adoption and Implementation of the Turkish Alphabet was passed on 1 November 1928, and the new script came into force on 1 January 1929. "Nation's Schools" (Millet Mektepleri) were opened across the country to teach the new letters, with Atatürk himself touring as "head teacher". Literacy rates rose substantially in the following decades.
