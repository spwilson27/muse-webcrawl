Triangulation (geometry)

FieldGeometry, computational geometry
Related conceptsPolygon triangulation, Delaunay triangulation, mesh generation

In geometry, a triangulation is a subdivision of a geometric object into triangles. Most typically, the object is a region of the plane — a polygon, a set of points, or a surface — and the subdivision covers it exactly with triangles whose interiors are disjoint and which meet only along shared edges or vertices.

Triangulations are fundamental in computational geometry, computer graphics, finite element analysis, and surveying, because triangles are the simplest polygons: any three non-collinear points determine exactly one triangle, and quantities such as area and interpolation are easy to compute on them.

Polygon triangulation

A polygon triangulation subdivides a polygon into triangles by adding non-crossing diagonals between its vertices. A classical result states that every simple polygon with n vertices has a triangulation, and every triangulation of it uses exactly n − 2 triangles and n − 3 diagonals. This can be proved by induction using the "two ears theorem": every simple polygon with more than three vertices has at least two ears — vertices at which the diagonal to the neighbouring vertices lies inside the polygon.

Triangulations underlie the art gallery theorem: any simple polygon with n vertices can be guarded by at most ⌊n/3⌋ guards (or cameras placed at vertices), a bound proved by Václav Chvátal and tight in the worst case.

Point set triangulation

Given a finite set of points in the plane, a triangulation is a maximal straight-line planar subdivision: points are connected by non-crossing segments until no further segment can be added, at which point every bounded face is a triangle. Any triangulation of a point set with n points, of which h lie on the convex hull, contains exactly 2n − 2 − h triangles.

Among all possible triangulations of a point set, the Delaunay triangulation is distinguished by maximising the minimum angle, which tends to avoid long, thin "sliver" triangles; it is dual to the Voronoi diagram of the points. Other criteria include minimum-weight triangulation, which minimises the total edge length and is known to be NP-hard in general settings.

Triangulation of surfaces

In topology, a triangulation of a surface or higher-dimensional manifold is a decomposition into simplices (triangles in dimension two, tetrahedra in dimension three) glued along faces. Triangulations allow topological invariants such as the Euler characteristic to be computed combinatorially, by counting vertices, edges, and faces:

χ = V − E + F

The existence of triangulations for smooth manifolds is a classical theorem; every compact surface admits a triangulation, and surfaces are classified up to homeomorphism by their Euler characteristic and orientability.

Applications

  • Finite element methods: domains are meshed into triangles so that partial differential equations can be solved approximately.
  • Computer graphics: surfaces of three-dimensional models are stored as triangle meshes for rendering.
  • Surveying and geodesy: positions are determined by measuring angles in a network of triangles from a known baseline (see triangulation in surveying).
  • Terrain modelling: triangulated irregular networks (TINs) approximate landscapes from elevation samples.
