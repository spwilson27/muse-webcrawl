In music, a sixteenth note (American) or semiquaver (British) is a note played for half the duration of an eighth note (quaver), hence the names. It is the equivalent of the semifusa in mensural notation, first found in 15th-century notation.

Notation

Sixteenth notes are notated with an oval, filled-in note head, a straight note stem, and two flags or beams. A corresponding symbol is the sixteenth rest (or semiquaver rest), which denotes a silence for the same duration. As with all notes with stems, sixteenth notes are drawn with stems to the right of the note head, facing up, when they are below the middle line of the staff, and with stems to the left, facing down, when they are on or above the middle line. When multiple sixteenth notes appear together, they may be connected with two thick beams, and groups of sixteenth notes are often beamed in groups corresponding to the beats of the time signature.

Rhythmic value

A single sixteenth note is always stemmed with two flags, while two or more are usually beamed in groups. Four sixteenth notes occupy the same amount of time as one quarter note, and sixteen sixteenth notes equal one whole note in duration. In time signatures such as 4/4, a beat of one quarter note subdivides naturally into four sixteenth notes, giving them a central role in fast passagework, drum patterns, and virtuosic instrumental writing.
