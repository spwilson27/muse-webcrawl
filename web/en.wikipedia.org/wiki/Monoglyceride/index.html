Monoglyceride

A monoglyceride (also called a monoacylglycerol) is a glyceride consisting of a molecule of glycerol linked to a single fatty acid via an ester bond. Because glycerol has three hydroxyl groups, the fatty acid may be attached at the 1-position or the 2-position, giving 1-monoglycerides and 2-monoglycerides; both occur naturally as intermediates in the digestion of fats, when pancreatic lipase cleaves triglycerides.

Monoglycerides — most commonly mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids together (food additive E471) — are widely used as emulsifiers in processed foods, where they help blend ingredients that would otherwise separate, such as oil and water. They improve texture and shelf life in bread, ice cream, margarine, peanut butter, and whipped toppings. Industrially, they are produced by glycerolysis of triglycerides or by direct esterification of glycerol with fatty acids. 2-Arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG), a monoglyceride, is an endocannabinoid.
