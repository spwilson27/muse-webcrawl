TACAN

The tactical air navigation system, commonly referred to by the acronym TACAN, is a navigation system used by military aircraft. It provides the user with bearing and distance (slant-range) to a ground or ship-borne station. It is a more accurate version of the VOR/DME system that provides bearing and distance information for civil aviation. The DME portion of TACAN is available for civil use; at VORTAC facilities where a VOR is combined with a TACAN, civil aircraft can receive VOR/DME service.

Operation

TACAN operates in the UHF band between 962 and 1213 MHz, divided into 126 channels, each split into X and Y sub-channels. The aircraft interrogates the ground station with pairs of pulses; the station replies after a fixed delay, and the airborne equipment derives slant-range distance from the round-trip time. Bearing is obtained from a rotating directional antenna pattern: the ground beacon transmits reference bursts as its rotating cardioid pattern passes set directions, and the phase of the amplitude modulation (15 Hz and 135 Hz components) at the aircraft gives the bearing to within about one degree.

Aircraft can also operate in air-to-air mode, giving range (though not bearing) relative to another TACAN-equipped aircraft, which is used for formation station-keeping and aerial refuelling rendezvous.

History and use

TACAN was developed in the United States in the 1950s, building on the civil DME concept but moving to UHF so that antennas could be small — suitable for ships and tactical deployable stations — and so that a single compact airborne set could serve both functions. It became the standard tactical navigation aid for NATO naval and military aviation. Although satellite navigation (GPS) has taken over much of its role, TACAN stations remain in widespread use as a ground-based backup and for carrier-based operations, where shipboard TACAN provides the reference for approaches to the moving deck.
