Numerical integration

In numerical analysis, numerical integration comprises a broad family of algorithms for calculating the numerical value of a definite integral, a problem also called numerical quadrature for one-dimensional problems. Numerical methods are used when an integral cannot be evaluated analytically in closed form, or when the integrand is known only at discrete sample points.

Methods

The simplest methods approximate the area under a curve with rectangles (the midpoint and trapezoid rules) or with parabolic segments (Simpson's rule). Newton–Cotes formulas generalise these by interpolating the integrand at equally spaced points, while Gaussian quadrature chooses the sample points optimally to integrate polynomials of high degree exactly. Refinements include Romberg integration, which applies Richardson extrapolation to the trapezoidal rule, adaptive quadrature, which subdivides the interval where the integrand varies rapidly, and Clenshaw–Curtis quadrature. For integrals in many dimensions, Monte Carlo methods, which sample random points, become more efficient than product rules — an issue known as the curse of dimensionality.

Applications

Numerical integration underlies the solution of differential equations (since integrating an ODE over a step is a quadrature problem), probability computations, signal processing, and engineering simulations. Error terms for the standard rules are expressed in terms of derivatives of the integrand and powers of the step size.
