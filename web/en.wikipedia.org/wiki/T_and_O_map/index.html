T and O map

TypeMedieval world map (mappa mundi)
FormCircle (O) divided by a T of waters
ContinentsAsia (top), Europe (lower left), Africa (lower right)
Source textIsidore of Seville, Etymologiae (7th century)
Earliest printed1472 (Augsburg edition of Isidore)

A T and O map or O–T or T-O map (Latin: orbis terrarum, "circle of the lands") is a type of early medieval world map that represents the known world as a circle — the letter O, formed by the encircling ocean — divided into three continents by a T of waterways. It is the classic diagram of the medieval world picture.

Form and meaning

In the diagram the vertical stem of the T is the Mediterranean Sea, separating Europe (lower left) from Africa (lower right); the left arm is the river Don (Tanais), bounding Europe from Asia; the right arm is the Nile, bounding Africa from Asia. Asia — believed to be twice the size of the other two continents combined — occupies the entire upper half. East is at the top. Jerusalem was often placed at the centre in later examples, and the three continents were traditionally assigned to the three sons of Noah: Shem (Asia), Japheth (Europe) and Ham (Africa).

History

The scheme derives from descriptions in Isidore of Seville's Etymologiae (c. 625) and appears in hundreds of surviving manuscripts, at first as tiny schematic diagrams and later elaborated into great mappaemundi such as the Beatus maps and, with much added detail, the Hereford Mappa Mundi (c. 1300). A T and O diagram in a 1472 Augsburg printing of Isidore is often cited as the first printed map in the West. The type declined as Ptolemaic geography and portolan charts reshaped European cartography in the 15th and 16th centuries.

Interpretation

T and O maps were never meant as navigational tools; they were theological and encyclopedic diagrams summarizing the order of the world. The modern phrase "T-O map" is a scholarly label — medieval writers called the figure simply the orbis terrarum.
