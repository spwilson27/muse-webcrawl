Seraph

A seraph with six wings, as described in the Book of Isaiah.

A seraph (plural seraphim) is a type of celestial or heavenly being originating in Ancient Judaism. The word derives from the Hebrew saraph, "the burning one". Seraphim are traditionally depicted as attendants of the throne of God.

In the Hebrew Bible

The principal description of seraphim appears in the Book of Isaiah, chapter 6, where the prophet sees a vision of God in the Temple attended by seraphim, each with six wings: "with two they covered their faces, and with two they covered their feet, and with two they flew." They call to one another the Trisagion: "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory." One seraph purifies Isaiah's lips with a burning coal taken from the altar. Elsewhere, "flying serpents" (seraphim) appear in Numbers and Deuteronomy, where the term refers to venomous snakes.

In later tradition

In the Christian angelic hierarchy systematised by Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, the seraphim form the highest of the nine orders of angels, closest to God and associated with fire, light, and ardent love. They appear throughout medieval and Renaissance art and literature, including Dante's Paradiso. Jewish liturgy and later mystical tradition also preserve the seraphim among the orders of angels.
