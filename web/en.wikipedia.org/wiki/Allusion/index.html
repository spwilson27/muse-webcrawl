Allusion

[Image: An illustrated page of classical literature rich in allusions]
Classical texts are a frequent source of allusions.

An allusion is a figure of speech that makes a brief, indirect reference to a person, place, thing, or idea of historical, cultural, literary, or political significance. It does not describe in detail the thing to which it refers; the writer expects the reader to recognize the reference and to grasp the additional layer of meaning it contributes. Allusion is a literary device used in literature, drama, film, and everyday speech.

Characteristics

Because an allusion depends on shared knowledge between author and audience, it can create a compact connection between a text and the larger culture. A successful allusion enriches a work by importing the associations of the referenced person or event — for instance, calling a betrayer a "Judas" or describing a disaster as someone's "Waterloo." When the audience lacks the background knowledge, however, the effect of the allusion is lost, which makes allusions among the most culture-bound of literary devices.

Types

Allusions may be classified by their source material. Literary allusions refer to earlier works of literature, such as the novels of Shakespeare, Milton, or Joyce, whose works are themselves densely allusive. Biblical allusions refer to scripture and are pervasive in Western literature. Classical allusions draw on Greek and Roman mythology and history. Historical and cultural allusions refer to real events, public figures, and works of art, music, or popular culture.

Examples

The poetry of T. S. Eliot, notably The Waste Land (1922), is famous for its dense web of allusions to the Bible, Dante, Shakespeare, and Eastern religions. James Joyce's Ulysses (1922) is structured as an extended allusion to Homer's Odyssey. In everyday English, phrases such as "Achilles' heel," "Pandora's box," "a Catch-22," and "Big Brother" are fossilized allusions that have entered common usage.

Allusion and related devices

Allusion differs from a direct quotation, in which source material is reproduced openly, and from an explicit reference, which names its target. It is related to intertextuality, the shaping of a text's meaning by other texts. Writers use allusions to establish tone, characterize speakers, create irony, or signal membership in a literary tradition.
