A proverb (from Latin proverbium) is a simple, concrete, traditional saying that expresses a perceived truth based on common sense or experience. Proverbs are often metaphorical and use formulaic language; they are handed down from generation to generation within a speech community, which recognises them as communal wisdom rather than as any individual author's coinage. The study of proverbs is called paremiology. Examples in English include "A stitch in time saves nine", "Don't count your chickens before they hatch", and "The early bird catches the worm"—although for almost every proverb a counter-proverb can be found ("Look before you leap" against "He who hesitates is lost"), because proverbs state strategies for situations, not universal laws.

Definition and characteristics

The paremiologist Archer Taylor famously confessed that "an incommunicable quality tells us this sentence is proverbial and that one is not", but scholars generally require that a proverb be traditional (in circulation over time), current in popular use, self-contained, and didactic or evaluative in intent. Proverbs favour brevity, rhythm, rhyme and alliteration ("spare the rod and spoil the child"), parallelism, and vivid imagery drawn from farming, weather, animals and the household. Many include a kernel statement generalised into advice or warning: "Red sky at night, shepherd's delight". Unlike an aphorism, which is credited to an author (such as La Rochefoucaud), or a maxim, which states a rule of conduct abstractly, a proverb is anonymous and concrete. An anti-proverb deliberately twists a known proverb for wit ("Absence makes the heart go wander").

History and collections

Proverbs are among the oldest recorded literature. Sumerian clay tablets of the third millennium BC preserve collections of sayings; the Aramaic Words of Ahiqar (c. 7th century BC) transmitted proverbial wisdom across the Near East. The biblical Book of Proverbs gathers Hebrew wisdom sayings attributed to Solomon. Greek and Roman antiquity produced paroemiographers who collected sayings, and in 1500 Erasmus published the Adagia, a humanist anthology of classical proverbs with commentary that grew to more than 4,000 entries and shaped European letters. Renaissance art literalised proverbs, most famously in Pieter Bruegel the Elder's Netherlandish Proverbs (1559), which stages more than a hundred Flemish sayings in a single village scene. Modern scholarly collections include the Oxford Dictionary of Proverbs and the multi-volume type-indexes of Matti Kuusi, which organise proverbs of many cultures by theme and structure.

Proverbs across cultures

Similar situations generate similar sayings worldwide: "Strike while the iron is hot" has analogues in dozens of languages, and a Yoruba proverb proudly declares that "a proverb is the horse of speech; if communication is lost, the proverb is sent to find it". At the same time, proverbs encode local ecologies and values—camel and date imagery in Arabic sayings, rice and monsoon imagery in Southeast Asian ones—and scholars use their distribution to trace cultural contact and migration. Borrowed proverbs (calques) spread through religion, trade and, in the modern period, media and the internet, which has also produced parody "proverbs" and misattributions to figures such as Confucius or Mark Twain.

Use and function

Proverbs do social work: they advise without lecturing, criticise while preserving politeness (the speaker disowns the sharp words as tradition's), argue by authority, mark solidarity, and season speech in law courts, sermons, negotiation and everyday talk. In many West African and other oral cultures, skill with proverbs is a mark of eloquence and elder status, and proverb exchanges structure palaver and dispute settlement. Psychologists and linguists study proverb comprehension as a window on figurative language: understanding that "the rolling stone gathers no moss" is not about stones requires mapping a concrete scene onto an abstract situation, a capacity probed in clinical assessment. Writers from Cervantes—whose Sancho Panza strings proverbs into every speech—to modern novelists exploit proverbs for characterisation, irony and folk voice.
