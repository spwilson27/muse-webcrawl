George Inness
Born: May 1, 1825, Newburgh, New York, U.S.
Died: August 3, 1894 (aged 69), Bridge of Allan, Scotland
Known for: Landscape painting
Movement: Hudson River School, Barbizon influence, Tonalism

George Inness (May 1, 1825 – August 3, 1894) was a prominent American landscape painter. His mature work — atmospheric, loosely painted scenes of meadows, woods, and twilight — moved American landscape painting away from the detailed naturalism of the early Hudson River School toward a subjective, tonal style infused with the theology of Emanuel Swedenborg, earning him comparison with Jean-Baptiste-Camille Corot and the title "father of American landscape painting".

Life and career

Inness was born in Newburgh, New York, the fifth of thirteen children of a grocer and farmer. Largely self-taught apart from brief study with John Jesse Barker and a few months with Régis François Gignoux, he worked early on as a map engraver. Trips to Italy and France in the early 1850s exposed him to the Barbizon school — especially Théodore Rousseau and Charles-François Daubigny — whose intimate, sketch-like landscapes reshaped his art. Back home he painted in New York, Boston, and later Eagleswood and Montclair, New Jersey, as well as Florida in his last years.

Style

Inness's early work shows the tight, panoramic Hudson River manner, as in The Lackawanna Valley (1855), commissioned by the Delaware, Lackawanna and Western Railroad. From the 1860s, under the influence of Swedenborgian doctrine — which saw nature as a correspondence of spiritual states — his forms softened: edges dissolved, compositions were built from broad tonal masses, and light became the true subject, as in Home of the Heron (1893) and Sunset at Montclair. He called his approach "civilised landscape" — nature modified by human presence — and his late paintings are often divided between a more structured "civilised" mode and vaporous, almost abstract late works.

Legacy

Although sales were uneven in his lifetime, Inness's reputation rose steadily; a retrospective at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in 1985 cemented his standing as one of the greatest American landscape painters of the 19th century. His son George Inness Jr. was also a painter and wrote a biography of his father.
