Ctesiphon was an ancient city in Mesopotamia, located on the eastern bank of the Tigris about 35 km south-east of modern Baghdad. It served as the capital and principal royal residence of the Parthian Empire and then of the Sasanian Empire for more than eight centuries, growing into one of the largest cities of the ancient world, with a population possibly in the hundreds of thousands.

History

Ctesiphon began around 120 BC as a Parthian military camp and royal winter residence opposite the Hellenistic city of Seleucia on the Tigris. Because of its position on the royal road and Silk Road trade routes, it flourished as a commercial metropolis and repeatedly became a prize in Rome's wars with Persia: Roman forces captured or sacked the city several times, under Trajan (116), Avidius Cassius (164), Septimius Severus (197) and Carus (283), yet it always reverted to Persian rule. Under the Sasanians, who took power in 224 AD, Ctesiphon and its surrounding districts — known collectively as the metropolis — became the seat of the King of Kings and a centre of administration, Zoroastrian learning and Nestorian Christianity.

Taq Kasra and decline

The city's most famous monument is the Taq Kasra ("Arch of Khosrow"), the facade of a Sasanian palace attributed to the era of Khosrow I (6th century AD). Its great iwan — a brick vault some 37 metres high and 26 metres across — remains the largest single-span unreinforced brick arch in the world. Ctesiphon fell to Arab Muslim armies after the Battle of al-Qadisiyyah and the siege of 637; its palaces were plundered and, with the founding of Baghdad in 762, the population drifted away, leaving the arch standing alone in the plain. Part of the facade collapsed in flooding in 2019, prompting restoration efforts.
