Via Augusta

The Via Augusta was the longest and busiest of the major roads built by the Romans in ancient Hispania (the Iberian Peninsula). At approximately 1,500 kilometres (930 mi) long, it ran along the Mediterranean coast from the Pyrenees, where it connected with the Via Domitia in Gaul, through the modern Spanish cities of Girona, Barcelona, Tarragona, Valencia, and Cartagena, to Gades (modern Cádiz) in the south.

The road was originally known as the Via Herculea or Via Heraclea, and was rebuilt and renamed in honour of the emperor Augustus during his reorganisation of the peninsula around 8–2 BC. It was the backbone of Roman administration and commerce in Hispania, lined with milestones (miliaria) recording distances and the emperor under whom repairs were made, and served by mansiones (way stations) at regular intervals.

Route

Beginning at La Jonquera in the Pyrenees, the Via Augusta passed through Gerunda (Girona), Barcino (Barcelona), Tarraco (Tarragona) — the capital of Hispania Citerior — Valentia (Valencia), and Carthago Nova (Cartagena), before turning inland through Corduba (Córdoba) and Hispalis (Seville) to terminate at Gades. Modern highways such as the A-7 and N-340 in Spain follow much of its course.

Legacy

Numerous milestones, bridges, and stretches of paving survive along the route. The Arch of Bará near Tarragona, a triumphal arch erected over the road, is one of the best-known monuments associated with it. In 2023 the route saw renewed attention as a cultural itinerary through eastern and southern Spain.
