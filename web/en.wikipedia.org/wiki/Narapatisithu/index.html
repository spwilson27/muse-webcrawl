Narapatisithu (Burmese: နရပတိစည်သူ; 1138 – 1211) was king of the Pagan dynasty of Burma (Myanmar) from 1174 to 1211. His reign of nearly four decades is remembered as a period of peace, prosperity, and extensive temple building, and he is credited with completing the Burmanization of the Pagan empire's administration and culture.

Accession

Narapatisithu was the younger brother of King Naratheinkha. According to the chronicles, relations between the brothers collapsed after Naratheinkha took one of his brother's wives. While Narapatisithu was campaigning in the north, he marched on the capital, and Naratheinkha was assassinated by Aung Zwa, the captain of the palace guard, in 1174. Narapatisithu then took the throne.

Reign

Unlike the warlike reigns of his predecessors, Narapatisithu's long rule was largely peaceful, allowing the kingdom to consolidate the conquests of Anawrahta and his successors. He appointed a chief minister, Ananda Thuriya, famed for his wisdom, and the administration was reorganized with Burmese officials increasingly replacing Mon officials. Burmese progressively became the language of inscriptions alongside Pali, Pyu, and Mon.

The king was a major patron of Theravada Buddhism. His best-known monument is the Dhammayazika Pagoda (1196), a distinctive pentagonal temple east of Pagan, built to house relics presented by the king of Ceylon. He also founded the Dhammayazika's associated structures and undertook repairs throughout the capital.

Death and legacy

Narapatisithu died in 1211 and was succeeded by his son Htilominlo, the last great temple builder of Pagan. Historians generally regard Narapatisithu's reign as the high-water mark of Pagan's stability before the empire's gradual decline in the later thirteenth century.
