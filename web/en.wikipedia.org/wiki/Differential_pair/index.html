Differential pair

A differential pair is a fundamental building block of analog integrated circuits, consisting of two matched amplifying devices (bipolar transistors, FETs, or vacuum tubes) sharing a common constant-current source. It produces an output proportional to the difference between two input signals, while largely rejecting signals common to both inputs.

Operation

The two transistors share a constant total current supplied by a high-impedance current source in their common emitter (or source) path. When the two inputs are equal, the current splits equally. Raising one input voltage increases that transistor's share of the current and decreases the other's. Because the total current is fixed, the outputs at the two collectors move in opposite directions, giving a differential output.

Properties

The circuit has high common-mode rejection: temperature drift and power-supply variations affect both devices similarly and largely cancel. Its transconductance and voltage gain depend on the tail current and load resistance. With an active (current-mirror) load, very high gain can be achieved in a single stage.

Applications

Differential pairs form the input stage of nearly every operational amplifier, as well as comparators, emitter-coupled logic gates, mixers and line receivers for differential signalling standards such as LVDS and RS-485.
