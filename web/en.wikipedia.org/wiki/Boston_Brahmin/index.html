Boston Brahmin

The Boston Brahmins or Boston elite are the members of Boston's traditional upper class, descendants of the earliest English settlers of New England, whose wealth derived from maritime trade, manufacturing, and finance. The term was coined by Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. in 1860, likening the caste-like position of the group to the Brahmins of India.

Families and institutions

Families associated with the group include the Adamses, Cabots, Lowells, Lodges, Peabodys, Quincys, Saltonstalls, and Winthrops — summed up in the verse about the Lowells speaking only to Cabots, and the Cabots speaking only to God. Their influence ran through Harvard University, the Boston Athenaeum, elite schools, Episcopal churches, and Beacon Hill, and later through philanthropy, publishing, and the abolitionist and reform movements.

Character and decline

The Brahmins cultivated an image of public service, cultural refinement, and restrained gentility, while also being satirised for snobbery and insularity. Their political dominance waned in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as Irish-American politicians took control of Boston city government, though the families retained social and institutional influence for decades.
