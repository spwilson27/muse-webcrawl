Microbial ecology

Microbial ecology (or environmental microbiology) is the ecology of microorganisms: their relationship with one another and with their environment. It concerns the three major domains of life — Eukaryota, Archaea, and Bacteria — as well as viruses. Microorganisms, by their omnipresence, impact the entire biosphere.

Overview

Microbes are the backbone of all ecosystems, but especially in niches where light is absent and energy instead comes from chemical reactions — chemosynthesis. Microbial life dominates the planet's subsurface, oceans, and atmosphere interfaces, and microbial processes drive the global cycling of carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus.

History

The field emerged from the work of pioneers such as Sergei Winogradsky, who discovered chemosynthesis and developed the Winogradsky column, and Martinus Beijerinck, who formulated enrichment culture techniques and the idea that "everything is everywhere, but the environment selects." The development of molecular tools — notably 16S rRNA gene sequencing championed by Carl Woese — revolutionized the field by revealing enormous microbial diversity that could not be cultured in the laboratory.

Symbiosis and interactions

Microbial ecologists study interactions including mutualism, commensalism, parasitism, competition, and predation. Important examples include mycorrhizal associations between fungi and plant roots, nitrogen-fixing rhizobia in legume root nodules, and the microbial communities of the human gut (the human microbiome).

Methods and applications

Modern microbial ecology relies on metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, stable isotope probing, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and high-throughput amplicon sequencing to study communities without culturing. Applications include bioremediation of polluted sites, wastewater treatment, agriculture, and understanding the microbial role in climate change.
