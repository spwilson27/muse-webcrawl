Phi phenomenon

The phi phenomenon is the optical illusion of perceiving a series of still images, presented in rapid succession, as continuous motion. It was first described by the Gestalt psychologist Max Wertheimer in 1912 in his paper "Experimental Studies on the Seeing of Motion", work that helped to found Gestalt psychology.

Description

In Wertheimer's experiments, two lights flashing in alternation at suitable intervals — roughly 60 milliseconds apart — were perceived not as two separate flickering lights but as a single light moving back and forth. Wertheimer distinguished this "pure" phi phenomenon, the perception of motion without a clearly seen moving object, from beta movement, in which an object is perceived to move across positions.

Significance

The phi phenomenon challenged the structuralist claim that perception is built from elementary sensations: the motion perceived was a property of the whole stimulus that could not be reduced to the individual static images. Together with related demonstrations, it became a foundational result for the Gestalt school and underlies the perception of motion in cinema and animation, where a rapid succession of still frames (typically 24 per second) is experienced as continuous movement.
