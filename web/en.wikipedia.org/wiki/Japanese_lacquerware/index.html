Japanese lacquerware

Image placeholder: Japanese lacquerware

Japanese lacquerware is a Japanese craft with a wide range of fine and decorative arts, as lacquer has been used in urushi-e, prints, and on a wide kind of objects from Buddha statues to bento boxes for food. The characteristic of Japanese lacquerware is the diversity of lacquerware using a decoration technique called maki-e (蒔絵) in which metal powder is sprinkled to attach to lacquer.

History

The craft of lacquer in Japan dates back to the Jōmon period. Evidence of the earliest lacquerware was discovered at the Kakinoshima B archaeological site in Hokkaido, where ornaments coated with vermilion lacquer were dated to about 9,000 years ago, making them among the oldest lacquered objects in the world.

During the Nara period (710–794), lacquerware was used in combination with other materials in temple construction and the production of Buddhist statuary. In the Heian period (794–1185), techniques such as maki-e — sprinkling gold or silver powder onto wet lacquer — were developed to a high level of refinement, and lacquerware became associated with aristocratic culture.

During the Edo period (1603–1868), lacquerware flourished both as a luxury export commodity to Europe, where it was known as "japanning," and in the production of inrō, writing boxes (suzuribako), and netsuke for the domestic market. Centers of production included Wajima in Ishikawa Prefecture, famous for its durable wajima-nuri, and Aizu in Fukushima.

Techniques

Lacquer (Japanese: urushi) is obtained from the sap of the lacquer tree (Toxicodendron vernicifluum). The sap contains urushiol, which polymerizes in humid conditions to form an extremely hard, durable, and water-resistant coating.

Decorative techniques include maki-e (sprinkled picture), raden (mother-of-pearl inlay), chinkin (incised gold), kamakura-bori (carved lacquer imitating wood carving), and tsugaru-nuri, built up from many layers of differently colored lacquer polished to reveal patterns.
