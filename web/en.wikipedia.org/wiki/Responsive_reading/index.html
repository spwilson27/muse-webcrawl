Responsive reading

Responsive reading is a form of liturgical reading in which a leader and a congregation (or two halves of a congregation) alternate reading verses or passages, usually from the Psalms or other scriptural texts. The leader reads one verse or line, and the congregation responds with the next, creating a dialogue between the worship leader and the assembled community.

Practice

Responsive readings are common in many Christian traditions, including Protestant, Roman Catholic, and Orthodox worship, and responsive chanting of psalms is also an ancient practice in Jewish synagogue worship. The texts are frequently printed in hymnals or service bulletins, with the leader's part in regular type and the congregation's response in bold type, or marked with symbols indicating which voice should read.

The practice is intended to engage the whole congregation actively in the reading of scripture rather than having them listen passively. Well-known examples include responsive arrangements of Psalm 23, Psalm 100, and Psalm 136, whose repeated refrain "his steadfast love endures forever" lends itself naturally to antiphonal delivery.

Related forms

Responsive reading is related to antiphonal singing and chanting, in which two choirs or a cantor and choir alternate musical phrases. It differs from unison reading, in which everyone reads the same words together, and from a simple solo reading by the officiant.
