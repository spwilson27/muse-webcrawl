Hellenistic philosophy

Hellenistic philosophy is the Western philosophy of the Hellenistic period, conventionally dated from the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC to the Battle of Actium in 31 BC, though its schools remained influential well into the Roman Empire. Its central concern shifted from the metaphysical systems of Plato and Aristotle toward ethics: how the individual can achieve tranquillity (ataraxia) and self-sufficiency in an uncertain world ruled by vast monarchies.

Major schools

Stoicism, founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC, taught that the universe is rationally ordered by a divine logos and that virtue alone is good; the wise person accepts fate and masters destructive emotions. Later Stoics include Chrysippus, Seneca, Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius.

Epicureanism, founded by Epicurus (341–270 BC), held that pleasure — understood as the absence of pain and mental disturbance — is the goal of life. Epicureans withdrew from politics, prized friendship, and explained the world through atomism, denying divine interference.

Scepticism took two main forms: Pyrrhonism, descending from Pyrrho of Elis, which suspended judgement on all matters to achieve peace of mind; and the Academic scepticism of Arcesilaus and Carneades, who argued that certainty is unattainable and one must act on probabilities.

Cynicism, heirs of Diogenes of Sinope, rejected convention, wealth and reputation, advocating a simple life according to nature and shameless self-sufficiency.

Precursors and context

The schools drew on Socratic antecedents — the Cyrenaics, Megarians and the early Academy — and developed in dialogue with one another in Athens, which remained philosophy's capital. Logic, physics and ethics formed the standard threefold division of philosophy in the Stoic and Epicurean curricula.

Influence

Hellenistic ethics shaped Roman thought, early Christian theology (notably through concepts such as natural law), and, after their rediscovery, Renaissance and Enlightenment moral philosophy.
