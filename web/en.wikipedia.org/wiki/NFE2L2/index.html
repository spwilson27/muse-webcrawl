FunctionTranscription factor; master regulator of antioxidant response

NFE2L2 (nuclear factor, erythroid 2 like 2) is a human gene encoding the transcription factor Nrf2, a basic leucine zipper (bZIP) protein that regulates the expression of antioxidant and detoxification genes. It is often described as a master switch of the cellular defence against oxidative and electrophilic stress.

Regulation by KEAP1

Under normal conditions Nrf2 is held in the cytoplasm by its repressor KEAP1, which serves as an adaptor for a Cullin-3 ubiquitin ligase and targets Nrf2 for continuous proteasomal degradation. When cells encounter reactive oxygen species or electrophiles, reactive cysteine residues in KEAP1 are modified, Nrf2 escapes degradation and accumulates, enters the nucleus, and binds — with small Maf proteins — to antioxidant response elements (AREs) in the promoters of target genes.

Target genes and function

Nrf2 induces a battery of cytoprotective genes including heme oxygenase-1 (HMOX1), NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1), glutamate–cysteine ligase (the rate-limiting enzyme of glutathione synthesis), glutathione S-transferases and enzymes of the pentose phosphate pathway. Mice lacking Nrf2 are viable but unusually sensitive to toxins and oxidative injury.

Disease and pharmacology

Somatic mutations that activate Nrf2 — in NFE2L2 itself or inactivating KEAP1 — are common in lung and other cancers, where constitutive Nrf2 activity promotes tumour survival and resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Conversely, pharmacological Nrf2 activators such as dimethyl fumarate (used in multiple sclerosis) and sulforaphane from cruciferous vegetables are studied for protective effects in chronic disease. Bardoxolone methyl, a potent activator, failed in late-stage kidney-disease trials because of cardiovascular side effects, illustrating the pathway's double-edged character.
