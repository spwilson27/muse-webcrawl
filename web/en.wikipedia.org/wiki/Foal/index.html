Foal

A foal is a young horse or other equine (such as a pony, donkey or zebra) in its first year of life. A newborn foal is also called a neonate; a male foal is a colt and a female foal is a filly. Foals are typically weaned at four to six months of age; a young horse that has been weaned is called a weanling, and one in its second year is a yearling. The verb "to foal" describes a mare giving birth.

Birth and development

Mares carry a foal for about 11 months, and foaling usually takes place at night and is rapid, often under 30 minutes. A healthy foal normally stands within an hour and nurses within two hours, receiving antibodies through the mare's colostrum, which is essential because foals are born with essentially no circulating immunoglobulins. Foals are precocial: they are born with open eyes, can regulate their temperature, and can run within hours. Their legs are disproportionately long — about 80–90% of adult leg length at birth — an adaptation for fleeing predators.

Care and health

Newborn foals require monitoring for failure of passive transfer, neonatal sepsis, meconium impaction and limb deformities. Because a foal's immune system is immature, it is vulnerable to diseases such as Rhodococcus equi pneumonia. Handling and halter training typically begin in the first days of life (imprint training is practised by some breeders), and vaccination and deworming schedules start in the first few months. Orphaned foals may be raised on nurse mares or milk replacer.

Terminology

The terms colt and filly continue until the horse is four years old; "foal" may be combined with the dam's name ("a foal by [sire] out of [dam]") in breeding records. In Thoroughbred racing in the Northern Hemisphere, all horses share an official birthday of January 1 regardless of actual foaling date.
