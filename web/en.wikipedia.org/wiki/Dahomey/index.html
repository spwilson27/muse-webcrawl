Dahomey

The Kingdom of Dahomey was a West African kingdom in present-day Benin that existed from about 1600 until 1904, when it was annexed into French West Africa. Centred on the city of Abomey, it became a major regional power, known for its centralised administration, its annual customs ceremonies, and its all-female military regiment, the Dahomey Amazons (Mino).

History

The kingdom was founded by the Fon people on the Abomey plateau; tradition dates the dynasty to the early 17th century with King Houegbadja, who built the first royal palaces. Under kings such as Agaja, Dahomey conquered the coastal kingdoms of Allada and Whydah in the 1720s, gaining direct access to the Atlantic slave trade at the port of Ouidah. In the 19th century, under Ghezo and Glele, the economy partially shifted to palm oil exports as the slave trade was suppressed.

Fall

Conflict with France over the port of Cotonou led to the First and Second Franco-Dahomean Wars (1890–1894). King Béhanzin was defeated and exiled; Dahomey became a French protectorate and then part of French Dahomey. The independent Republic of Dahomey was proclaimed in 1960 and renamed Benin in 1975.

Culture

The royal palaces of Abomey, with their bas-reliefs recounting the deeds of the kings, are a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Dahomean religion, centred on the vodun spirits and veneration of royal ancestors, strongly influenced Vodou in the Americas.
