Ornamental turning

Tools: Rose engine lathe; Holtzapffel lathe

Materials: Ivory, hardwood, horn, metal

Heyday: 18th–19th centuries

Related craft: Guilloché engraving

Ornamental turning is a form of woodturning and metal turning in which a lathe fitted with patterned cams, called rosettes, moves the workpiece or cutter in controlled oscillations, producing intricate geometric patterns — waves, spirals, scallops and interlaced guilloché figures — impossible to cut freehand. It flourished as a gentlemanly and courtly art in Europe from the sixteenth to the nineteenth century.

Technique

On a rose engine lathe the rotating workpiece is rocked or pumped against a fixed cutter, guided by the profile of interchangeable rosette cams; the combination of rosette shape, phase and cutter position yields a vast family of patterns. Work was typically done in dense materials that hold fine detail: ivory, ebony, boxwood, lignum vitae, horn and brass. Famous applications include turned ivory goblets and chessmen, snuff boxes, watch cases and the patterned dials and plates of fine clocks.

History

The craft was practised by princes as well as professionals: Peter the Great owned ornamental lathes, and the London firm of Holtzapffel built superbly engineered lathes and codified the art in the multi-volume Turning and Mechanical Manipulation (1843–1884). By the early twentieth century machine production and changing taste nearly extinguished the craft.

Revival

Collector organisations such as the Ornamental Turners Group of Australia and the American Association of Woodturners' ornamental chapters preserve historic lathes and teach the techniques; modern CNC and 3D-printed rose engines have renewed interest in the patterns.
