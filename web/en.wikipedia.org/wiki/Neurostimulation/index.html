Neurostimulation

Neurostimulation is the purposeful modulation of the nervous system's activity using invasive or non-invasive means, such as electrical stimulation delivered by implanted electrodes, magnetic stimulation, or (less commonly) optical and ultrasonic stimulation. It is the technological core of the medical field of neuromodulation. Neurostimulation technology can improve the life quality of patients with severe symptoms of conditions such as chronic pain, movement disorders, and epilepsy, and it restores function in prosthetic devices such as cochlear implants and retinal implants.

Invasive techniques

  • Spinal cord stimulation — epidural electrodes over the dorsal columns for chronic pain.
  • Deep brain stimulation — electrodes in deep brain targets for Parkinson's disease, tremor, dystonia, and epilepsy.
  • Vagus nerve stimulation — a cuff electrode on the cervical vagus nerve for epilepsy and depression.
  • Sacral and peripheral nerve stimulation — for bladder dysfunction and focal pain.
  • Cortical stimulation and responsive neurostimulation — subdural or depth electrodes, sometimes closed-loop.

Non-invasive techniques

  • Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) — magnetic pulses induce currents in the cortex; used for depression and in neuroscience research.
  • Transcranial electrical stimulation (tDCS/tACS) — weak currents applied through scalp electrodes.
  • Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) — surface electrodes for pain relief.
  • Focused ultrasound — an emerging method for deep, non-invasive stimulation.

How it works

Electrical stimulation recruits axons near the electrode according to their diameter, distance, and orientation. Depending on the target, frequency, and waveform, stimulation can excite neural tissue, block conduction, or disrupt pathological rhythms. High-frequency stimulation of deep brain targets, for example, is thought to override abnormal oscillatory activity in motor circuits. Closed-loop ("adaptive" or "responsive") systems sense biomarkers such as local field potentials or seizures and deliver stimulation only when needed.

History

Systematic therapeutic stimulation began in the 1960s with spinal cord stimulation following the gate control theory of pain; deep brain stimulation followed in the 1980s–90s. Non-invasive brain stimulation expanded after the introduction of repetitive TMS in 1985 and its regulatory approval for depression in 2008.
