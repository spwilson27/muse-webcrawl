Atomic radius

The atomic radius of a chemical element is a measure of the size of its atom, usually the mean or typical distance from the center of the nucleus to the outermost isolated electron. Since the boundary of the electron shell is not a well-defined entity, a variety of definitions are used: the empirical radius from measured bond lengths, the metallic radius in metal crystals, the covalent radius from single-bond lengths, the ionic radius of ions in crystals, and the van der Waals radius from non-bonded contacts.

Explanation

Because atoms have no definite boundary, their "radius" depends on how it is measured. In many cases the radius is inferred from the distance between the nuclei of two atoms joined in a bond, or between two adjacent atoms in a crystal lattice, divided by two.

Trends in the periodic table

Empirically measured atomic radii generally decrease along each period (row) of the periodic table, from the alkali metals to the noble gases, because the increasing nuclear charge pulls the electron cloud inward while electrons are added to the same shell. They generally increase down each group (column), because new electron shells are added. Between the group 3 elements Sc, Y and La/lanthanide contraction effects make the heavier transition metals smaller than expected.

Definitions

  • Metallic radius — half the distance between two adjacent metal atoms in a metallic lattice.
  • Covalent radius — half the single-bond length between two identical atoms.
  • Ionic radius — the radius of an ion in an ionic crystal, apportioned from lattice distances.
  • Van der Waals radius — half the minimum distance between nuclei of two non-bonded atoms of the element.
  • Calculated (theoretical) radius — the radius of the outermost peak of the electron density from quantum-mechanical calculations.

Widely used compilations include the empirical radii of J. C. Slater (1964) and the calculated radii of Clementi and co-workers. Typical values range from about 25 picometres (helium, calculated) to over 300 picometres for francium-sized heavy alkali atoms.
