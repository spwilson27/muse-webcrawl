Dimitri Tsafendas

Born: 14 January 1918, Lourenço Marques, Portuguese Mozambique
Died: 7 October 1999 (aged 81), Krugersdorp, South Africa
Known for: Assassination of Hendrik Verwoerd

Dimitri Tsafendas (Greek: Δημήτρης Τσαφέντας; 14 January 1918 – 7 October 1999) was a South African messenger and former seaman who assassinated South African Prime Minister Hendrik Verwoerd, widely regarded as the architect of apartheid, in the House of Assembly in Cape Town on 6 September 1966. Tsafendas stabbed Verwoerd four times with a dagger during a parliamentary session, in full view of members of parliament.

Early life

Tsafendas was born in Lourenço Marques (now Maputo), Mozambique, to a Greek father, Michalis Tsafentakis of Crete, and a Mozambican mother of mixed race, Amelia Williams. Classified under South Africa's racial system in ambiguous terms, he lived a peripatetic life across Mozambique, South Africa, the United States, Canada, and Europe, working as a seaman, welder, and salesman, and spent periods in psychiatric institutions in several countries. He harboured a long-standing grievance that apartheid's racial classification prevented him from marrying across the colour line.

The assassination

In 1966 Tsafendas obtained a job as a parliamentary messenger in Cape Town. Having originally considered shooting Verwoerd, he bought a dagger, and on 6 September 1966 stabbed the prime minister at his desk as the session began. Verwoerd was declared dead on arrival at Groote Schuur Hospital. It was the first assassination of a South African head of government; Verwoerd had survived an earlier attempt by David Pratt in 1960.

Trial and aftermath

At a summary trial, Tsafendas was found unfit to stand trial by reason of insanity — he claimed a giant tapeworm influenced his actions — and was detained "at the State President's pleasure". He spent the rest of his life in prison and psychiatric custody, for many years in cells near Nelson Mandela's on Robben Island and later at Sterkfontein Hospital, where he died in 1999. Later historical research, notably by Harris Dousemetzis, has argued from Tsafendas's extensive political writings and associations that he was a committed anti-apartheid activist with coherent political motives, challenging the official "madman" portrayal propagated by the apartheid state.
