Pálinka

Pálinka is a traditional fruit spirit originating from the medieval Kingdom of Hungary, distilled from a variety of fruits grown in the Carpathian Basin. It is a protected designation of origin in the European Union: only fruit spirits produced in Hungary (and certain Austrian apricot spirits) may legally be marketed under the name. Similar drinks are found throughout Central and Eastern Europe under names such as pálenka in Slovakia.

Production

Pálinka is made by fermenting mashed fruit — most commonly plum (szilvapálinka), apricot (barackpálinka), pear (körtepálinka), apple, cherry, or grape pomace (törkölypálinka) — and then distilling the fermented mash, traditionally twice in a pot still. No sugar or other additives may be introduced before distillation in genuine pálinka. Alcohol content typically ranges from 37.5% to over 50%, with homemade versions sometimes considerably stronger.

History

Distillation of fruit spirits in Hungary dates to at least the 14th century, when aqua vitae was produced at the court of King Charles I. The word "pálinka" derives from the Slovak verb páliť, "to burn, to distill". For centuries, distilling was a peasant household activity tied to the harvest, and it remains legal under licence for Hungarian households to distill limited quantities for personal consumption.

Varieties and consumption

Notable protected regional varieties include szatmári plum, kecskeméti apricot, békési plum, gönci apricot, újfehértói sour cherry, pannonhalmi grape pomace, göcseji pear, and szabolcsi apple pálinka. Pálinka is traditionally drunk neat at room temperature from a tulip-shaped glass, which concentrates the aroma, and is a customary welcome drink offered to guests. Hungary celebrates it at festivals such as the Budapest Pálinka Festival.
