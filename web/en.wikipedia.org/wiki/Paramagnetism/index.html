Paramagnetism

Paramagnetism is a form of magnetism whereby some materials are weakly attracted by an externally applied magnetic field. Unlike ferromagnets, paramagnetic materials do not retain magnetisation when the external field is removed: thermal motion randomises the orientations of their atomic magnetic moments. Paramagnetic materials have a relative magnetic permeability slightly greater than 1 and a small, positive magnetic susceptibility.

Mechanism

Paramagnetism arises in atoms, ions, or molecules with unpaired electrons, which give them a permanent magnetic moment. In the absence of a field these moments point randomly and cancel out; an applied field partially aligns them, producing a net magnetisation in the direction of the field. Because thermal agitation opposes alignment, the susceptibility of most paramagnets decreases with temperature, following Curie's law (χ = C/T), generalised by the Curie–Weiss law for interacting moments. Aluminium, magnesium, oxygen, and many transition-metal and rare-earth salts (such as ferric and cupric sulfates) are paramagnetic.

Theory

The classical Langevin theory and its quantum-mechanical refinements describe magnetisation in terms of the Brillouin function. Conduction electrons in metals contribute a weak, nearly temperature-independent Pauli paramagnetism. Paramagnetic behaviour contrasts with diamagnetism, in which induced moments oppose the applied field and the susceptibility is small and negative.

Applications

Paramagnetism is exploited in electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy, which probes unpaired-electron environments; in paramagnetic oxygen analysers; in magnetic refrigeration using the magnetocaloric effect at very low temperatures (adiabatic demagnetisation); and in MRI contrast agents based on gadolinium compounds.
