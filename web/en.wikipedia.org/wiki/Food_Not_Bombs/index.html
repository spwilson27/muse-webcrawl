Food Not Bombs is an all-volunteer global movement that shares free vegan and vegetarian meals with the hungry. The movement has no formal leaders or structure; each chapter is an independent, autonomous group that makes decisions using the consensus process. Food Not Bombs shares food as a form of protest against war, poverty, and the destruction of the environment, recovering food that would otherwise be thrown away by grocery stores, bakeries, and markets.

History

Food Not Bombs was founded in 1980 in Cambridge, Massachusetts by anti-nuclear activists including Keith McHenry and C. T. Lawrence Butler, who formed the group after protests against the Seabrook Station Nuclear Power Plant and related actions. The name expresses the idea that society's resources should feed people rather than fund weapons: as the movement puts it, money spent on the military could instead end hunger. The group participated in protests against the 1991 Gulf War, and McHenry and others were arrested repeatedly in San Francisco in 1988–89 for serving food without a permit, drawing support from Amnesty International, which adopted some of the arrested volunteers as prisoners of conscience.

From the 1990s the movement spread worldwide, and chapters responded to disasters including Hurricane Katrina in 2005 and the aftermath of earthquakes and conflicts, as well as supporting protest encampments from anti-globalization summits to Occupy Wall Street in 2011.

Principles

The movement is organized around three core tenets on which all autonomous chapters are expected to agree:

  • The food is always free to anyone without restriction
  • Each group is independent and makes decisions by consensus
  • The food shared is vegetarian or vegan and the movement is dedicated to nonviolence

Beyond these, chapters differ widely. Food is typically recovered surplus — produce, bread, and packaged goods near their sell-by dates — cooked in volunteers' kitchens, churches, or community spaces and served outdoors in public places, where the meal doubles as visible protest and builds community with people who are homeless or food insecure.

Conflicts with authorities

Because chapters serve food in public without permits, share without regard to licensing regimes for restaurants, and associate openly with anarchist and anti-war politics, they have periodically clashed with municipal authorities. Cities including San Francisco, Orlando, Tampa, and Houston have passed or enforced ordinances restricting public food sharing, leading to arrests and litigation. In 2018 the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit held (in a case involving the Fort Lauderdale chapter) that outdoor food sharing can constitute expressive conduct protected by the First Amendment; other rulings have varied. Volunteers were also targeted in the 2000s: in 2006, Food Not Bombs volunteer Eric McDavid was prosecuted in an FBI-linked conspiracy case and later released after evidence problems emerged.
