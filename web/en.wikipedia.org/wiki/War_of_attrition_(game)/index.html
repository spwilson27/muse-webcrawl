War of attrition (game)

In game theory, the war of attrition is a dynamic timing game in which two players each incur costs while waiting, and the player who quits first loses a contested prize to the other. It was formalized by the evolutionary biologist John Maynard Smith in 1974 to model animal contests, such as two animals disputing a territory or mate, where the eventual winner is determined by which contestant is willing to persist longest.

The model

Each player chooses a persistence time. Both pay a cost that accumulates with elapsed time (for example, energy expended or opportunities forgone). The player who persists longer wins the prize of value V, while both players bear the cost accumulated up to the moment the loser quits. If both quit at the same time, the prize is shared or lost.

Equilibria

The game has a continuum of asymmetric pure-strategy equilibria, in which one player quits immediately and the other persists, but these require coordination. The symmetric mixed-strategy equilibrium has each player randomizing the quitting time according to an exponential distribution, so that at every instant each player is indifferent between quitting and continuing. In evolutionary settings this mixed strategy can be an evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS).

Applications

The war of attrition has been applied to animal behaviour, auctions, business rivalry, strikes, and political standoffs — any situation in which delay is costly and the prize goes to the most patient contestant. It is closely related to the dollar auction and to all-pay auctions.
