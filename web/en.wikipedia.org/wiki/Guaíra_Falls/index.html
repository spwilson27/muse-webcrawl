The Guaíra Falls (Portuguese: Salto das Sete Quedas do Guaíra; Spanish: Saltos del Guairá) were a series of immense waterfalls on the Paraná River along the border between Brazil and Paraguay. In terms of total volume they were among the greatest waterfalls on Earth — with an average flow around 13,300 cubic metres per second, roughly one and a half times that of Niagara Falls and well above Victoria Falls. In 1982 the falls were inundated by the reservoir of the Itaipu Dam and ceased to exist.

Description

The falls consisted of 18 cataracts in seven groups, where the Paraná, more than a kilometre wide upstream, was forced through a narrow basalt gorge about 60 metres wide. The roar of the water could reportedly be heard 30 kilometres away. They were a major tourist attraction and a site of cultural significance to the Guaraní people. The nearby towns of Guaíra (Brazil) and Salto del Guairá (Paraguay) took their names from the falls.

Destruction

Under the 1973 Treaty of Itaipu, Brazil and Paraguay built one of the world's largest hydroelectric stations just downstream. As the reservoir filled in October 1982, the falls were gradually submerged; crowds gathered to see them for the last time, and in January 1982 a crowded viewing bridge had collapsed, killing dozens of tourists. To remove a navigation hazard, the Brazilian army dynamited the submerged rock ledges. The drowning of the falls remains a symbol of the environmental costs of mega-dams in South America.
