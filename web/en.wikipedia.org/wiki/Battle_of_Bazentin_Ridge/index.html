Battle of Bazentin Ridge

The Battle of Bazentin Ridge was a British attack of the Battle of the Somme, fought on 14 July 1916 by the British Fourth Army under General Henry Rawlinson against the German 2nd Army. In a daring dawn assault, preceded by a five-hour intense bombardment and a night-time advance into no man's land, five British divisions captured the German second position along the Bazentin Ridge in northern France, taking the villages of Bazentin-le-Petit, Bazentin-le-Grand and Longueval. The battle is often cited as one of the most successful British operations of 1916, marred only by the failure of the cavalry exploitation and by heavy subsequent fighting for High Wood and Delville Wood.

Planning and assault

After two weeks of fighting on the Somme, Rawlinson planned an attack on the German second position along the ridge. Learning from the failure of 1 July, the infantry assembled in no man's land during the night of 13/14 July and attacked at 3:25 a.m. behind a short, intense creeping barrage, achieving near-total surprise. By mid-morning the ridge and both Bazentin villages were in British hands, and the German second position had been breached along a 5.5 km front — a striking contrast with the disaster of the first day.

Exploitation and High Wood

The success created a gap facing High Wood, but exploitation faltered: the 7th Dragoon Guards and the Deccan Horse advanced late in the evening — in one of the war's rare mounted actions on the Western Front — but could not hold the wood, and German reserves sealed the breach. High Wood itself, defended tenaciously, was not finally cleared until 15 September, the day tanks were first used, while Delville Wood became the scene of a legendary and costly South African stand. The Bazentin attack nonetheless demonstrated that with limited objectives, surprise and overwhelming short bombardment, trench lines could be broken.
