Feather

Feathers are epidermal growths that form the distinctive outer covering, or plumage, of birds and some non-avian dinosaurs and other archosaurs. They are among the most complex integumentary structures found in vertebrates. Feathers serve in flight, thermal insulation, waterproofing, camouflage and visual display.

Structure

A typical feather consists of a central shaft with two vanes. The hollow lower part of the shaft, embedded in the skin, is the calamus or quill; the solid upper part is the rachis, from which branch numerous barbs. Each barb bears smaller filaments called barbules, many of them equipped with tiny hooklets (barbicels) that fasten neighbouring barbules together, zipping the vane into a smooth, resilient surface. Birds maintain their feathers by preening, applying oil from the uropygial gland.

Types

Contour feathers cover the body and give birds their streamlined shape; the long, stiff flight feathers of the wings (remiges) and tail (rectrices) power and steer flight. Beneath the contour feathers lie soft, fluffy down feathers, which lack a full set of hooklets and trap air for insulation. Other specialised types include filoplumes, bristles and powder down.

Evolution and use

Feathers evolved in theropod dinosaurs before the origin of birds, most likely for insulation or display; fossil discoveries in China, from the 1990s onwards, revealed feathered dinosaurs such as Sinosauropteryx and the four-winged Microraptor. Humans have long used feathers for bedding, quill pens, ornament and fishing flies, and down remains valued for insulation in clothing and duvets.
