Paraphyly

FieldCladistics, phylogenetics, taxonomy
DefinitionA group containing a common ancestor and some, but not all, of its descendants
Classic examplesReptiles (excluding birds), fish (excluding tetrapods), dicotyledons

In taxonomy and cladistics, a grouping of organisms is called paraphyletic if it consists of the group's last common ancestor and most of its descendants, but excludes one or more subgroups that are also descended from that ancestor. Such a grouping is a grade rather than a clade. Paraphyly contrasts with monophyly (a group containing an ancestor and all its descendants) and polyphyly (a group whose members derive from more than one ancestor and does not include the common ancestor).

Examples

The classic example is the traditional class Reptilia: reptiles as conventionally defined include the common ancestor of snakes, lizards, turtles, and crocodilians, but exclude birds, even though birds descend from dinosaur ancestors within that radiation. Similarly, "fish" is paraphyletic because the common ancestor of all fish is also an ancestor of tetrapods, which are excluded. In botany, the dicotyledons (Dicotyledonae) are paraphyletic because monocots descend from within them. Invertebrates — animals without backbones — form a paraphyletic assemblage defined by the exclusion of vertebrates.

Cladistics and debate

Cladists, following Willi Hennig, hold that classification should reflect phylogeny exactly, and therefore recognise only monophyletic groups as valid taxa. From this standpoint, paraphyletic groups should be either abandoned or redefined to include the excluded descendants (e.g., treating birds as dinosaurs, or using Sauropsida in place of Reptilia). Evolutionary taxonomists, by contrast, have defended paraphyletic groups as useful where a descendant lineage has diverged radically (in a process sometimes called a "grade shift"), arguing that classification should capture both branching and subsequent divergence. Most modern phylogenetic classifications avoid formal paraphyletic taxa, though informal terms like "fish" remain in everyday and even scientific use.
