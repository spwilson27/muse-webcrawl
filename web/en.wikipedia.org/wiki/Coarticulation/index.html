Coarticulation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Coarticulation in phonetics is the phenomenon in which the articulation of a speech sound is influenced by the sounds that precede or follow it, because articulatory gestures overlap in time rather than being produced as discrete, beads-on-a-string segments. For example, the /k/ in "key" is articulated further forward in the mouth than the /k/ in "coo," because the tongue anticipates the following vowel.

Types

Anticipatory (right-to-left) coarticulation occurs when a feature of a later sound appears early — as when the lips round during the /s/ of "sue" in anticipation of the rounded vowel. Carryover (left-to-right) coarticulation occurs when a feature persists into a following sound, as in the nasalisation of a vowel after a nasal consonant. Effects may be gradient and mechanical, or categorical and planned as part of a language's phonology; linguists debate where coarticulation ends and secondary articulation or assimilation begins.

Significance

Coarticulation explains why isolated speech sounds are hard to identify in the acoustic signal (the "lack of invariance" problem) and is crucial in speech perception research, speech synthesis, and automatic speech recognition. Models of speech production such as articulatory phonology treat overlapping gestures as the basic units of pronunciation.
