Kainate receptor

Kainate receptors, or kainic acid receptors (KARs), are ionotropic glutamate receptors that respond to the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate and are selectively activated by the agonist kainic acid, a neurotoxin first isolated from the red alga Digenea simplex. Together with AMPA and NMDA receptors, they form the three classes of ionotropic glutamate receptor in the vertebrate central nervous system.

Structure and subunits

Kainate receptors are tetrameric ligand-gated ion channels assembled from five subunits: GluK1, GluK2 and GluK3 (formerly GluR5–GluR7), which can form functional homomeric and heteromeric channels, and GluK4 and GluK5 (formerly KA1 and KA2), which only function in heteromers with the GluK1–3 subunits. RNA editing of the GluK1 and GluK2 subunits at the Q/R site in the pore loop controls calcium permeability. Auxiliary proteins such as Neto1 and Neto2 modulate gating kinetics.

Function

Unlike the largely postsynaptic AMPA receptors, kainate receptors act both postsynaptically, producing relatively slow excitatory postsynaptic currents, and presynaptically, where they bidirectionally regulate neurotransmitter release at synapses such as the hippocampal mossy fibre–CA3 connection. Through G-protein-coupled (metabotropic-like) signalling they also modulate neuronal excitability and GABA release.

Clinical significance

Excessive kainate receptor activation is excitotoxic: systemic kainic acid is a standard experimental tool for inducing seizures and temporal-lobe-epilepsy-like damage in rodents. Kainate receptor genes and antagonists have been investigated in epilepsy, pain, migraine and mood disorders.
