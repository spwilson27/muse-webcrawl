Foreign exchange reserves

Held by: Central banks and monetary authorities
Largest holder: China
Main reserve currency: US dollar

Foreign exchange reserves (or forex reserves) are assets held by a central bank or other monetary authority in foreign currencies, foreign government securities, gold and reserve positions with the International Monetary Fund, including Special Drawing Rights. They serve as a buffer for international payments and a tool for managing the exchange rate.

Purposes

Reserves allow a country to pay for imports and service foreign debt, defend a pegged or managed exchange rate by selling foreign currency, reassure investors and rating agencies, and provide insurance against external shocks such as sudden capital outflows or commodity price swings. Central banks may also intervene in currency markets to moderate volatility or influence competitiveness.

Composition and scale

The US dollar is the dominant reserve currency, accounting for roughly three-fifths of reported global reserves, followed by the euro, Japanese yen and pound sterling. China holds the world's largest reserves, exceeding three trillion US dollars, a legacy of export surpluses and managed exchange-rate policy. Japan, Switzerland and India also hold very large reserves.

Costs and risks

Holding reserves carries an opportunity cost: reserves typically earn low returns in safe assets such as US Treasury securities, and their value fluctuates with exchange rates. Reserves can also be frozen by sanctions, as occurred with Russian central bank assets in 2022, prompting debate about reserve diversification and gold purchases.
