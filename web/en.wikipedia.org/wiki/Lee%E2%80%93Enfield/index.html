The Lee–Enfield is a bolt-action, magazine-fed repeating rifle that served as the main firearm of the British Empire and Commonwealth forces during the first half of the 20th century. It was the standard rifle of the British Army from its official adoption in 1895 until 1957, and remains in limited service with some militaries and police forces today.

Design

The rifle combined James Paris Lee's bolt action and detachable magazine with a barrel rifled at the Royal Small Arms Factory at Enfield. Its rear-locking bolt and short throw allowed an exceptionally rapid rate of fire for a bolt-action — trained soldiers could perform the "mad minute", firing 20–30 aimed rounds in sixty seconds. The 10-round magazine, double the capacity of most contemporaries, could be reloaded with five-round charger clips.

Variants and service

The most famous variants are the Short Magazine Lee–Enfield (SMLE) Mk III of the First World War and the Rifle No. 4 of the Second World War, along with the No. 5 "Jungle Carbine". More than 17 million Lee–Enfields of all types were produced. Converted and surplus rifles remain popular sporting and hunting arms, and the action underpinned sniper rifles such as the L42A1 into the 1980s.
