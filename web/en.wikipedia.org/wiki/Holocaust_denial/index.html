Holocaust denial is an antisemitic conspiracy theory that asserts that the Holocaust—the genocide of European Jews by Nazi Germany during the Second World War—was invented or exaggerated by Jews as part of a plot. Holocaust deniers generally claim that Nazi Germany did not have a policy of extermination, that gas chambers did not exist, or that the death toll of approximately six million Jews is vastly overstated.

Evidence for the Holocaust

The Holocaust is one of the most thoroughly documented events in history. Evidence includes Nazi Germany's own records and orders, the camps and killing sites themselves, photographs and film, testimony of survivors, perpetrators, and bystanders, and the records of the Nuremberg trials. Historians regard denial claims as refuted; courts, notably in the 2000 Irving v Penguin Books case in London, have found that prominent deniers deliberately distorted evidence.

History of denial

Denial began during the war itself in Nazi propaganda and grew after 1945 among neo-Nazi and antisemitic writers such as Paul Rassinier, Harry Elmer Barnes, and Arthur Butz. The Institute for Historical Review, founded in 1978, promoted denial under a veneer of scholarship. More recently denial has spread through internet media and has been adopted by some political movements and states.

Responses and legality

Historians and educators counter denial by documenting the evidence; Deborah Lipstadt's work Denying the Holocaust (1993) is a standard reference. A number of countries criminalize Holocaust denial as a form of hate speech, while in the United States it is protected speech under the First Amendment. Denial is widely condemned by governments, religious bodies, and international organizations.
