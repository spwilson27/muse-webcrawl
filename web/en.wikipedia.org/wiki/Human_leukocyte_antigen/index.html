Human leukocyte antigen

The human leukocyte antigen (HLA) system is a complex of genes on chromosome 6 in humans that encode cell-surface proteins responsible for regulation of the immune system. The HLA system is also known as the human version of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) found in many animals. Mutations in HLA genes may be linked to autoimmune diseases, and certain HLA alleles are associated with susceptibility to or protection from various conditions.

Functions

The proteins encoded by HLA genes are present on the surface of almost all cells and act as "self" markers. Class I molecules (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C) present peptides from inside the cell to cytotoxic T cells; if the peptides are foreign, the cell is destroyed. Class II molecules (HLA-DP, HLA-DQ, HLA-DR) are found on professional antigen-presenting cells and present peptides from outside the cell to helper T cells.

Genetic diversity

The HLA genes are highly polymorphic, meaning many different alleles exist in the population, allowing the immune system to respond to a wide range of pathogens. This diversity is maintained by balancing selection, including heterozygote advantage and frequency-dependent selection by pathogens.

Clinical significance

HLA typing is essential for matching donors and recipients in organ and stem cell transplantation; mismatch increases the risk of rejection and graft-versus-host disease. Specific alleles are associated with diseases such as ankylosing spondylitis (HLA-B27), coeliac disease (HLA-DQ2/DQ8) and type 1 diabetes, and with adverse reactions to certain drugs.
