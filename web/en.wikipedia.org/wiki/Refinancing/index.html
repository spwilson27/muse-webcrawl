Refinancing

Refinancing
Field: Personal finance, corporate finance
Definition: Replacing an existing debt with a new debt on different terms
Common forms: Mortgage refinance, cash-out refinance, debt restructuring

Refinancing is the replacement of an existing debt obligation with another debt obligation under different terms. The most common consumer example is the refinancing of a home mortgage: the borrower takes out a new loan, uses it to pay off the old one, and continues with new — usually cheaper — terms.

Reasons to refinance

  • Lower interest rate — borrowing at a better rate when market rates fall or the borrower's credit improves.
  • Lower payments — extending the repayment term reduces monthly outlays (though it may increase total interest).
  • Shorter term — paying the debt off faster and building equity sooner.
  • Switching rate type — moving between adjustable-rate and fixed-rate loans.
  • Cash-out refinancing — borrowing more than the outstanding balance and taking the difference in cash, converting home equity into liquidity.
  • Debt consolidation — combining several debts into one loan.

Costs and risks

Refinancing usually involves closing costs, appraisal and origination fees, and sometimes prepayment penalties on the old loan, so the savings must be weighed against the break-even point. Cash-out refinancing increases the secured debt on the home, and serial refinancing that strips equity while resetting the repayment clock ("loan flipping") was a significant abusive practice in the run-up to the 2008 financial crisis.

Corporate and sovereign refinancing

Companies refinance bonds and credit facilities to extend maturities, cut interest expense, or change covenants; distressed refinancing shades into debt restructuring. Governments routinely refinance maturing public debt by issuing new securities.
