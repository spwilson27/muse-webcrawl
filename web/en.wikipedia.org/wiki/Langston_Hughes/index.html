Langston Hughes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Langston Hughes in 1936, photographed by Carl Van Vechten: the poet smiling in a suit, Harlem's laureate]

James Mercer Langston Hughes (1 February 1901 – 22 May 1967) was an American poet, social activist, novelist, playwright and columnist, a leader of the Harlem Renaissance and the poet who, more than any other, set Black American vernacular — blues, jazz, church and street — at the centre of American poetry.

His first famous poem, 'The Negro Speaks of Rivers', was written at 18 on a train to Mexico and published in The Crisis in 1921. Books followed: The Weary Blues (1926), Fine Clothes to the Jew (1927), the novel Not Without Laughter (1930), the story collection The Ways of White Folks (1934), and the poetry volumes Shakespeare in Harlem (1942) and Montage of a Dream Deferred (1951), source of the question — what happens to a dream deferred — that Lorraine Hansberry lifted for A Raisin in the Sun.

Life and work

Born in Joplin, Missouri, raised largely by his grandmother in Lawrence, Kansas, Hughes worked as a seaman (Africa and Europe), a busboy in Paris and Washington — where he famously slipped his poems beside Vachel Lindsay's plate — and from the 1930s lived the writer's life: journalism, the 'Simple' stories for the Chicago Defender, theatre (the Suitcase Theatre in Harlem), translations of Lorca and Neruda, and travels from Haiti to the Soviet Union.

Politics and legacy

Summoned before McCarthy's committee in 1953, Hughes — whose radical thirties verse embarrassed him in the fifties — gave careful testimony and kept writing. His ashes rest beneath a floor medallion at Harlem's Schomburg Center, inscribed with his rivers; his home on East 127th Street is a landmark, and his dream-deferred question remains one of American literature's most quoted single lines — cited here by description, not quotation.
