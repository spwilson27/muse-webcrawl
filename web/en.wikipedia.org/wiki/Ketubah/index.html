Ketubah

A ketubah (Hebrew: כְּתוּבָּה, "written thing"; plural ketubot) is a Jewish marriage contract. It is considered an integral part of a traditional Jewish marriage, and outlines the rights and responsibilities of the groom in relation to the bride.

History

The ketubah dates to antiquity; the rabbis of the Talmudic era formalised it, according to tradition under Simeon ben Shetach in the 1st century BCE, so that divorcing a wife would not be a trivial matter for a husband. Traditionally it was written in Aramaic, the vernacular of the time.

Contents

The standard text records the date and place of the wedding, the names of the bride and groom, and the groom's obligations: to provide food, clothing and marital relations, and to pay a specified sum (the traditional 200 zuz for a virgin bride, 100 for a widow) in the event of divorce or his death. It is signed by two witnesses.

Ceremony and art

The ketubah is read aloud under the chuppah (wedding canopy) and given to the bride for safekeeping. Illuminated ketubot, richly decorated with floral and geometric motifs, are a notable genre of Jewish folk art, particularly from Italy and the Ottoman world.
