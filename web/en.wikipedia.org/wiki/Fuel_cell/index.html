Fuel cell

A fuel cell is an electrochemical cell that converts the chemical energy of a fuel (often hydrogen) and an oxidising agent (often oxygen) into electricity through a pair of redox reactions. Unlike a battery, a fuel cell requires a continuous supply of fuel and oxygen to sustain the reaction. When hydrogen is the fuel, the only products are electricity, water and heat.

How it works

A typical hydrogen fuel cell consists of an anode, a cathode and an electrolyte membrane between them. At the anode, a catalyst splits hydrogen molecules into protons and electrons; the protons pass through the membrane while the electrons flow through an external circuit, producing an electric current. At the cathode, protons, electrons and oxygen combine to form water. Individual cells produce under one volt, so many are combined in a "stack" to reach useful voltages.

Types

Fuel cells are classified by their electrolyte and operating temperature. Proton-exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) operate at relatively low temperatures and are used in vehicles and portable power. Alkaline fuel cells powered spacecraft including the Space Shuttle. Phosphoric acid, molten carbonate and solid oxide fuel cells operate at progressively higher temperatures and are used mainly for stationary power generation; solid oxide cells can exceed 60 percent electrical efficiency and use heat recovery to go higher.

History and applications

The principle was demonstrated by William Grove in 1839–1842, and practical cells were developed in the twentieth century by Francis Bacon and others. NASA used fuel cells in the Gemini and Apollo programmes. Applications today include forklifts, buses and cars such as the Toyota Mirai and Hyundai Nexo, backup power, combined heat and power units, and submarines. Barriers to wider adoption include the cost of catalysts such as platinum, durability, and the production and distribution of hydrogen.
