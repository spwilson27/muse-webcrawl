Fabergé egg

A Fabergé egg is a jewelled egg created by the workshop of Peter Carl Fabergé in Saint Petersburg, Russia. About 69 eggs were made between 1885 and 1917, of which 57 survive. The most famous are the 52 "Imperial" eggs commissioned by Tsars Alexander III and Nicholas II as Easter gifts for their wives and mothers.

History

The first Imperial egg, the Hen Egg of 1885, was a white enamelled shell opening to a golden yolk containing a golden hen and a miniature crown. The tradition continued annually until 1917. Each egg contained a "surprise" — miniatures, portraits, clockwork animals or replicas such as the Trans-Siberian train. After the Russian Revolution the eggs were confiscated by the Bolsheviks, and many were later sold abroad.

Locations today

Surviving Imperial eggs are held by museums and collectors including the Fabergé Museum in Saint Petersburg (which holds the Vekselberg collection), the Kremlin Armoury, the British Royal Collection, and the Metropolitan Museum of Art. Several eggs remain missing. Individual Imperial eggs have fetched over US$10 million at auction, and the Rothschild Egg sold for a record price in 2007.
