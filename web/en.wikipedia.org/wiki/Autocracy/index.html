Autocracy

Autocracy is a system of government in which absolute power is held by the autocrat, one person whose decisions are subject to no legal restraints or regularised mechanisms of popular control. The word derives from the Greek autokrateia (αὐτοκράτεια), "self-power", combining autos (self) and kratos (power, rule). Autocracy is a form of authoritarianism in which a single individual, rather than a party, military, or class, is the locus of power.

Autocracy and related concepts

Autocracy differs from oligarchy (rule by a small elite) and from democracy (rule by the people). Historically, the title "autocrat" was used by the emperors of Byzantium (autokrator) and by the tsars of Russia, whose ideology of autocracy (Russian: samoderzhavie) held that the monarch ruled by divine right, accountable only to God. An absolute monarchy is an autocratic form of government, though some absolute monarchs are constrained by custom, while republican dictatorships can also be autocratic.

Political scientists distinguish autocracies by their supporting institutions: personalist regimes, single-party states, and military regimes, noting that real-world autocracies often combine elections, legislatures, and courts that are subordinated to the ruler. Modern measures such as the Polity data series and the V-Dem Institute's datasets classify regimes along a spectrum between closed autocracy and liberal democracy, and track processes of "democratic backsliding" or autocratisation.

Historical examples

Autocratic rule has been the most common form of government through much of history: the Roman emperors after Augustus, the Byzantine basileus, the caliphs, imperial China under the Mandate of Heaven, and the absolutist monarchies of early modern Europe, such as Louis XIV of France, exemplify it. In the 20th century, autocratic power was exercised by dictators including Benito Mussolini, Adolf Hitler, Joseph Stalin, Mao Zedong, and others, typically legitimated by mass parties and personality cults.

Characteristics

Autocracies typically exhibit concentrated executive power, limited political pluralism, suppression of opposition media and parties, and control of the judiciary. Succession is a persistent weakness of autocratic systems, since the transfer of power is rarely institutionalised. Scholars debate the performance of autocracies in economic growth and stability, noting both rapid modernisation cases and systematic problems of information flow, corruption, and policy error associated with unchecked rule.
