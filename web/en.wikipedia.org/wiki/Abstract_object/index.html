Abstract object

In metaphysics and the philosophy of mathematics, an abstract object is an object that does not exist in space or time and is causally inert — it cannot be touched, seen in the ordinary sense, or act upon anything. Standard candidate examples include numbers, sets, propositions, properties and universals, fictional characters (on some views), and the objects studied by pure mathematics. Abstract objects are contrasted with concrete objects such as tables, stars and people, which are spatiotemporal and causally active.

The distinction

The abstract–concrete distinction is usually drawn by three overlapping criteria: abstract objects lack spatial and temporal location; they have no causal powers; and they are not perceivable by the senses. Some cases are contested — for example, whether musical works, games, or corporations are abstract objects or a special kind of concrete (socially constructed) entity. Plato's theory of Forms is the classic ancestor of the view that abstract entities are fully real and independent of minds.

Debates

Platonists (realists) hold that abstract objects exist independently of language and thought; nominalists deny this and attempt to paraphrase away apparent reference to them. The indispensability argument of Quine and Putnam holds that because mathematics — which quantifies over numbers and sets — is indispensable to science, we are committed to the existence of abstract objects. Benacerraf's dilemma poses an epistemological challenge: if abstract objects are causally inert, how can we know anything about them? These questions drive work in philosophy of mathematics, philosophy of language (where propositions are often treated as abstract objects) and metaontology.

Abstract object theory

"Abstract object theory" is also the name of a formal system developed by Edward Zalta, in which objects can exemplify or encode properties; abstract objects are those that encode properties without exemplifying spatial ones. The theory has been applied to model fictional objects, possible worlds and mathematical structures within a single axiomatised framework.
