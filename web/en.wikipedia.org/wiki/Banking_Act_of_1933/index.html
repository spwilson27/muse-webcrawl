The Banking Act of 1933 (Pub. L. 73–66) was a statute enacted by the United States Congress that established the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) and imposed various banking reforms. Its provisions separating commercial banking from investment banking activities are commonly known as the Glass–Steagall Act, after its sponsors Senator Carter Glass and Representative Henry B. Steagall.

Background

The Act was a response to the collapse of the American banking system during the Great Depression: some 9,000 banks failed in the years after the Wall Street Crash of 1929, wiping out depositors' savings. Congressional investigations, notably the Pecora Commission hearings, documented conflicts of interest and speculative abuses by banks that combined deposit-taking with securities underwriting. The Act followed the bank holiday declared in March 1933 and the Emergency Banking Act.

Provisions

Sections 16, 20, 21, and 32 — the Glass–Steagall provisions — prohibited commercial banks from underwriting or dealing in securities (with limited exceptions such as government bonds), barred securities firms from accepting deposits, and prohibited interlocking directorates between the two. The Act also created federal deposit insurance, administered by the new FDIC with initial coverage of $2,500 per depositor, and introduced Regulation Q, which prohibited interest on demand deposits and capped rates on savings deposits.

Later history

Over the following decades the separation was progressively eroded by regulatory interpretation and court decisions. The Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act of 1999 repealed Sections 20 and 32, allowing affiliations between commercial banks, investment banks, and insurers. Debate over the repeal's role in the 2008 financial crisis led to partial re-imposition of separation-like rules in the Volcker Rule of the Dodd–Frank Act of 2010. The deposit-insurance provisions of the 1933 Act remain in force.
