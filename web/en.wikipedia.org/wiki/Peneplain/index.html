A peneplain (from Latin paene, "almost", hence "almost a plain") is a low-relief plain formed by prolonged erosion. In the theory of the cycle of erosion proposed by William Morris Davis, a peneplain is the final stage of landscape evolution: mountains and valleys are worn down over tens of millions of years of tectonic stability into a featureless surface close to sea level, interrupted only by residual hills called monadnocks.

The Davisian concept

Davis proposed that landscapes evolve through "youth", "maturity", and "old age". Rivers first cut deep valleys; then valley floors widen by lateral erosion and slopes decline; finally the whole region is reduced to a peneplain at base level. Uplift could restart the cycle, leaving elevated peneplains — "uplifted peneplains" — identifiable by accordant summit levels.

Criticism and modern views

The peneplain concept was central to geomorphology for half a century but came under heavy criticism in the mid-20th century. Critics noted that the immense spans of tectonic quiet required are rare, that isostatic rebound complicates reduction to sea level, and that many supposed peneplains could be explained by other processes (etching, pediplanation, marine planation). Walther Penck proposed an alternative model of slope retreat producing pediplains. Nevertheless, very old, low-relief surfaces such as parts of the Canadian Shield, Western Australia, and the African interior are still commonly described as peneplains, and the term survives in general use for extensive planation surfaces.
