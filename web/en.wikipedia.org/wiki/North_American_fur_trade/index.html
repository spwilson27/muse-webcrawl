North American fur trade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The North American fur trade is the (typically) historical commercial trade in furs and animal pelts from 16th to 19th century North America. Europeans traded manufactured goods — metal tools, firearms, textiles, and alcohol — to Indigenous peoples in exchange for pelts, especially beaver, whose underfur was prized by European hat makers for making felt.

Origins

From the early 16th century, fishermen and explorers such as Jacques Cartier traded intermittently with Indigenous peoples along the Atlantic coast and St. Lawrence River. After the founding of Quebec in 1608 by Samuel de Champlain, the trade became organized and pushed steadily westward, through the Great Lakes and eventually across the continent to the Pacific.

Colonial and company era

The trade shaped colonial politics: France built alliances with the Huron and Algonquian nations, English traders operated from Hudson Bay and the Atlantic colonies, and the ensuing Beaver Wars and colonial wars were partly struggles over fur country. Chartered companies dominated: the Hudson's Bay Company (founded 1670) from its posts on Hudson Bay, and the North West Company (1779) from Montreal. Their rivalry ended in a forced merger in 1821. In the United States, John Jacob Astor's American Fur Company and the Rocky Mountain fur trade's rendezvous system opened the trans-Mississippi West in the 1820s–1840s.

Indigenous peoples

Indigenous trappers, traders, guides, and women who processed pelts and acted as intermediaries were indispensable to the trade. It brought wealth and new goods but also epidemic disease, alcohol, dependency, and violent competition over hunting territories, transforming Indigenous societies across the continent.

Decline

By the mid-19th century, silk hats displaced beaver felt in fashion, beaver populations were depleted, and settlement advanced; the trade contracted, though a commercial fur trade in northern Canada and Alaska continued into the 20th century.
