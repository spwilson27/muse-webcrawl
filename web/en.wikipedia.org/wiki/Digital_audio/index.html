Digital audio

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Digital audio is a representation of sound recorded in, or converted into, digital form. In a digital audio system, sound (an analogue electrical signal) is converted into a stream of discrete numbers representing the air pressure at successive instants, stored and processed numerically, and converted back to analogue for playback. The technology underpins compact discs, digital audio workstations, streaming services, telephony, and virtually all modern music production and distribution.

Conversion

An analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) samples the signal at a fixed rate and quantises each sample to a fixed number of bits. The sampling rate determines the highest frequency that can be represented: by the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem, frequencies up to half the sampling rate (the Nyquist frequency) can be captured. Compact Disc audio uses 44,100 samples per second and 16 bits per sample, giving a frequency range up to about 22 kHz and a dynamic range of about 96 dB. Bit depth determines the quantisation noise floor; dithering is used to decorrelate quantisation error from the signal. On playback, a digital-to-analogue converter (DAC) reconstructs a smooth waveform from the samples.

The standard method of encoding is pulse-code modulation (PCM), a technique patented by Alec Reeves in 1937 and first used commercially in telephony and in the SIGSALY secure voice system during the Second World War. The first commercial digital recordings were made in the 1970s by Denon and Soundstream; the launch of the Compact Disc in 1982 brought digital audio to consumers.

Formats and compression

Uncompressed formats include WAV and AIFF. Because PCM requires about 10 MB per minute of stereo audio, compression became central to distribution. Lossless codecs (FLAC, ALAC) reduce size by roughly half while preserving every bit; lossy codecs (MP3, AAC, Opus) exploit psychoacoustic masking to discard inaudible information, achieving much smaller files. Streaming services typically deliver lossy audio at bit rates between 96 and 320 kbit/s, with lossless tiers increasingly offered.

Production and use

Digital audio workstations (DAWs) such as Pro Tools, Logic Pro, and Ableton Live allow non-destructive editing, mixing, and processing impossible with tape. Digital audio is also integral to broadcasting (DAB radio), cinema sound, video games, podcasting, and voice communication. Debates persist among audiophiles about high-resolution audio (sample rates above 44.1 kHz and depths above 16 bits), though controlled tests generally find standard CD resolution transparent for listening.
