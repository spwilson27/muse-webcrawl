Formation and evolution of the Solar System

Solar System formation
Age4.568 billion years
LocationLocal Interstellar Cloud, Orion Arm
Nearest starProxima Centauri (4.2465 ly)
Prevailing theoryNebular hypothesis

The formation and evolution of the Solar System began about 4.568 billion years ago with the gravitational collapse of a small part of a giant molecular cloud. Most of the collapsing mass collected in the centre, forming the Sun, while the rest flattened into a protoplanetary disk out of which the planets, moons, asteroids and other small Solar System bodies formed.

Nebular hypothesis

The widely accepted model, known as the nebular hypothesis, was first developed in the 18th century by Emanuel Swedenborg, Immanuel Kant and Pierre-Simon Laplace. Under this model, the cloud collapse may have been triggered by shock waves from a nearby supernova. As the cloud contracted, conservation of angular momentum caused it to rotate faster and flatten into a disk.

Formation of the planets

Within the disk, dust grains collided and stuck together, growing through accretion into kilometre-sized planetesimals, and eventually into planetary embryos. Inside the frost line, where temperatures were too high for volatile compounds to condense, the rocky terrestrial planets — Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars — formed. Beyond it, the cores of the giant planets grew massive enough to capture hydrogen and helium, becoming Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.

Late Heavy Bombardment and after

Models such as the Nice model propose that the giant planets migrated after formation, scattering planetesimals and possibly causing the Late Heavy Bombardment of the inner planets about 4 billion years ago. Since then, the Solar System's evolution has been comparatively quiet, dominated by impacts, tidal interactions and the slow brightening of the Sun.

Future

In roughly 5 billion years the Sun will exhaust the hydrogen in its core and expand into a red giant, engulfing the inner planets, before shedding its outer layers and becoming a white dwarf.
