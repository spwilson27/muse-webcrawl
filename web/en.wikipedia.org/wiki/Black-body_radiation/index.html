Black-body radiation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Graph of Planck's law: spectral radiance versus wavelength for black bodies at several temperatures]
Black-body radiation curves according to Planck's law; classical theory (Rayleigh–Jeans) diverges at short wavelengths.

Black-body radiation is the thermal electromagnetic radiation emitted by an idealised opaque, non-reflective body (a black body) in thermodynamic equilibrium with its environment. It has a specific, continuous spectrum of wavelengths inversely related to intensity, depending only on the body's temperature.

The attempt to derive this spectrum from classical physics failed at short wavelengths — the ultraviolet catastrophe — and Max Planck's resolution of the problem in 1900, introducing energy quanta E = hν, marks the birth of quantum theory. The Cosmic microwave background radiation left over from the early universe is the most perfect black-body spectrum measured in nature.

Laws of black-body radiation

Planck's law gives the spectral radiance at each wavelength as a function of temperature alone. Two simpler laws follow from it: Wien's displacement law (the peak wavelength is inversely proportional to temperature — the Sun's spectrum peaks in the visible, a human body's in the infrared) and the Stefan–Boltzmann law (total radiated power grows as the fourth power of temperature, P ∝ T⁴). At long wavelengths Planck's law reduces to the classical Rayleigh–Jeans law; at short wavelengths it approaches Wien's law.

History

Gustav Kirchhoff defined the black body and established that its spectrum was a universal function of temperature (1859–60). Josef Stefan (1879) and Ludwig Boltzmann (1884) derived the T⁴ law, and Wilhelm Wien proposed a distribution law (1896) that failed at long wavelengths, while the classical Rayleigh–Jeans derivation diverged at short ones. On 14 December 1900 Planck presented to the German Physical Society his derivation based on quantised energy exchange — a step he took reluctantly and whose revolutionary meaning was recognised first by Albert Einstein in 1905.

Applications

Black-body laws govern the radiation of stars (whose temperatures are read from their spectra), thermal imaging, incandescent light, industrial pyrometry, and cosmology: the 2.725 K cosmic microwave background, measured by COBE to match a black body to within parts in 10⁵, is key evidence for the Big Bang.
