The corpus luteum (Latin for "yellow body"; plural corpora lutea) is a temporary endocrine structure in the female ovary formed from the ovarian follicle after ovulation. It develops from the granulosa and theca cells left behind when the mature follicle ruptures and releases its oocyte, and it secretes chiefly progesterone — together with moderate amounts of oestradiol and inhibin A — which prepare and maintain the endometrium for a possible pregnancy during the luteal phase of the menstrual (or oestrous) cycle.

Development and structure

After ovulation, the surge of luteinising hormone (LH) transforms the follicular cells: granulosa cells enlarge into granulosa lutein cells and the theca interna forms theca lutein cells around them, while blood vessels invade the former follicular cavity. The structure takes its yellow colour from carotenoid pigments, notably lutein, concentrated in its steroid-producing cells. In humans the corpus luteum reaches roughly 2 cm in diameter within about a week of ovulation.

Function

Progesterone from the corpus luteum converts the proliferative endometrium into a secretory lining capable of supporting implantation, suppresses further follicular development through negative feedback, and maintains early pregnancy by preventing menstruation. Inhibin A helps suppress follicle-stimulating hormone during the luteal phase.

Fate

If fertilisation does not occur, the corpus luteum regresses after about 10–14 days in a process called luteolysis, driven in part by prostaglandin F2α; progesterone falls, menstruation follows, and the structure is replaced by a pale fibrous scar, the corpus albicans. If pregnancy occurs, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) secreted by the implanting embryo "rescues" the corpus luteum, which continues producing progesterone until the placenta takes over steroid production at roughly 7–10 weeks of gestation.

Clinical significance

Corpus luteum cysts form when the structure fills with fluid or blood; most are asymptomatic and resolve spontaneously, though rupture can cause acute abdominal pain and internal bleeding. Insufficient progesterone production ("luteal phase defect") has been proposed as a cause of infertility and recurrent miscarriage, though its definition and treatment remain debated.
