Infinitesimal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Infinitesimals were used heuristically in early calculus.

In mathematics, an infinitesimal number is a quantity that is closer to zero than any standard real number, but is not zero itself. Infinitesimals do not exist in the standard real number system, because the real numbers satisfy the Archimedean property: for any positive real, some multiple of it exceeds 1. Nevertheless, infinitesimal reasoning was central to the invention of calculus by Newton and Leibniz, and rigorous frameworks for infinitesimals were developed in the twentieth century.

History

The method of indivisibles of Cavalieri and the adequality of Fermat anticipated infinitesimal methods in the seventeenth century. Leibniz's notation dx and dy treated differentials as infinitely small increments, and much of eighteenth-century analysis proceeded on this basis. Critics, most famously George Berkeley in The Analyst (1734), mocked infinitesimals as "the ghosts of departed quantities". In the nineteenth century, Cauchy, Bolzano, and Weierstrass rebuilt calculus on the rigorous concept of the limit, and infinitesimals were banished from standard analysis.

Nonstandard analysis

In 1960 Abraham Robinson constructed the hyperreal numbers, an ordered field extending the reals that contains genuine infinitesimals and infinitely large numbers, using the compactness theorem of mathematical logic. In this framework, the derivative of a function can be defined as the standard part of a ratio of infinitesimal increments, justifying much of Leibniz's intuition. A transfer principle guarantees that first-order statements true of the reals are true of the hyperreals.

Other frameworks

The surreal numbers also contain infinitesimals. In smooth infinitesimal analysis, developed within category theory and topos models, nilpotent infinitesimals (quantities whose square is zero) support a synthetic treatment of differential geometry, at the price of using intuitionistic logic. Dual numbers, with a nilpotent unit ε where ε² = 0, implement automatic differentiation in computing.

Properties

A number ε is infinitesimal if |ε| < 1/n for every positive integer n. Sums and products of infinitesimals with finite numbers behave intuitively: a finite number plus an infinitesimal is infinitely close to that number, and every finite hyperreal has a unique standard part — the real number infinitely close to it.
