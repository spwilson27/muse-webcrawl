Jacobean architecture

Jacobean architecture is the second phase of Renaissance architecture in England, following the Elizabethan style. It is named after King James I of England (James VI of Scotland), whose reign (1603–1625) it corresponds to. The style continued the Elizabethan mixture of late Gothic forms with classical detailing, increasingly influenced by Flemish and German pattern books, and is seen mainly in the great country houses of the period.

Characteristics

Jacobean houses are typically built of brick or stone in symmetrical plans, often E- or H-shaped, with large mullioned and transomed windows, strapwork decoration, shaped gables curving in Flemish fashion, turrets and towers, and elaborate chimneys. Interiors feature rich plaster ceilings, carved wooden screens, ornate fireplaces, and long galleries. Classical orders were applied decoratively rather than structurally, often stacked on façades in a manner more picturesque than archaeologically correct.

Examples

Notable examples include Hatfield House in Hertfordshire (built by Robert Cecil, 1607–1611), Audley End in Essex, Knole in Kent, Bramshill House in Hampshire, and Charlton House in London. The architect Inigo Jones, who introduced a purer Italian Palladian classicism with the Queen's House at Greenwich (begun 1616) and the Banqueting House in Whitehall (1619–1622), signaled the eclipse of the Jacobean style by disciplined classical design.
