Tektite

Tektites (from Ancient Greek tēktós, "molten") are gravel-sized bodies composed of black, green, brown or grey natural glass formed from terrestrial debris ejected during meteorite impacts. The term was coined by Austrian geologist Franz Eduard Suess (1867–1941), son of Eduard Suess. Tektites generally range in size from millimetres to centimetres.

Characteristics

Tektites are among the driest rocks known, containing on average only about 0.005% water — far less than the volcanic glasses they superficially resemble. They have a homogeneous composition, contain virtually no crystalline inclusions other than rare lechatelierite (fused silica), and frequently show distinctive sculptured, aerodynamic shapes such as spheres, teardrops, dumbbells and discs, produced as molten droplets solidified during flight.

Strewn fields

Tektites are found in only a handful of widely scattered regions called strewn fields, each associated with a known or inferred impact crater:

  • The Australasian strewn field, the largest, covers Southeast Asia, Australia and surrounding oceans; the source crater is believed to lie in Indochina but has not been conclusively located.
  • The Central European strewn field contains the green moldavites of Bohemia and Moravia, linked to the Ries crater in Germany about 15 million years ago.
  • The North American strewn field includes the bediasites of Texas and georgiaites, about 35 million years old and associated with the Chesapeake Bay impact crater.
  • The Ivory Coast strewn field is associated with the Bosumtwi crater in Ghana.

Origin

Although exotic origins — including lunar volcanism — were once proposed, the scientific consensus is that tektites form when the energy of a large impact melts surface rock and ejects it into the upper atmosphere, where it cools and falls back over a wide area.
