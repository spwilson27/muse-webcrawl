Claim of Right Act 1689

The Claim of Right Act 1689 is an Act of the Parliament of Scotland, passed on 11 April 1689. It is one of the key documents of Scottish constitutional law and of the "Glorious Revolution" settlement, declaring that James VII of Scotland had forfeited the Crown and setting out the conditions on which William II and Mary II accepted the Scottish throne.

Content

The Claim of Right listed the "grievances" of James VII's reign — his attempts to impose Roman Catholicism, his dispensing and suspending of laws, the use of standing armies and torture — and declared that, by these acts, he had "forfeited" the Crown of Scotland (a stronger formulation than the English Convention's fiction that James II had "abdicated"). It asserted the liberties of Parliament, including free elections and freedom of speech, barred Roman Catholics and those married to Catholics from the throne, and required the monarch to swear to uphold the Protestant religion. William and Mary accepted the Crown on these terms on 11 May 1689.

Legacy

Together with the English Bill of Rights 1689, the Claim of Right forms a foundation of the uncodified constitution of the United Kingdom. Unlike the English act, it is still cited as an example of a doctrine that a king could forfeit his crown — a principle invoked in later Scottish constitutional debates, including arguments about popular sovereignty traced to the Declaration of Arbroath (1320).
