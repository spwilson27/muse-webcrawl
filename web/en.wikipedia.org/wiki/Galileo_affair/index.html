Galileo affair

The Galileo affair was a sequence of events, beginning around 1610 and culminating with the trial and condemnation of Galileo Galilei by the Roman Catholic Inquisition in 1633 for his support of heliocentrism — the astronomical model in which the Earth and planets revolve around the Sun at the centre of the Solar System.

In 1610 Galileo published Sidereus Nuncius ("Starry Messenger"), describing observations that supported the Copernican model, including the moons of Jupiter and the roughness of the Moon's surface. His views met with opposition from some philosophers and clerics, and his support of heliocentrism was declared "foolish and absurd in philosophy, and formally heretical" by a panel of theologians in 1616. Galileo was forbidden to hold, teach or defend the Copernican doctrine.

The Dialogue and the trial

In 1632 Galileo published his Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, which, despite having received permission to publish, appeared to favour heliocentrism and placed the argument of Pope Urban VIII into the mouth of a character named Simplicio. Galileo was summoned to Rome in 1633, tried by the Inquisition, and found "vehemently suspect of heresy". He was forced to recant and spent the rest of his life under house arrest at his villa in Arcetri. The Dialogue was placed on the Index of Forbidden Books.

Aftermath

The affair became a defining episode in the perceived conflict between science and religion. Galileo's works were gradually rehabilitated; in 1992 Pope John Paul II acknowledged errors by the Church in its handling of the case. The famous phrase "Eppur si muove" ("And yet it moves"), said to have been muttered by Galileo after his recantation, is regarded as apocryphal.
