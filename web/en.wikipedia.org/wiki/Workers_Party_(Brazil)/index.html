The Workers' Party (Portuguese: Partido dos Trabalhadores, PT) is a major political party in Brazil. Founded in 1980 by trade unionists, intellectuals, Catholic activists influenced by liberation theology, and former leftists opposed to Brazil's military dictatorship, it grew from the industrial unions of São Paulo's ABC region into the largest left-wing party in Latin America.

History

The PT emerged from the strikes of 1978–1980 led by metalworkers' union leader Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. It built a distinctive internal structure of organized tendencies and participatory governance, pioneered in cities such as Porto Alegre with its participatory budgeting. Lula ran for president in 1989, 1994 and 1998 before winning in 2002.

In government

Under Lula (2003–2010), Brazil combined market-friendly macroeconomic policy with expanded social programs such as Bolsa Família, which sharply reduced poverty. Dilma Rousseff succeeded Lula in 2011 but was removed in a controversial impeachment in 2016 amid recession and the vast Petrobras corruption scandal (Operation Car Wash), which implicated PT figures; Lula himself was convicted in 2017, imprisoned in 2018 (barring him from that year's election), and later released after the Supreme Court annulled his convictions in 2021 on jurisdictional grounds. In 2022 Lula defeated incumbent Jair Bolsonaro and returned to the presidency in January 2023.

Organization and ideology

The party describes its goal as democratic socialism and combines currents from Marxism to social democracy. Its symbol is a red flag with a white star bearing the letters PT. It remains one of Brazil's largest parties in Congress, the state governments and union and social movements, including close (though formally independent) ties to the Central Única dos Trabalhadores (CUT) union federation and the Landless Workers' Movement (MST).
