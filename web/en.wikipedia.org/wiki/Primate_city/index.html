Primate city

A primate city dominates the urban hierarchy of its country.

A primate city (Latin: primus, "first") is the largest city in its country or region, disproportionately larger than any others in the urban hierarchy. A primate distribution is a rank-size distribution with one very large city and many much smaller cities and towns, and no intermediate-sized ones.

Definition

Geographer Mark Jefferson coined the term and developed the "law of the primate city" in 1939, defining a primate city as being "at least twice as large as the next largest city and more than twice as significant." A primate city need not be the capital: Bangkok, Thailand, is the archetypal example — primate city, capital, and overwhelmingly the country's dominant centre of population, commerce, and government.

Causes

Primacy is often associated with countries having a colonial past, in which a single port or administrative capital monopolised trade and government; a small territory; a population concentrated in one region; or a centralised political system. Economists and geographers debate how primacy relates to development: some argue it concentrates scarce resources productively, others that it starves regional centres and encourages rural-to-capital migration.

Measurement

Primacy is measured with indices such as the two-city index (population of the largest city divided by that of the second), the four-city index, and the eleven-city index. A two-city index above 2 is typically taken to indicate primacy.

Examples

  • Bangkok, Thailand — many times larger than the second city, Chiang Mai.
  • London, United Kingdom — roughly an order of magnitude larger than Birmingham.
  • Paris, France — long dominated by a centralised state.
  • Reykjavík, Iceland — contains well over half the national population in its metro area.
