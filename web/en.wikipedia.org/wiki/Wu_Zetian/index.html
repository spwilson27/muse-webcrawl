Wu Zetian

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Seated stone statue traditionally identified as Wu Zetian at Guangyuan, Sichuan]

Wu Zetian (624 – 16 December 705), also called Wu Zhao, was the only woman in Chinese history to rule as emperor in her own name. Entering the palace as a low-ranking concubine of Emperor Taizong, she rose under his son Gaozong to become empress (655), de facto co-ruler, then regent for her sons, and in 690 deposed the dynasty itself, proclaiming her own Zhou dynasty with herself as huangdi — Sacred and Divine Emperor. Her fifteen-year reign (690–705) expanded the examination system's role, patronised Buddhism as legitimation, governed through much-feared secret police, and kept the Tang realm prosperous and expanding, before she was forced to abdicate in old age and the Tang was restored.

Rise to power

Wu's path was unprecedented and ruthless: recalled from a convent by Gaozong, she displaced Empress Wang (whom she was accused of framing for the murder of Wu's own infant daughter — a charge historians still debate) and methodically eliminated Tang princes and ministers who opposed her. As Gaozong's health failed she governed jointly ('the Two Sages'); after his death (683) she ruled through, then deposed, her sons Zhongzong and Ruizong, founding her Zhou in 690 with a new capital cult at Luoyang and Buddhist prophecy (the Great Cloud Sutra) marshalled to justify a woman on the throne.

Reign and legacy

Her government promoted talent through examinations and recommendation regardless of birth, developed the civil service that later Tang rulers inherited, campaigned successfully in the north-west, and maintained prosperity — while her reign's first half ran on informers and purges (the酷吏 'cruel officials'), relaxed in her later years. In 705 a palace coup restored Zhongzong; Wu died months later and was buried with Gaozong at the Qianling mausoleum, before a famous wordless stele — whether humility, arrogance or later loss, its blankness is itself debated. Confucian historiography damned her as a usurping monster; modern reassessment credits a capable, if ruthless, ruler and a pivotal figure for women's history.
