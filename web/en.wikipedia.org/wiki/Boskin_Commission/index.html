The Boskin Commission, formally the Advisory Commission to Study the Consumer Price Index, was a body appointed by the United States Senate in 1995 to evaluate whether the Consumer Price Index (CPI) accurately measured changes in the cost of living. It was chaired by economist Michael Boskin, former chairman of the Council of Economic Advisers.

Findings

The commission's December 1996 report concluded that the CPI overstated inflation by approximately 1.1 percentage points per year. It identified several sources of bias: substitution bias (consumers shifting purchases when relative prices change), outlet substitution bias, new-product bias, and quality-change bias, where improvements in goods were not fully captured by price measurement.

Impact

The findings had potentially enormous fiscal implications, since Social Security benefits, tax brackets, and many contracts are indexed to the CPI; a lower measured inflation rate would slow the growth of benefits and federal spending. The Bureau of Labor Statistics subsequently made a number of methodological changes, including geometric-mean formulas for many categories and hedonic quality adjustments. The report remains influential and debated in discussions of price measurement.

Criticism

Some economists and labor groups criticized the report as overstating the bias and as motivated by deficit-reduction politics. Later studies generally found a smaller residual bias in the revised CPI.
