Russian names

Russian names are generally composed of three parts: a given name, a patronymic, and a family name (surname). This full name is used in official documents and formal address. Russians also use a rich system of diminutive forms of given names in informal contexts. The naming customs derive from the East Slavic tradition shared in varying forms with Ukraine and Belarus.

Given names

Russian given names are typically chosen from a traditional stock, historically drawn from the Orthodox Church calendar of saints' names, many of them of Greek, Latin or Hebrew origin (Ivan, Nikolai, Anna, Maria, Olga), alongside older Slavic names (Vladimir, Boris, Svetlana, Lyudmila). Parents choose the name; historically a child's name was strongly influenced by the saint whose feast day fell near the birth or baptism. In the Soviet era some new names were coined from revolutionary vocabulary (e.g. Vladlen, from Vladimir Lenin; Ninel; Oktyabrina), though most never became widespread.

Patronymics

The patronymic is formed from the father's given name with a suffix: -ovich / -evich for men and -ovna / -evna for women (e.g. Ivan Petrovich, Anna Petrovna—children of Pyotr). The combination of given name and patronymic is the standard polite form of address in Russian, roughly equivalent to using "Mr" or "Ms" plus surname in English, and is used in workplaces, schools and official settings. Icelanders in Russia and foreigners may adapt or omit patronymics.

Surnames

Russian surnames typically end in -ov/-ev, -in/-yn (masculine) or -ova/-eva, -ina/-yna (feminine), or in -sky/-skaya, originally a toponymic or clerical form. Surnames were adopted gradually: nobility from the 16th century, merchants and townspeople later, and the peasantry largely only after the abolition of serfdom in 1861 and into the early Soviet period. Common surnames include Ivanov, Smirnov, Kuznetsov, Popov and Lebedev.

Diminutives

Almost every Russian given name has one or more diminutive forms formed with suffixes such as -sha, -ya, -enka or -ochka, expressing familiarity and affection: Alexander → Sasha, Sanya, Shura; Ekaterina → Katya, Katya–Katenka; Maria → Masha. Some diminutives are used as everyday names among friends and family, while the full form (with patronymic) is reserved for formal use. Use of a diminutive toward a stranger can be considered over-familiar.
