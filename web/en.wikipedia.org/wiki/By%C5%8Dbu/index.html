Byōbu
Japanese屏風 ("wind wall")
MediumInk and colour on paper or silk, often with gold leaf
FormFolding screen, commonly six panels

Byōbu (屏風, literally "wind wall") are Japanese folding screens made from several joined panels, used to separate interiors and enclose private spaces; they are typically decorated with paintings in ink and colour on paper or silk. Byōbu were introduced to Japan from China in the Nara period (710–794), developing from Chinese prototypes, and by the Momoyama and Edo periods they had become one of the most prestigious formats of Japanese painting.

Construction

A byōbu consists of wooden lattice frames covered with layers of paper or silk, joined by paper hinges that allow the screen to fold in a zigzag and stand freely. The most common form is the six-panel screen (rokumai byōbu), often made in pairs; smaller two-panel screens and tall formats also exist. A continuous painted composition flows across the panels, and from the late 16th century many luxury screens used gold leaf grounds (kinji), producing luminous effects by candlelight.

Art history

Byōbu were decorated by the great schools of Japanese painting — Kano, Rinpa and Tosa — with subjects including landscapes, seasonal flowers and birds, scenes from literature such as The Tale of Genji, and panoramic views of Kyoto (rakuchū rakugai zu). Masterpieces by artists such as Hasegawa Tōhaku and Ogata Kōrin are designated National Treasures. Portable, decorative and functional, byōbu remain iconic furnishings of Japanese interiors and a major category in museum collections worldwide.
