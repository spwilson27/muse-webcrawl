Currency board

A currency board is a monetary authority required by law to maintain a fixed exchange rate with a foreign currency and to back the domestic monetary base fully with reserves of that foreign currency. Unlike a central bank, a currency board has no discretionary monetary policy: it issues notes and coins only when it receives equivalent reserves, and it cannot lend to the government or act as lender of last resort.

How it works

Under a currency board, domestic currency is convertible on demand into the anchor currency at the fixed rate. The money supply expands and contracts automatically with the balance of payments: inflows of foreign currency increase reserves and hence the domestic money supply, while outflows reduce it. Interest rates are therefore determined by conditions in the anchor-currency market.

History and examples

Currency boards originated in Britain's colonies in the nineteenth century, beginning with Mauritius in 1849, and operated across the British Empire. Modern examples include Hong Kong's Linked Exchange Rate System (since 1983), Bulgaria's currency board (since 1997), and Bosnia and Herzegovina's. Argentina's Convertibility Law (1991–2002) resembled a currency board but deviated from orthodox rules, and the system collapsed during the Argentine great depression.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advocates argue that currency boards deliver low inflation, credibility, and stable trade conditions, especially for small open economies with a history of monetary instability. Critics point to the loss of an independent monetary policy, the inability to respond to domestic recessions or banking crises, and the risk that the fixed rate becomes overvalued.
