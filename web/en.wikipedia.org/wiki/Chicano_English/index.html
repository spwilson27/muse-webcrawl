Chicano English

Chicano English, also known as Mexican-American English, is a dialect of American English spoken primarily by Mexican Americans, especially in the southwestern United States, ranging from Texas to California, and in Chicago. It is a natively acquired variety of English: most of its speakers are native English speakers, many of them monolingual, and the dialect is distinct from the English spoken by Spanish speakers learning English as a second language.

Origins

The dialect developed in Mexican-American communities in the decades after the Mexican–American War (1846–1848), when the United States annexed the northern territories of Mexico and large Spanish-speaking populations came under US rule. In the twentieth century, segregation of Mexican-American children in schools and neighbourhoods helped consolidate a distinct ethnic dialect that persisted even as later generations shifted from Spanish to English.

Phonology

Characteristic features include:

  • A tendency toward syllable-timed rhythm influenced by Spanish, with less reduction of unstressed vowels than in other American dialects.
  • Merger or interchange of the vowels /ɪ/ and /i/, so that pairs such as ship and sheep may be less distinct.
  • Realisation of /tʃ/ toward [ʃ] in some contexts, and variable devoicing of /z/.
  • Final consonant clusters may be simplified, and /v/ may be pronounced closer to [b].

Grammar and vocabulary

Chicano English borrows some lexical items and calques from Spanish (for example, using "barely" in the sense of "just recently", reflecting Spanish apenas), and exhibits some grammatical features shared with other vernacular dialects of English. Importantly, linguists emphasise that it is a fully formed dialect with its own stable norms, not an "accented" or imperfect form of English; many speakers can style-shift between Chicano English and more standard varieties depending on audience and setting.

Perceptions

Because the dialect is strongly associated with ethnic identity, its features are sometimes imitated — and stigmatised — in popular media. Sociolinguistic research treats it as an important case study of how a dialect can emerge from language contact and then be maintained by speakers who no longer speak the ancestral language.
