The true airspeed (TAS; also KTAS, for knots true airspeed) of an aircraft is the speed of the aircraft relative to the air mass through which it is moving. Unlike the speed shown directly on the airspeed indicator, true airspeed accounts for the lower density of air at altitude and non-standard temperatures, and it is the speed used in flight planning and navigation before wind is applied to obtain ground speed.

The airspeed chain

Pilots work through a series of corrections:

• Indicated airspeed (IAS) – read directly from the airspeed indicator.
• Calibrated airspeed (CAS) – IAS corrected for instrument and position error.
• Equivalent airspeed (EAS) – CAS corrected for compressibility at high speed.
• True airspeed (TAS) – EAS corrected for air density: TAS increases roughly 2% above CAS per 1,000 ft of altitude at constant indicated speed, because the thinner air must be traversed faster to produce the same dynamic pressure.

Calculation and use

TAS can be computed from Mach number and the local speed of sound (TAS = Mach × speed of sound at the ambient temperature), or approximately on a flight computer (E6B) from CAS, pressure altitude, and outside air temperature. A common rule of thumb adds about 2% of IAS per thousand feet. Navigation combines TAS with forecast wind to give heading and ground speed; range and fuel planning in aircraft handbooks are expressed in terms of TAS. At high altitude, jets flying at an indicated 250–280 knots may have a true airspeed above 450 knots.
