Royal Maundy is a religious service in the Church of England held on Maundy Thursday, at which the British monarch (or a royal representative) distributes small silver coins known as "Maundy money" to elderly pensioners in recognition of their service to their churches and communities.

History

The ceremony derives from the mandatum — Christ's command to love one another, exemplified by washing the disciples' feet — and English sovereigns have given alms on Maundy Thursday since at least the reign of King John (1210). Monarchs once washed recipients' feet, a practice discontinued after James II; from the 18th century the sovereign often sent a substitute, but Elizabeth II revived personal attendance, distributing the money herself at a different cathedral almost every year of her reign. Charles III continued the tradition.

The ceremony

The number of recipients equals the sovereign's age: that many men and that many women, nominated by local clergy of all denominations. Each receives two purses: a red purse containing ordinary coinage (£5.50 in recent practice, symbolising food and clothing money) and a white purse containing Maundy coins — 1p, 2p, 3p and 4p silver pieces totalling the sovereign's age in pence. The coins are legal tender but are treasured as souvenirs, trading well above face value. Yeomen of the Guard carry the purses on alms dishes, and participants wear ceremonial posies in memory of the perfumes once used against the smell of the poor.
