The Essex was an American whaleship from Nantucket, Massachusetts, launched in 1799. She is famous for having been attacked and sunk by a sperm whale in the remote Pacific Ocean in 1820; her crew's desperate open-boat voyage and resort to cannibalism became one of the best-known maritime disasters of the nineteenth century and a principal inspiration for Herman Melville's novel Moby-Dick (1851).

The final voyage

Under Captain George Pollard Jr., with first mate Owen Chase, Essex sailed in August 1819 for the whaling grounds off South America. On 20 November 1820, while her whaleboats pursued a pod of sperm whales, an exceptionally large bull sperm whale—estimated at 85 feet—rammed the ship twice, staving in her bow. The vessel sank in the equatorial Pacific, some 2,000 nautical miles from South America.

Ordeal and rescue

Twenty men took to three small whaleboats with meagre provisions. Fearing reports of cannibals in the nearer Society Islands, they attempted the far longer passage to South America. Starvation reduced the crews; the dead were eaten, and in one boat lots were drawn and Pollard's young cousin Owen Coffin was shot so the others might live. Eight men ultimately survived; Pollard's and Chase's boats were rescued in February 1821.

Legacy

Owen Chase published his Narrative of the Most Extraordinary and Distressing Shipwreck of the Whale-Ship Essex in 1821. Cabin boy Thomas Nickerson's account, written later, was published in 1984. The story was retold in Nathaniel Philbrick's In the Heart of the Sea (2000), adapted as a 2015 film.
