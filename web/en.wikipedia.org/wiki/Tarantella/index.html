Tarantella

The tarantella is a group of various southern Italian folk dances characterised by a fast, upbeat tempo, usually in 6/8 time, accompanied by mandolins, guitars, accordions and tambourines. The dance takes its name from the city of Taranto in Apulia, and is bound up with the legend of tarantism — the supposed cure, through frenzied dancing, of the bite of the "tarantula" wolf spider.

Tarantism legend

From at least the medieval period into the 20th century, peasants in southern Italy believed that the bite of the spider caused a hysterical condition whose only remedy was to dance for hours or days to the tarantella's driving rhythm until the poison was sweated out. The phenomenon, centred on the chapel of Saint Paul at Galatina, was studied by scholars such as Ernesto de Martino as a ritual of symbolic healing.

Regional forms and classical use

Regional variants include the Neapolitan tarantella, the Calabrian viddaneddha, and the Apulian pizzica, which has enjoyed a major folk revival through festivals such as the Notte della Taranta in Salento. Composers from Rossini ("La Danza") and Chopin to Mendelssohn and Liszt wrote stylised tarantellas, and the dance remains a staple of Italian celebrations, especially weddings.
