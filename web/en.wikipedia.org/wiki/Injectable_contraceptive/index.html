Injectable contraceptive

An injectable contraceptive is a hormonal method of birth control administered by injection, providing long-acting but reversible contraception without daily action by the user. The most widely used form is depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA), marketed as Depo-Provera, a progestin-only injection given every three months. Other forms include norethisterone enanthate (NET-EN), given every two months, and combined injectable contraceptives containing both a progestin and an estrogen, given monthly.

Mechanism and effectiveness

Injectables work primarily by suppressing ovulation; they also thicken cervical mucus and thin the uterine lining. With perfect use DMPA is more than 99% effective, though typical-use effectiveness is lower when repeat injections are late. A subcutaneous self-injectable version of DMPA (Depo-SubQ Provera 104, and the Sayana Press device) allows administration at home.

Side effects and considerations

Common effects include irregular bleeding or amenorrhea, weight gain, and a temporary reduction in bone mineral density, which prompted a boxed warning from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration; bone density generally recovers after discontinuation. Return of fertility can be delayed for many months after the last injection. Injectable contraceptives do not protect against sexually transmitted infections.
