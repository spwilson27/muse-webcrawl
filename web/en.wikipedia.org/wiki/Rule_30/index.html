Rule 30

Rule 30 is an elementary cellular automaton introduced by Stephen Wolfram in 1983. It produces complex, seemingly chaotic patterns from very simple deterministic rules, and is classified as a Class III (chaotic) behaviour in Wolfram's scheme. Rule 30 is of particular interest because its centre column passes statistical tests for randomness and was used as a random number generator in Wolfram's Mathematica software.

Ruleset

In an elementary cellular automaton, each cell has two possible states, 0 or 1, and its next state depends on its current state and the states of its two immediate neighbours. For Rule 30, the next state is given by the table below; the binary digits of the outputs, read left to right, are 00011110, which is 30 in decimal — hence the rule's name.

Current pattern111110101100011010001000
New state00011110

Equivalently, the new state of a cell with left neighbour L, centre C and right neighbour R is L XOR (C OR R).

Structure and appearance

Starting from a single black cell, Rule 30 generates a pattern that is aperiodic and irregular on the right side but contains recognisable triangular structures on the left. Wolfram conjectured that the centre column is effectively random; it has been shown to pass many tests for randomness, and in 2020 Wolfram announced prizes for resolving open questions about Rule 30, including whether the centre column is eventually periodic.

Applications

The centre column of Rule 30 was used as the default pseudorandom number generator in Mathematica. A pattern resembling Rule 30's output appears naturally on the shell of the cone snail Conus textile.
