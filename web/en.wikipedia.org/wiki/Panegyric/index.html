Panegyric

A panegyric is a formal public speech or written verse, delivered or composed in high praise of a person or thing. The word derives from the Greek panēgyrikos, originally meaning a speech suitable for a panēgyris, a public assembly or festival gathering. Panegyric is a species of epideictic (demonstrative) oratory, alongside its opposite, the invective or philippic.

Classical origins

In classical Greece, panegyrics were delivered at festivals such as the Olympic Games. The most famous example is the Panegyricus of Isocrates (380 BC), which ostensibly praised Athens while urging Greek unity against Persia. Gorgias and Lysias also composed festival orations of this kind. From these origins the genre came to denote any elaborate speech of praise.

Roman and late antique panegyric

In Rome the panegyric developed into a formal address of praise to the emperor, often delivered by a provincial delegate or court orator. Pliny the Younger's Panegyricus (AD 100), thanking Trajan for his consulship, became the model for the genre. A collection known as the Panegyrici Latini preserves twelve such speeches from the late 3rd and 4th centuries AD, addressed to emperors including Constantine and Theodosius. The court poet Claudian composed celebrated verse panegyrics for Honorius and the general Stilicho. Though constrained by flattery, these works are valuable historical sources for the late Roman Empire.

Later use

Panegyric remained a recognised literary form through the Byzantine, medieval, and Renaissance periods, applied to saints, kings, and patrons. In modern usage the word can carry an ironic shade, suggesting praise that is fulsome or uncritical. Its close relative is the eulogy, a speech of praise typically delivered at a funeral.
