Small group

A small group is a small number of people who interact with one another, share similar characteristics, and collectively have a sense of unity. In social psychology and communication studies, small groups are usually considered to range from about three to around twenty members — large enough for varied interaction, small enough that every member can perceive and interact with every other member as an individual.

Characteristics

Small groups are distinguished by direct, face-to-face (or mediated but personal) interaction, shared goals or interests, interdependence among members, and the development of norms, roles, and a group identity. The smallest meaningful group is often treated as the triad, since a dyad dissolves if one member leaves; sociologist Georg Simmel analysed how group dynamics change qualitatively when a third member joins a pair, enabling coalitions, mediation, and majority–minority splits.

Types of small groups

Common types include primary groups such as families and close friendships, which are long-lasting and emotionally significant; task or work groups formed to accomplish a specific objective; committees, teams, and juries; study and support groups; and reference groups against which individuals compare themselves. Small groups may be formally structured, with assigned roles and procedures, or informal and emergent.

Group development and dynamics

Bruce Tuckman's influential model describes group development through the stages of forming, storming, norming, and performing, later adding adjourning. Research on small groups examines leadership emergence, conformity, groupthink, social loafing, cohesion, decision-making processes, and conflict. Classic studies — such as Solomon Asch's conformity experiments and Irving Janis's work on groupthink — used small groups to reveal how social context shapes individual judgement and collective decisions.

Study

Small-group research spans social psychology, sociology, communication studies, organizational behaviour, and education. Because small groups are a basic unit of social life — the setting of families, workplaces, classrooms, and civic organizations — understanding their internal processes is central to understanding how individuals coordinate, deliberate, and influence one another.
