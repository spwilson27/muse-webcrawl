United States federal courts

The United States federal courts are the courts of the federal judiciary of the United States, established under Article III of the Constitution and by acts of Congress. They are distinct from state courts and have limited jurisdiction, chiefly federal questions and diversity of citizenship.

Federal judiciary
Supreme Court: 1
Courts of Appeals: 13 circuits
District Courts: 94 districts

Structure

At the top is the Supreme Court. Below are 13 courts of appeals — 12 geographic circuits plus the Federal Circuit — including the D.C. Circuit. Trial courts are the 94 district courts, supplemented by specialized courts such as bankruptcy courts, the Court of International Trade, and the Court of Federal Claims.

Judges

Article III judges are nominated by the President, confirmed by the Senate, and hold office during good behaviour, protecting judicial independence. The judiciary is administered by the Judicial Conference and the Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts.

Jurisdiction

Federal courts hear cases under federal law, treaties, and the Constitution, disputes between states, and cases between citizens of different states above a statutory amount.
