Tort

The law of torts allocates losses from civil wrongs

A tort is a civil wrong that causes someone else to suffer loss or harm, resulting in legal liability for the person who commits the tortious act. Tort law aims to provide relief to injured parties for harms caused by others, to impose liability on those responsible, and to deter others from committing harmful acts. The primary aims of tort law are to provide relief to injured parties, impose liability on those responsible for the harm, and deter others from committing harmful acts.

Categories

Torts fall into three general categories: intentional torts (e.g., battery, false imprisonment, conversion, trespass), negligent torts (where harm results from a breach of the duty of reasonable care, as in most traffic accidents), and strict liability torts (where liability attaches without proof of intent or negligence, as for abnormally dangerous activities and defective products).

Elements and defences

A negligence claim generally requires duty, breach, causation, and damages. Landmark authorities include Donoghue v Stevenson [1932], which articulated the neighbour principle underlying the modern duty of care, and Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad Co. (1928) on proximate cause and foreseeability. Common defences include consent, contributory or comparative negligence, and assumption of risk.

Tort reform

In the United States, "tort reform" debates concern proposals to limit damages, restrict class actions, or cap attorneys' fees, with proponents citing litigation costs and opponents citing deterrence and compensation of victims.
