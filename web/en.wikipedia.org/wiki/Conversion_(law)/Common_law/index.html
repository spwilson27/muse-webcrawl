Common law

A common law courtroom

Common law (also known as judicial precedent, judge-made law, or case law) is the body of law primarily developed through decisions of courts rather than by legislative statutes or executive regulation. A common law system gives precedential weight to past court decisions: where the facts of a case are similar, judges are bound by the principle of stare decisis to reach similar outcomes.

History

The common law originated in medieval England, where royal courts after the Norman Conquest gradually unified local customs into a law "common" to the whole realm. It was exported throughout the British Empire and remains the foundation of the legal systems of countries including the United States, Canada, Australia, India, and England and Wales, where it contrasts with civil law systems derived from Roman law traditions.

Characteristics

Common law systems are adversarial, rely on juries in many serious cases, and develop doctrine incrementally through published judicial opinions. Alongside case law, the historical common law tradition included the separate courts of equity, whose principles of fairness supplemented rigid common law rules; in most jurisdictions the two have been administratively merged while remaining doctrinally distinct.

Interaction with statute

Legislatures may override or codify common law rules by statute, and in fields such as commercial law much common law doctrine has been codified — for example in the Uniform Commercial Code in the United States. Courts, in turn, interpret statutes, and those interpretations themselves acquire precedential force.
