Trover

The action of trover in an early legal treatise

Trover is the name of a legal action in English common law for the recovery of the value of personal property that has been wrongfully converted by another person. The action of trover gave rise to the modern tort of conversion.

History

Trover developed in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries as an offshoot of trespass on the case. Plaintiffs originally alleged, as a legal fiction, that they had casually lost their goods, that the defendant had "found" them (French: trouvé, hence trover), and had converted them to his own use. The allegations of losing and finding became untraversable fictions, allowing the action to be used for any wrongful appropriation of goods, including by bailees and purchasers from thieves.

Nature of the action

In trover, the plaintiff recovered damages equal to the full value of the goods at the time of conversion, rather than the goods themselves (which were returned by actions such as replevin or detinue). Judgment for the plaintiff therefore operated as a forced judicial sale of the property to the defendant. Conversion is committed by an intentional exercise of dominion or control over another's chattel that so seriously interferes with the right of control that the actor may justly be required to pay the full value, as restated in the Restatement (Second) of Torts § 222A.
