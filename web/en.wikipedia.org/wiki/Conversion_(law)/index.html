Conversion (law)

Conversion is an intentional tort in common law jurisdictions consisting of the wrongful exercise of dominion or control over another person's personal property (chattels) in a manner inconsistent with the owner's rights. It is sometimes described as the civil-law counterpart of theft, though it does not require an intent to steal.

Elements

A claim for conversion generally requires: (1) the plaintiff's ownership or right to possession of the property; (2) the defendant's conversion by a wrongful act or disposition of the property; and (3) resulting damage. Acts constituting conversion include taking property without authority, refusing to return it after a lawful demand, misusing property bailed for a limited purpose, or destroying it. Unlike the related tort of trespass to chattels, conversion involves interference serious enough to justify requiring the defendant to pay the property's full value — in effect, a forced sale.

Scope and limits

Traditionally conversion applied only to tangible personal property, but courts have extended it to some intangible rights embodied in documents and, in many jurisdictions, to electronic funds and certain other intangibles. Conversion does not generally lie for land (real property). A good-faith purchaser or an innocent intermediary may still be liable, though statutes and commercial codes modify this for negotiable instruments and entrusted goods.

Remedies

The standard remedy is damages equal to the fair market value of the property at the time of conversion, plus interest; in some cases the owner may instead seek the property's return (replevin) or restitution.
