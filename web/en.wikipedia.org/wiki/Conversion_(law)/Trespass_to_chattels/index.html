Trespass to chattels

Chattels — tangible movable property

Trespass to chattels is a tort in common law jurisdictions whereby a person intentionally interferes with another's lawful possession of a chattel — movable personal property — by dispossessing them of it, using or intermeddling with it, or moving it. The interference must cause actual harm, such as damage to the property, deprivation of its use for a substantial time, or impairment of its condition, quality, or value.

Distinction from conversion

Trespass to chattels is a lesser interference than conversion, in which the defendant's dominion over the property is so serious that they may justly be required to pay its full value, effecting a forced sale. Moving someone's car a short distance may be trespass to chattels; taking and keeping it, or destroying it, is conversion.

Electronic trespass

The doctrine has been applied to electronic intrusions on computer systems. In eBay, Inc. v. Bidder's Edge, Inc. (2000), automated web crawlers aggregating auction data were preliminarily enjoined on a trespass to chattels theory. In Intel Corp. v. Hamidi (2003), the California Supreme Court limited the doctrine, holding that mass unsolicited emails to a company's servers, without damage to the system, did not constitute trespass to chattels.
