Catalyst

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without itself being consumed overall. It works by providing an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy, so more reactant particles have enough energy to react.

Catalysts are vital to industry and life: enzymes are biological catalysts; iron catalyses ammonia synthesis in the Haber process; platinum-group metals in catalytic converters clean car exhausts; vanadium(V) oxide drives the contact process for sulfuric acid. Catalysts do not change the equilibrium position or the overall energy change — they only speed the approach to equilibrium.

[Image: A catalytic converter honeycomb structure coated with catalyst metals]

Homogeneous and heterogeneous

A homogeneous catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants (e.g. acid catalysis in solution); a heterogeneous catalyst is in a different phase, usually a solid surface on which gas or liquid reactants adsorb — most industrial catalysts are heterogeneous.

History

Jöns Jacob Berzelius coined the term 'catalysis' in 1835 for a growing list of puzzling contact effects, including Döbereiner's platinum sponge igniting hydrogen (1823).
