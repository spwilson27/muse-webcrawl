Public rights of way in England and Wales

This article is about legally protected routes in England and Wales. For the general concept, see Right of way.

Public rights of way in England and Wales are routes over land on which members of the public have a legally protected right to pass and repass. Unlike access land under the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000, a right of way is a linear right along a defined line, and it persists even where the underlying land is privately owned. The surface of a public right of way is generally vested in the local highway authority, while the subsoil usually remains with the landowner.

Categories

Rights of way are recorded in four legal categories:

  • Public footpaths — for walkers (and dogs) only; the largest category.
  • Public bridleways — for walkers, horse riders and cyclists, who must give way to walkers and riders.
  • Restricted byways — for all non-motorised users, including horse-drawn vehicles; created by the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 to replace the older "road used as a public path" (RUPP) designation.
  • Byways open to all traffic (BOATs) — usable by all traffic including motor vehicles, though often unmetalled tracks.

The definitive map

Under the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949, surveying authorities must maintain a definitive map and statement showing the rights of way in their area. The map is conclusive evidence of the existence of the ways shown, though the absence of a route from the map does not prove that no right exists; unrecorded historic rights can still be claimed on the basis of documentary evidence or long use. The Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 originally set a cut-off date after which certain unrecorded historic rights would be extinguished, a provision whose implementation has been repeatedly revised.

Creation, diversion and extinguishment

Rights of way can be created by express dedication, by statutory order, or by deemed dedication after twenty years of public use "as of right" under section 31 of the Highways Act 1980. They may be diverted or extinguished only by legal order; the popular maxim is "once a highway, always a highway". Obstruction of a public right of way is an offence, and highway authorities are under a duty to assert and protect the public's rights.

Signposting and waymarking

Authorities must signpost footpaths and bridleways where they leave a metalled road. Paths are commonly waymarked with coloured arrows: yellow for footpaths, blue for bridleways, red for byways and purple plum for restricted byways. Long-distance routes such as National Trails carry the acorn symbol.

Scotland and Northern Ireland

Scotland has a different tradition: rights of way there rest on common law rather than a definitive map, and the Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003 created general statutory access rights over most land, subject to the Scottish Outdoor Access Code. Northern Ireland has comparatively few public rights of way, recorded under a different framework.
