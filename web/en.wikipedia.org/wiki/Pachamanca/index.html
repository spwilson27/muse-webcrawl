Pachamanca


Origin: Andean Peru. Method: earth oven with heated stones. Name: from Quechua pacha (earth) + manka (pot).

Pachamanca is a traditional Peruvian dish in which meats, tubers, and other foods are baked in an earth oven over heated stones. The name comes from the Quechua words pacha, "earth," and manka, "pot" — together, "earth pot." The dish honours Pachamama, the Andean earth goddess, and has pre-Inca roots; it is related to the Chilean curanto and other earth-oven traditions.

Preparation

A hole is dug in the ground and lined with stones heated in a fire until glowing. Marinated meats — typically lamb, pork, chicken, or guinea pig (cuy) — are placed on the stones along with potatoes (often native Andean varieties), sweet potatoes, corn, broad beans in their pods, and sometimes cheese-filled peppers. Aromatic herbs such as chincho and huacatay (black mint) are layered between the food, which is then covered with wet cloths or banana leaves and sealed with earth. After one to several hours the oven is ceremonially opened and the food served, often directly on cloths spread on the ground.

Cultural significance

Pachamanca is a communal, festive meal associated with harvest celebrations and gatherings in the central and southern Peruvian highlands, particularly Junín, Ayacucho, Huancavelica, and Cusco. In 2003 Peru's National Institute of Culture declared pachamanca a Cultural Patrimony of the Nation.
