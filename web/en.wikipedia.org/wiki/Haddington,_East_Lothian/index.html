Haddington is a town in East Lothian, Scotland. It is the main administrative, cultural and geographical centre for East Lothian. It lies about 17 miles (27 kilometres) east of Edinburgh. The name Haddington is Anglo-Saxon, dating from the sixth or seventh century AD, when the area was incorporated into the Kingdom of Bernicia. The town, like the rest of the Lothian region, was ceded by the English king Edgar to Kenneth II of Scotland in 973 and became part of Scotland in the tenth century.

Haddington received burgh status, one of the earliest to do so, during the reign of David I (1124–1153), giving it trading rights which encouraged its growth into a market town. It was once the fourth largest town in Scotland, after Edinburgh, Aberdeen, and Roxburgh.

History

The town suffered repeatedly in the wars between Scotland and England: it was burned by English forces in the twelfth and sixteenth centuries, and was occupied and fortified during the Rough Wooing, when the siege of Haddington (1548–49) became a notable episode. Ada de Warenne, Countess of Northumberland, founded a Cistercian nunnery nearby in the twelfth century. The Franciscan church, St Mary's, known as the "Lamp of Lothian", was badly damaged in the 1540s and restored in the twentieth century. Severe floods of the River Tyne in 1775 and 1948 caused extensive damage.

Landmarks

Notable buildings include St Mary's Parish Church, one of Scotland's largest parish churches; the Town House (1748), designed with input from William Adam; and the Jane Welsh Carlyle House museum. Nearby Lennoxlove House and Amisfield House are significant country houses. Haddington is also known as the birthplace of the reformer John Knox (c. 1514), a claim supported by long tradition.

Transport and education

The town is bypassed by the A1 trunk road and served by buses to Edinburgh; the nearest railway stations are at Drem and Longniddry. Knox Academy is the local secondary school. The Haddington branch railway closed to passengers in 1949.
