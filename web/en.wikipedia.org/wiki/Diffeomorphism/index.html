Diffeomorphism

Diffeomorphism
FieldDifferential geometry, differential topology
DefinitionSmooth bijection with smooth inverse between smooth manifolds

In mathematics, a diffeomorphism is an isomorphism of smooth manifolds: a smooth bijection between two manifolds whose inverse is also smooth. If two manifolds are diffeomorphic, they are identical from the viewpoint of differential topology — every smooth structure on one transports to the other — and one writes M ≅ N. Diffeomorphisms are the structure-preserving maps of differential topology, just as homeomorphisms are for topology in general.

More precisely, a function f : M → N between smooth manifolds is a diffeomorphism if it is bijective, it is differentiable (smooth), and its inverse f−1 : N → M is differentiable (smooth) as well. A map is called a local diffeomorphism if every point has a neighbourhood on which the map restricts to a diffeomorphism onto its image, even if the global map is not injective.

Derivative and the inverse function theorem

At each point p, the derivative (differential) of f is a linear map between tangent spaces, dfp : TpM → Tf(p)N. A necessary condition for f to be a (local) diffeomorphism is that this derivative be a linear isomorphism at every point. The inverse function theorem states that this condition is also sufficient for f to be a local diffeomorphism: if the derivative is invertible at a point, then f is a diffeomorphism between suitable neighbourhoods of that point and its image. On Euclidean space this is the condition that the Jacobian determinant not vanish.

Examples

Any smooth change of coordinates is a local diffeomorphism. The map x ↦ x3 on the real line is a smooth bijection whose inverse is not differentiable at 0, hence not a diffeomorphism — a standard illustration that "smooth bijection" alone is not enough. Stereographic projection is a diffeomorphism between a sphere minus a point and the plane. There also exist exotic spheres: manifolds homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to the standard sphere, first discovered by John Milnor in 1956 in dimension 7, showing that the diffeomorphism classification is strictly finer than the homeomorphism classification.

Diffeomorphism groups

The set of all diffeomorphisms of a manifold M forms a group under composition, denoted Diff(M), which is central to the study of mapping class groups and to physics, where diffeomorphism invariance is the gauge symmetry of general relativity: the physical content of Einstein's theory is unchanged under smooth relabelling of spacetime coordinates. Time-dependent families of diffeomorphisms are generated by vector fields, and every diffeomorphism isotopic to the identity arises as the flow of such a field.

Related concepts

Diffeomorphisms generalise to maps between manifolds of different dimensions only in the local sense. Related notions include homeomorphism (continuous, with continuous inverse), isometry (distance-preserving), and conformal maps (angle-preserving). The group of diffeomorphisms equipped with suitable topologies is an infinite-dimensional Lie group in the Fréchet sense.
