Tiberius Claudius Caesar Augustus Germanicus (1 August 10 BC – 13 October AD 54) was a Roman emperor, ruling from AD 41 to 54. Born to Nero Claudius Drusus and Antonia Minor at Lugdunum in Roman Gaul, he was the fourth emperor of the Julio-Claudian dynasty and an unlikely candidate for the throne, having been largely excluded from public life because of a limp and slight deafness attributed to childhood illness.

Claudius proved to be an able administrator. During his reign the empire expanded, most notably through the conquest of Britain in AD 43, and he undertook extensive public works, including the harbour at Portus and the Aqua Claudia aqueduct. He reformed the civil service, relying heavily on freedmen such as Narcissus and Pallas, and took a personal interest in law, presiding over court cases and issuing numerous edicts.

Early life

Claudius was the youngest son of Drusus the Elder and Antonia Minor. His family considered him an embarrassment; his mother reportedly called him "a monster of a man, not finished but merely begun by nature." He devoted himself to scholarship, writing histories of the Etruscans and Carthaginians, an autobiography, and works on the Roman alphabet, in which he proposed adding three new letters.

Accession

After the assassination of his nephew Caligula on 24 January AD 41, Claudius was discovered by soldiers of the Praetorian Guard hiding behind a curtain in the palace. The Guard proclaimed him emperor, and the Senate, having briefly considered restoring the Republic, confirmed him the following day. He was the first emperor proclaimed by the Praetorians rather than the Senate.

Reign

Claudius extended Roman citizenship widely, admitted provincial notables to the Senate, and reorganised the imperial bureaucracy into departments headed by freedmen. In AD 43 he launched the invasion of Britain, personally joining the campaign for sixteen days and receiving the surrender of Camulodunum. He annexed provinces including Lycia, Mauretania, Noricum, and Thrace, and celebrated a triumph in AD 44.

His domestic life was turbulent. His third wife, Messalina, was executed in AD 48 after a notorious bigamous marriage to Gaius Silius. He then married his niece Agrippina the Younger, who persuaded him to adopt her son Nero and advance him over Claudius's own son Britannicus.

Death and legacy

Claudius died on 13 October AD 54. Ancient sources allege that Agrippina poisoned him with mushrooms, though modern historians treat the story with caution. The Senate deified him, and the satirist Seneca mocked the apotheosis in the Apocolocyntosis. Modern reassessments, aided by Robert Graves's novel I, Claudius (1934), have been far kinder to his record than the hostile senatorial tradition.
