Ames test

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ames test is a widely employed method that uses bacteria to test whether a given chemical can cause mutations in the DNA of the test organism. It is a biological assay to assess the mutagenic potential of chemical compounds, developed in the 1970s by Bruce Ames and his group at the University of California, Berkeley. A positive test indicates that the chemical is mutagenic and therefore may act as a carcinogen, because cancer is often linked to mutation.

Procedure

The test uses several strains of the bacterium Salmonella typhimurium that carry mutations in genes involved in histidine synthesis, making them unable to produce histidine and requiring it for growth. The test compound is applied, often together with a rat liver extract (the S9 fraction) that simulates the metabolic activation that occurs in mammals. If the chemical causes reverse mutations restoring the bacteria's ability to synthesize histidine, colonies will grow on a histidine-free medium; the number of revertant colonies is compared against a control.

Significance

Because the assay is cheap, fast, and sensitive, it became a standard preliminary screen for potential carcinogens among food additives, industrial chemicals, and environmental contaminants. Although a positive Ames test does not prove a substance causes cancer in humans, a large fraction of known carcinogens are detected by it, and the test is embedded in regulatory guidelines such as the OECD test guideline 471.
