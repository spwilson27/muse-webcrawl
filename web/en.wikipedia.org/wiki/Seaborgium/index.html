Seaborgium is a synthetic chemical element with the symbol Sg and atomic number 106. It is named after the American nuclear chemist Glenn T. Seaborg — unusually, the name was adopted while Seaborg was still alive. Like all elements beyond fermium, seaborgium does not occur in nature and can only be produced in laboratories by bombarding lighter elements with charged particles; only a small number of atoms have ever been made.

Discovery

Seaborgium was first reported in 1974, nearly simultaneously by teams at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna, USSR (who bombarded lead with chromium ions) and at the Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory in California (who bombarded californium-249 with oxygen-18). The Berkeley work was confirmed, and after a long naming dispute IUPAC recognised the name seaborgium in 1997.

Properties

Seaborgium sits in group 6 of the periodic table below chromium, molybdenum and tungsten, and its chemistry is expected to resemble tungsten's. Experiments on a few atoms at a time have confirmed the formation of compounds such as seaborgium hexacarbonyl, Sg(CO)6, and oxyhalides, consistent with its group placement. Its isotopes are highly radioactive; the longest-lived known, 269Sg, has a half-life of roughly 14 minutes.
