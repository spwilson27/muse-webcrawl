Greenstone belts are elongated zones of variably metamorphosed mafic to ultramafic volcanic sequences with associated sedimentary rocks that occur within Archean and Proterozoic cratons between granite and gneiss bodies. The name comes from the greenish colour imparted by metamorphic minerals such as chlorite, actinolite and epidote that replace the original volcanic minerals.

Characteristics

Greenstone belts range from a few kilometres to more than 200 km long. Their typical succession begins with ultramafic lavas (komatiites, found almost nowhere younger than the Archean) and tholeiitic basalts — often pillowed, showing they erupted underwater — passing up into felsic volcanic rocks, chert, banded iron formation and clastic sediments. They are commonly folded into synclines, intruded by tonalite–trondhjemite–granodiorite plutons, and metamorphosed mostly at greenschist to amphibolite grade. Their tectonic origin is debated: proposed settings include ancient oceanic plateaus, island arcs, back-arc basins and rifts.

Distribution and importance

Major examples include the Abitibi belt of Canada (one of the world's largest, and host to giant gold and base-metal camps), the Barberton belt of South Africa (whose ~3.5-billion-year-old rocks preserve some of the earliest evidence of life), the Yilgarn belts of Western Australia, and belts in Zimbabwe, India (Dharwad), Brazil and Finland. Because their shear zones and iron formations concentrate hydrothermal fluids, greenstone belts are among the world's most important hosts of orogenic gold deposits, as well as komatiite-hosted nickel sulphide ores.
