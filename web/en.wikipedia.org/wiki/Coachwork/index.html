Coachwork
MeaningThe body of a horse-drawn coach or motor vehicle
Notable coachbuildersPininfarina, Zagato, Mulliner, Hooper, Figoni et Falaschi, Saoutchik

Coachwork is the body of a vehicle — originally a horse-drawn coach, later an automobile — and by extension the craft of designing and building such bodies. In the early automobile era, manufacturers sold rolling chassis (frame, engine, and drivetrain), and specialist coachbuilders crafted bespoke bodies to order for wealthy customers, a practice inherited directly from carriage making.

History

Coachbuilding was one of the great artisan industries of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Firms such as Hooper, Mulliner, and Barker in Britain; Chapron, Figoni et Falaschi, and Saoutchik in France; Castagna and Touring in Italy; and Brewster and LeBaron in the United States produced bodies on Rolls-Royce, Duesenberg, Delahaye, Bugatti, and Hispano-Suiza chassis, ranging from sedate limousines to flamboyant aerodynamic show cars. Construction was traditionally ash-framed with steel or aluminum panels; the Italian superleggera ("super-light") tube-and-panel method was a notable innovation.

Decline and survival

Mass-produced all-steel unit bodies from the 1930s onward destroyed the bespoke market, and most coachbuilders closed or were absorbed by manufacturers by the 1960s. A few survived by becoming design houses — Pininfarina and Bertone chief among them — while modern coachbuilding persists in limited runs, conversions, and one-off commissions for marques such as Rolls-Royce, Ferrari, and Zagato-built specials.
