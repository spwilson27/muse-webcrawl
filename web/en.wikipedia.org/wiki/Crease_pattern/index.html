Crease pattern

FieldOrigami, mathematics of paper folding
ElementsMountain folds, valley folds, creases
Key rulesMaekawa's theorem, Kawasaki's theorem
RelatedBox pleating, circle packing, Miura fold

A crease pattern is a diagram used in origami that shows all of the creases in the finished model, laid out on the unfolded sheet of paper. Unlike step-by-step folding diagrams, a crease pattern is drawn on a single square representing the paper, with lines marking mountain folds and valley folds—conventionally distinguished by colour or line style (for example, red for mountain, blue or black for valley).

Use in origami design

Crease patterns are the standard way for advanced origami designers to communicate complex models, whose folding sequences would be prohibitively long to diagram step by step. Folding from a crease pattern generally requires collapsing the paper along all creases simultaneously into the "base", and then shaping the base into the final model—a process that demands considerable skill and interpretation from the folder. Modern design techniques such as circle packing and box pleating produce crease patterns computationally, and software such as Robert J. Lang's TreeMaker generates them from a stick-figure specification of the desired model.

Mathematical properties

For a crease pattern to fold flat, it must satisfy local conditions around each interior vertex, including Maekawa's theorem (the numbers of mountain and valley creases at a vertex differ by two) and Kawasaki's theorem (alternating angles around a vertex sum to 180 degrees). Determining global flat-foldability of an arbitrary crease pattern is computationally hard (NP-hard), a result due to Marshall Bern and Barry Hayes.
