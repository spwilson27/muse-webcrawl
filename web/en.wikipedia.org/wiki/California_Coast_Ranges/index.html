The California Coast Ranges are a series of mountain ranges that run alongside the Pacific coast of California for more than 600 kilometres (400 miles), from Del Norte and Humboldt counties in the north to Santa Barbara County in the south. They form part of the Pacific Coast Ranges of western North America and are separated from the San Joaquin Valley and California's interior by the Great Valley.

Geography

The ranges include, from north to south, the Klamath Mountains' southern neighbours, the Mendocino Range, the Mayacamas Mountains, the Santa Cruz Mountains, the Diablo Range, the Santa Lucia Range, the Gabilan Range and the Temblor Range. Elevations are generally modest, mostly between 600 and 1,800 metres, but the mountains rise abruptly from the sea, notably at Big Sur. Major valleys between the ranges include the Santa Clara, Salinas and Santa Maria valleys.

Geology and ecology

Much of the Coast Ranges consists of the Franciscan Complex, a jumbled accretionary assemblage of greywacke, chert, basalt and serpentinite formed during subduction along the California margin, cut by strands of the San Andreas Fault system. The ranges support coast redwood forests in the north and, further south, oak woodland, chaparral and grassland, with a Mediterranean climate of wet winters and dry summers.
