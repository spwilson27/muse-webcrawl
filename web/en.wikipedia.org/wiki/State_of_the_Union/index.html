State of the Union

The President delivering the State of the Union address before Congress.

The State of the Union address (sometimes abbreviated to SOTU) is an annual message delivered by the president of the United States to a joint session of the United States Congress near the beginning of each calendar year. The address fulfills the requirement of Article II, Section 3, Clause 1 of the U.S. Constitution, which directs the president to "from time to time give to the Congress Information of the State of the Union, and recommend to their Consideration such measures as he shall judge necessary and expedient."

The address typically reports on the condition of the nation, the administration's accomplishments, and the president's legislative proposals for the coming year. It is one of the most prominent events in American politics, broadcast live on television and radio, and is followed by an official response from the opposition party.

History

George Washington delivered the first annual message in person on January 8, 1790, in New York City. Thomas Jefferson discontinued the practice of delivering the address in person, considering it too monarchical, and instead sent a written message to Congress; for more than a century, presidents followed Jefferson's precedent. Woodrow Wilson revived the in-person delivery in 1913, and his successors have largely continued the practice, although the last president to deliver only a written message was Jimmy Carter in 1981.

Calvin Coolidge's 1923 address was the first broadcast on radio, and Harry S. Truman's 1947 address was the first televised. Lyndon B. Johnson moved the address to the evening in 1965 to attract a larger television audience, a practice that has continued ever since.

Protocol and ceremony

The address is delivered in the chamber of the House of Representatives before members of both houses of Congress, the justices of the Supreme Court, the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the Cabinet, and the diplomatic corps. Customarily, one Cabinet member — the "designated survivor" — does not attend, in order to preserve continuity of government in the event of a catastrophe. The president is escorted into the chamber, introduced by the sergeant at arms ("Mr. Speaker, the President of the United States!"), and traditionally asks the Speaker's permission to address the joint session.

Members of Congress often bring guests to sit in the gallery, and presidents have, since the 1980s, highlighted individual guests whose stories illustrate their policy proposals.

Opposition response

Since 1966, the party not holding the presidency has delivered a televised response immediately following the address. The first such response was given by Republican leaders Everett Dirksen and Gerald Ford. In recent decades, Spanish-language responses have also been delivered, sometimes by a different official than the English-language respondent.
