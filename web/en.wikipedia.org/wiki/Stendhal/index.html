Marie-Henri Beyle (23 January 1783 – 23 March 1842), better known by his pen name Stendhal, was a 19th-century French writer. Best known for the novels Le Rouge et le Noir (The Red and the Black, 1830) and La Chartreuse de Parme (The Charterhouse of Parma, 1839), he is highly regarded for the acute analysis of his characters' psychology and considered one of the early and foremost practitioners of realism. A self-proclaimed egotist, he coined a body of autobiographical and travel writings, including De l'amour (1822), which described "crystallisation" in falling in love.

Life

Born in Grenoble, Beyle served as an officer and commissary in Napoleon's armies, taking part in the invasion of Russia in 1812. After the fall of Napoleon he settled in Milan, developing a lifelong love of Italy. Suspected of espionage, he returned to Paris in 1821 and later served as French consul at Civitavecchia from 1831 until his death in Paris in 1842.

Works and style

The Red and the Black follows the provincial Julien Sorel's attempts to climb French society after the Bourbon Restoration through the church and the army—the title's two paths. The Charterhouse of Parma, dictated in 52 days, opens with a celebrated account of the Battle of Waterloo seen through its naive hero Fabrice del Dongo. Stendhal claimed to write for "the happy few" of a future generation; his reputation grew steadily after his death, and he is now ranked among the greatest of French novelists.

Stendhal syndrome

The psychosomatic condition of dizziness and fainting before great works of art was named Stendhal syndrome after his description of his overwhelming emotional reaction to the Basilica of Santa Croce in Florence in 1817.
