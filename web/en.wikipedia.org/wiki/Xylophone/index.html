Xylophone

The xylophone (from Greek xylon, "wood", and phōnē, "sound") is a percussion instrument in the idiophone family, consisting of tuned wooden bars struck with mallets. Bars are arranged in a keyboard layout, with resonators beneath each bar amplifying the bright, penetrating tone.

Construction and range

Concert xylophones are usually made of rosewood, padauk, or synthetic materials, and typically span three and a half to four octaves (F5–C9 in written pitch, sounding an octave higher). The bars are undercut in an arch at the center to tune the overtones. Players use hard or medium mallets for brilliance; softer mallets produce a rounder sound.

History and uses

Bar percussion instruments are ancient and widespread: Southeast Asian gamelan metallophones and African instruments such as the balafon and marimba are close relatives. The European xylophone entered the orchestra in the 19th century — Saint-Saëns used it in Danse macabre (1874) to evoke rattling bones and again in "Fossils" from The Carnival of the Animals. It became a staple of ragtime and early jazz novelty bands, and remains important in orchestral, band, and educational music. In elementary classrooms, small barred instruments in the Orff tradition are often also called xylophones.

Related instruments

  • Marimba — larger, lower, with warmer tone
  • Glockenspiel — metal bars
  • Vibraphone — metal bars with motor-driven resonators
