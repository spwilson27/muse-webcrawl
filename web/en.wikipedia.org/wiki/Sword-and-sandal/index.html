Sword-and-sandal

Sword-and-sandal, also known by the Italian term peplum (from the Greek garment), is a subgenre of historical epic films set in ancient Greece, Rome, Egypt or the biblical world, typically characterised by muscle-bound heroes, mythological adventures and relatively low budgets. The label is frequently used with affectionate irony.

History

The genre has Italian roots: silent epics such as Cabiria (1914) pioneered the ancient-world spectacle. After Hollywood's biblical epics of the 1950s (Samson and Delilah, The Ten Commandments, Ben-Hur) proved hugely popular, Italian studios mounted a wave of cheaper imitations beginning with Hercules (1958), starring American bodybuilder Steve Reeves. Its international success launched hundreds of peplum productions through the mid-1960s, with heroes such as Hercules, Maciste, Goliath and Ursus played by Reeves, Reg Park, Gordon Scott and other bodybuilders, battling tyrants, monsters and corrupt empires.

The cycle faded as the Spaghetti Western rose in the mid-1960s. Later films have revived the genre in new forms: Gladiator (2000) restored the prestige historical epic, while 300 (2007) and television series such as Spartacus offered stylised, graphic-novel and premium-cable takes on the material.

Characteristics

Peplum films are marked by dubbed dialogue in export versions, reused sets and costumes from Cinecittà, feats-of-strength set pieces, dance sequences, and narratives loosely assembled from myth, history and the Bible. Once dismissed as kitsch, the genre has been reappraised by historians of popular European cinema.
