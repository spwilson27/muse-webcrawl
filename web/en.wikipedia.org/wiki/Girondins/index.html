Girondins

The Girondins (French: [ʒiʁɔ̃dɛ̃]), or Girondists, were a political faction in the Legislative Assembly and the National Convention during the French Revolution. Named after the Gironde department in southwestern France, from which several leading deputies — notably those around the Bordeaux delegation — came, they are associated with figures such as Jacques Pierre Brissot (hence the older name Brissotins), Pierre Vergniaud, Jean-Marie Roland and his wife Madame Roland, and the Marquis de Condorcet.

Politics

The Girondins were largely bourgeois, commercially minded republicans influenced by Enlightenment political economy. They favoured free trade, the rights of property, a decentralised republic and war against the European monarchies — Brissot engineered France's declaration of war on Austria in April 1792. In the Convention they clashed with the more radical, Paris-based Montagnards led by Robespierre, Danton and Marat over the trial of Louis XVI, price controls, the power of the Paris Commune and the authority of the provinces against the capital.

Fall

Defeated in the power struggle, twenty-nine Girondin deputies and their associates were purged from the Convention in the insurrection of 31 May – 2 June 1793, prosecuted before the Revolutionary Tribunal, and twenty-one of them were guillotined on 31 October 1793. Others fled to the provinces, where their cause fed federalist revolts in Normandy, Bordeaux and Lyon. Survivors were recalled to the Convention after the fall of Robespierre in 1794. The Girondins' martyrdom was romanticised by Lamartine's Histoire des Girondins (1847), which helped shape their liberal legend.
