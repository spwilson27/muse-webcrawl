SubjectLegal codes of the scalds / early Danish law tradition

Scald Law refers to early medieval Scandinavian legal material associated in antiquarian tradition with the provincial laws of Denmark and the skaldic court culture of the Viking and early post-Viking age. In modern scholarship the term is most often encountered in older English-language histories of Danish law, where it is used loosely for the body of customary law that preceded and fed into the written provincial codes of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.

Context

Before the written provincial laws — the Scanian Law, the laws of Zealand, and the Jyske Lov (Law of Jutland, 1241) — Danish law was customary and oral, preserved by law-speakers (lögsögumaðr) who recited the law at the thing assemblies. The skaldic and legal cultures overlapped: verse was a mnemonic technology, and poets attached to royal courts worked in the same milieu in which royal authority and law were proclaimed.

Transmission

No independent code under the name "Scald Law" survives; the term survives mainly as a label in early modern and nineteenth-century legal antiquarianism for archaic customary rules thought to date from the age of the scalds. Scholars today generally treat the surviving medieval Danish provincial laws, rather than any hypothetical earlier code, as the authoritative record.
