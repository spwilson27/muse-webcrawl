Game artificial intelligence

Game artificial intelligence (game AI) refers to the techniques used in video games to produce the illusion of intelligence in non-player characters (NPCs) and to control other game behaviour such as procedural content generation. Unlike academic artificial intelligence, game AI is judged by the player's experience rather than by optimal decision-making; it is traditionally built from rule-based systems rather than machine learning.

Overview

The term came into use in the 1950s for game-playing programs, but in the games industry it covers the code that drives enemies, companions, crowds and directors. Its classic formulation is a sense–think–act loop: the NPC perceives the world (often with generous cheats, such as knowing the player's location), decides, and moves or acts. Because players read intent into behaviour, simple, readable systems often feel "smarter" than complex ones.

Techniques

  • Finite state machines — NPCs switch between states such as patrol, chase and attack; the ghosts of Pac-Man (1980) each combined a target tile with state changes.
  • Pathfinding — usually the A* algorithm over navigation meshes, letting characters move intelligently through levels.
  • Decision trees and behaviour trees — hierarchical structures that became standard in shooters such as Halo 2.
  • Utility AI — scoring possible actions and choosing the best, used prominently in The Sims.
  • Goal-oriented action planning (GOAP) — NPCs chain actions to satisfy goals, as in F.E.A.R. (2005), whose apparently tactical enemies were widely praised.
  • Director systems — pacing engines such as Left 4 Dead's AI Director, which modulates intensity to shape drama.
  • Monte Carlo tree search and machine learning — used in strategy titles and, increasingly, research agents such as AlphaStar and OpenAI Five.

Cheating AI

Many games quietly grant computer opponents advantages — extra resources in strategy games, rubber-banding in racing games — or let them perceive through fog of war, because a fair but dumb opponent is less fun. Well-disguised assistance is a long-standing design practice, though players resent it when it becomes obvious.

Academic field

Computational intelligence in games is an active research area with dedicated conferences (IEEE CoG, AIIDE) covering player modelling, procedural generation and general game-playing agents.
