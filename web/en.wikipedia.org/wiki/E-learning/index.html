E-learning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A learner taking part in an online course.

E-learning (short for electronic learning), also called online learning or digital learning, is education delivered through digital technologies, principally the internet. It encompasses a wide range of formats, from self-paced online courses and massive open online courses (MOOCs) to live videoconference classes, virtual classrooms, and computer-based training in workplaces. The term is sometimes used interchangeably with educational technology, although the latter more broadly covers the use of technology in education of all kinds.

History

Computer-based training emerged in the 1960s with systems such as PLATO at the University of Illinois, which pioneered online forums and screen-based instruction. The spread of the World Wide Web in the 1990s made web-based courses practical, and learning management systems such as Moodle (2002) and Blackboard became standard infrastructure in schools and universities. The launch of large MOOC platforms — Coursera, edX and Udacity — around 2012 brought free courses from major universities to millions of learners. During the COVID-19 pandemic from 2020, remote learning was adopted at emergency scale by schools and universities worldwide, exposing both its flexibility and inequalities in access to devices and broadband.

Forms and technology

E-learning may be synchronous (learners and instructors together in real time, e.g. by videoconference) or asynchronous (recorded lectures, discussion boards, quizzes completed at the learner's pace). Blended learning mixes online components with face-to-face teaching, and the "flipped classroom" moves content delivery online so class time can be used for exercises and discussion. Common supporting technologies include learning management systems, video platforms, automated assessment, learning analytics, and increasingly generative artificial intelligence tutors and content tools.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages commonly cited are flexibility of time and place, lower marginal cost per learner, easy updating of materials, and access to expertise regardless of geography. Disadvantages include high dropout rates in self-paced courses, reduced social interaction, difficulties with practical and laboratory subjects, academic integrity concerns in remote assessment, and the digital divide.
