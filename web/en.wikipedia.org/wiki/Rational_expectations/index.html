Rational expectations

Rational expectations is an assumption in economics that economic agents form expectations of future variables by optimally using all available information, including knowledge of the economic model and of government policy: on average, their predictions are correct, and errors are random rather than systematic. The idea was first proposed by John Muth in 1961 and was developed in the 1970s by Robert Lucas Jr., Thomas Sargent and others into a foundation of the new classical macroeconomics.

Implications

Rational expectations replaced the older adaptive expectations hypothesis, in which agents extrapolate from past values and so can be systematically fooled. Its best-known consequences include:

  • Policy ineffectiveness proposition (Sargent and Wallace): if expectations are rational and prices flexible, anticipated monetary policy cannot systematically change real output or unemployment; only surprises move real variables.
  • The Lucas critique (1976): econometric models estimated from past data cannot reliably predict the effects of policy changes, because the "structure" of such models — agents' decision rules — itself changes when policy changes. Only models built on "deep parameters" (preferences, technology) are policy-invariant.
  • Expectations-augmented Phillips curve: there is no stable long-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment, since attempts to exploit it shift expectations and the curve itself — an explanation offered for 1970s stagflation.

Use in modelling

Rational expectations became standard in dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models used by central banks and in asset-pricing theory, where it underlies the efficient-market hypothesis. In such models, an equilibrium requires that agents' subjective expectations coincide with the model's objective predictions (a fixed point).

Criticism

Critics note that real people do not possess the information, computational capacity or correct model that the hypothesis assumes; survey measures of expectations show systematic biases; and behavioural economics documents predictable departures from full rationality. Defenders reply that rational expectations is a modelling benchmark — roughly right in markets with strong learning incentives — rather than a literal description of individual psychology. Weaker alternatives include learning models, adaptive expectations revived in some New Keynesian work, and "rational inattention".
