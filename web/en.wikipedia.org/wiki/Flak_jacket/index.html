Flak jacket

A flak jacket is a form of body armour designed to protect the wearer from fragments ("flak") generated by explosive munitions — shell splinters, grenade fragments, bomb casing shards and flying debris. Unlike a bulletproof vest built to stop handgun or rifle rounds, a flak jacket is primarily a fragmentation garment, though modern designs combine both functions.

History

The name derives from the German Fliegerabwehrkanone, the anti-aircraft artillery whose shell bursts filled the sky around Allied bomber crews in the Second World War. The United States developed the first widely issued flak jackets for aircrew in 1942–43, working with the Wilkinson Sword Company of Britain: heavy vests of overlapping manganese-steel plates sewn into canvas, later replaced by ballistic nylon and Doron, an early fibreglass laminate. Crews of B-17 and B-24 bombers wore them over their flight suits; some sat on armour panels as well, since flak often came from below.

Ground troops received fragmentation vests in the Korean War — the M-1951 and related models — after studies showed that fragments, not bullets, caused the majority of casualties. In Vietnam the standard US Marine Corps M-1955 vest and the Army's M69 used layers of ballistic nylon. Modern successors, such as PASGT vests and today's plate-carrier systems, use aramid fibres (Kevlar) for fragmentation protection and add pockets for ceramic or steel plates rated against rifle fire, blurring the old distinction between flak jacket and bulletproof vest.

Design

Protection works by spreading and absorbing the energy of small, fast fragments across strong woven fibres or rigid plates; effectiveness is measured by the V50 ballistic limit, the velocity at which half of test fragments penetrate. Flak jackets cover the torso and sometimes the groin and neck, and typical military vests weigh from about 3 kg for a nylon fragmentation vest to over 15 kg for a fully plated modern carrier.
