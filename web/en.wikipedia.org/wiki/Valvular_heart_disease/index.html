Valvular heart disease

Valvular heart disease is any cardiovascular disease process involving one or more of the four valves of the heart: the aortic and mitral valves on the left side, and the pulmonary and tricuspid valves on the right. Valves may become narrowed (stenosis), restricting forward blood flow, or may fail to close completely (regurgitation or insufficiency), allowing blood to leak backward. Many people live with mild valve disease for years, but severe disease strains the heart and can lead to heart failure, arrhythmia and death if untreated.

Major types

  • Aortic stenosis — most often calcific degeneration in older adults, or a congenital bicuspid valve; causes exertional breathlessness, angina and syncope.
  • Mitral regurgitation — from prolapse, rheumatic disease, or dilation of the left ventricle.
  • Mitral stenosis — almost always a late consequence of rheumatic fever, still common where rheumatic heart disease is endemic.
  • Aortic regurgitation — from valve degeneration, endocarditis or aortic root dilation.
  • Right-sided lesions — tricuspid and pulmonary disease, less common and usually secondary to other conditions.

Causes and diagnosis

Causes include age-related calcification, rheumatic fever, infective endocarditis, congenital malformations and connective tissue disorders. Clinicians typically first detect a heart murmur on auscultation; echocardiography is the key investigation, quantifying valve area, pressure gradients and regurgitant severity, and guiding surveillance.

Treatment

No drug reverses structural valve disease, so management centres on monitoring, treating complications such as atrial fibrillation and heart failure, and timely valve intervention. Options include surgical repair or replacement with mechanical or biological prostheses, and catheter-based techniques — notably transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) and transcatheter mitral edge-to-edge repair — which have transformed treatment for patients at high surgical risk.
