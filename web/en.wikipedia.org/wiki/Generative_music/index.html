Generative music is a term popularised by Brian Eno to describe music that is ever-different and changing, created by a system. The composer designs rules, processes or software and then allows the system to produce performances that are never exactly repeated, rather than fixing every note in advance.

History

Precedents include the Musikalisches Würfelspiel (musical dice games) attributed to Mozart's era, John Cage's chance procedures, and the systems pieces of Steve Reich and Terry Riley, in which short patterns phase against each other. Eno's tape-delay experiments of the 1970s, leading to Ambient 1: Music for Airports (1978), established the ambient approach. In 1996 Eno released Generative Music 1 using the SSEYO Koan software, letting a home computer generate unique pieces from his rules in real time.

Approaches

Generative systems may be rule-based, stochastic, biological (cellular automata such as Conway's Game of Life), or, more recently, based on machine learning models trained on corpora of music. Input can also come from the environment — weather data, movement, or the listener — making each performance contingent. Eno later applied the idea in the app Bloom (2008, with Peter Chilvers) and the "generative film" of music in Reflection (2017).

Reception

Generative music raises questions about authorship and the role of the composer, which Eno likened to gardening: the composer plants seeds and tends conditions rather than constructing each artefact. The approach has influenced video game soundtracks (e.g. Spore), functional music services, and AI music generation.
