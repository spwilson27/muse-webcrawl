Cerro Rico, the "Rich Mountain", above Potosí.

Cerro Rico ("Rich Mountain"), also known as Cerro de Potosí, is a mountain in the Andes near the city of Potosí, Bolivia. It is famous for its silver deposits, mined since 1545, which made Potosí one of the largest and wealthiest cities in the Spanish Empire and supplied a large share of the world's silver during the colonial era.

History

According to tradition, the silver was revealed in 1545 by the Indigenous labourer Diego Gualpa. Under Spanish rule the mountain was worked through the mita system of forced Indigenous labour, supplemented by enslaved Africans; conditions were lethal, and Cerro Rico earned the grim epithet of a mountain that "eats men". Its silver was minted at the Casa de la Moneda in Potosí into pieces of eight that circulated globally, reaching as far as China.

Modern mining

After silver declined, tin became the main product in the 20th century. Today the mountain is worked by mining cooperatives extracting silver, tin, zinc and lead under dangerous conditions, and centuries of tunneling have left the summit honeycombed and at risk of collapse; the cone has visibly sunk and deformed. Potosí and Cerro Rico are a UNESCO World Heritage Site, placed on the danger list in 2014 because of mining damage.
