T. E. Lawrence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Thomas Edward Lawrence (16 August 1888 – 19 May 1935) was a British archaeologist, army officer, diplomat and writer who became renowned for his role in the Arab Revolt (1916–1918) and the Sinai and Palestine campaign (1915–1918) against the Ottoman Empire during the First World War. The breadth and variety of his activities and associations, and his ability to describe them vividly in writing, earned him international fame as Lawrence of Arabia.

Early life and archaeology

Lawrence was born in Tremadog, Wales, and educated at Jesus College, Oxford, where he studied history and undertook a solo walking tour of Syria researching Crusader castles. From 1911 to 1914 he worked on the excavations at Carchemish on the Euphrates under the British Museum, travelling widely and learning Arabic.

First World War

On the outbreak of war Lawrence joined British military intelligence in Cairo. Sent to Arabia in 1916, he became liaison to the forces of the Hashemite revolt under Faisal, son of Sharif Hussein of Mecca. He helped develop the guerrilla strategy of raiding the Hejaz railway rather than assaulting Medina, and took part in the capture of Aqaba in July 1917 and the campaign culminating in the entry into Damascus in October 1918.

Later life

Lawrence attended the Paris Peace Conference as Faisal's adviser and later served as an adviser on Arab affairs to Winston Churchill at the Colonial Office. Seeking anonymity, he enlisted in the Royal Air Force and later the Royal Tank Corps under assumed names, finally serving as Aircraftman T. E. Shaw. His memoir Seven Pillars of Wisdom (privately printed 1926; abridged as Revolt in the Desert, 1927) remains a classic of war literature. He died on 19 May 1935, six days after a motorcycle accident near his home at Clouds Hill, Dorset.

Legacy

Lawrence was portrayed by Peter O'Toole in David Lean's film Lawrence of Arabia (1962), which won the Academy Award for Best Picture. Scholars continue to debate the accuracy of his accounts and the scale of his personal contribution to the revolt, but his place in British and Arab cultural memory is secure.
