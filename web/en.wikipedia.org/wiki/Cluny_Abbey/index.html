Cluny Abbey

Cluny Abbey (French: Abbaye de Cluny) is a former Benedictine monastery in Cluny, Saône-et-Loire, France, founded in 910 by William I, Duke of Aquitaine. It became the mother house of the Cluniac order and the centre of a monastic reform movement that reshaped medieval Christianity.

History

William granted Cluny freedom from secular and episcopal interference, answerable only to the pope — an unusual liberty that let its abbots, notably Odo, Hugh of Semur and Peter the Venerable, build a network of over a thousand priories across Europe. The third abbey church, Cluny III (begun 1088), was the largest church in Christendom until St Peter's in Rome, a masterpiece of Romanesque architecture.

Decline and destruction

From the 13th century Cluny declined amid debt, commendatory abbots and the rise of newer orders such as the Cistercians. Suppressed in the French Revolution, the abbey was sold and quarried for stone; only the south transept arm and its tower survive, about 8% of the great church.
