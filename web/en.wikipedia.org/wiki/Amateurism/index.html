Amateurism

Amateurism in sport is the principle and practice of participating in athletics for the love of the activity rather than for payment. From the late 19th century until the late 20th century, amateur status was a formal condition of eligibility in many sports — above all the Olympic Games — and the amateur ethos deeply shaped organised sport, the arts and science. The word amateur derives from the Latin amator, "lover". Definitions of amateurism typically excluded anyone who had competed for money, taught or coached for pay, or in some eras even competed against professionals.

Origins in Victorian Britain

Amateurism crystallised in Victorian Britain's public schools and universities, where sport was valued as moral training. Governing bodies used amateur definitions to exclude working-class competitors: the Amateur Rowing Association long disqualified anyone who had been "by trade or employment for wages a mechanic, artisan or labourer", and gentlemen cricketers ("Gentlemen" versus working-class "Players") maintained separate status until 1963. Critics have since characterised early amateurism as a class filter as much as a philosophy.

The Olympic Games

Baron Pierre de Coubertin made amateurism the ideological core of the modern Olympics. The rules produced famous controversies: Jim Thorpe was stripped of his 1912 decathlon and pentathlon gold medals for having played semi-professional baseball (they were restored posthumously, and in 2022 he was reinstated as sole champion). In practice, the system favoured wealthy athletes and state-supported "amateurs" from the Soviet bloc — the so-called shamateurism of full-time state athletes. Under IOC president Juan Antonio Samaranch, eligibility rules liberalised from the 1980s; professional tennis players returned in 1988, and the 1992 United States basketball "Dream Team" of NBA stars symbolised the end of Olympic amateurism.

College sport and other fields

In the United States, the NCAA long enforced amateurism for college athletes, prohibiting pay beyond scholarships. The model faced mounting legal and public challenge, culminating in the Supreme Court's NCAA v. Alston decision (2021) and the adoption of name, image and likeness (NIL) compensation for student-athletes. Beyond sport, "amateur" retains positive connotations in fields such as amateur astronomy and amateur radio — where unpaid enthusiasts make genuine contributions — but a pejorative sense ("amateurish") where it suggests incompetence.
