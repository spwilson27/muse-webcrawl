Penman equation

The Penman equation describes evaporation (E) from an open water surface or saturated soil, formulated by the British physicist Howard Latimer Penman in 1948 in his paper "Natural evaporation from open water, bare soil and grass."

The combination approach

Penman's insight — the "combination" method — was to combine an energy-balance (radiation) term with an aerodynamic (ventilation) term, weighting each by the slope of the saturation vapour pressure curve. In one common form, the evaporation rate weighted between net radiation and a wind/drying-power function:

E = ( Δ·Rn + γ·Ea ) / ( Δ + γ )

where Rn is available energy (net radiation) expressed as an equivalent evaporation rate, Ea is an aerodynamic evaporation term depending on wind speed and vapour pressure deficit, Δ is the slope of the saturation vapour pressure curve at air temperature, and γ is the psychrometric constant. The formulation elegantly avoids the need to measure the surface temperature, which earlier methods required.

Development and use

Penman developed the equation using experimental data from the Rothamsted Experimental Station. John Monteith extended it in 1965 to vegetated surfaces by incorporating surface (stomatal/canopy) resistance, producing the Penman–Monteith equation, which the Food and Agriculture Organization adopted in 1990 (in the FAO-56 reference form) as the standard method for estimating reference evapotranspiration for irrigation scheduling, hydrology, and climate studies.

The original Penman equation remains widely used for estimating evaporation from lakes, reservoirs, and open water, and as the conceptual basis for most modern physically based evaporation models.
