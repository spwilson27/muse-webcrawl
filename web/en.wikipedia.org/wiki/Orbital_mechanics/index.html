Orbital mechanics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An elliptical orbit around a central body.

Orbital mechanics (or astrodynamics) is the study of the motion of artificial satellites and spacecraft under the influence of gravity and thrust. It builds on Kepler's laws of planetary motion and Newton's law of universal gravitation: a body in orbit follows a conic section — circle, ellipse, parabola or hyperbola — with the central body at one focus.

Key quantities include the semi-major axis, which sets the orbital period; eccentricity, which sets the ellipse's shape; and the apoapsis and periapsis, the highest and lowest points of the orbit. Changing an orbit requires changing velocity, measured as Delta-v — the currency of spaceflight, limited by the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation. Energy-efficient transfers between circular orbits use the Hohmann transfer orbit, while spacecraft visiting other planets use Gravity assist manoeuvres to gain or shed energy.

Applications

Orbital mechanics governs launch windows, rendezvous and docking, station-keeping, interplanetary trajectories and re-entry. Real orbits are perturbed by atmospheric drag, the Earth's equatorial bulge and the gravity of the Moon and Sun, all of which mission designers must model.
