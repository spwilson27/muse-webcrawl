Self-incompatibility

Self-incompatibility (SI) is a widespread genetic mechanism in flowering plants that prevents self-fertilization and thereby promotes outcrossing. A self-incompatible plant, though producing functional pollen and ovules, cannot set seed when pollinated by itself or by genetically similar individuals sharing its incompatibility type; it rejects its own pollen either on the stigma, during pollen-tube growth in the style, or (in late-acting systems) in the ovary.

In sporophytic and gametophytic systems alike, "self" pollen is recognized and stopped.

Mechanisms

SI is usually controlled by a single highly polymorphic locus, the S-locus, with dozens of alleles maintained in natural populations by negative frequency-dependent selection: rare S-types enjoy a mating advantage, so diversity persists.

  • Gametophytic self-incompatibility (GSI): the pollen's behavior is determined by its own haploid S-genotype. Found in the nightshade, rose, and grass-adjacent families; in the best-studied systems (e.g., in Nicotiana and petunia) the style expresses cytotoxic S-RNases that degrade RNA in incompatible pollen tubes, while compatible pollen detoxifies non-self S-RNases.
  • Sporophytic self-incompatibility (SSI): pollen behavior is determined by the diploid genotype of the parent plant, through proteins deposited on the pollen coat. Characteristic of the cabbage family (Brassicaceae), where a stigma receptor kinase (SRK) recognizes a pollen ligand (SCR/SP11) of the same S-haplotype and blocks pollen hydration.
  • Other systems: heteromorphic SI associated with floral morphs (heterostyly, as in primroses), and the mechanistically distinct self-recognition in grasses and poppies — in Papaver, self pollen triggers a calcium-mediated programmed cell death.

Significance

Self-incompatibility occurs in an estimated 40% or more of angiosperm species and is a major reason flowering plants maintain genetic diversity. It can break down — through mutation, polyploidy, or selection in colonizing situations — giving self-compatible lineages such as the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, whose ancestors lost SSI. In agriculture, SI complicates breeding and seed production of crops like brassicas and fruit trees (many apple and cherry cultivars require cross-pollination from a different cultivar), but it is also exploited to produce hybrid seed.
