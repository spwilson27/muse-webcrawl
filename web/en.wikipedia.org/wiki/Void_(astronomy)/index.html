Void (astronomy)

Cosmic voids are vast spaces between filaments (the largest-scale structures in the universe), which contain very few or no galaxies. Voids typically have a diameter of 10 to 100 megaparsecs (30 to 300 million light-years); particularly large voids, defined by the absence of rich superclusters of galaxies, are sometimes called supervoids.

The distribution of galaxies in a slice of the universe, showing the foam-like pattern of voids and filaments.

Discovery

Voids were first clearly identified in the late 1970s through redshift surveys by Gregory and Thompson, and by Jõeveer, Einasto and Tago. The Boötes void, discovered in 1981, spans roughly 330 million light-years and remains one of the largest known structures.

Properties

Because voids contain far less matter than the cosmic average, galaxies within them tend to be fainter, bluer and less evolved. Voids expand faster than denser regions, and their shapes and abundances provide tests of dark energy and the cosmological constant. Matter drains out of voids into surrounding walls and filaments, in accordance with gravitational instability theory.
