Critical phenomena

Critical opalescence: a fluid scatters light strongly near the critical point

In physics, critical phenomena is the collective name for the behaviour of physical systems near continuous (second-order) phase transitions, such as the ferromagnetic transition at the Curie temperature or the liquid–gas critical point. Near such points, quantities like the correlation length diverge, fluctuations occur on all length scales, and many properties follow power laws described by critical exponents.

Critical exponents and universality

Approaching a critical temperature Tc, the order parameter, susceptibility, specific heat, and correlation length behave as powers of the reduced temperature, characterised by exponents conventionally denoted α, β, γ, δ, ν, and η. Remarkably, these exponents take the same values for physically very different systems — fluids, magnets, alloys — that share only dimensionality and the symmetry of the order parameter. Such families are called universality classes; the three-dimensional Ising model class is the most famous example.

Renormalisation group

The theoretical explanation of universality came from the renormalisation group, developed by Leo Kadanoff and Kenneth G. Wilson (Nobel Prize, 1982): by repeatedly coarse-graining a system and rescaling, one can show that microscopic details become irrelevant near criticality, leaving only the features that define the universality class. At the critical point systems are scale-invariant and often conformally invariant, with fractal-like fluctuations.

Examples

Critical phenomena include critical opalescence (the milky scattering of light by a fluid near its critical point, caused by huge density fluctuations), the divergence of magnetic susceptibility at the Curie point, and critical slowing down of dynamics. Analogous ideas have been applied to percolation, superconductors, the liquid crystal transitions, and — by analogy — to phenomena outside physics.
