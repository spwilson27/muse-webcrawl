Chabad, also known as Lubavitch, Habad and Chabad-Lubavitch, is an Orthodox Jewish Hasidic dynasty and movement. "Chabad" is a Hebrew acronym for the three intellectual faculties of chochmah (wisdom), binah (understanding) and da'at (knowledge), reflecting the movement's emphasis on the intellectual study of Hasidic philosophy. It is among the world's best-known Hasidic movements, particularly for its global outreach to unaffiliated Jews.

History

The movement was founded by Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi in late 18th-century Belarus; his magnum opus, the Tanya (1797), systematised Chabad Hasidic thought. Leadership passed hereditarily through seven rebbes. The sixth rebbe, Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn, moved the movement to the United States in 1940, escaping Nazi-occupied Europe; under his son-in-law and successor, Menachem Mendel Schneerson (1950–1994), Chabad expanded dramatically from its Crown Heights, Brooklyn headquarters at 770 Eastern Parkway.

Outreach and institutions

Chabad is renowned for its network of emissaries (shluchim), thousands of couples who staff Chabad houses, synagogues, schools, campus centres and community institutions in over 100 countries, offering religious services, education and social support to Jews of all backgrounds. Campaigns popularised by the seventh rebbe encourage practices such as men putting on tefillin, women lighting Shabbat candles, and building sukkahs and public menorahs; giant public menorah lightings at Chanukah are a signature Chabad activity. Since Rabbi Schneerson's death in 1994 without a successor, the movement has continued under its existing institutions, while a messianist faction within it proclaims him the Messiah — a matter of internal and wider controversy.

Philosophy

Chabad philosophy, expounded in the Tanya and hundreds of later works, stresses the contemplation of God's unity and immanence, the spiritual anatomy of the soul, and joy in divine service; it seeks to integrate the mystical teachings of Kabbalah and Hasidism with rigorous Talmudic study.
