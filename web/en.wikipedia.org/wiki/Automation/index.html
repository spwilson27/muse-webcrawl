Automation

Automation describes a wide range of technologies that reduce human intervention in processes, mainly by predetermining decision criteria and implementing them through control systems and information technologies. Automation has been achieved by various means including mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, electrical, electronic devices, and computers, usually in combination. Its applications include manufacturing, transportation, utilities, defence, and information technology operations.

History

Automatic control devices date to antiquity — float regulators and water clocks — and were refined in feedback mechanisms such as the centrifugal governor used on steam engines. The term "automation" was coined in the automobile industry around 1946 to describe the increased use of automatic devices and controls in mechanised production lines, and was popularised by Ford executive Delmar S. Harder. Numerical control, programmable logic controllers, and industrial robots spread through factories in the later 20th century.

Types and technologies

  • Industrial automation: fixed, programmable, and flexible automation in manufacturing; robotics; computer-aided design and manufacturing.
  • Control systems: feedback loops, process control, distributed control systems, and SCADA.
  • Office and IT automation: workflow software, robotic process automation, and automated testing.
  • Home automation: building systems for lighting, climate, and security.
  • Emerging: machine learning and artificial intelligence extending automation into cognitive tasks.

Advantages and disadvantages

Automation can increase productivity, consistency, and safety, and reduce labour costs and human error. Its disadvantages include high capital costs, reduced flexibility, new technical failure modes, and displacement of workers. The economic and social effects — whether automation destroys jobs or ultimately creates new ones — remain a subject of debate and study, often discussed under the heading of technological unemployment.
