Cross-cutting

Cross-cutting is an editing technique most often used in film to establish action occurring at the same time, and often in the same place. In a cross-cut, the camera will cut away from one action to another, which can suggest the simultaneous nature of the actions depicted; it can also be used to contrast or foreshadow events.

History

Cross-cutting was pioneered by early filmmakers such as Edwin S. Porter in The Great Train Robbery (1903) and developed extensively by D. W. Griffith, whose films The Birth of a Nation (1915) and Intolerance (1916) used accelerating parallel editing to build suspense toward climactic rescues — the celebrated "last-minute rescue" structure. Soviet theorists later analysed the technique within montage theory.

Use

The technique is closely related to parallel editing, in which two or more storylines, often in different locations, are interwoven. Cross-cutting is a staple of thrillers and telephone conversations, and directors vary the length of the intercut shots to control pace and tension: as a climax approaches, the shots typically become shorter. Unlike a cutaway to unrelated material, cross-cut shots are understood by the audience to be happening simultaneously with the surrounding action.
