Franklin Pierce (November 23, 1804 – October 8, 1869) was the 14th president of the United States, serving from 1853 to 1857. A northern Democrat who believed that the abolitionist movement was a fundamental threat to the nation's unity, he alienated anti-slavery groups by supporting and signing the Kansas–Nebraska Act and enforcing the Fugitive Slave Act.


President Franklin Pierce

Early life and career

Pierce was born in Hillsborough, New Hampshire, the son of Benjamin Pierce, a governor of the state. He graduated from Bowdoin College, where he befriended the writer Nathaniel Hawthorne, and became a lawyer and Democratic politician, serving in the U.S. House of Representatives and the Senate. He served as a brigadier general in the Mexican–American War.

Presidency

A compromise "dark horse" candidate, Pierce won the 1852 election in a landslide over Whig Winfield Scott. Weeks before his inauguration, his last surviving son, Benjamin, was killed in a train accident, casting a shadow over his presidency and his wife Jane's life.

Pierce's administration completed the Gadsden Purchase of land from Mexico and saw Commodore Perry's opening of Japan. But it was dominated by the slavery question: the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854, which Pierce supported, repealed the Missouri Compromise and unleashed violence in "Bleeding Kansas." His enforcement of the Fugitive Slave Act and the Ostend Manifesto episode further damaged him. The Democratic Party denied him renomination in 1856 — the only elected sitting U.S. president to be refused his party's nomination for a second term.

Later life and legacy

Pierce retired to New Hampshire, his reputation further hurt by criticism of the Union during the Civil War. He died in 1869. Historians and scholars generally rank him among the worst and least memorable U.S. presidents, often faulting his failure to stem the nation's slide toward civil war.
