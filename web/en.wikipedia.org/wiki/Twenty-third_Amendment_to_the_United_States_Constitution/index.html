Twenty-third Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Twenty-third Amendment to the United States Constitution extends the right to participate in presidential elections to the District of Columbia. It grants the District electors in the Electoral College as if it were a state, though no more than the least populous state — in practice, three electors.

Proposed16 June 1960
Ratified29 March 1961
LocationNational Archives

Text

Section 1 provides that the District constituting the seat of government shall appoint, as Congress directs, electors equal to the whole number of Senators and Representatives it would have if it were a state, but no more than the least populous state; they are treated as electors appointed by a state. Section 2 gives Congress enforcement power.

Background and ratification

Residents of Washington, D.C. had been unable to vote for President since 1800. Congress passed the amendment in June 1960; Hawaii ratified first the same day, and Ohio's ratification on 29 March 1961 made it the 38th state to approve, completing ratification. District residents first voted in the 1964 presidential election, supporting Lyndon B. Johnson.

Significance

The amendment did not give D.C. voting representation in Congress, leaving the D.C. statehood debate — reflected in the licence-plate slogan "Taxation Without Representation" — unresolved.
