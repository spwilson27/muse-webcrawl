Greek wrestling

Wrestlers in the palaestra, the training ground of Greek athletics.

Greek wrestling, or pale (Greek: πάλη), was the most popular organised sport of ancient Greece and a central discipline of its athletic festivals. Wrestling was added to the Olympic Games in 708 BCE, in the 18th Olympiad, and formed part of the pentathlon as well as being contested as a separate event. The wrestler Milo of Croton, six-time Olympic victor in the sixth century BCE, became the archetype of the Greek athlete.

Rules and technique

In upright wrestling (orthē palē) the object was to throw the opponent to the ground; a fall counted when a wrestler's back, shoulders or chest touched the ground, and victory went to the man who won three falls — a feat commemorated in the expression "triaktēr" (winner of three). There were no weight classes at Olympia, though boys competed separately. Wrestlers competed naked, anointed with olive oil and dusted with fine sand, which made gripping the oiled body possible. Holds were taken on the arms, neck and torso; tripping and leg holds were permitted. Biting and gouging were forbidden at Olympia (the Spartans reportedly allowed them at home). A ground-wrestling variant, kylisis, continued the struggle on the ground until one man acknowledged defeat.

Competition took place in a dug and watered pit of sand or mud (the skamma) in the palaestra or stadium, under the supervision of officials armed with forked sticks to separate and discipline the wrestlers. Pairs were drawn by lot from a silver urn sacred to Zeus.

Training

Wrestling was taught in the palaestra, the wrestling school attached to every gymnasium, under a paidotribes. It was a core element of Greek education (paideia): Plato and other writers treat wrestling as training in alertness, balance and self-command, and the wrestling metaphor pervades Greek philosophy. In myth, the art was credited to heroes — Heracles wrestled the giant Antaeus and the river god Achelous, and Odysseus wrestled Ajax to a draw at the funeral games of Patroclus in the Iliad.

Famous wrestlers

Milo of Croton won the boys' event and five (or six) men's titles at Olympia and many victories at the other crown games; legends accrued about his strength, including carrying a growing calf daily until it was a full-grown bull. Other renowned wrestlers include Leontiscus of Messana, who won by bending back his opponents' fingers, and Polydamas of Skotoussa, credited with killing a lion with his bare hands.

Distinction from pankration

Wrestling should be distinguished from the pankration, introduced at Olympia in 648 BCE, which combined wrestling with boxing, kicking and submission holds and permitted almost everything except biting and eye-gouging.
