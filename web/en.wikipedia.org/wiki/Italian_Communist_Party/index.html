Italian Communist Party

Emblem of the Partito Comunista Italiano (PCI).

The Italian Communist Party (Italian: Partito Comunista Italiano, PCI) was a communist political party in Italy, existing from 1921 to 1991. For much of the postwar period it was the largest communist party in the Western world and the second-largest party in Italy, regularly winning between a quarter and a third of the vote.

History

The party was founded in Livorno in January 1921 as the Communist Party of Italy, a split from the Italian Socialist Party. Its early leaders included Amadeo Bordiga and Antonio Gramsci. Suppressed under Mussolini's fascist regime, many of its members were imprisoned or exiled, and Gramsci wrote his famous Prison Notebooks before dying in 1937. During the Second World War the party played a leading role in the resistance, and under Palmiro Togliatti it was rebuilt as a mass party after 1944.

The republican era

After the war the PCI joined the constituent governments but was expelled from cabinet in 1947 under American pressure. It remained the principal opposition party for four decades, building strongholds in the "red belt" regions of Emilia-Romagna, Tuscany, and Umbria. Under Enrico Berlinguer in the 1970s the party championed "Eurocommunism," distancing itself from the Soviet Union, and reached its peak vote of 34.4% in the 1976 general election. During the "historic compromise" it supported Christian Democratic governments from outside the cabinet between 1976 and 1979.

Dissolution

After the fall of the Berlin Wall, secretary Achille Occhetto led the party's transformation into the post-communist Democratic Party of the Left (PDS) in 1991; a minority split to form the Communist Refoundation Party. The PCI is widely studied for its distinctive "Italian road to socialism" and its deep roots in local government, unions, and cultural life.
