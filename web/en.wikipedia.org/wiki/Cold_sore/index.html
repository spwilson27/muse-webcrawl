Cold sore

A cold sore (herpes labialis) at the vermilion border of the lip.

A cold sore, also called a fever blister or herpes labialis, is a small blistering lesion on or around the lips caused by the herpes simplex virus, usually type 1 (HSV-1). Cold sores are extremely common and recur throughout life because the virus persists latently in nerve ganglia.

Course

An outbreak typically begins with tingling, itching or burning at the site, followed within a day by clusters of tiny fluid-filled blisters on reddened skin at the lip margin. The blisters rupture, crust over and heal without scarring in about 7–10 days. Triggers for reactivation include fever, sun exposure, stress, illness and local trauma—hence the names "cold sore" and "fever blister".

Transmission

The virus spreads by direct contact with lesions or saliva, including asymptomatic shedding; most people acquire HSV-1 in childhood. Contact can also transfer the virus to the eye or, through oral sex, cause genital herpes.

Treatment

There is no cure, but antiviral drugs such as aciclovir and valaciclovir—taken orally at the first prodromal symptoms, or applied as cream—can shorten outbreaks, and daily suppressive therapy helps people with frequent recurrences. Avoiding triggers such as ultraviolet light (with lip sunblock) also reduces episodes.
