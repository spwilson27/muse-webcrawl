Gnaeus Marcius Coriolanus was a legendary Roman general of the 5th century BC. He received his toponymic cognomen "Coriolanus" because of his exceptional valour in a Roman siege of the Volscian city of Corioli. He was subsequently exiled from Rome, and led troops of Rome's enemy the Volsci to besiege the city.

In later ancient times, it was generally accepted by historians that Coriolanus was a real historical individual, and a consensus narrative story of his life was assembled. In modern scholarship, he is usually regarded as a legendary figure, the product of the Roman annalistic tradition.

Legendary biography

According to Plutarch, the leading men of Rome considered Marcius, a young Roman of the patrician class, outstanding for his unmatched abilities in the art of war. He first distinguished himself at the siege of Corioli (493 BC), where his bravery in storming the city earned him the name Coriolanus and a proposed triumph.

When famine struck Rome, Coriolanus opposed distributing grain to the plebeians, insisting they first surrender the tribunate they had recently won. Charged by the tribunes and banished, he fled to the Volsci, Rome's enemies, and persuaded them to make war on Rome. Marching to the gates of the city, he was met by an embassy of Roman women — his mother Veturia and his wife Volumnia, with his children — whose pleas persuaded him to withdraw. The Volsci, enraged at being denied their victory, are said to have killed him, though some accounts have him living into exile and old age.

Cultural depictions

The story was most famously dramatised by William Shakespeare in his tragedy Coriolanus (c. 1605–1608), based mainly on Plutarch's Life. Ludwig van Beethoven's Coriolan Overture (1807) was written for Heinrich Joseph von Collin's play on the subject, and T. S. Eliot wrote the unfinished sequence "Coriolan". A 2011 film adaptation directed by and starring Ralph Fiennes set the story in a modern Balkan-like setting.
