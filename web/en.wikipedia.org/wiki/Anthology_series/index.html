Anthology series

Anthology series
DefinitionA broadcast series presenting a different story and cast in each episode or season
OriginRadio drama; early television
Notable examplesThe Twilight Zone, Alfred Hitchcock Presents, Black Mirror, True Detective, Fargo

An anthology series is a radio, television or streaming series that presents a different story — and usually a different set of characters — in each episode, or in each season. The episodes are united by a common theme, genre, format, host or production team rather than by continuing characters or plot. The format descends from radio drama and was a mainstay of early television.

History

Radio and early television

Anthology drama thrived on American radio with programmes such as The Mercury Theatre on the Air and Suspense. In the "Golden Age of Television" of the 1950s, live anthology dramas such as Kraft Television Theatre, Studio One and Playhouse 90 presented prestigious single plays weekly. Rod Serling's The Twilight Zone (1959–1964) and Alfred Hitchcock Presents defined the genre-based anthology, using fantasy, science fiction and suspense stories with twist endings.

Decline and revival

As production moved from live broadcast to filmed series with continuing characters — which were more valuable in syndication — the episodic anthology declined, though horror and science-fiction anthologies persisted. In the 2010s the seasonal anthology, in which each season tells one complete story with a new cast, revived the format: American Horror Story, True Detective and Fargo demonstrated that the model could attract major film actors for single-season commitments. Episode-based anthologies also returned with Black Mirror and the revived Twilight Zone.

Characteristics

Anthology series offer creative freedom — each episode can experiment with tone, period and casting — and convenience for viewers, who can watch episodes in any order. Their disadvantages include inconsistent quality between episodes and the difficulty of building audience attachment to characters who appear only once. A host or narrator, such as Serling or Hitchcock, traditionally provided continuity.
