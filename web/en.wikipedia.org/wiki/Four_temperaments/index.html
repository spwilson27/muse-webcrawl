Four temperaments

The four temperaments is a proto-psychological theory that suggests there are four fundamental personality types: sanguine, choleric, melancholic, and phlegmatic. It derives from the ancient theory of humourism, according to which health and temperament depend on the balance of four bodily fluids, or humours.

The types

The sanguine temperament, associated with blood and the element air, is cheerful, sociable, optimistic, and pleasure-seeking. The choleric, associated with yellow bile and fire, is ambitious, decisive, and short-tempered. The melancholic, associated with black bile and earth, is thoughtful, analytical, and prone to sadness. The phlegmatic, associated with phlegm and water, is calm, steady, and slow to anger.

History

The theory traces to Hippocratic medicine and was systematised by Galen in the second century CE. It dominated Western medicine and psychology for over a thousand years, influenced thinkers from Avicenna to Immanuel Kant, and survives in popular personality typologies. In the twentieth century Hans Eysenck mapped the four temperaments onto his two dimensions of extraversion and neuroticism, and modern personality science largely replaced type theories with trait models such as the Big Five.
