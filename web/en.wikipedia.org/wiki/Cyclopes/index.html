Cyclopes

In Greek mythology and later Roman mythology, the Cyclopes (Cyclops, plural Cyclopes; Greek: Κύκλωπες, "Circle-eyes" or "Round-eyes") are giant one-eyed creatures. Three groups are distinguished in antiquity: the Hesiodic Cyclopes, three brothers who forge Zeus's thunderbolts; the Homeric Cyclopes, represented by Polyphemus, uncivilised shepherds encountered by Odysseus; and the Cyclopean builders, credited with the massive walls of Mycenae and Tiryns.

The Hesiodic Cyclopes

In Hesiod's Theogony, the Cyclopes Brontes ("Thunderer"), Steropes ("Lightning") and Arges ("Bright") are the children of Uranus (Sky) and Gaia (Earth). Uranus, hating them, hid them away in Tartarus; later freed by Zeus, they repaid him by giving him thunder and the thunderbolt, and forged the weapons of the other gods: Poseidon's trident and Hades' cap of invisibility. They were later killed by Apollo in revenge for Zeus killing Apollo's son Asclepius with a thunderbolt.

The Homeric Cyclopes

In Book 9 of the Odyssey, the Cyclopes are lawless, solitary shepherds who neither plant nor plough, have no assemblies or laws, and do not honour the gods or the customs of hospitality. Odysseus and his men are trapped in the cave of Polyphemus, son of Poseidon, who eats several of the crew before Odysseus blinds him with a heated stake and escapes by clinging beneath the rams. Polyphemus's curse on Odysseus shapes the rest of the poem.

Cyclopean masonry

Late Greeks believed that only Cyclopes could have lifted the enormous stones of Bronze Age fortifications, hence the term "Cyclopean masonry" for the great walls of Mycenae, Tiryns and other citadels.

Origins

Various origins have been proposed for the single eye, including misread skulls of dwarf elephants (whose nasal cavity resembles an eye socket), the sight of blacksmiths wearing an eyepatch, and folk memory of a congenital condition. The one-eyed smith giants have parallels in other mythologies.
