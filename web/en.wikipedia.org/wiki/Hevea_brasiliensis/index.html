Hevea brasiliensis, the Pará rubber tree, sharinga tree, or simply the rubber tree, is a species of flowering plant in the spurge family, Euphorbiaceae, native to the Amazon basin. It is the most economically important source of natural rubber: the milky latex extracted from its bark accounts for nearly all commercial natural rubber production worldwide.

Description

The tree grows to 30–40 m in the wild, though plantation trees are usually kept smaller. Its leaves are trifoliate, spirally arranged. The bark, when cut, exudes a milky latex stored in vessels (laticifers) in the inner bark. In cultivation the tree is "tapped" by making a thin diagonal shaving cut in the bark, allowing latex to run into a cup; trees can be tapped for 25–30 years.

History of cultivation

Indigenous peoples of Amazonia used rubber long before Europeans arrived. In 1876 the English explorer Henry Wickham collected some 70,000 seeds from the Tapajós region of Brazil and shipped them to Kew Gardens — an episode Brazilians have long characterised as biopiracy. Seedlings were forwarded to Ceylon and Singapore, where Henry Ridley developed tapping techniques. Plantations in British Malaya and the Dutch East Indies rapidly outcompeted wild Amazonian production, partly because plantation trees in Asia escaped the South American leaf blight fungus (Microcyclus ulei) that devastates dense plantings in the species' home range.

Production

Today most natural rubber comes from Southeast Asia, above all Thailand, Indonesia, Vietnam, and Malaysia. Latex is coagulated with acid, rolled into sheets or processed into block rubber, and used principally for tyres, but also for gloves, footwear, hoses, and medical goods. Rubberwood, once burned as waste, is now a significant source of plantation timber.
