Centuriate Assembly

The Centuriate Assembly (Latin: comitia centuriata) was one of the three assemblies of the Roman Republic. Composed of citizen-soldiers organized into voting units called centuries, it elected the magistrates with imperium — consuls, praetors, and censors — enacted laws, declared war, and served as the court of final appeal in capital cases. Because of its military character, it met outside the sacred boundary (pomerium) of Rome, usually on the Campus Martius.

Organization

Tradition attributed the assembly's structure to King Servius Tullius, who divided the citizen body by wealth into classes for military service. In its developed form the assembly had 193 centuries: 18 of cavalry (equites), 170 of infantry divided into five property classes, and 5 unarmed centuries of engineers, musicians, and the propertyless. Each century cast a single collective vote, determined by majority within the century.

Voting and bias

Voting proceeded by class, beginning with the cavalry and the first (wealthiest) class, and stopped as soon as a majority of centuries was reached. Since the cavalry and first class together controlled 98 of the 193 centuries, they could decide a question before the lower classes voted at all — a structural bias in favour of the wealthy that ancient authors such as Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus noted. A reform, usually dated to the late 3rd century BC, reorganized the centuries in relation to the 35 voting tribes and reduced, though it did not eliminate, this imbalance.

Decline

Under the Principate the assembly's electoral functions were transferred to the Senate by Tiberius in AD 14, and its legislative role faded as imperial legislation supplanted popular enactment, though the comitia continued to meet ceremonially for some time.
