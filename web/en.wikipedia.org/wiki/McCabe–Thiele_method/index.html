McCabe–Thiele method

The McCabe–Thiele method is a graphical technique developed by Warren L. McCabe and Ernest Thiele in 1925 for the analysis of binary distillation. It is considered the simplest and perhaps most instructive method for determining the number of theoretical plates (or equilibrium stages) required to achieve a given separation in a fractionating column.

Construction

The method is plotted on an x–y diagram of liquid-phase versus vapour-phase mole fraction of the more volatile component. An equilibrium curve, derived from vapour–liquid equilibrium data, is drawn together with the 45° diagonal. Two operating lines are then constructed: the rectifying line for the section of the column above the feed tray, and the stripping line for the section below it. The two lines intersect on the q-line, whose slope depends on the thermal condition of the feed.

Starting from the distillate composition at the top of the column, "steps" are drawn between the operating lines and the equilibrium curve; each step represents one theoretical stage. Stepping continues until the bottoms composition is reached, giving the total number of stages, the optimal feed-tray location, and the minimum reflux ratio (at the "pinch point" where the operating lines meet the equilibrium curve).

Assumptions

The classic method assumes constant molar overflow — that the molar flows of liquid and vapour are constant in each section of the column — which holds when the components have similar molar latent heats, the mixture behaves nearly ideally, and heat losses are negligible. For systems where these assumptions fail, enthalpy–concentration methods such as the Ponchon–Savarit method must be used instead.

Applications

Although modern column design relies on computer simulation, the McCabe–Thiele diagram remains a standard teaching tool in chemical engineering and a quick means of estimating stage requirements, reflux ratio and the feasibility of a proposed separation.
