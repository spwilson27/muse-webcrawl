Abolition of the Ottoman caliphate

The abolition of the Ottoman caliphate was enacted by the Grand National Assembly of Turkey on 3 March 1924, ending the institution of the caliphate as held by the Ottoman dynasty since the early sixteenth century and removing the last Caliph, Abdülmecid II, from office. It was one of the foundational reforms of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk's secular republic.

Background

Following the Turkish War of Independence, the Assembly abolished the sultanate on 1 November 1922, deposing Mehmed VI; the sultan's cousin Abdülmecid was then elected caliph, a purely religious office stripped of temporal power. The Treaty of Lausanne (1923) recognised the new Turkish state. Kemal, however, saw the caliphate as a rival focus of loyalty, a potential rallying point for conservative opposition, and an obstacle to the secular national state he intended to build. A letter from the Aga Khan and Ameer Ali in late 1923 urging Turkey to preserve the caliphate backfired, being read in Ankara as foreign interference.

The law and its aftermath

On 3 March 1924 the Assembly passed Law No. 431, deposing the caliph, abolishing the office, and exiling all members of the Ottoman dynasty; the same session abolished the ministry of religious affairs and unified education under the state. Abdülmecid left Istanbul for exile in Switzerland and later Paris, where he died in 1944. The abolition provoked dismay in the wider Muslim world: Sharif Hussein of Mecca claimed the caliphate, congresses in Cairo (1926) and Jerusalem (1931) debated a successor, but no claimant gained general recognition, and the office has never been restored by consensus.
