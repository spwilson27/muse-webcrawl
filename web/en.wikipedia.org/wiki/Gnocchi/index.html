Gnocchi

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A bowl of potato gnocchi in sage butter, ridges holding the sauce]

Gnocchi are Italian dumplings — small, soft, ridged — most often made today from potato, flour and egg, boiled briefly until they float, and sauced with anything from sage butter to ragù. The word is usually linked to nocca (knuckle) or nocchio (a knot in wood). Potato gnocchi are younger than they taste: potatoes reached Italy in the 16th century but gnocchi di patate only became standard in the 19th. Older gnocchi were made from semolina (baked in milk as gnocchi alla romana), breadcrumbs, or plain flour, and dumpling cousins exist across Europe — German Schupfnudeln, French choux-based gnocchi à la parisienne, Hungarian galuska.

Making

The craft is lightness: starchy potatoes boiled in their skins, riced while hot, worked with just enough flour to hold, rolled into ropes, cut, and pressed against a fork or ridged board so each dumpling carries grooves on one side and a thumbprint hollow on the other — both there to catch sauce. Overwork them and they turn to bullets; underwork and they dissolve. They cook in the time it takes to say so, rising to the surface when done, ready for brown butter and sage, fresh tomato, pesto (the Ligurian pairing, classically with green beans and potato in the same pot), or a blue-cheese cream.

Traditions

Rome keeps "giovedì gnocchi" — gnocchi on Thursdays, in the old weekly rhythm of the city's trattorias (gnocchi Thursday, fish Friday, tripe Saturday). In Argentina and Uruguay, where Italian immigration ran deep, ñoquis are eaten on the 29th of each month, a coin left under the plate for luck — a payday-eve ritual of stretching money into plenty. Few dishes travel so well: five ingredients, one pot, and the whole Italian diaspora at the table.
