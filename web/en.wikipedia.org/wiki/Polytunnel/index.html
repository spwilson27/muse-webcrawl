Polytunnel

A polytunnel (also known as a polyhouse, hoop greenhouse, or high tunnel) is a tunnel typically made from steel and covered in polythene, usually semi-circular, square, or elongated in shape. The interior heats up because incoming solar radiation from the sun warms plants, soil, and other things inside the building faster than heat can escape the structure. Air warmed by the heat from hot interior surfaces is retained in the building by the roof and walls.

Uses

Polytunnels are used to extend the growing season, protect crops from wind, rain, and pests, and provide a warmer microclimate that allows tender or out-of-season crops to be grown. They are widely used for soft fruit such as strawberries and raspberries, for salad crops, tomatoes, and flowers, and by gardeners and allotment holders for early and late crops. Commercial polytunnels may span many metres and be equipped with roll-up sides for ventilation, irrigation lines, and heating systems, while smaller garden tunnels can be erected by hand over a single bed.

Construction

The frame is usually made of galvanised steel hoops anchored to ground tubes, over which a sheet of ultraviolet-stabilised polythene film is stretched and secured. The film typically lasts four to five seasons before replacement. Ventilation — by doors, louvres, or rolling side skirts — is essential to prevent overheating and fungal disease.

Advantages and criticism

Compared with glasshouses, polytunnels are much cheaper to build, quicker to erect, and easier to relocate, though the covering has a shorter lifespan and provides less insulation. Their spread across landscapes such as the fruit-growing districts of southern Spain and parts of Britain has drawn criticism on visual and planning grounds, and the plastic film raises questions about recycling and disposal. Planning rules vary; in England many agricultural polytunnels have been treated as permitted development, though large or permanent structures may require consent.
