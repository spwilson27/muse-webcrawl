Camp (style)

Camp delights in exaggeration, theatricality and artifice.

Camp is an aesthetic sensibility that regards something as appealing because of its irony, exaggeration, theatricality or bad taste. The concept was brought to wide attention by Susan Sontag's 1964 essay "Notes on 'Camp'", which defined camp as a love of the unnatural, the artificial and the exaggerated, and as a way of seeing the world in quotation marks — "not a lamp, but a 'lamp'".

Characteristics

Camp objects and performances typically display extravagance, flamboyance and a playful subversion of high and low culture. Sontag distinguished "naïve" camp — works that are unintentionally serious and failed, which she regarded as purest — from deliberate, self-aware "camp" that knows itself to be campy. Camp embraces failed seriousness: it converts the serious into the frivolous and finds delight in texture, style and manner rather than content.

History and culture

The sensibility has roots in pre-Stonewall gay subculture, where it functioned as a shared code and a strategy of survival through wit, and it permeates drag performance and queer aesthetics. Christopher Isherwood had earlier described "high camp" in his 1954 novel The World in the Evening. Camp later entered the mainstream through figures such as Andy Warhol, John Waters (whose film Pink Flamingos is a canonical example) and performers celebrated for deliberate excess.

In the mainstream

The 2019 Metropolitan Museum Costume Institute exhibition Camp: Notes on Fashion, and its accompanying Met Gala, brought the term to a global popular audience, showcasing designs whose irony, parody and theatricality embodied the camp sensibility.
