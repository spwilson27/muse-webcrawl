Saturation diving

Saturation diving is a commercial diving technique in which divers live for days or weeks in a pressurised chamber system at a pressure equivalent to their working depth, making repeated excursions to the seabed in a diving bell. Because the divers' body tissues become fully saturated with the inert gas of their breathing mix, they require only one lengthy decompression at the end of the tour, regardless of how many dives they make — an enormous efficiency gain for deep offshore work.

Principle and practice

Divers are compressed in deck chambers ("the system") to storage pressure and transferred under pressure to a closed bell, which is lowered to the work site, typically on oil and gas installations in water 30–300 m deep. They breathe helium–oxygen mixtures (heliox) to avoid nitrogen narcosis, supplied through umbilicals with hot water for heating their suits. Tours commonly last up to 28 days including decompression, which may take days as pressure is reduced very slowly to avoid decompression sickness.

History

The technique grew from US Navy and commercial experiments in the 1960s — including Edwin Link's Man-in-Sea project and Jacques Cousteau's Conshelf habitats and the Navy's SEALAB programme — and became standard in North Sea oil operations in the 1970s.

Risks

Saturation diving is among the most hazardous occupations: risks include decompression illness, high-pressure nervous syndrome, oxygen toxicity, and catastrophic accidents such as the Byford Dolphin explosive decompression of 1983, which killed five men, and the Star Canopus and Wildrake accidents. Strict regulation in Norway and the UK followed.
