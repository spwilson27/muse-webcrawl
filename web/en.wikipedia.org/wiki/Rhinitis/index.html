Rhinitis, also known as coryza, is irritation and inflammation of the mucous membrane inside the nose. Common symptoms are a stuffy nose, runny nose, sneezing, and post-nasal drip. Rhinitis is one of the most common chronic conditions worldwide and a frequent reason for visits to primary care physicians.

The inflammation is caused by viruses, bacteria, irritants, or allergens. The most common form is allergic rhinitis, which is triggered by airborne allergens such as pollen, dust, mould, or animal dander. Allergic rhinitis may be seasonal (often called hay fever when caused by pollen) or perennial, occurring throughout the year. Non-allergic rhinitis includes infectious rhinitis (such as the common cold), vasomotor rhinitis, occupational rhinitis, hormonal rhinitis, and rhinitis medicamentosa caused by prolonged use of decongestant nasal sprays.

Many types of rhinitis are linked to other inflammatory conditions such as asthma, sinusitis, conjunctivitis, and otitis media. Allergic rhinitis and asthma frequently coexist, and the concept of "one airway, one disease" has been proposed to reflect the shared inflammatory mechanisms of the upper and lower airways.

Signs and symptoms

The characteristic symptoms of rhinitis include:

  • Rhinorrhoea (runny nose)
  • Nasal congestion or stuffiness
  • Sneezing, often in paroxysms
  • Nasal itching and itching of the eyes, ears, and throat
  • Post-nasal drip, which may cause cough and throat clearing
  • Reduced sense of smell (hyposmia)

Allergic rhinitis is often accompanied by allergic conjunctivitis, with red, watery, itchy eyes. The physical examination may reveal swollen, pale or bluish nasal turbinates, a transverse nasal crease from repeated rubbing of the nose (the "allergic salute"), and dark circles under the eyes known as "allergic shiners".

Causes

Allergic rhinitis

Allergic rhinitis is an immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated inflammatory response to inhaled allergens. In a sensitized individual, exposure to an allergen causes mast cells in the nasal mucosa to release mediators such as histamine, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins, producing the early-phase response of sneezing, itching, and watery discharge. A late-phase reaction several hours later brings eosinophils and other inflammatory cells into the tissue, causing congestion and hyperresponsiveness.

Common allergens include pollens from trees, grasses, and weeds (notably ragweed), house dust mite faecal particles, cockroach debris, mould spores, and proteins in the skin, saliva, and urine of furry animals.

Infectious rhinitis

Infectious rhinitis is most often viral, as in the common cold, usually caused by rhinoviruses but also by coronaviruses, influenza viruses, and others. Acute bacterial rhinosinusitis may follow a viral infection.

Non-allergic, non-infectious rhinitis

Vasomotor rhinitis is triggered by non-specific stimuli such as cold air, changes in humidity, strong odours, and spicy food (gustatory rhinitis). Hormonal changes in pregnancy, puberty, and thyroid disease can cause rhinitis. Rhinitis medicamentosa is rebound congestion following prolonged use of topical decongestants such as oxymetazoline. Occupational rhinitis results from workplace exposures, for example in bakers, laboratory animal handlers, and woodworkers.

Pathophysiology

The nasal mucosa contains a rich network of blood vessels, glands, and sensory nerves. Inflammation causes vasodilation and increased vascular permeability, leading to plasma leakage, glandular secretion, and oedema of the turbinates. Stimulation of sensory nerves produces sneezing and itching, while parasympathetic activation increases glandular output. In chronic disease, persistent inflammation may lead to tissue remodelling and the development of nasal polyps in some patients.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually clinical, based on the history and examination. Allergy testing, either by skin prick tests or measurement of serum-specific IgE, can identify causative allergens when the diagnosis is uncertain or when allergen avoidance or immunotherapy is being considered. Nasal endoscopy and imaging are reserved for atypical cases or suspected complications such as chronic rhinosinusitis.

Management

Management strategies include:

  • Allergen avoidance – reducing exposure to identified triggers where feasible, such as dust mite avoidance measures or keeping windows closed during high pollen counts.
  • Intranasal corticosteroids – the most effective maintenance treatment for allergic rhinitis, controlling inflammation and most symptoms including congestion.
  • Antihistamines – oral second-generation antihistamines (such as cetirizine, loratadine, and fexofenadine) and intranasal antihistamines relieve sneezing, itching, and rhinorrhoea but are less effective for congestion.
  • Decongestants – topical or oral agents relieve congestion short-term; topical agents should not be used for more than a few days because of the risk of rhinitis medicamentosa.
  • Other agents – intranasal cromolyn, anticholinergics such as ipratropium for watery rhinorrhoea, leukotriene receptor antagonists, and saline irrigation.
  • Allergen immunotherapy – subcutaneous or sublingual administration of gradually increasing doses of allergen, which can induce lasting tolerance and modify the course of allergic disease.

Epidemiology

Allergic rhinitis affects an estimated 10–30% of adults and up to 40% of children in many countries, and its prevalence has increased over recent decades, particularly in industrialized nations. It causes significant impairment of sleep, school and work performance, and quality of life, and is associated with substantial direct and indirect economic costs.

Classification

Rhinitis is commonly classified as allergic or non-allergic. The ARIA (Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma) guidelines further classify allergic rhinitis by pattern (intermittent or persistent) and severity (mild, or moderate-to-severe based on impairment of sleep and daily activities).
