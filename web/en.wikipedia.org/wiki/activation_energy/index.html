Activation energy

In chemistry and physics, activation energy is the minimum amount of energy that must be provided for compounds to result in a chemical reaction. The activation energy (Ea) of a reaction is measured in joules per mole (J/mol), kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol) or kilocalories per mole (kcal/mol). Activation energy can be thought of as the magnitude of a potential barrier (sometimes called an energy barrier) separating the minima of the potential energy surface pertaining to the initial and final thermodynamic states. For a chemical reaction to proceed at a reasonable rate, the temperature of the system should be high enough to allow a significant number of molecules to possess translational energy equal to or greater than the activation energy.

History

The term "activation energy" was introduced in 1889 by the Swedish scientist Svante Arrhenius, who incorporated it into what is now called the Arrhenius equation: k = A e−Ea/(RT), where k is the reaction rate constant, A is the pre-exponential factor, R is the gas constant, and T is the absolute temperature. The equation shows that the rate of a reaction increases exponentially with temperature and decreases exponentially with activation energy.

Transition state theory

Transition state theory, developed in the 1930s by Henry Eyring and others, provides a more detailed picture: reactants pass through a high-energy configuration called the transition state or activated complex before forming products. The activation energy is the difference in energy between the reactants and the transition state.

Catalysis

A catalyst speeds up a reaction by providing an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, without being consumed in the process. Enzymes are biological catalysts that lower activation energies enormously, allowing biochemical reactions to proceed rapidly at body temperature. Catalytic converters in automobiles use platinum-group metals to lower the activation energy for converting pollutant gases into less harmful products.

Negative activation energy

Some reactions have rates that decrease with increasing temperature, corresponding to an apparent negative activation energy. These are typically barrierless association reactions whose rate is limited by other factors, such as the formation of an intermediate complex.
