Schleswig-Holstein Question

The Schleswig-Holstein Question (German: Schleswig-Holsteinische Frage; Danish: Spørgsmålet om Slesvig-Holsten) was a complex set of diplomatic and national issues arising in the 19th century from the relations of the two duchies, Schleswig and Holstein, to the Danish crown and to the German Confederation. It became proverbial for its impenetrability: Lord Palmerston is said to have remarked that only three people ever understood it — the Prince Consort, who was dead; a German professor, who had gone mad; and Palmerston himself, who had forgotten all about it.

The problem

Schleswig was a Danish fief with a mixed Danish- and German-speaking population; Holstein was a member of the German Confederation but ruled by the Danish king as duke. The 1460 Treaty of Ribe had declared the duchies "forever undivided" (up ewig ungedeelt), yet they followed different rules of succession: when the Danish royal line threatened to die out in the male line, Denmark could pass to a female heir while the duchies, under Salic law, could not — threatening to split the Danish monarchy apart. Danish national liberals wanted to incorporate Schleswig into Denmark proper ("Denmark to the Eider"), while German nationalists demanded a united German Schleswig-Holstein.

Wars and settlement

The dispute produced the First Schleswig War (1848–1852), inconclusively settled by the London Protocol of 1852, and the Second Schleswig War of 1864, in which Prussia and Austria defeated Denmark and took both duchies. Prussia annexed them outright as the province of Schleswig-Holstein in 1867. The question was finally resolved after the First World War by the Schleswig plebiscites of 1920, which partitioned Schleswig along linguistic lines: the north voted to join Denmark, the south to remain German — a border that endures today.
