The Oberkommando der Wehrmacht (German: [ˈoːbɐkoˌmando deːɐ̯ ˈveːɐ̯ˌmaxt], "High Command of the Armed Forces", abbreviated OKW) was the supreme military headquarters of Nazi Germany's armed forces during the Second World War. It was created on 4 February 1938, after Adolf Hitler abolished the War Ministry in the wake of the Blomberg–Fritsch affair and assumed personal command of the Wehrmacht.

Structure and role

The OKW was headed throughout its existence by Field Marshal Wilhelm Keitel, with General Alfred Jodl as chief of the Armed Forces Operations Staff (Wehrmachtführungsstab), which prepared strategic plans. Nominally the OKW coordinated the three services, but in practice the Army High Command (OKH) ran the war on the Eastern Front with little OKW involvement, while the OKW directly directed operations in the West, the Mediterranean, and other secondary theatres — a divided command structure that historians regard as a significant weakness. The OKW also housed the Abwehr, the military intelligence service under Admiral Wilhelm Canaris, until its dissolution in 1944.

End and trials

Keitel signed Germany's unconditional surrender at Karlshorst, Berlin, on 8 May 1945. At the Nuremberg trials the OKW's leadership was prosecuted; Keitel and Jodl were convicted of war crimes and crimes against humanity and executed in 1946, and the High Command Trial of 1947–48 prosecuted further senior officers. The OKW was formally dissolved by the Allied Control Council on 20 August 1946.
