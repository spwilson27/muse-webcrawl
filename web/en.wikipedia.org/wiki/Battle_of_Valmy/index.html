Battle of Valmy

The Battle of Valmy, also known as the Cannonade of Valmy, was the first major engagement of the French Revolutionary Wars, fought on 20 September 1792 near the village of Valmy in the Champagne region of northeastern France. A French army under Generals Dumouriez and Kellermann withstood the advance of a Prussian-Austrian army under the Duke of Brunswick, which withdrew ten days later, saving the young French Revolution.

Background

Brunswick's army, accompanied by French émigrés, invaded France to restore Louis XVI; the Brunswick Manifesto's threats against Paris helped provoke the insurrection of 10 August. After Verdun fell, the road to Paris lay open, but Dumouriez and Kellermann combined their armies across the invaders' path at Valmy.

The cannonade

In rain and mist, the armies deployed on opposing ridges. After a prolonged artillery duel — the famous "cannonade" — Brunswick ordered an advance, but French infantry, rallying to Kellermann's cry of "Vive la Nation!", stood firm, and the Prussians halted and withdrew. Goethe, present with the Prussian army, told his companions: "From this place, and from this day forth, commences a new era in world history."

Aftermath

Hampered by disease, supply problems and worsening weather, Brunswick retreated across the frontier by 30 September. The day after the battle the National Convention abolished the monarchy and proclaimed the French First Republic. Valmy became a founding myth of the citizen-soldier and of the revolutionary nation in arms.
