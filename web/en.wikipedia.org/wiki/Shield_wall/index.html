Shield wall

A shield wall (Old English: bordweall or scyldburh) is a military formation in which soldiers stand shoulder to shoulder with their shields overlapping, presenting a continuous barrier of shield boards to the enemy. It was a characteristic infantry tactic of early medieval warfare in northern and western Europe.

History

Similar formations appear in many ancient armies, from the Greek phalanx to the Roman testudo. In the early medieval period, Anglo-Saxon, Frankish, Scandinavian and Byzantine infantry all fought in shield walls. The formation features prominently in descriptions of battles such as Hastings (1066), where the Norman army struggled for hours to break the English shield wall on Senlac Hill, and in Old English poetry and Norse sagas.

Tactics

The front ranks presented interlocked shields while warriors struck over or under the wall with spears, swords and axes. Depth gave the formation staying power: rear ranks replaced fallen men in the front. The shield wall was strong against frontal assault and missile fire but vulnerable to flanking, broken ground, and prolonged combat that eroded its cohesion. Feigned retreats and concentrated archery were classic counters.
