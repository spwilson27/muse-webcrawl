Black consciousness is a philosophy and political movement that emerged in South Africa in the late 1960s, holding that Black people must first free themselves psychologically from the internalised inferiority imposed by apartheid before genuine liberation could follow. Its best-known proponent was Steve Biko, founder of the South African Students' Organisation (SASO) in 1968.

Philosophy

Drawing partly on Frantz Fanon, the Black Power movement in the United States, and Africanist thought, black consciousness defined "Black" broadly to include all those oppressed by apartheid — Africans, Coloureds, and Indians — who identified with the struggle. Biko argued that the most potent weapon of the oppressor is the mind of the oppressed, and that Black people should reject white liberal tutelage, build their own institutions, and take pride in their own culture and history.

History

SASO spread from university campuses into schools and townships through the Black People's Convention (founded 1972) and community programmes. The movement helped inspire the Soweto uprising of 1976, in which school students protested the imposition of Afrikaans as a medium of instruction. Biko was arrested in August 1977 and died in police custody the following month from head injuries, becoming an international symbol of resistance. In October 1977 the apartheid government banned the movement's organisations and publications.

Legacy

Black consciousness reshaped South African resistance politics, reviving mass mobilisation after the banning of the ANC and PAC in 1960, and its emphasis on dignity and self-reliance continues to influence South African political thought.
