Least common multiple

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In arithmetic and number theory, the least common multiple (also called the lowest common multiple or smallest common multiple) of two integers a and b, usually denoted by lcm(a, b), is the smallest positive integer that is divisible by both a and b. Since division of integers by zero is undefined, this definition has meaning only if a and b are both different from zero.

Overview

A multiple of a number is the product of that number and an integer. For example, 10 is a multiple of 5 because 5 × 2 = 10, so 10 is divisible by 5 and by 2. Because 10 is the smallest positive integer that is divisible by both 5 and 2, it is the least common multiple of 5 and 2. By the same principle, 10 is the least common multiple of −5 and 2 as well.

Computation

The least common multiple can be computed from the greatest common divisor (gcd): lcm(a, b) = |a · b| / gcd(a, b). Alternatively, from the prime factorizations of the numbers, the lcm is formed by taking, for each prime, the highest power appearing in any factorization. For example, lcm(8, 9, 21) = 504, since 8 = 2³, 9 = 3², and 21 = 3 × 7, giving 2³ × 3² × 7 = 504.

Applications

The lcm is used when adding or subtracting fractions, to obtain a common denominator (the least common denominator). In astronomy and engineering, the lcm gives the period after which two cyclic events with different periods coincide—for example, the synodic alignments of planets or the meshing of gears.
