Hogarth Press

The Hogarth Press was a British publishing house founded in 1917 by Leonard Woolf and Virginia Woolf. Named after Hogarth House, their home in Richmond, London, it began as a hand-printing hobby and grew into one of the most influential small presses of the twentieth century, publishing much of the Woolfs' own work, the first English editions of Freud's psychoanalytic writings, and major modernist literature.

History

The Woolfs bought a small hand press in 1917 and set type themselves in their dining room, partly as a therapeutic occupation for Virginia. Their first publication was Two Stories (1917), containing Leonard's "Three Jews" and Virginia's "The Mark on the Wall". Early successes included Katherine Mansfield's Prelude (1918) and T. S. Eliot's Poems (1919); the press later published Eliot's The Waste Land in book form in 1923.

From 1924 the Hogarth Press was the authorised English publisher of the International Psycho-Analytical Library, issuing the works of Sigmund Freud in translations overseen by James Strachey. It also published nearly all of Virginia Woolf's books from Jacob's Room onward, freeing her from commercial publishers, as well as works by E. M. Forster, Vita Sackville-West and Christopher Isherwood. In 1938 the poet John Lehmann became a partner and manager; in 1946 the press entered a partnership with Chatto & Windus, ending its independent existence, though the Hogarth name survived as an imprint and was revived in the twenty-first century.

Legacy

The press is remembered as a model of author-run publishing and a central institution of literary modernism and the Bloomsbury Group.
