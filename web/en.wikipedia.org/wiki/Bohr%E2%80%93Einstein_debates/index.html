Bohr–Einstein debates

[Image placeholder: Bohr–Einstein debates]

The Bohr–Einstein debates were a series of public disputes between Albert Einstein and Niels Bohr about the foundations of quantum mechanics, conducted over several decades in articles, conference discussions and thought experiments. The debates centred on whether quantum theory was complete and whether its probabilistic character reflected fundamental indeterminacy in nature.

The Solvay conferences

At the 1927 and 1930 Solvay Conferences, Einstein proposed thought experiments intended to show that the uncertainty principle could be circumvented — most famously the photon-box experiment of 1930, which Bohr countered by invoking Einstein's own general theory of relativity.

EPR and later developments

In 1935 Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen published the EPR paradox, arguing that quantum mechanics must be incomplete because it implied "spooky action at a distance". Bohr replied that the phenomenon of "entanglement" could not be analysed apart from the experimental arrangement. John Bell's theorem (1964) and subsequent experiments largely supported the quantum-mechanical predictions, though interpretation of the results remains debated.
