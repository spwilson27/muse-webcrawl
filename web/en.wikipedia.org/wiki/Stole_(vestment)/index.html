Stole (vestment)

The stole is a liturgical vestment of various Christian denominations. It consists of a band of cloth, usually silk, about 7.5–10 cm (3–4 inches) wide and some 2.4 metres (eight feet) long, decorated at the ends with crosses or other ornaments and worn around the neck. It is a symbol of ordained ministry: in the Roman Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, and many other Western churches, bishops and priests wear the stole hanging straight down in front, while deacons wear it over the left shoulder and fastened at the right side.

History

The stole probably derives from a cloth of office worn by officials of the late Roman Empire (the orarium or sudarium), repurposed in the early medieval Church as a sign of clerical rank. By the Carolingian period it was standard attire for bishops, priests, and deacons at the Eucharist, worn over the alb and under the chasuble or dalmatic. In the Eastern tradition the corresponding vestments are the epitrachelion for bishops and priests and the orarion for deacons.

Use and symbolism

The colour of the stole follows the liturgical season — white, red, green, violet, and others according to the calendar — and priests traditionally kiss the cross embroidered at its centre before putting it on, reciting a prayer asking to be clothed again in the immortality lost by Adam. In the Roman Rite the stole is worn for sacraments and blessings, not merely for the Mass: a priest hearing confessions or administering the Anointing of the Sick traditionally wears a purple stole, making it the working emblem of priestly ministry in popular imagery. Some Protestant ministers wear stoles as well, and in many traditions an ordination gift of a first stole is customary.
