The Amhara (Amharic: አማራ, romanized ʾÄmara) are a Semitic-speaking ethnic group indigenous to Ethiopia, traditionally inhabiting parts of the northwest highlands, in particular the Amhara Region. They speak Amharic, an Afroasiatic language of the Semitic branch, which serves as the working language of the federal government of Ethiopia and was historically the language of the imperial court.

History

The Amhara trace their regional roots to the province of Amhara in the central highlands. After the fall of the Zagwe dynasty, the Solomonic dynasty was restored in 1270 under Yekuno Amlak, and Amharic gradually supplanted Ge'ez as the spoken language of the court while Ge'ez remained the liturgical language. Amhara emperors such as Amda Seyon and Zara Yaqob expanded and consolidated the Ethiopian Empire. Amhara elites, together with the Tigrayans, dominated imperial politics for centuries, and Amharic spread widely through administration, the church, and military settlement (the chewa system).

Culture

Amhara culture is strongly associated with the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, whose festivals (such as Timkat and Meskel), fasting practices, and church schools shaped social life. Traditional dress includes the white cotton habesha kemis and netela shawls woven with coloured borders (tibeb). Cuisine centres on injera flatbread served with stews (wat), and the coffee ceremony (buna) is an important social ritual. Ge'ez-derived poetry (qene) and a tradition of ambiguous double-meaning verse known as "wax and gold" (sem-ena-werq) are celebrated literary forms.

Distribution

Most Amhara live in the Amhara Region of northwestern Ethiopia, with significant historic communities in Gondar, Gojjam, Wollo, and northern Shewa, and urban populations in Addis Ababa. A substantial diaspora exists in North America, Europe, and Israel.
