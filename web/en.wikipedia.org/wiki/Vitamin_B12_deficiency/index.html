Vitamin B12 deficiency, also known as cobalamin deficiency, is the medical condition in which the blood and tissue have a lower than normal level of vitamin B12. In mild deficiency a person may feel tired and have a reduced number of red blood cells (anaemia). In moderate deficiency there may be soreness of the tongue (glossitis), mouth ulcers, disturbed touch sensation, and changes in personality; severe, prolonged deficiency can cause irreversible damage to the brain and nervous system, including subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord.

Causes

Because B12 absorption requires gastric acid, intrinsic factor from gastric parietal cells, and an intact terminal ileum, deficiency most often results from malabsorption rather than diet alone. Causes include pernicious anaemia (autoimmune destruction of parietal cells or intrinsic factor), gastrectomy and bariatric surgery, Crohn's disease and coeliac disease, chronic use of proton-pump inhibitors or metformin, nitrous oxide exposure, and — particularly in those avoiding all animal products without supplementation — a vegan diet. The liver stores several years' supply, so deficiency develops slowly.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on a low serum B12, supported by elevated methylmalonic acid and homocysteine, a raised mean cell volume, and hypersegmented neutrophils on the blood film; neurological symptoms can precede anaemia, and folate repletion can mask the anaemia while neurological injury progresses. Treatment is B12 replacement — intramuscular hydroxocobalamin or cyanocobalamin where absorption is impaired, or high-dose oral B12 otherwise — together with treatment of the underlying cause. Haematological recovery is usually rapid; neurological recovery may be incomplete if treatment is delayed.
