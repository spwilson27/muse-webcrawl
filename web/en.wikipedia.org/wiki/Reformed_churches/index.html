Reformed churches


A typically austere Reformed church interior.

Reformed churches are the churches and denominations that belong to the Reformed (Calvinist) branch of Protestantism, tracing their origins to the Reformation of the 16th century in Switzerland, France, the Netherlands, Scotland, and parts of Germany and Hungary. This article surveys the family as a whole; for its theology see Reformed Christianity and Reformed tradition.

Families of churches

  • Continental Reformed churches — the historic churches of Switzerland, France, the Netherlands, and Germany, and their immigrant offshoots such as the Christian Reformed Church in North America and the Reformed Church in the United States.
  • Presbyterian churches — originating in Scotland and governed by elders in presbyteries, now spread worldwide, especially in Korea and North America.
  • Congregational churches — Reformed in doctrine but with autonomous local congregations; many merged into united churches in the 20th century.
  • United and uniting churches — unions of Reformed with Lutheran, Methodist, and other churches, such as the United Church of Canada and the Uniting Church in Australia.

Global fellowship

The World Communion of Reformed Churches, formed in 2010 by the merger of the World Alliance of Reformed Churches and the Reformed Ecumenical Council, links more than 230 member churches representing over 100 million Christians. More conservative bodies are gathered in the International Conference of Reformed Churches and the World Reformed Fellowship.

Characteristics

Reformed churches share confessional documents such as the Westminster Confession, the Heidelberg Catechism, and the Belgic Confession; a strong emphasis on preaching; and the offices of minister, elder, and deacon. Their worship historically favoured psalm singing and plain, whitewashed interiors.
