Blue and white pottery

Ming dynasty blue-and-white vase

Blue and white pottery (Chinese: 青花瓷) covers a wide range of white porcelain and pottery decorated under the glaze with a blue pigment, generally cobalt oxide. The decoration was commonly applied by hand, originally by brush painting, later by stamping or transfer printing. Blue and white wares were first produced in China, from where they spread across East Asia and, through trade, to the Islamic world and Europe.

Origins in China

Although earlier examples exist from the Tang dynasty, blue and white porcelain reached maturity under the Yuan dynasty (1271–1368) at the kilns of Jingdezhen, using cobalt imported from Persia. Under the Ming dynasty it became the dominant Chinese export ware; the reign of the Xuande Emperor (1426–1435) is often regarded as the high point of imperial quality, and the later "Kraak" porcelain carried blue and white designs to Europe in quantity.

Influence abroad

Imported Chinese wares inspired imitations in Persia, in the Ottoman world, and in Japan, where Imari and Kakiemon porcelain was exported to Europe when Chinese trade was disrupted. In the Netherlands, the scarcity of imports during the Ming–Qing transition spurred the development of Delftware, tin-glazed earthenware painted with Dutch and Chinese scenes. English factories later industrialised the style through transfer printing, as in the famous Willow pattern.

Technique

The cobalt pigment is painted onto the unfired body and covered with a transparent glaze; after a single high-temperature firing, the decoration appears bright blue beneath the glassy surface, protected from wear. Because cobalt withstands the highest firing temperatures, blue was for centuries the only colour usable for underglaze decoration on porcelain.
