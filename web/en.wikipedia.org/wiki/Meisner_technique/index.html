The Meisner technique is an approach to acting developed by the American theatre practitioner Sanford Meisner. The technique is designed to train actors to respond truthfully and spontaneously to their partners and circumstances rather than performing predetermined emotional states. Meisner's famous definition of acting is "living truthfully under imaginary circumstances."

Meisner developed the approach over decades of teaching at the Neighborhood Playhouse School of the Theatre in New York City, drawing on his experience with the Group Theatre and his differences with purely internal, memory-based methods. His teaching is systematized in the book Sanford Meisner on Acting (1987).

The repetition exercise

The foundation of the training is the repetition exercise, in which two actors face each other and repeat a simple observation back and forth ("You have a blue shirt." / "I have a blue shirt."), allowing genuine impulses, emotional shifts, and changes in behavior to emerge organically from listening. As training progresses, repetition is combined with independent activities, emotional preparation, improvisation, and scene work.

Principles

  • Acting is reacting: attention is placed on the partner, not on oneself.
  • Emotion arises from doing and from the imaginary circumstances, not from forcing feeling.
  • "The reality of doing": actors perform genuinely difficult tasks so that behavior is real rather than illustrated.
  • Impulse and instinct are trusted over intellectual planning.

Notable students

Actors trained in the technique include Gregory Peck, Robert Duvall, Diane Keaton, Jeff Goldblum, James Gandolfini, and many others.
