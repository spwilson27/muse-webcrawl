Torture

Torture
Definition: Deliberate infliction of severe pain or suffering
Status in law: Absolutely prohibited; peremptory norm (jus cogens)
Key instrument: UN Convention against Torture (1984)
Observance: 26 June — UN International Day in Support of Victims of Torture
[Image: A historical print depicting the use of the rack, among the judicial torture instruments once authorised in European legal proceedings.]

Torture is the deliberate infliction of severe pain or suffering on a person for reasons such as obtaining information or a confession, punishment, intimidation or coercion, or discrimination. Under Article 1 of the United Nations Convention against Torture (1984), the definition is limited to pain or suffering inflicted by, or with the consent or acquiescence of, a public official acting in an official capacity, and excludes pain arising from lawful sanctions. Torture inflicted in private settings is addressed under other bodies of law, including domestic violence and criminal law.

History

Torture was for centuries a regular part of legal procedure: Roman law permitted the torture of slaves (and later of free persons in treason cases), and medieval and early modern European courts admitted judicial torture to obtain confessions, regulated — notoriously — by rules such as those of the Constitutio Criminalis Carolina (1532). Enlightenment critics, above all Cesare Beccaria in On Crimes and Punishments (1764), attacked torture as both cruel and epistemically worthless, since pain makes the innocent confess as readily as the guilty. European states abolished judicial torture between the mid-eighteenth and mid-nineteenth centuries. Torture persisted and expanded, however, in colonial settings, in the secret police practices of twentieth-century regimes, and in counter-insurgency and "war on terror" contexts, prompting its absolute prohibition in modern human rights law.

Prohibition

The prohibition of torture is absolute and non-derogable under the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Art. 5), the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (Art. 7), the European Convention on Human Rights (Art. 3), and the Convention against Torture, which adds obligations to criminalise torture, establish jurisdiction (including universal jurisdiction), refuse to return persons to states where they face a risk of torture (non-refoulement), and exclude evidence obtained by torture. The prohibition is recognised as a peremptory norm of international law from which no derogation is permitted — no exceptional circumstances whatsoever, whether war, instability, or public emergency, may be invoked as justification.

Methods and effects

Methods documented by monitoring bodies range from beatings, electric shocks, asphyxiation and stress positions to mock execution, sleep deprivation, and threats against family members; so-called "torture lite" debates concern methods designed to leave no marks. Medical and psychological research documents long-lasting physical injury and high rates of post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, and chronic pain among survivors, and rehabilitation is provided by centres coordinated through the International Rehabilitation Council for Torture Victims.

Debate

Although the legal prohibition is absolute, public debate recurrently raises hypothetical "ticking bomb" scenarios in favour of coercive interrogation; the mainstream legal and professional consensus, supported by official inquiries, rejects the practice as unlawful and unreliable. Documented programmes of coercive interrogation in the twenty-first century have been condemned by treaty bodies and have led to litigation, compensation, and findings of torture by international courts in several cases.
