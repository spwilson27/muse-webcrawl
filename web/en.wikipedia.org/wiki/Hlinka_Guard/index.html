Hlinka Guard

The Hlinka Guard (Slovak: Hlinkova garda, HG) was the paramilitary militia of the Hlinka Slovak People's Party (HSĽS) during the period of the Slovak State (1939–1945). It was named after the party's founder, the Catholic priest Andrej Hlinka, who died in August 1938, shortly before the Guard's formal establishment in October 1938.

Formation and role

The Guard emerged from the party's earlier Rodobrana organisation and was legalised as Slovakia gained autonomy within Czechoslovakia after the Munich Agreement. Styled partly on the German SA, its members wore black uniforms and participated in intimidation of political opponents, Czechs, and Jews. Commanders included Karol Sidor and, from 1940, Alexander Mach, a leading radical in the party. The Guard served as an instrument of the regime of Jozef Tiso, though its radical wing often pushed for closer alignment with Nazi Germany.

Persecution of Jews

The Hlinka Guard took part in enforcing anti-Jewish legislation in Slovakia, including "Aryanisation" of Jewish property, round-ups, and guarding assembly points during the deportations of 1942, in which some 58,000 Slovak Jews were sent to Nazi extermination and concentration camps in German-occupied Poland. Members of the Guard's Emergency Divisions (Pohotovostné oddiely Hlinkovej gardy) participated in atrocities against Jews, Roma, and Slovak partisans during the suppression of the Slovak National Uprising in 1944–1945.

Dissolution

The Guard was dissolved with the collapse of the Slovak State in spring 1945. After the war, several leading figures were tried; Mach was sentenced to thirty years' imprisonment, while Tiso was executed in 1947.
