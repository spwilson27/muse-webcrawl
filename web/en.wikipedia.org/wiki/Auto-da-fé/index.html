Auto-da-fé

Literal meaning"Act of faith" (Portuguese/Spanish, from Latin actus fidei)
Associated withSpanish and Portuguese Inquisitions
Era15th – early 19th centuries

An auto-da-fé (from Portuguese auto da fé, "act of faith") was the ritual of public penance carried out between the 15th and 19th centuries, by which condemned heretics and apostates were sentenced by the Inquisition. It was a major public spectacle in Spain and Portugal and their colonies, designed both to edify the faithful and to terrorise the population into orthodoxy.

The ceremony

The auto-da-fé was a carefully staged public event, often held in a town's main square on a feast day and attended by clergy, civic authorities and large crowds. After a procession, Mass and sermon, the sentences of the prisoners were read out: the penitent were reconciled to the Church with punishments such as fines, flogging, wearing the penitential garment called the sambenito, or imprisonment. Those condemned to death — the "relaxed" — were handed over to the secular authorities, since the Church formally did not shed blood, and were executed, usually by burning, after the ceremony itself.

History

The first recorded auto-da-fé of the Spanish Inquisition took place in Seville in 1481. The ritual spread through Spain, Portugal and their overseas territories, including Mexico City, Lima, Goa and Lisbon, where the great auto of 1540 inaugurated the Portuguese tribunal. Autos declined in the eighteenth century as Enlightenment ideas weakened the Inquisitions; the last person executed following an auto-da-fé in Spain was the schoolmaster Cayetano Ripoll, hanged in 1826.

Popular misconception

Strictly speaking the auto-da-fé was the ceremony of sentencing, not the execution itself, though in popular usage the term has come to denote the burning of heretics generally.
