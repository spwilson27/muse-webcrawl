Tide pool

A tide pool or rock pool is a shallow pool of seawater that forms on the rocky intertidal shore. Many of these pools exist as separate bodies of water only at low tide, when they are cut off from the sea, and are refilled as the tide rises.

Formation and conditions

Tide pools form in depressions in rock, in crevices, and around boulders in the intertidal zone. Between tides, their inhabitants face sharply changing conditions: temperature and salinity fluctuate with sun, wind, rain and evaporation; oxygen levels fall at night as algae respire; and pools at higher shore levels may remain isolated for many hours. Organisms living in pools must therefore tolerate much harsher and more variable conditions than those in the open sea.

Life in tide pools

Tide pools support a rich community of marine life adapted to these stresses, including sea anemones, sea stars, mussels, limpets, barnacles, periwinkles, hermit and shore crabs, small fish such as sculpins and blennies, nudibranchs, sea urchins and many kinds of seaweed. Competition and predation are intense: classic ecological experiments on the sea star Pisaster ochraceus in Pacific Northwest pools and rock surfaces demonstrated the keystone predator concept. Zonation is evident between pools, with higher pools dominated by the hardiest species such as periwinkles and rough limpets, and lower pools holding a greater diversity including kelps and surfgrass.

Human interest

"Tidepooling" — exploring pools at low tide — is a popular educational activity on rocky coasts, though visitors are urged not to remove animals, since many are sensitive to disturbance and protected by law in some areas.
