Galaxy cluster
MembersHundreds to thousands of galaxies
SizeSeveral megaparsecs across
Mass1014–1015 solar masses, mostly dark matter
ExamplesVirgo Cluster, Coma Cluster, Perseus Cluster, Bullet Cluster

A galaxy cluster, or a cluster of galaxies, is a structure that consists of anywhere from hundreds to thousands of galaxies that are bound together by gravity, with typical masses ranging from 1014 to 1015 solar masses. They are the second-largest known gravitationally bound structures in the universe after galaxy filaments and were believed to be the largest until superseded in the 1980s by the discovery of superclusters.

Composition

Only a small fraction of a cluster's mass is in galaxies and stars. Most of the visible baryonic mass is a hot intracluster medium — plasma at tens of millions of degrees that glows in X-rays — and roughly 80–90% of the total mass is dark matter, inferred from galaxy motions, X-ray gas temperatures, and gravitational lensing. The Bullet Cluster, where lensing mass is offset from the colliding gas, is celebrated evidence for dark matter.

Properties and observation

Clusters usually have a dominant giant elliptical galaxy near their centre and member galaxies spread over several megaparsecs. They are observed optically, in X-rays (e.g., by Chandra), and via the Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect on the cosmic microwave background. Clusters grow by merging and sit at the nodes of the cosmic web; clusters themselves group into superclusters such as Laniakea, which contains our own Local Group's Virgo Cluster.
