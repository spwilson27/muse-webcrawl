War Is a Racket is a speech and a 1935 short book by Smedley D. Butler, a retired United States Marine Corps major general and two-time Medal of Honor recipient. Butler argues that wars are conducted for the financial benefit of a small elite — arms manufacturers, bankers and corporations — while ordinary soldiers pay the cost.

Contents

The book opens with the declaration: "War is a racket. It always has been. It is possibly the oldest, easily the most profitable, surely the most vicious. It is the only one international in scope. It is the only one in which the profits are reckoned in dollars and the losses in lives."

Drawing on his 33 years of service — during which he fought in the Philippines, China, Central America and the Caribbean, and in the First World War — Butler recounts how American military interventions served the interests of oil companies, banks and fruit companies, describing himself as having been "a high class muscle man for Big Business, for Wall Street and the bankers... a racketeer, a gangster for capitalism."

In its five chapters — "War is a racket", "Who makes the profits?", "Who pays the bills?", "How to smash this racket!" and "To hell with war!" — Butler lists the extraordinary profits of munitions makers and corporations during the First World War, and proposes remedies: conscripting capital and industry before conscripting soldiers, limiting the military to home defence, and deciding war by a vote of those who would have to fight it.

Influence

The booklet has remained in print for decades and is regarded as an early and influential statement of what later became criticism of the military-industrial complex. Butler's related 1933 speeches against fascism, and his testimony about the so-called Business Plot, reinforce his late-life reputation as a critic of the forces he had once served.
