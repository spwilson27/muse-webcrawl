1755 Lisbon earthquake

The 1755 Lisbon earthquake, also known as the Great Lisbon earthquake, struck Portugal on the morning of 1 November 1755, All Saints' Day. The earthquake, followed by a tsunami and fires, almost totally destroyed Lisbon and adjoining areas. Its magnitude is estimated at 7.7 or greater, and total deaths in Lisbon alone are commonly estimated between 10,000 and 50,000.

Effects

The quake struck at about 09:40 local time while churches were crowded for the feast day. Fissures up to 5 metres wide opened in the city centre. Survivors who fled to the waterfront saw the sea recede and then return as a tsunami wave that engulfed the harbour and downtown. Fires from toppled candles and kitchens burned for five days. The royal Ribeira Palace, the opera house and much of the Baixa district were destroyed, and the shock was felt across Europe and North Africa; the tsunami reached the Caribbean.

Response and legacy

The Marquis of Pombal, chief minister to Joseph I, directed relief and ordered the Baixa rebuilt on a grid plan with earthquake-resistant timber-lattice construction (the gaiola pombalina), an early example of anti-seismic design. The disaster shook Enlightenment thought: Voltaire attacked philosophical optimism in Candide and his Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne, while Kant and Rousseau wrote essays on the event. Modern seismology traces part of its origin to the systematic data collection Pombal ordered from parishes across Portugal.
