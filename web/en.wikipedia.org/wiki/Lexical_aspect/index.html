Lexical aspect

Lexical aspect, also called Aktionsart or situation aspect, is the inherent temporal structure of a situation expressed by a verb or verb phrase — whether it is static or dynamic, bounded (telic) or unbounded (atelic), punctual or durative. It is distinct from grammatical aspect, which is the speaker's viewpoint on a situation (for example, perfective versus imperfective).

Vendler's classification

The most influential taxonomy is that of Zeno Vendler (1957), who divided verb predicates into four classes:

ClassPropertiesExamples
StatesStatic, durative, atelicknow, love, be tall
ActivitiesDynamic, durative, atelicrun, swim, push a cart
AccomplishmentsDynamic, durative, telicbuild a house, run a mile
AchievementsDynamic, punctual, telicarrive, win the race, notice

A fifth class, semelfactives (cough, knock), was added by later scholars. Tests such as compatibility with the progressive, with duration adverbials ("for an hour" versus "in an hour"), and entailment patterns help diagnose class membership.

Composition and coercion

Lexical aspect is compositional: the class of a verb phrase depends on the verb plus its arguments and adjuncts — "run" is an activity, "run a mile" an accomplishment, and a definite object can make a predicate telic ("eat cake" versus "eat the cake"). Speakers can also shift a predicate's class in context, a process called aspectual coercion. Lexical aspect interacts with tense, grammatical aspect, and in Slavic languages with the perfectivising prefixes that systematically convert activities into accomplishments or achievements.
