Hejaz Railway

The Hejaz railway was a narrow-gauge railway (1,050 mm) built by the Ottoman Empire from Damascus to Medina, through the Hejaz region of modern-day Jordan and Saudi Arabia. It was part of the empire's railway network and was built to facilitate the transport of pilgrims to the holy cities of Islam and to extend Ottoman military control in the Arabian Peninsula. The line ran 1,320 kilometres (820 mi) and was built in only eight years; the first train reached Medina on 1 September 1908, the anniversary of Sultan Abdul Hamid II's accession.

Construction and operation

Announced in 1900, the project was financed by donations from Muslims worldwide and constructed largely by Ottoman soldiers under the direction of German engineer Heinrich August Meissner Pasha. Trains could complete the Damascus–Medina journey in about four days, compared with some forty days by camel caravan. The southernmost section into Mecca was never completed. A branch line to the port of Haifa, the Jezreel Valley railway, opened in 1905.

First World War and after

During the First World War the line became a target of the Arab Revolt; raiding parties associated with Emir Faisal and T. E. Lawrence repeatedly attacked bridges, stations and trains, tying down Ottoman troops. After the war the railway was divided between French-mandated Syria, Transjordan and Saudi Arabia; through services to Medina ceased, and the southern section in Saudi Arabia was abandoned. In Jordan, the surviving railway still operates freight (mainly phosphates) and occasional tourist trains from Amman, while restored stations and locomotives survive in Medina, Mada'in Saleh (Hegra), Tabuk and elsewhere. Periodic proposals to rebuild the full route have not been realised.
