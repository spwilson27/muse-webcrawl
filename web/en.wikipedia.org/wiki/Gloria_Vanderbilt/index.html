Gloria Laura Vanderbilt (20 February 1924 – 17 June 2019) was an American artist, author, actress, fashion designer and socialite, a member of the wealthy Vanderbilt family. As a child she was the subject of one of the most sensational custody trials of the 1930s, dubbed "the trial of the century", and as an adult she reinvented herself repeatedly — as a painter, a theatre actress, a bestselling author and the designer behind one of the first lines of designer jeans, bearing her swan logo and signature.

The custody trial

Vanderbilt was the only child of Reginald Claypoole Vanderbilt, a grandson of Cornelius Vanderbilt II, who died when she was 18 months old, leaving her a multimillion-dollar trust fund. Raised largely in France by her nurse and grandmother while her mother, Gloria Morgan Vanderbilt, pursued a social life abroad, she became the object of a bitter 1934 custody battle between her mother and her paternal aunt, Gertrude Vanderbilt Whitney, founder of the Whitney Museum. The trial generated enormous press coverage — the child was called "the poor little rich girl" — and custody was awarded to Whitney, with her mother's access restricted. The episode was later dramatised in the book and television miniseries Little Gloria... Happy at Last (1982).

Career

After marriages to agent Pasquale DiCicco and conductor Leopold Stokowski, Vanderbilt studied art and built a career as a painter and textile designer; she also acted on stage and television in the 1950s. In the 1970s she licensed her name and designs, and in 1979 Murjani Worldwide launched Gloria Vanderbilt jeans — among the first designer denim lines, tapered, high-waisted and embroidered with a swan — which became a commercial phenomenon and made her a fashion mogul. She wrote novels, memoirs and the book It Seemed Important at the Time (2004), and with her youngest son, journalist Anderson Cooper, co-authored The Rainbow Comes and Goes (2016), an exchange of letters that became a bestseller.

Personal life and death

Vanderbilt married four times: to DiCicco (1941–45), Stokowski (1945–55), director Sidney Lumet (1956–63) and author Wyatt Emory Cooper (1963–78), who died during heart surgery. She had four sons; her son Carter Vanderbilt Cooper died by suicide in 1988, a loss she wrote about in A Mother's Story (1996). In later years she was defrauded of millions by a psychiatrist and an associate, winning a court judgment largely uncollectable. She remained a fixture of New York cultural life into her nineties and died at home in Manhattan on 17 June 2019, aged 95.
