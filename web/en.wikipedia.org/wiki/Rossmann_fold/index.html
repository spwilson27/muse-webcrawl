Rossmann fold

The Rossmann fold is a tertiary fold found in proteins that bind nucleotides, such as enzyme cofactors FAD, NAD+ and NADP+. It is composed of a series of alternating beta strands and alpha helical segments, in which the beta strands are hydrogen-bonded to each other in a parallel beta sheet and form together with the helices a three-layer sandwich. The fold is named after Michael Rossmann, who first described it in dehydrogenases in the early 1970s.

Structure

The classic Rossmann fold consists of six parallel beta strands connected by alpha helices in the order β-α-β-α-β, the strands forming a central sheet with the topology 321456. The nucleotide-binding site lies at the C-terminal edge of the sheet, where a characteristic glycine-rich loop (typically with the sequence motif Gly-X-Gly-X-X-Gly) wraps around the pyrophosphate group of the dinucleotide. Because the fold binds the adenosine-containing portion shared by NAD, FAD and coenzyme A, it appears in a wide range of oxidoreductases and other enzymes.

Variations

Many nucleotide-binding domains use slightly modified versions: the number of strands may vary, and in some enzymes two Rossmann-like halves bind one cofactor each. The related P-loop NTPase fold differs in strand order and is characteristic of ATP- and GTP-binding proteins.

Significance

The Rossmann fold is one of the most ancient and widespread protein folds, and its prevalence among enzymes of central metabolism has led to suggestions that it originated very early in the evolution of life, possibly before the last universal common ancestor.
