Ma'luf (Arabic: المألوف, literally "the familiar" or "customary") is a genre of art music in the Andalusian classical music tradition of the Maghreb, found principally in Tunisia, Libya, and the eastern Algerian city of Constantine. It descends from the court music of Muslim-ruled al-Andalus (Iberia), carried to North Africa by refugees during and after the Christian reconquest, especially following the expulsion of the Moriscos in 1609.

Structure

Ma'luf is organised into the nūba, a suite of pieces — instrumental preludes and songs — all in a single melodic mode (ṭabʿ, analogous to the maqam of the Arab east) and progressing through a sequence of rhythmic cycles of increasing animation. The traditional Tunisian repertoire counts thirteen nūbāt, each associated with a mode and, traditionally, a time of day or an affective quality. Texts are classical Arabic poetic forms — muwashshaḥ and zajal — treating themes of love, wine and spiritual longing.

Instruments and performance

A traditional ensemble (firqa) combines the ʿūd (lute), rabāb (spike fiddle), qānūn or guitar, violins, nay (flute), and percussion including the ṭār (frame drum) and darbūka. The lead singer, often also directing the ensemble, performs with a chorus in a largely heterophonic texture interspersed with instrumental interludes (taqsīm-like improvisations in some traditions).

Preservation

In Tunisia, ma'luf was codified and promoted in the 20th century by the Rashidiyya Institute (founded 1934 under Baron Rodolphe d'Erlanger, whose palace at Sidi Bou Said hosted the 1932 Congress of Arab Music era research), which collected, notated and taught the repertoire. Radio ensembles and state troupes in Tunisia and Libya kept the genre central to national musical identity, though it faces declining youth audiences and competition from popular music. Libya's ma'luf tradition, centred on Tripoli, shares the Andalusian inheritance with distinctive local modes and instrumentation.
