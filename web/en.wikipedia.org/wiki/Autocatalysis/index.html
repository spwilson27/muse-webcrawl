Autocatalysis

Autocatalysis is a chemical process in which one of the products of a reaction acts as a catalyst for the reaction itself. Because the amount of catalyst grows as the reaction proceeds, autocatalytic reactions characteristically display an induction period of slow reaction followed by rapid acceleration, producing a sigmoid (S-shaped) concentration profile.

Mechanism and kinetics

In the simplest autocatalytic scheme, a reactant A is converted into product B, and B catalyses its own formation: A + B → 2B. The rate of reaction is proportional to the product of the concentrations of A and B, so as B accumulates the reaction accelerates until A is depleted. This produces the characteristic logistic growth curve familiar from population dynamics, to which autocatalysis is mathematically analogous.

Examples

Well-known examples include the acidic hydrolysis of esters, in which the carboxylic acid product catalyses further hydrolysis; the reaction of permanganate with oxalate, autocatalysed by the manganese(II) ions produced; and the formose reaction, in which glycolaldehyde catalyses the synthesis of sugars from formaldehyde — a reaction of interest in prebiotic chemistry. Oscillating reactions such as the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction contain autocatalytic steps that help produce their striking periodic colour changes. The chlorite–iodide and "iodine clock" systems also depend on autocatalytic generation of intermediates.

Autocatalytic sets and the origin of life

A set of molecules is called an autocatalytic set if each member's formation is catalysed by another member of the set, so that the set as a whole is self-sustaining and self-reproducing. Stuart Kauffman and others have proposed that such sets could have arisen spontaneously in prebiotic chemistry and represent a plausible step toward the origin of life, preceding the emergence of template-replicating molecules such as RNA.
