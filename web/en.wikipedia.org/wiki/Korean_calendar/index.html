The Korean calendar is a lunisolar calendar derived from the Chinese calendar, traditionally used to mark festivals, agricultural seasons and auspicious dates in Korea. Like the Chinese system, it divides the year into 12 lunar months, inserting a leap month roughly seven times every 19 years to keep the calendar in step with the solar year, and organises time by the 24 solar terms (jeolgi) and the sexagenary cycle of heavenly stems and earthly branches.

History

Korea adopted Chinese calendrical systems from antiquity; the kingdoms of Goguryeo, Baekje and Silla used Chinese calendars, and the Goryeo dynasty published its own. During Joseon, the Bureau of Astronomy (Seoun'gwan) produced calendars, most notably the Chiljeongsan (1442) compiled under King Sejong. Era names were used for regnal dating, and from 1896 the Gregorian calendar was officially adopted under the Geonyang ("sunshine") era proclamation of King Gojong.

Modern usage

Both Koreas now use the Gregorian calendar for civil purposes, but the traditional lunisolar calendar still fixes Korea's great holidays: Seollal (Lunar New Year) on the first day of the first lunar month, and Chuseok, the harvest festival, on the 15th day of the eighth lunar month; Buddha's Birthday falls on the eighth day of the fourth month. South Korea used the Dangi era — counting years from the legendary founding of Gojoseon by Dangun in 2333 BC — between 1948 and 1961. North Korea since 1997 has used the Juche calendar, whose year 1 is 1912, the birth year of Kim Il-sung, alongside the Gregorian month and day. Traditional age reckoning, in which a newborn was counted as one year old and everyone aged together at New Year, was standardised to international reckoning in South Korea in 2023.
