Term structure of interest rates

In economics and finance, the term structure of interest rates (also known as the yield curve) is the relationship between interest rates or bond yields and different terms or maturities. When graphed, the term structure is known as a yield curve and plays a central role in identifying the current state and future expected path of the economy.

A normal (upward-sloping) yield curve

Shapes

The yield curve is usually upward sloping ("normal"), because longer maturities carry greater risk and demand higher yields. A flat curve suggests transition or uncertainty; an inverted curve — long rates below short rates — has preceded most modern recessions and is closely watched as a recession indicator.

Theories

  • Expectations hypothesis — long rates equal the average of expected future short rates; the curve's slope reflects expected rate changes.
  • Liquidity preference theory — investors demand a term premium for holding longer bonds, so the curve slopes up even when rates are expected to be flat.
  • Market segmentation theory — supply and demand in each maturity segment are determined by different classes of investors with distinct preferences (preferred habitat).

Construction and use

Term structures are constructed from the prices of coupon bonds (for example by bootstrapping) or from interest-rate swaps. Central banks and treasuries publish curves daily; a well-known model is the Nelson–Siegel (and Svensson) parameterisation. The term structure is used to price bonds and derivatives, to value pension liabilities, and to infer market expectations of growth, inflation and monetary policy.
