Strong mayor

The strong mayor form of government, or mayor–council government, is one of the two main forms of municipal government in the United States (the other being council–manager government). In a strong-mayor system, the elected mayor serves as the city's chief executive, holding substantial administrative and budgetary authority comparable to that of a governor or president at their levels of government.

A mayor signing legislation at a desk in city hall.

Characteristics

Under a strong-mayor system, the mayor typically:

  • is directly elected by the voters of the city;
  • appoints and removes department heads, often without council approval;
  • prepares and administers the municipal budget;
  • may veto ordinances passed by the city council (subject to override);
  • directly oversees the day-to-day operations of city government.

The council acts primarily as a legislative body. Large U.S. cities such as New York, Chicago, Houston and Los Angeles use versions of the mayor–council system. By contrast, in a "weak mayor" system, the mayoralty is largely ceremonial, executive power is shared with or held by the council and appointed officials, and the mayor may be chosen by the council from among its members — a model related to the town council form of government.

Debate

Supporters argue the strong-mayor model provides clear democratic accountability and decisive leadership; critics argue it concentrates power, can encourage patronage, and depends heavily on the competence of one officeholder, whereas council–manager government vests administration in a professional manager.
