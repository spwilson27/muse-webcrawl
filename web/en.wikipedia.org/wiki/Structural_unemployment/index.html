Structural unemployment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Structural unemployment
TypeInvoluntary unemployment
CausesSkills mismatch, technological change, geographic mismatch, industry decline

Structural unemployment is a form of involuntary unemployment caused by a mismatch between the skills that workers in the economy can offer and the skills demanded of workers by employers, or by workers living too far from regions where jobs are available. It differs from frictional unemployment, the short-term joblessness of people moving between jobs, and from cyclical unemployment, which rises in recessions because of deficient demand.

Causes

  • Technological change: automation and new production methods make some occupations obsolete while creating demand for new skills.
  • Industry decline and trade: when an industry contracts—such as coal mining or traditional manufacturing in many industrialised countries—workers' specialised skills may not transfer to growing sectors.
  • Geographic mismatch: jobs may be available in one region while unemployed workers live in another and face barriers to moving, such as housing costs.
  • Institutional factors: occupational licensing, minimum wages set above some workers' productivity, and efficiency-wage practices can contribute to structural joblessness.

Measurement

Structural unemployment is associated with an outward shift of the Beveridge curve—the relationship between job vacancies and unemployment—meaning more unemployment coexists with any given level of vacancies. It raises the economy's natural rate of unemployment (the NAIRU), and its victims tend to experience long spells of unemployment; long spells can themselves erode skills, an effect known as hysteresis.

Policy responses

Because structural unemployment stems from mismatch rather than weak demand, demand stimulus alone cannot cure it. Commonly proposed remedies include retraining and apprenticeships, education aligned with employer needs, relocation assistance, portable benefits, and reducing barriers to occupational mobility and housing supply in job-rich areas.

Examples

Classic cases include displaced handloom weavers during industrialisation, coal miners in 1980s Britain, and Rust Belt manufacturing workers in the United States after the decline of steel and automotive employment. More recently, economists debated whether the slow recovery of employment after the Great Recession reflected structural mismatch or simply weak demand; most evidence favoured demand deficiency, though some sectors showed genuine skill shortages.
