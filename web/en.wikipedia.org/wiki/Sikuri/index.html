Sikuri

Sikuri is a traditional musical genre and dance of the Aymara and Quechua peoples of the Altiplano of Bolivia and Peru, performed by large ensembles of siku (Andean panpipe) players accompanied by drums such as the bombo and caja. The players themselves are also called sikuris.

Instruments and organisation

The siku consists of two rows of bamboo tubes. Its notes are divided between two complementary halves, ira and arca, traditionally played by two different musicians whose parts interlock — a technique called hocket — so that the melody only exists when the community plays together. Ensembles may number from a dozen to over a hundred performers, organised into rows of different-sized instruments (toyo, zanka, malta, chuli) that play in parallel octaves.

Cultural role

Sikuri is performed at Catholic and indigenous festivals, weddings, and community celebrations in regions such as Italaque (Bolivia), regarded as a cradle of the genre, and in Conima and Puno (Peru), where the sikuri de Conima style is especially esteemed. The music is closely tied to agricultural cycles, reciprocity (ayni), and the ritual calendar of Altiplano communities. In the twentieth century sikuri ensembles also became a symbol of indigenous cultural identity in urban settings, universities, and political gatherings.
