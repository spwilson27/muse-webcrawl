Green tea is a type of tea made from the leaves and buds of Camellia sinensis that have not undergone the withering and oxidation process used for oolong and black teas. Originating in China, it spread across East and Southeast Asia and is now produced and consumed worldwide. Freshly plucked leaves are quickly heated — steamed in the Japanese style or pan-fired in the Chinese style — to halt oxidation, then rolled and dried, preserving their green colour and high content of catechin polyphenols.

History

Tea drinking in China is traditionally dated to the legendary emperor Shennong (c. 2737 BC). Steamed green tea became the dominant form during the Tang and Song dynasties; the monk Eisai is credited with bringing tea seeds and Zen tea culture to Japan in the 12th century, where it developed into the tea ceremony. Loose-leaf pan-fired styles such as Dragon Well (Longjing) rose to prominence in Ming-dynasty China.

Varieties

Famous Chinese green teas include Longjing, Biluochun, Huangshan Maofeng and gunpowder tea. Japanese green teas include sencha, gyokuro, kabusecha, tencha (ground to make matcha), genmaicha and hōjicha. Korean production centres on Boseong and Hadong counties. Green teas differ by cultivar, harvest season, shading and processing, and are usually brewed with water below boiling (about 70–85 °C) to avoid bitterness.

Health research

Green tea contains caffeine, the amino acid L-theanine, and catechins such as epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG). It has been extensively studied for possible effects on metabolism, cardiovascular risk and cancer, but regulatory bodies have generally found the evidence for health claims inconclusive; very high doses of concentrated extracts have been linked to liver injury.
