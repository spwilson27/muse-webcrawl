Thomas–Fermi model

Thomas–Fermi model
Developed: 1927
By: Llewellyn Thomas and Enrico Fermi
Field: Quantum mechanics, atomic physics

The Thomas–Fermi model is a quantum mechanical theory of the electronic structure of many-body systems developed independently by Llewellyn Thomas and Enrico Fermi in 1927. It is a semiclassical approximation in which the electrons in an atom are treated as a gas whose density determines the kinetic energy locally, ignoring shell structure.

Theory

In the model, the kinetic energy of a non-uniform electron gas is written as a functional of the electron density n(r): the density at each point is related to the local Fermi momentum, as in a uniform free-electron gas. Combined with the classical electrostatic interaction of the electrons with the nucleus and with each other, minimising the total energy yields the Thomas–Fermi equation, a nonlinear differential equation for the screening potential in the atom.

Significance and limitations

The model gives reasonable total energies and electron densities for heavy atoms and correctly predicts scaling laws with atomic number, but it fails to reproduce atomic shell structure, does not bind molecules, and is inaccurate for light atoms. Nevertheless, it was the first density functional theory — the precursor of modern approaches such as density functional theory (DFT) and the Hohenberg–Kohn theorems — and remains useful in plasma physics, astrophysics (dense stellar matter), and materials modelling.

Extensions

Paul Dirac added an exchange correction in 1930, giving the Thomas–Fermi–Dirac model; gradient (von Weizsäcker) corrections and relativistic versions have also been developed.
