Fact–value distinction

The fact–value distinction is a fundamental epistemological distinction described by philosophers who argue that there is a difference between descriptive statements of fact and evaluative statements of value. Statements of fact ("is" statements) describe what is the case, while statements of value ("ought" statements) express judgements about what should be the case.

The distinction separates descriptive claims from prescriptive ones.

Origins

The distinction is commonly traced to David Hume, who in A Treatise of Human Nature (1739) observed that authors often slide imperceptibly from "is" propositions to "ought" propositions without explaining the inference — a passage now known as Hume's law or the is–ought problem. G. E. Moore's concept of the naturalistic fallacy similarly warned against defining value in purely factual terms.

Debates

The distinction was central to logical positivism and to Max Weber's ideal of value-free social science. Critics such as Hilary Putnam have argued that fact and value are entangled, pointing to "thick" ethical concepts such as cruelty that are simultaneously descriptive and evaluative.
