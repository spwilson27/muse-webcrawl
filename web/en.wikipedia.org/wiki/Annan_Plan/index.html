Annan Plan

Presented31 March 2004 (final version)

ResultApproved by Turkish Cypriots (64.9%), rejected by Greek Cypriots (75.8% against)

The Annan Plan, named after United Nations Secretary-General Kofi Annan, was a proposal by the United Nations for resolving the Cyprus dispute by reunifying the island — divided since 1974 — as a federation of two constituent states under a common government, to be called the United Cyprus Republic.

Provisions

The plan, revised five times between November 2002 and March 2004, envisaged a loose confederation-style federation loosely modelled on Switzerland, with a Greek Cypriot state and a Turkish Cypriot state linked by a federal government. It provided for a rotating presidency, a bicameral parliament, a reduced Turkish military presence, territorial adjustments returning areas including parts of Famagusta to Greek Cypriot administration, and phased property restitution or compensation for those displaced in 1974.

Referendums

The plan was put to simultaneous referendums on 24 April 2004. Turkish Cypriots accepted it by 64.9% to 35.1%, while Greek Cypriots rejected it by 75.8% to 24.2%, following a campaign in which President Tassos Papadopoulos urged a "resounding no". Because approval by both communities was required, the plan failed.

Consequences

One week later, on 1 May 2004, the Republic of Cyprus joined the European Union, with the application of EU law suspended in the north. The outcome shaped subsequent UN-sponsored negotiations, including the talks that collapsed at Crans-Montana in 2017.
