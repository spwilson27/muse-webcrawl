Hydroboration: syn addition of a B–H bond across a carbon–carbon double bond.

Hydroboration is a chemical reaction in which a hydrogen–boron bond adds across a carbon–carbon double or triple bond, typically giving an organoborane product. The reaction was developed by Herbert C. Brown in the 1950s and became one of the most useful transformations in organic synthesis; Brown shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his work on boron compounds.

Mechanism and regioselectivity

Hydroboration proceeds through a concerted, four-centre transition state in which the B–H bond adds across the double bond in a single step. Because boron and hydrogen are delivered to the same face of the alkene, the addition is a syn addition. Regioselectively, the boron atom ends up on the less substituted carbon of the double bond — an anti-Markovnikov orientation — largely for steric reasons, since the bulky boron fragment preferentially approaches the less hindered carbon.

Hydroboration–oxidation

The most common application is the hydroboration–oxidation sequence, in which the organoborane intermediate is oxidised with alkaline hydrogen peroxide to an alcohol. The net result is the hydration of an alkene to give the anti-Markovnikov alcohol with overall syn stereochemistry — the opposite regiochemical outcome to acid-catalysed hydration. Terminal alkenes give primary alcohols, making the sequence a standard method for converting alkenes into alcohols that cannot be obtained directly.

Reagents

Borane itself is usually handled as its tetrahydrofuran complex (BH3·THF) or as diborane gas. More selective reagents include disiamylborane, 9-borabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane (9-BBN), catecholborane and pinacolborane; the latter two are widely used in transition-metal-catalysed hydroborations, including rhodium- and iridium-catalysed variants that can reverse the usual regioselectivity.

Applications

Beyond alcohol synthesis, organoboranes from hydroboration are intermediates in the Suzuki–Miyaura cross-coupling reaction, in amination, halogenation and carbon-chain extension (carbonylation) reactions, and in the synthesis of boron-containing pharmaceuticals such as the proteasome inhibitor bortezomib.
