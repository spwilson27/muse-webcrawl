Heroin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Heroin, also known as diamorphine in medical contexts, is a morphinan opioid substance synthesised from the naturally occurring opiate morphine, itself derived from the opium poppy. It is typically used recreationally for its euphoric effects and is highly addictive; repeated use produces tolerance, physical dependence, and a severe substance use disorder. Medically, diamorphine is still used in some countries for severe pain, including in palliative care.

Pharmacology

Heroin (diacetylmorphine) is a prodrug: after crossing the blood–brain barrier it is converted to 6-monoacetylmorphine and morphine, which act as agonists at μ-opioid receptors. Routes of use include injection, smoking, and insufflation. Overdose causes respiratory depression and can be fatal; it is treated with the antagonist naloxone.

History

Diamorphine was first synthesised in 1874 by C. R. Alder Wright in London and commercialised by Bayer from 1898 under the name "Heroin", marketed as a cough suppressant and supposedly non-addictive morphine substitute. Its addictive potential was soon recognised, and it became internationally controlled under the drug conventions; it is a Schedule I substance in the United States, while remaining available by prescription in the United Kingdom.

Society and harm

Illicit heroin is produced chiefly from opium grown in Afghanistan, Myanmar, and Mexico. Public-health responses include opioid substitution therapy with methadone or buprenorphine, needle exchanges, and, in some countries, supervised heroin-assisted treatment for refractory dependence.
