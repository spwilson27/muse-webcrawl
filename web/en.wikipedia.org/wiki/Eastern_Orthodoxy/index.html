Eastern Orthodoxy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Saint Basil's Cathedral in Moscow, an emblem of Russian Orthodoxy

Eastern Orthodoxy, also known as Eastern Orthodox Christianity, is the second-largest Christian church, with approximately 220 million baptised members. It operates as a communion of autocephalous (self-governing) national and regional churches — including the patriarchates of Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem, and the churches of Russia, Greece, Serbia, Romania, Bulgaria and Georgia — united in doctrine and sacramental communion rather than by a central authority. The Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople is recognised as first among equals.

History

The Orthodox Church traces its history to the early Church and the Byzantine Empire. Differences with the Latin West accumulated over centuries — over the filioque clause in the Creed, papal authority, and liturgical practice — and culminated in the East–West Schism conventionally dated to 1054. Orthodoxy spread among the Slavs through the mission of Saints Cyril and Methodius, survived the fall of Constantinople in 1453 under Ottoman rule, and spread worldwide through emigration. The 20th century brought persecution under communist regimes and, in 2018–19, a schism between Moscow and Constantinople over the autocephaly of the Orthodox Church of Ukraine.

Belief and practice

Orthodox theology rests on Scripture and Holy Tradition, the Nicene Creed (without the filioque), and the definitions of the seven ecumenical councils. Distinctive emphases include theosis (deification) as the goal of the Christian life, hesychast prayer, the veneration of icons, and a rich liturgical life centred on the Divine Liturgy. All seven sacraments ("mysteries") are recognised; married men may be ordained to the priesthood, while bishops are chosen from monastics.
