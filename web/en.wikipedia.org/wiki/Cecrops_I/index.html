Cecrops I (Ancient Greek: Κέκροψ) was a mythical king of Attica in Greek mythology, said to have reigned for fifty years. He was depicted as half man and half serpent, an image of his identity as an autochthon — one born directly from the earth of Attica itself. The Athenians traced their claim to be indigenous to their land, rather than immigrants, to Cecrops and his line.

Mythology

Cecrops founded Athens — called Cecropia after him — and established the citadel on the Acropolis. He was credited with introducing fundamental institutions of civilisation: marriage, reading and writing, burial customs, and the worship of Zeus and Athena. He is said to have abolished bloody sacrifices, substituting offerings of cakes.

Most famously, Cecrops acted as witness or judge in the contest between Athena and Poseidon for patronage of the city. Poseidon struck the rock of the Acropolis with his trident, producing a salt-water spring; Athena planted the first olive tree. Cecrops testified that Athena had planted the olive, and the city took her name. The marks of the trident and the sacred olive tree were preserved in the Erechtheion on the Acropolis.

Family

Cecrops married Aglaulus, daughter of Actaeus, the earlier king of the region. Their daughters were Aglaulus, Herse, and Pandrosus — figures in the myths of the daughters entrusted with the infant Erichthonius — and their son Erysichthon died childless. Cecrops was succeeded by Cranaus.

Legacy

The Athenian tribe Cecropis was named for him, and he received cult honours on the Acropolis. A later, distinct figure Cecrops II appears in some king lists as a descendant in the line of Erechtheus.
