Ménière's disease is an inner ear disorder characterized by episodes of vertigo, fluctuating hearing loss, tinnitus, and a feeling of fullness in the affected ear. Attacks typically last from two to twelve hours.

Signs and symptoms

Attacks begin suddenly and may occur daily or as rarely as once a year. Vertigo is usually the most disabling symptom, often accompanied by nausea and imbalance. Hearing loss is initially fluctuating and low-frequency, but over years it tends to become permanent and involve all frequencies.

Cause and mechanism

The underlying mechanism is associated with endolymphatic hydrops, an excess of endolymph fluid in the inner ear's labyrinth. Why the fluid accumulates is unclear; proposed factors include viral infection, autoimmune processes, constriction of blood vessels, and genetic predisposition.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based on the characteristic symptom pattern and audiometric confirmation of sensorineural hearing loss, after exclusion of other causes such as vestibular migraine. There is no cure; treatment aims to reduce attack frequency and severity through dietary sodium restriction, betahistine, diuretics, and, in refractory cases, intratympanic injections or surgery.

History

The condition is named after Prosper Ménière, the French physician who in 1861 reported that vertigo could originate from inner ear damage rather than from the brain.
