Quiver (mathematics)

Quiver
Field:Representation theory, algebra
Introduced by:Peter Gabriel (1972)

In mathematics, especially representation theory, a quiver is another name for a directed graph (a multidigraph allowing loops and multiple arrows). Quivers became central objects in representation theory after Peter Gabriel's fundamental work in the early 1970s, which used them to classify algebras of finite representation type.

Definitions

A quiver Q consists of a set of vertices Q0, a set of arrows Q1, and two maps assigning to each arrow its starting and ending vertex. A representation of a quiver over a field k assigns a vector space to each vertex and a linear map to each arrow, following the direction of the arrow.

The path algebra kQ of a quiver is the algebra whose basis is the set of all paths in Q, with multiplication given by concatenation of paths. Representations of Q correspond to modules over its path algebra (with mild finiteness conditions), which makes quivers a bridge between combinatorics and algebra.

Gabriel's theorem

Gabriel's theorem (1972) states that a connected quiver has only finitely many indecomposable representations (up to isomorphism) if and only if its underlying undirected graph is a Dynkin diagram of type A, D, or E. This discovery linked representation theory to Lie theory and launched the modern study of quiver representations.

Applications

Quivers and their representations appear across mathematics and physics: in the study of finite-dimensional algebras, cluster algebras (via quiver mutation), Donaldson–Thomas theory, string theory (quiver gauge theories), persistent homology in topological data analysis, and the construction of moduli spaces such as Nakajima quiver varieties.

Quivers with relations — adding an ideal of relations to the path algebra — generalise further, since every finite-dimensional algebra over an algebraically closed field can be presented as a quiver with relations.
