Endothelium

Endothelial cells forming the inner lining of a vessel

The endothelium is a single layer of squamous endothelial cells that lines the inner surface of blood vessels and lymphatic vessels, forming the interface between circulating blood or lymph and the wall of the vessel. It is a selectively permeable barrier that also regulates vascular tone, blood coagulation, inflammation and the formation of new blood vessels (angiogenesis).

Structure

Endothelial cells are flat, polygonal cells joined by tight and adherens junctions, resting on a basement membrane. Depending on the tissue, the endothelium may be continuous (as in most vessels and the blood–brain barrier), fenestrated with small pores (as in the kidney glomerulus and endocrine glands), or discontinuous/sinusoidal (as in the liver), matching the exchange needs of each organ. The total endothelial surface in an adult human is enormous — often estimated in the hundreds of square metres — leading it to be described as a distributed organ in its own right.

Functions

  • Vasomotor control: release of nitric oxide and prostacyclin (vasodilators) and endothelin (a vasoconstrictor).
  • Barrier and exchange: regulation of the passage of fluids, nutrients, hormones and cells between blood and tissues.
  • Haemostasis: a normally antithrombotic surface; on injury, expression of procoagulant molecules such as von Willebrand factor and tissue factor.
  • Inflammation: expression of adhesion molecules that recruit leukocytes to sites of infection or injury.
  • Angiogenesis: sprouting of new vessels in development, wound healing and disease.

Dysfunction and disease

Endothelial dysfunction — characterised by reduced nitric oxide availability, increased permeability and a pro-inflammatory, pro-thrombotic state — is an early event in atherosclerosis and is associated with hypertension, diabetes, smoking and hypercholesterolaemia. It is implicated in cardiovascular disease, sepsis and complications of viral infections.
