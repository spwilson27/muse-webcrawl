The Khrushchev Thaw (Russian: хрущёвская о́ттепель) is the period from the mid-1950s to the mid-1960s when political repression and censorship in the Soviet Union were partially relaxed under Nikita Khrushchev, following the death of Joseph Stalin in 1953. The name comes from Ilya Ehrenburg's 1954 novella The Thaw, published soon after Stalin's death.

De-Stalinisation

The defining moment came on 25 February 1956, when Khrushchev delivered his "Secret Speech" (On the Cult of Personality and Its Consequences) to a closed session of the 20th Congress of the Communist Party, denouncing Stalin's purges and cult of personality. Millions of prisoners were released from the Gulag under amnesties, victims were rehabilitated, deported peoples were partly restored to their homelands, and the feared secret police chief Lavrentiy Beria was arrested and executed. Mass housing construction — the prefabricated apartment blocks nicknamed khrushchyovka — transformed Soviet cities, and consumer goods became more available.

Culture and foreign policy

The Thaw saw an efflorescence of literature and cinema: Alexander Solzhenitsyn's One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich was published in 1962 with Khrushchev's personal approval; poets such as Yevgeny Yevtushenko read to stadium crowds; and films like The Cranes Are Flying (1957) won international prizes. Moscow hosted the World Festival of Youth and Students in 1957, opening the country to foreigners on an unprecedented scale, while Khrushchev pursued "peaceful coexistence" with the West — even as crises over Hungary (1956), Berlin and Cuba (1962) showed the Thaw's limits.

End

The liberalisation was always uneven — Khrushchev himself berated avant-garde artists at the 1962 Manege exhibition — and it ended with his ouster in October 1964. Under Leonid Brezhnev, controls tightened again into the period later called the Era of Stagnation, and many Thaw-era hopes were deferred until glasnost a generation later.
