Dire wolf
Scientific nameAenocyon dirus (formerly Canis dirus)
PeriodLate Pleistocene – early Holocene (c. 125,000–9,500 years ago)
RangeNorth and South America
SizeAbout 1.5 m long; 50–68 kg on average
StatusExtinct

The dire wolf is an extinct species of canid that lived in the Americas during the Late Pleistocene and early Holocene. First described from fossils found in Indiana in the 1850s, it was long known as Canis dirus, but a 2021 study of ancient DNA showed it belonged to a distinct lineage that diverged from other canids about 5.7 million years ago, and it is now placed in its own genus, Aenocyon.

Description

The dire wolf was roughly the size of the largest modern grey wolves but more heavily built, with a broader head, more massive teeth and shorter, sturdier limbs — adaptations suggesting it preyed on large Ice Age megafauna such as horses, bison, camels and young mammoths, and scavenged carcasses. Over 4,000 individuals have been excavated from the La Brea Tar Pits in Los Angeles, making it one of the best-known extinct mammals.

Extinction

Dire wolves disappeared around 9,500–13,000 years ago during the Quaternary extinction event, which removed most of the large prey animals of North America amid rapid climate change and the arrival of humans. Unlike grey wolves, they appear not to have interbred with other canids and left no living descendants.

In popular culture

The species gained renewed fame as the sigil animal of House Stark in George R. R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire novels and the television series Game of Thrones, which greatly increased public interest in the real animal.
