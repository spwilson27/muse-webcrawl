Military strategy

Strategy links military means to political objectives.

Military strategy is a set of ideas implemented by military organizations to pursue desired strategic goals. It is the art and science of employing armed forces to achieve the objectives of national policy, occupying a level above tactics (the conduct of individual engagements) and below grand strategy and policy. In modern doctrine, the operational level of war links strategy and tactics.

Classical theorists

Sun Tzu's The Art of War (5th century BC) emphasized deception, intelligence, and subduing the enemy without fighting. In the 19th century, Antoine-Henri Jomini systematized lines of operation and decisive points, while Carl von Clausewitz's On War (1832) framed war as "the continuation of politics by other means," stressing friction, the fog of war, and the destruction of enemy forces. Naval strategy was shaped by Alfred Thayer Mahan's sea power thesis, and airpower theorists such as Giulio Douhet argued for strategic bombing.

Concepts

Core concepts include center of gravity, deterrence, attrition versus maneuver, defense in depth, blitzkrieg, guerrilla warfare, counterinsurgency, nuclear strategy, and the post-Cold War revolution in military affairs based on precision weapons and networks. Strategy is constrained by logistics — summarized in the maxim that amateurs discuss tactics while professionals discuss logistics — and by economics, as reflected in a nation's military budget and overall military spending.
