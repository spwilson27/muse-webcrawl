Isotopes of carbon

Carbon (atomic number 6) has 15 known isotopes, from 8C to 22C. Two are stable: carbon-12 (12C, about 98.9% of natural carbon) and carbon-13 (13C, about 1.1%). A third, carbon-14 (14C), is radioactive with a half-life of about 5,730 years and is continuously produced in the atmosphere by cosmic rays.

The principal isotopes of carbon.

Carbon-12

Carbon-12 is the standard against which atomic masses are defined: the dalton (unified atomic mass unit) is exactly one twelfth of the mass of a 12C atom, and the mole was historically defined in terms of 12 grams of 12C.

Carbon-13

Carbon-13 has nuclear spin 1/2 and is the basis of 13C NMR spectroscopy. Variations in 13C/12C ratios are used in geochemistry, palaeoclimatology and ecology to trace carbon sources.

Carbon-14

Carbon-14 decays to nitrogen-14 by beta decay. Because living organisms maintain equilibrium with atmospheric 14C and it decays after death, measuring its remaining proportion enables radiocarbon dating of organic material up to about 50,000–60,000 years old.
