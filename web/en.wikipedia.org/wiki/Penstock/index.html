Penstock
Type: Conduit / sluice gate
Uses: Hydroelectric power, water supply, flood control

Penstocks conveying water to a hydroelectric powerhouse

A penstock is a sluice or gate, or a pipe or conduit, that controls the flow of water. The term is especially used for the inclined pipelines that carry water from a reservoir or headrace down to the turbines of a hydroelectric power station, where the pressure built up by the height difference — the hydraulic head — drives power generation.

Design

Hydroelectric penstocks are typically made of steel (sometimes reinforced concrete or HDPE for smaller installations) and must withstand high static pressures plus transient pressure surges known as water hammer, which occur when turbine valves close. Design features include surge tanks or shafts to absorb pressure waves, expansion joints, anchor blocks at bends, and air valves. Penstocks may be buried or exposed; historically, in the American West, they were sometimes built of wood staves bound with steel bands.

Other uses

The word also denotes sluice gates controlling flow in canals, mills, and flood-control structures. In British usage a penstock is a vertically sliding gate used to isolate sections of sewers and waterworks. Failures of penstocks — such as the 2005 Taum Sauk reservoir disaster in Missouri, though that involved the reservoir itself — underline the importance of inspection for corrosion, fatigue, and foundation movement.
