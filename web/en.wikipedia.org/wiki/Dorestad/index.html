Dorestad was an early medieval emporium (trading port) in the south-east of the modern Netherlands, near the present-day town of Wijk bij Duurstede. It flourished from the 7th to the mid-9th century as one of the most important trading centres of north-western Europe, linking the Frankish interior with Frisia, England and Scandinavia.

Geography and trade

Dorestad stood where the river Lek branched from the lower Rhine, close to the border between the Frankish Empire and Frisian territory. Its position let goods moving down the Rhine — wine, grain, pottery, quernstones from the Eifel, and metalwork — be transferred to seagoing ships for distribution around the North Sea, while imports such as Scandinavian furs and slaves, Frisian cloth and English goods flowed inland. The settlement stretched for kilometres along the riverbank, with jetties, warehouses and workshops, and it had its own mint striking coins for the Carolingian rulers.

History

The town changed hands repeatedly during the Frankish–Frisian wars of the late 7th and early 8th centuries, passing under Frankish control after the conquests of Pepin of Herstal and Charles Martel. Under Charlemagne and Louis the Pious it reached its height, mentioned frequently in annals and capitularies as a customs post and trading hub. Saint Boniface landed near Dorestad in 716 at the start of his mission to Frisia.

Decline

From 834 onwards Dorestad was raided repeatedly by Vikings, and it was granted by Lothar I to the Danish chieftains Rorik and Harald. At the same time the Lek channel silted up and the main course of the Rhine shifted, undermining the harbour. By about 850 the emporium had faded, its trade largely passing to places such as Tiel and Deventer.

Archaeology

Extensive excavations in the 1960s and 1970s uncovered the harbour works, cemeteries and settlement areas, making Dorestad one of the best-studied emporia of the Viking Age North Sea world, comparable to Hedeby, Ribe and Kaupang.
