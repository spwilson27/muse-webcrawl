Nuclear weapons and France

France is one of the five nuclear-weapon states recognised under the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons. France's nuclear deterrent, known as the force de frappe ("strike force"), was developed independently after the Second World War, largely under the impetus of President Charles de Gaulle, who sought strategic independence from the United States and the United Kingdom.

History

France's programme began in earnest in 1954 under Prime Minister Pierre Mendès France. The Commissariat à l'énergie atomique (CEA), founded in 1945, directed the work. France conducted its first nuclear test, codenamed Gerboise bleue, on 13 February 1960 at Reggane in the Algerian Sahara, with a yield of about 70 kilotons — making France the fourth nuclear power. Testing later moved to French Polynesia, at Mururoa and Fangataufa atolls, where 193 tests were conducted between 1966 and 1996.

Force de frappe

France originally maintained a triad of land-based missiles, submarine-launched ballistic missiles, and aircraft-delivered bombs. The land-based component on the Plateau d'Albion was dismantled in the 1990s. The modern deterrent rests on two legs: four Triomphant-class ballistic missile submarines based at Île Longue, armed with M51 missiles, and air-launched ASMP-A cruise missiles carried by Rafale aircraft of the French Air and Space Force and the French Navy.

Testing and moratorium

President Jacques Chirac announced a final series of tests in 1995–1996, which drew international protest, after which France signed the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty in 1996 and closed its Pacific test sites. Since then France has maintained its arsenal through simulation, including the Laser Mégajoule facility near Bordeaux.

Arsenal and doctrine

France is estimated to possess roughly 290 nuclear warheads. French doctrine holds that the deterrent protects the nation's "vital interests," whose definition is deliberately left to the President of the Republic, and provides for a possible final warning strike (avertissement nucléaire) before a full retaliation. Unlike during the Cold War, France reduced its arsenal unilaterally and dismantled its fissile material production facilities at Pierrelatte and Marcoule.
