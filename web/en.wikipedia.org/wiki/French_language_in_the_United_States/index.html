French language in the United States

French is concentrated historically in Louisiana and northern New England.

The French language is a minority language in the United States, spoken at home by roughly 1.2–2 million people according to recent census estimates, making it one of the most commonly spoken languages in the country after English and Spanish. French speakers arrived through colonial settlement, immigration from France, Canada, Haiti and francophone Africa, and include long-established regional communities whose distinctive varieties developed largely in isolation.

Regional varieties

Louisiana French, descended from the speech of French and Acadian settlers, is spoken in southern Louisiana by Cajun and Creole communities, closely related to the Louisiana Creole language. New England French developed among French-Canadian immigrants who came to work in the textile mills of Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, Massachusetts and Rhode Island in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Smaller historic varieties include Missouri French, once spoken by settlers in the upper Mississippi valley, now nearly extinct.

Status and education

French has no official status federally, though Louisiana has given it special recognition and supports immersion education through CODOFIL (founded 1968). French remains the most widely taught foreign language in American schools after Spanish. Both Louisiana and New England varieties have declined sharply under pressure from English-only schooling in the 20th century, but revitalisation efforts, French immersion programmes and francophone immigration have sustained the language's presence in American life.
