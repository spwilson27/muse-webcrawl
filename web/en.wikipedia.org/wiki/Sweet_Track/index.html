The Sweet Track is an ancient timber trackway, or causeway, in the Somerset Levels of south-west England. Built in the winter of 3807/3806 BC, it is one of the oldest engineered roads known in Britain and, at the time of its discovery, among the oldest timber trackways in northern Europe. It was constructed across a reed swamp between what was then an island at Westhay and a ridge of high ground at Shapwick, a distance of almost 2 kilometres.

Preserved timbers of the Sweet Track.

Discovery and dating

The track was discovered in 1970 by peat cutter Ray Sweet, after whom it is named, during commercial peat extraction for the firm E. J. Godwin. Excavations by the Somerset Levels Project, led by John Coles and Bryony Coles, revealed a sophisticated structure of oak planks laid over a foundation of crossed poles and rails of ash, oak and lime, pinned with wooden pegs. Dendrochronology (tree-ring dating) dated the felling of the timbers precisely to 3807 or 3806 BC, making the track nearly 6,000 years old. It appears to have been used for only about ten years before rising water levels submerged it, preserving the wood in waterlogged peat.

Construction and finds

The Sweet Track was built along the line of an even earlier trackway, the Post Track, dated to about 3838 BC. Its construction required large quantities of timber, including mature oaks split into planks using wedges and stone axes. Artefacts found along the route include fine flint and stone axes (one of jadeite), pottery, a child's toy axe, wooden artefacts and the remains of a possible votive offering, suggesting the track may have had ritual as well as practical significance.

Preservation

Most of the track remains buried in situ within the Shapwick Heath National Nature Reserve, kept waterlogged to prevent decay, with water levels actively managed by pumping. Some timbers were lifted and are preserved at the British Museum and in local museums; a replica section has been built along part of the original route for visitors.
