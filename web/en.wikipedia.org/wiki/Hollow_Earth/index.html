Hollow Earth

A diagram of the hollow Earth concept with openings at the poles

The Hollow Earth is a concept proposing that the planet Earth is entirely hollow or contains a substantial interior space. Notably suggested by Edmond Halley in the late 17th century, the notion has been repeatedly disproven, at first by Pierre Bouguer in 1740, then by Charles Hutton in his Schiehallion experiment around 1774. It was still occasionally defended in the early 19th century, notably by John Cleves Symmes Jr., but by then it had been thoroughly discredited by modern seismology, which established a solid understanding of the Earth's internal structure by the end of the 19th century.

History

In ancient times, the concept of a subterranean land inside the Earth appeared in mythology, folklore, and legends of underworlds such as the Greek Hades and Tartarus. In 1692 Edmond Halley proposed that the Earth consisted of nested concentric shells, suggesting aurorae might be caused by luminous matter escaping from within. In the 19th century, John Cleves Symmes Jr. popularised the idea of openings at the poles ("Symmes Holes") and campaigned for an expedition to the interior; his followers, including Jeremiah N. Reynolds, lobbied the US Congress, and the notion influenced early polar exploration.

In fiction

The hollow Earth became a staple of adventure fiction. Jules Verne's Journey to the Center of the Earth (1864) sent explorers down an Icelandic volcano into vast caverns, while Edgar Allan Poe's The Narrative of Arthur Gordon Pym (1838) and Edgar Rice Burroughs's Pellucidar novels (from 1914) depicted inhabited interior worlds lit by a central sun. The concept persists in modern novels, films, and conspiracy theories despite being flatly contradicted by geophysics.

Scientific refutation

Seismic wave propagation, the Earth's measured mass and moment of inertia, and observations of gravity all demonstrate that the Earth has a dense interior: a silicate mantle and an iron-nickel core. A hollow Earth of the proposed kind would have far too little mass to produce the observed surface gravity.
