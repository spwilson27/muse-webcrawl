DC motor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A DC motor is an electrical motor that uses direct current (DC) to produce mechanical force. The most common types rely on magnetic forces produced by currents in the coils. Nearly all types of DC motors have some internal mechanism, either electromechanical or electronic, to periodically change the direction of current in part of the motor.

DC motors were the first form of motor widely used, since they could be powered from existing direct-current lighting power distribution systems. A DC motor's speed can be controlled over a wide range, using either a variable supply voltage or by changing the strength of current in its field windings. Small DC motors are used in tools, toys, and appliances; larger DC motors are used in electric vehicles, elevator and hoist drives, and drives for steel rolling mills.

Brushed DC motor

The brushed DC electric motor generates torque directly from DC power supplied to the motor by using internal commutation, stationary magnets (permanent or electromagnets), and rotating electromagnets. The brushes and commutator switch the current through the rotor windings as it turns, so that the magnetic poles of the rotor are continuously repelled and attracted by the stator poles in the correct sequence to maintain rotation. Advantages include low initial cost, high reliability, and simple control of speed; disadvantages are brush wear, maintenance, and electrical noise.

Brushless DC motor

Brushless DC motors (BLDC) replace the mechanical commutator with electronic commutation: the permanent magnets are on the rotor and the windings on the stator, switched by a controller using rotor position feedback. They offer higher efficiency, longer life, and higher speeds, and are used extensively in computer fans, drones, electric vehicles, and precision servo applications.

Field types

In wound-field motors the stator field may be connected in series (series motor — very high starting torque, used in traction and starter motors), in parallel (shunt motor — roughly constant speed), or in a combination (compound motor). Permanent-magnet DC motors are common in small sizes.

History

The commutator DC motor was among the earliest practical electric motors of the 19th century, developed from the experiments of Michael Faraday and the inventions of Thomas Davenport, Moritz von Jacobi, and later Zénobe Gramme, whose ring armature made the DC machine commercially practical in the 1870s. Reversible operation as a generator (dynamo) underpinned early DC power systems.
