Motte-and-bailey castle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A reconstruction of a motte-and-bailey castle.

A motte-and-bailey castle is a European fortification with a wooden or stone keep situated on a raised area of ground called a motte, accompanied by a walled courtyard, or bailey, surrounded by a protective ditch and palisade. Relatively easy to build with unskilled labour, but militarily formidable, these castles were built across northern Europe from the 10th century onward, spreading from Normandy and Anjou to England, Scotland, Ireland, Wales, and the Low Countries and Germany.

Design

The motte was a raised earthwork, often artificial, ranging from about 3 to 30 metres in height, topped with a wooden palisade and a tower that served as lookout, fighting platform, and lordly residence. The bailey, an enclosed courtyard beside or below the motte, contained the hall, kitchens, stables, workshops, and chapel, defended by a timber palisade, ditch, and bank. A bridge or causeway linked the two.

History

The design emerged in the 10th and 11th centuries in the region between the Loire and the Rhine. The Norman Conquest of 1066 brought the type to England in large numbers — hundreds were raised to control the conquered kingdom — and the Bayeux Tapestry famously depicts the construction of a motte at Hastings. Because they could be built in weeks using local materials, motte-and-bailey castles were ideal instruments of conquest and colonisation. From the 12th century many were rebuilt in stone or abandoned; thousands of their earthworks survive today.
