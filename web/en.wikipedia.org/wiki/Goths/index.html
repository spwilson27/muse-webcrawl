Goths

The Goths (Gothic: Gutþiuda; Latin: Gothi) were an East Germanic people who played a major role in the fall of the Western Roman Empire and the emergence of medieval Europe. In the 3rd and 4th centuries AD they established powerful kingdoms north of the Black Sea, came into sustained contact and conflict with the Roman Empire, and — under pressure from the Huns — entered Roman territory, defeating the Romans at the Battle of Adrianople in 378. Two major groupings, the Visigoths and Ostrogoths, went on to found kingdoms in Gaul, Spain and Italy. The last Gothic kingdom fell to Muslim conquerors in 711, while Crimean Goths persisted into the early modern period.

Origins

According to the 6th-century Gothic historian Jordanes, the Goths originated in Scandinavia (the island of "Scandza") and migrated to the southern Baltic coast, a tradition partly supported by the archaeological Wielbark culture in modern Poland, which many scholars associate with early Goths. From the Vistula basin they moved southeast toward the Black Sea during the 2nd and 3rd centuries. By the 3rd century they had split into groups west (Thervingi, later associated with the Visigoths) and east (Greuthungi, later Ostrogoths) of the Dniester river, within the broader Chernyakhov cultural zone.

Early contact with Rome

Goths raided the Roman Balkans and Asia Minor by land and sea in the mid-3rd century, killing the emperor Decius at the Battle of Abrittus (251) and sacking cities before being defeated by Claudius II at Naissus (268/269) and by Aurelian. In the 4th century the Thervingi under leaders such as Athanaric fought and negotiated with the emperors Constantine and Valens. The missionary Ulfilas (Wulfila), consecrated bishop in 341, translated the Bible into Gothic — creating the earliest substantial text in any Germanic language — and spread Homoian ("Arian") Christianity among the Goths, a faith that long distinguished them from their Nicene Roman subjects.

The Hunnic invasion and Adrianople

Around 375 the Huns destroyed the Ostrogothic kingdom of Ermanaric and drove westward. In 376 the Thervingi under Fritigern were allowed to cross the Danube into the empire; abused by Roman officials, they revolted, beginning the Gothic War. On 9 August 378 the Goths annihilated the eastern Roman army at Adrianople and killed the emperor Valens. The war ended in 382 with a treaty settling the Goths in the Balkans as foederati.

Visigoths

Under Alaric I, the Goths in the Balkans coalesced into the Visigoths. After years of maneuvering between the eastern and western courts, Alaric's forces sacked Rome in August 410 — a shock across the Mediterranean world. His successor Athaulf led the Visigoths into Gaul and Spain; in 418 they were settled in Aquitaine as foederati. The Visigothic kingdom, centered first on Toulouse, defeated the invading Vandals and Alans in Spain, fought alongside Rome against Attila at the Catalaunian Plains (451), and under Euric became effectively independent. Driven from most of Gaul by the Franks after the Battle of Vouillé (507), the kingdom recentered on Toledo in Spain, converted to Nicene Christianity under Reccared (589), and endured until the Umayyad conquest of 711.

Ostrogoths

The Ostrogoths spent decades under Hunnic domination, then settled in Pannonia after Attila's empire collapsed. In 488 Theodoric the Great led them into Italy at the instigation of the emperor Zeno, defeating and killing Odoacer and establishing the Ostrogothic Kingdom of Italy (493–553), ruling from Ravenna. Theodoric maintained Roman institutions and religious toleration between his Arian Goths and Catholic Romans. After his death in 526, succession disputes gave Justinian I a pretext for reconquest; the long and devastating Gothic War (535–554) destroyed the Ostrogothic kingdom and much of Italy's late-antique fabric.

Crimean Goths and legacy

Gothic communities in Crimea, less touched by the upheavals, preserved a Gothic language — documented as late as the 1560s in a wordlist recorded by the Flemish ambassador Ogier de Busbecq — into at least the 17th or 18th century. The Gothic language survives mainly through Ulfilas's Bible translation, including the Codex Argenteus. The Goths' name was later applied, via Renaissance usage of "Gothic," to medieval architecture and, much later, to Gothic literature and the modern goth subculture.
