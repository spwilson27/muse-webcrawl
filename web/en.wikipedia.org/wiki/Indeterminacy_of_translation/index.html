Indeterminacy of translation

The indeterminacy of translation is a thesis about language and meaning attributed to the philosopher W. V. O. Quine, developed most fully in his 1960 book Word and Object. It holds that there is no unique fact of the matter about the correct translation of one language into another: multiple translation manuals can be constructed, each compatible with all possible behavioral evidence — the totality of speakers' dispositions to verbal behavior — yet incompatible with one another, and there is no objective ground for choosing between them.

The gavagai example

Quine illustrates the thesis with a thought experiment about radical translation, in which a field linguist attempts to translate a previously uncontacted language using only observation of native speakers' behavior. When a rabbit scurries by, a native says "gavagai". The linguist tentatively translates this as "Lo, a rabbit", but the observable evidence is equally consistent with "Lo, an undetached rabbit part", "Lo, a temporal stage of a rabbit", or "Lo, rabbithood manifesting itself". Analytical hypotheses needed to fix reference go beyond anything the behavioral evidence can determine.

Argument and significance

Quine connects the thesis to his broader naturalism and behaviorism about meaning, and to the inscrutability (or ontological relativity) of reference. Because translation is indeterminate even for utterances in one's own language and community, Quine argues that traditional notions of meaning — propositions, synonymy, analyticity — lack an objective foundation. The thesis has been widely debated: critics such as Noam Chomsky have questioned whether the argument applies beyond behaviorist assumptions, while Donald Davidson developed related ideas about radical interpretation.

Related concepts

The indeterminacy thesis is often discussed alongside Quine's rejection of the analytic–synthetic distinction in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) and his doctrine of ontological relativity, according to which reference can only be specified relative to a background language.
