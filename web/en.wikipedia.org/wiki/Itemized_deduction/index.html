Itemized deduction

An itemized deduction is an expense that a United States taxpayer may subtract from adjusted gross income when computing federal income tax, in place of the standard deduction. Itemized deductions are listed on Schedule A of Form 1040 and are worthwhile when their total exceeds the standard deduction available to the taxpayer.

Major categories

  • State and local taxes (SALT) — income or property taxes, capped at $10,000 per year by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 (a cap scheduled to change under later law).
  • Home mortgage interest — on acquisition debt up to statutory limits.
  • Charitable contributions — to qualifying organisations, subject to percentage-of-income limits.
  • Medical and dental expenses — to the extent they exceed 7.5% of adjusted gross income.
  • Casualty and theft losses — since 2018, generally only for federally declared disasters.

History

Itemized deductions date to the early federal income tax; the standard deduction was introduced in 1944 to simplify filing. The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act roughly doubled the standard deduction and capped or eliminated several itemized deductions, so the share of returns itemizing fell from about 30% to around 10%. Many states have their own itemized deduction rules that differ from federal law.
