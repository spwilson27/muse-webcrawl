Endogenous growth theory

Sustained growth driven by innovation in endogenous models

Endogenous growth theory is a body of economic theory arguing that long-run economic growth is generated primarily by forces internal ("endogenous") to the economic system — investment in human capital, innovation, and knowledge — rather than by unexplained external ("exogenous") technological progress. It emerged in the 1980s as a reaction to the neoclassical Solow–Swan model, in which the long-run growth rate is determined by an exogenous rate of technological progress and policy has no effect on it.

Key models

The foundational contribution is Paul Romer's 1986 paper, which modelled growth through increasing returns arising from knowledge spillovers. Robert Lucas (1988) emphasised investment in human capital and its externalities. Romer's 1990 model made technological change itself the result of profit-seeking research and development, with monopolistically competitive firms producing new designs.

The simpler AK model — in which output is linear in a broad capital stock that includes knowledge and human capital — shows how the absence of diminishing returns to accumulable factors can sustain growth and give saving and investment rates permanent growth effects. Other branches include Schumpeterian models (Aghion and Howitt), in which innovation creates rents that are destroyed by later innovation ("creative destruction"), and models of technology diffusion and adoption.

Implications

Because knowledge is non-rival and generates spillovers, endogenous growth models imply that private markets underinvest in research and education, giving a rationale for government policy — subsidies to R&D, patent protection, and public education — to raise the long-run growth rate. The theory also undermines the neoclassical prediction that poor countries necessarily converge to rich-country income levels.

Criticism

Critics have noted that early R&D-based models implied implausible "scale effects" (growth rising with population size), which the data did not support; later "semi-endogenous" models (e.g. Charles Jones) restored a role for population growth and made long-run growth depend on it. Empirical identification of the theory's mechanisms remains debated.
