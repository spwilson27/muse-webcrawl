Capgras delusion

Other namesCapgras syndrome, impostor syndrome
SpecialtyPsychiatry, neurology
Named afterJoseph Capgras

Capgras delusion or Capgras syndrome is a psychiatric disorder in which a person holds a delusion that a friend, spouse, parent, another close family member, or pet has been replaced by an identical impostor. It is named after Joseph Capgras (1873–1950), the French psychiatrist who first described the disorder. Capgras delusion is classified as a delusional misidentification syndrome, a class of delusional beliefs that involve misidentifying people, places, or objects.

Signs and symptoms

The defining feature is the fixed belief that a familiar person — most often a spouse or close relative — is an impostor, double, or replica, despite the person looking exactly the same. The belief is typically restricted to people with whom the patient has a strong emotional bond. The delusion may extend to pets, possessions, or the patient's own reflection.

Causes

Capgras delusion occurs most often in patients diagnosed with schizophrenia. It also occurs in association with brain injury, dementia (particularly Lewy body dementia), and other neurodegenerative diseases. A leading neuropsychological account holds that the delusion arises from a disconnection between face recognition and the emotional (limbic) response to familiar faces: the patient recognises the face but feels no sense of familiarity, and resolves the conflict by concluding the person must be an impostor. This is sometimes described as the mirror image of prosopagnosia.

History

Capgras and Reboul-Lachaux first described the condition in 1923 in the case of a French woman who claimed her husband and others had been replaced by doubles. It was initially considered a purely psychiatric symptom; later work linked it to organic brain lesions, particularly in the right hemisphere.

Treatment

Treatment addresses the underlying condition, commonly with antipsychotic medication and, where present, management of the associated neurological disease. Individual therapies have not been rigorously studied, and care is largely supportive.
