Master mason

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A master mason was, in the medieval building trades, a highly skilled stonemason who had completed his training and was qualified both to practise the craft independently and to take contracts, employ journeymen and apprentices, and direct major works. In the building of the great cathedrals, the master mason acted as what would now be called the architect: designing the structure, preparing drawings and templates, and supervising construction on site.

Training and status

Under the guild system a boy was apprenticed for a term of years (often seven) to a master, then worked as a journeyman for wages before presenting a masterwork, or satisfying other requirements, to be received as a master. Masters enjoyed privileged status within the guild or lodge: they alone could take contracts, and their marks — the personal banker marks cut into finished stones — identify individual workmanship to this day.

Master masons as designers

Famous master masons combined practical craft knowledge with geometry. Villard de Honnecourt's 13th-century sketchbook records the drawings of a travelling master. In England, men such as William of Sens, who rebuilt the choir of Canterbury Cathedral after 1174, and later Henry Yevele and William Wynford, directed royal and ecclesiastical works. The master mason typically worked from full-size tracings incised on a tracing floor and from wooden templates issued to the cutters in the lodge.

In Freemasonry

The term was carried into speculative Freemasonry, where Master Mason is the third and highest of the traditional craft degrees, conferred after Entered Apprentice and Fellow Craft. The degree centres on the legend of Hiram Abiff, the master builder of Solomon's Temple. A Master Mason in good standing is a full member of the fraternity with the right to vote, hold office and visit other lodges. This usage descends from the older operative tradition but is no longer connected with the practice of stonemasonry.
