Clinker (cement)

Cement clinker is the intermediate product in the manufacture of Portland cement. It consists of dark grey nodules, typically 3–25 mm in diameter, formed when a raw mixture of limestone, clay, and other materials is heated to about 1,450 °C in a cement kiln. Clinker is ground with a small amount of gypsum to produce cement powder.

Composition

Clinker contains four principal mineral phases: alite (tricalcium silicate, roughly 50–70%), belite (dicalcium silicate, 15–30%), tricalcium aluminate (5–10%), and tetracalcium aluminoferrite (5–15%). Alite is chiefly responsible for early strength development, while belite contributes to later strength.

Production

Raw meal is preheated in a cyclone tower, calcined (releasing CO2 from the limestone), and then sintered in a rotary kiln to form clinker, which is rapidly cooled in a grate cooler. Clinker production is energy-intensive and accounts for most of the cement industry's carbon emissions — both from fuel combustion and from the calcination reaction itself. The industry therefore pursues supplementary cementitious materials, alternative fuels, and clinker substitution to reduce the clinker factor of finished cements.

Trade

Because clinker stores and ships well, it is traded internationally; coastal grinding plants import clinker and grind it with local materials where limestone or kiln capacity is lacking.
