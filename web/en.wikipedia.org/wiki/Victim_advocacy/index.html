Victim advocacy

Victim advocacy is the provision of support, information and accompaniment to victims of crime, abuse and trauma, and the defence of their rights within the criminal justice system and wider society. A victim advocate works to ensure that victims' voices are heard, that they understand proceedings affecting them, and that they can access services such as counselling, shelter, compensation and legal assistance.

Advocates help victims navigate the justice system and recovery services

Roles

Advocates typically provide crisis intervention immediately after an offence, explain victims' legal rights (such as notification of hearings and the right to give a victim impact statement), accompany victims to court and interviews, help with protective orders and compensation claims, and make referrals to housing, medical and mental-health services. Advocates may be based in police departments, prosecutors' offices, courts, hospitals, or independent community organisations such as rape crisis centres and domestic-violence shelters; many operate confidential community-based services independent of law enforcement.

History

The modern victims' movement grew in the 1970s out of feminist activism against rape and domestic violence, civil-rights advocacy, and law-and-order concern with victims' neglect by the justice system. In the United States it led to the Victims of Crime Act (VOCA) of 1984, which created a federal fund financed by criminal fines, and to victims' rights amendments in many states. Similar frameworks developed elsewhere, such as codes of practice for victims in the United Kingdom and the EU Victims' Rights Directive (2012). Key milestones in case law and legislation include Marsy's Law in California and other states, named after a murder victim, which constitutionalised rights to notice, presence and restitution.
