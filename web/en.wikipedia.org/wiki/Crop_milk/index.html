Crop milk

Crop milk is a secretion from the lining of the crop of parent birds in some species that is regurgitated to feed young birds. It is found among pigeons and doves (where it is often called pigeon's milk), flamingos, and some penguins. Despite the name, crop milk bears little resemblance to mammalian milk, being more like a cottage-cheese-like slurry of sloughed crop cells rich in protein and fat.

Production and composition

In pigeons, both parents produce crop milk under the influence of the hormone prolactin — the same hormone responsible for milk production in mammals, an example of convergent evolution. During incubation the crop lining thickens and its cells accumulate protein and lipids; after hatching these cells are shed into the crop and regurgitated to the nestlings (squabs), which feed exclusively on crop milk for their first days and grow extremely rapidly, roughly doubling in weight in two days.

Crop milk is exceptionally rich: it contains high levels of protein and fat, growth factors and immunoglobulins, and carries beneficial bacteria that seed the nestling's gut. Flamingos produce a similar secretion along more of the upper digestive tract and feed their chicks for weeks, both parents losing condition and their pink colouration during the effort. Male emperor penguins produce a crop secretion that can sustain a newly hatched chick briefly until the female returns from the sea.
