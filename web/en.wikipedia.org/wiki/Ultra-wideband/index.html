Ultra-wideband

Ultra-wideband (UWB)
TypeRadio communication technology
Frequency range3.1–10.6 GHz (FCC allocation)
StandardIEEE 802.15.4 (HRP UWB)
Key propertyVery short pulses; precise time-of-flight ranging

Ultra-wideband (UWB) is a radio technology that can use a very low energy level for short-range, high-bandwidth communications over a large portion of the radio spectrum. UWB is defined as an antenna transmission for which the emitted signal bandwidth exceeds the lesser of 500 MHz or 20% of the arithmetic center frequency. Unlike conventional narrowband systems, impulse-radio UWB transmits information in extremely short pulses (often less than a nanosecond), spreading the signal energy across several gigahertz of spectrum at very low power spectral density.

Characteristics

The short pulses of UWB give it fine time resolution, enabling very accurate time-of-flight measurements and hence centimetre-level ranging and positioning — a capability hard to achieve with narrowband radio. The wide bandwidth also provides robustness against multipath fading and some ability to penetrate materials, which is exploited in ground-penetrating and through-wall radar. Regulatory masks (notably the FCC's 2002 rules) restrict UWB emissions to protect incumbent services.

Applications

Early hopes that UWB would become a high-speed wireless USB replacement faded, but the technology found a second life in precision location: real-time location systems in factories and hospitals, secure car key fobs and digital keys, and smartphone features such as Apple's U1/U2 chips in AirTag tracking and point-to-share functions. The IEEE 802.15.4z amendment added secure ranging features.
