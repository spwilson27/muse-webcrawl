Infantry mobility vehicle

An infantry mobility vehicle (IMV) is a wheeled armoured personnel carrier designed to transport infantry while providing protection against small arms fire, shell splinters and, increasingly, mines and improvised explosive devices. IMVs prioritise mobility, protection and relatively low cost over the heavy firepower of an infantry fighting vehicle.

The Australian Bushmaster is a well-known IMV design.

Characteristics

IMVs are almost always wheeled (typically 4×4 or 6×6) for strategic mobility, range and ease of maintenance, carry a driver plus roughly eight to ten dismounts, and are armed at most with a heavy machine gun or remote weapon station. Mine protection is achieved through V-shaped hulls that deflect blast away from the crew compartment, high ground clearance, and energy-absorbing seats. Well-known examples include the Australian Bushmaster, the South African RG-31 family, and vehicles of the MRAP programmes fielded by the United States in Iraq and Afghanistan.

History

The type descends from the mine-protected vehicles developed by Rhodesia and South Africa during the border wars of the 1970s, whose V-hull designs proved highly effective against land mines. Counter-insurgency operations after 2001 made the IMV a standard category in Western and other armies, filling the gap between unarmoured utility vehicles and tracked armoured fighting vehicles.
