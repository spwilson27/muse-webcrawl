Yaqui

The Yaqui (in their own language, Yoeme) are an Indigenous people of the Mexican state of Sonora and of southern Arizona in the United States. Historically they lived in farming towns along the Río Yaqui valley in Sonora; a substantial population also lives in Arizona, where the Pascua Yaqui Tribe is federally recognized, with communities at Tucson (including Old Pascua and New Pascua) and Guadalupe near Phoenix.

History

The Yaqui were first contacted by Spaniards in 1533 and famously resisted conquest; a Spanish expedition under Diego de Guzmán was defeated in battle. Jesuit missionaries entered Yaqui territory peacefully from 1617, and the Yaqui reorganized into Eight Sacred Towns along the river — Cócorit, Bácum, Tórim, Vícam, Pótam, Ráhum, Huirivis, and Belem — which remain the political and ceremonial heart of the nation. After Mexican independence the Yaqui repeatedly rose to defend their land and autonomy, notably under Juan Banderas in the 1820s–30s and José María Leyva ("Cajeme") in the 1880s. During the Porfirian era thousands of Yaqui were deported to henequen plantations in Yucatán; many others fled to Arizona. Under President Lázaro Cárdenas in 1937–1940 Mexico formally recognized Yaqui land rights along the river.

Culture and language

Yaqui is a Uto-Aztecan language of the Cahitan branch, closely related to Mayo. Yaqui ceremonial life blends Catholic and Indigenous practice, most famously in the Lent and Holy Week (Waehma) observances with their Chapayeka (fariseo) figures, and in the deer dance (maaso yiihuia), in which a dancer wearing a deer head imitates the movements of a deer to the music of singers, a harp and violin, or a flute and drum. The pascola — an elder dancer and storyteller — is another central figure of Yaqui fiesta tradition.
