Enzyme inhibitor

Enzyme inhibitor
TypesReversible (competitive, non-competitive, uncompetitive), irreversible
Binding sitesActive site or allosteric site
UsesPharmaceuticals, pesticides, metabolic regulation

An enzyme inhibitor is a molecule that binds to an enzyme and blocks its activity. Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions necessary for life, from digestion to DNA synthesis; inhibitors can therefore be potent medicines or deadly poisons. Many drugs are enzyme inhibitors, as are naturally occurring toxins and regulatory molecules.

Types

Reversible inhibitors bind non-covalently. Competitive inhibitors compete with the substrate for the active site and can be overcome by raising substrate concentration. Non-competitive inhibitors bind elsewhere (an allosteric site) and reduce activity regardless of substrate concentration, while uncompetitive inhibitors bind only the enzyme–substrate complex. Irreversible inhibitors react covalently with the enzyme, permanently disabling it — examples include penicillin, which acylates a bacterial transpeptidase, and organophosphate pesticides, which block acetylcholinesterase.

Applications and natural roles

Enzyme inhibitors are among the most common classes of drugs: statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase, ACE inhibitors block angiotensin-converting enzyme, HIV protease inhibitors treat HIV/AIDS, and many cancer drugs inhibit kinases. Within cells, inhibition is a normal mechanism of metabolic control, including feedback inhibition of biosynthetic pathways. Plants and microbes also produce inhibitors as chemical defences.
