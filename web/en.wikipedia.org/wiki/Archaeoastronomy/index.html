Archaeoastronomy (also called astroarchaeology) is the interdisciplinary study of how people in the past understood the phenomena in the sky, how they used these phenomena, and what role the sky played in their cultures. It draws evidence from archaeology, especially the orientation of buildings and monuments, together with texts, calendars, art, and ethnographic analogy. The related North American field that works mainly with ethnographic and historical records is termed ethnoastronomy.

History of the field

Speculation about astronomical alignments at Stonehenge dates to the 18th century, but the modern discipline emerged in the 1960s–70s with Gerald Hawkins's Stonehenge Decoded (1965) and Alexander Thom's surveys of British megalithic sites, which claimed high-precision solar and lunar alignments. Their statistical and interpretive claims were sharply debated by archaeologists; the field subsequently matured under scholars such as Clive Ruggles, who emphasised rigorous field methods, cultural context, and statistical caution.

Methods and case studies

Researchers measure the azimuths of structures against the local horizon and compare them with the rising and setting points of the Sun (solstices and equinoxes), the Moon (lunar standstills), and bright stars, accounting for precession and horizon altitude. Widely accepted examples include the winter-solstice illumination of the passage tomb at Newgrange in Ireland, the solar observatory towers of Chankillo in Peru (c. 300 BCE), the equinox hierophanies at Chichen Itza's El Castillo in Mexico, and the calendar-related alignments of Ancestral Pueblo sites such as the Fajada Butte "Sun Dagger" in Chaco Canyon.

Criticism

Because any landscape offers many possible sightlines, critics warn of "alignment hunting" — the selection of chance correspondences — and of imposing modern astronomical concepts on past cultures. Modern practice therefore combines statistical testing with independent archaeological, textual, or ethnographic evidence.
