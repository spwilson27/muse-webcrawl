The épée (French: [epe], "sword") is the largest and heaviest of the three weapons used in the sport of fencing, the others being the foil and the sabre. It descends from the duelling sword of the nineteenth century: as in a real duel, the entire body is a valid target, there is no right-of-way rule, and simultaneous hits by both fencers each score a point.

Description

A modern épée has a stiff blade with a V-shaped or triangular cross-section, up to 90 cm long, tipped with a spring-loaded button that registers a hit when depressed with a force of at least 750 grams. The bell guard is a large hemispherical cup up to 135 mm in diameter, and the total weight of the weapon may not exceed 770 grams. Body cords connect the weapon to the electronic scoring apparatus through a three-pronged plug.

Rules and technique

Épée bouts are fenced to 15 hits (or in pools to 5). Because the whole body is target and double touches are possible, épée rewards patience, counter-time, and precise attacks to near targets such as the wrist, forearm, and foot. If both fencers score simultaneously in the final tied hit, both points are annulled rather than counted. A non-combativity rule penalizes prolonged passages without a hit.

Competitive épée has been an Olympic event for men since the Paris Games of 1900 and for women since Atlanta 1996; team events followed. Strong épée nations include France, Italy, Hungary, and more recently South Korea and Japan, whose men's team won gold at Tokyo 2020.

History

The sporting épée developed in the 1880s from the French duelling sword (épée de combat), with which duels were fought to first blood — hence the rule that a hit anywhere counts. Electronic scoring was introduced for épée at the 1936 Olympics, decades before its adoption for the other weapons.
