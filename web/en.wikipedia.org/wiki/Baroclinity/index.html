Baroclinity

In fluid dynamics, baroclinity is a measure of the stratification of a fluid in which surfaces of constant density and constant pressure are misaligned. A fluid or flow is baroclinic when density depends on both temperature and pressure; it is barotropic when density depends only on pressure, so the two families of surfaces coincide.

Physical significance

The baroclinic vector, proportional to the cross product of the density and pressure gradients, acts as a source of vorticity: where pressure and density gradients cross, circulation is generated. In the atmosphere, baroclinity arises because temperature varies along pressure surfaces — for instance, toward the poles — and it is central to the thermal wind relation and to the growth of mid-latitude weather systems through baroclinic instability, which converts potential energy stored in horizontal temperature gradients into the kinetic energy of cyclones.

Oceanography

In the ocean, sloping density surfaces associated with fronts and currents are baroclinic features; much of the general circulation, including western boundary currents, has a strong baroclinic component distinct from the depth-independent barotropic flow.
