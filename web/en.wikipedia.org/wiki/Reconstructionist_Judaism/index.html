Reconstructionist Judaism

Reconstructionist Judaism is a Jewish movement that views Judaism as a progressively evolving civilization rather than solely a religion. It originated as a stream within Conservative Judaism in the United States during the late 1920s and became an independent movement in the mid-20th century. Its founder was Rabbi Mordecai Kaplan.

Beliefs

Kaplan taught that Judaism comprises the evolving religious civilization of the Jewish people — its language, culture, history, art, ethics, and religious practices. Reconstructionism generally rejects supernaturalism; Kaplan described God not as a personal supernatural being but as "the Power that makes for salvation" — the sum of natural processes that allow human beings to achieve self-fulfillment. Jewish law (halakha) is valued as a rich cultural inheritance but is not considered binding in the traditional sense; Kaplan famously said the past "has a vote, not a veto."

History

Kaplan developed his ideas at the Jewish Theological Seminary and in his 1934 book Judaism as a Civilization. The Reconstructionist Rabbinical College was founded in 1968 in Philadelphia. The movement was a pioneer in egalitarian practice, ordaining women as rabbis (beginning with Sandy Eisenberg Sasso in 1974) and emphasizing democratic congregational governance and social justice.
