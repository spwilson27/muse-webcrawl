Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction

TypeOscillating chemical reaction
DiscoveredBoris Belousov, 1950s; developed by Anatol Zhabotinsky, 1960s
Typical reagentsBromate, malonic acid, cerium or ferroin catalyst, sulfuric acid
FieldNon-equilibrium thermodynamics, nonlinear dynamics

The Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction, or BZ reaction, is one of a class of reactions that serve as a classical example of non-equilibrium thermodynamics, resulting in the establishment of a nonlinear chemical oscillator. It is a family of oscillating chemical reactions in which a mixture — commonly potassium bromate, malonic acid and a cerium salt (or the red-blue indicator ferroin) in dilute sulfuric acid — repeatedly changes colour, for example between colourless and yellow, or between red and blue, over the course of many cycles.

History

The Soviet chemist Boris Pavlovich Belousov discovered the reaction in the 1950s while seeking an inorganic analogue of the Krebs cycle. His attempts to publish were repeatedly rejected by editors who considered a homogeneous oscillating reaction impossible, so the work appeared only in an obscure compiled volume in 1959 and posthumously in full. Anatol Markovich Zhabotinsky, a graduate student in Moscow, took up the system in 1961, characterised the oscillations and publicised them, and the reaction became famous after the 1968 Prague conference on biological oscillators. In 1972 Field, Kőrös and Noyes published the FKN mechanism explaining the oscillations.

Behaviour

In an unstirred thin layer, the reaction produces striking travelling waves — concentric target patterns and rotating spirals — as oxidation fronts propagate through the medium; these are a chemical analogue of waves in excitable media such as heart tissue and slime-mould aggregation. The BZ reaction became a paradigm system for self-organisation, pattern formation and chaos, and a standard demonstration in chemistry teaching. It has also been used as a medium for chemical computing experiments.
