Sofrito

Sofrito is a sauce or aromatic base used in Mediterranean, Latin American, Spanish, Italian, and Portuguese cooking. It typically consists of aromatic ingredients cut into small pieces and slowly sautéed or braised in oil, often olive oil, for a long time at low heat. The word derives from the Spanish verb sofreír, "to fry lightly."

Regional variations

Spain

The Spanish sofrito (Catalan sofregit) is one of the oldest documented cooking bases in Europe, mentioned in medieval Catalan cookbooks. It classically combines onion and tomato cooked down in olive oil, sometimes with garlic, peppers, or herbs, and forms the foundation of paella, stews, and bean dishes.

Italy

The Italian soffritto parallels French mirepoix: finely diced onion, carrot, and celery sweated in oil or butter as the base for ragù, risotto, and soups.

Latin America and the Caribbean

In Puerto Rican and Dominican cooking, sofrito is a puréed blend of onions, cubanelle and ají dulce peppers, garlic, cilantro, and culantro (recao), stored and added by the spoonful to beans, rice, soups, and stews. Cuban sofrito typically uses onion, green pepper, garlic, oregano, and cumin in olive oil. Similar bases appear throughout Latin America under names such as hogao (Colombia) and refogado (Brazil, for the Portuguese-style base of onion, garlic, and tomato).

Preparation

The defining technique is slow, gentle cooking: the vegetables release moisture, soften, and sweeten without browning, concentrating flavor. Caribbean-style sofrito is often made in large batches, blended raw, frozen in ice-cube trays, and thawed as needed.
