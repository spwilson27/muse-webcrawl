Saturation (color theory)

Saturation is one of the three principal attributes of color appearance, alongside hue and lightness (or value/brightness). In color theory it describes the perceived intensity or purity of a color: a highly saturated color appears vivid and deep, whereas a desaturated color appears muted, washed out, or greyish. Together with hue and lightness, saturation forms the basis of cylindrical color models such as HSL and HSV.

Definitions

Several related but distinct quantities are used in color science. The CIE defines chroma as the colorfulness of an area judged in proportion to the brightness of a similarly illuminated white, and saturation as the colorfulness of an area judged in proportion to its own brightness. In the Munsell color system, the corresponding attribute is called chroma, measured in steps outward from the neutral grey axis. In the HSV (HSB) model, saturation is the percentage of white mixed into a pure hue; in HSL, saturation is defined relative to a grey of the same lightness.

Perception and applications

Saturation interacts with lightness and hue: the Hunt effect describes how colorfulness increases with luminance, and highly saturated yellows appear brighter than equally luminous blues because of the Helmholtz–Kohlrausch effect. In art, design, printing, and photography, adjusting saturation is a basic tool for emphasis and mood. Physically, saturation corresponds roughly to spectral purity — monochromatic light is maximally saturated, while mixtures approaching white light are minimally saturated. Fluorescent and laser sources can appear "oversaturated" relative to ordinary surface colors.
