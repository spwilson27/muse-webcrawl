Architrave

In classical architecture, an architrave (from Italian architrave, "chief beam", from Greek ἀρχι- and Latin trabs, "beam") is the lintel or beam that rests on the capitals of columns. It is the lowest division of the entablature, consisting of the architrave itself, the frieze above it, and the cornice crowning the whole.

In the classical orders

In the Doric order the architrave is plain or divided, and is surmounted by a band of triglyphs and metopes in the frieze; a projecting strip, the taenia, runs along its top, with guttae pegged beneath the triglyphs. In the Ionic order the architrave is typically divided into two or three stepped horizontal bands (fasciae), and in the Corinthian order it is elaborated further, often with carved mouldings. Because a stone beam can span only limited distances, the architrave dictated the close colonnade spacing of Greek temples.

Other uses

By extension, "architrave" also denotes the moulded frame surrounding a door, window, or other opening — the trim against which a door closes in modern carpentry. In post-and-lintel construction generally, any principal horizontal member resting directly on vertical supports may be called an architrave.

History

The architrave descends from the wooden beams of prehistoric construction, translated into stone in Egyptian temples and codified in Greek and Roman practice. Roman engineers extended its spans with monolithic shafts and relieving arches, and Renaissance theorists such as Alberti and Palladio re-established its canonical proportions for classical revival architecture.
