Lambda repressor

Repressor protein cI (lambda)
OrganismEscherichia phage lambda
GenecI
Length236 amino acids
FunctionTranscriptional repressor / activator

The lambda repressor, also known as the cI protein, is a transcriptional regulator encoded by bacteriophage lambda. It is the master switch of the phage's life cycle: when present and active, it maintains the phage in the dormant lysogenic state integrated into the host chromosome; when it is inactivated, the phage enters the lytic cycle, replicates, and destroys the host cell.

Structure and binding

The protein has 236 amino acids and consists of two domains joined by a flexible linker. The N-terminal domain binds DNA through a helix-turn-helix motif and also mediates cooperative interactions between dimers; the C-terminal domain mediates dimerisation and the self-cleavage reaction by which the protein is inactivated. Repressor dimers bind cooperatively to operator sites in the phage's left and right control regions (OL and OR). At OR, binding to sites OR1 and OR2 blocks transcription of the lytic genes (including cro) while the repressor simultaneously activates transcription of its own gene, cI, from the promoter PRM — a positive feedback loop that maintains lysogeny. At very high concentrations, repressor also fills OR3 and turns its own synthesis down.

Induction

Damage to the host DNA activates the bacterial SOS response: the RecA protein becomes a coprotease that stimulates the repressor's latent self-cleavage activity, splitting it between residues Ala-111 and Gly-112. Cleaved repressor can no longer bind DNA, the lytic promoters are derepressed, Cro protein accumulates and represses PRM, and the phage commits to lytic growth. The lambda switch — worked out largely by Mark Ptashne and colleagues — became a paradigm for gene regulatory circuits, cooperative binding and epigenetic stability, and the repressor is still used as a model system in biophysics and synthetic biology.
