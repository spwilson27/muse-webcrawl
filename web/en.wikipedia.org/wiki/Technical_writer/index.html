Technical writer

A technical writer is a professional information communicator whose task is to transfer information between two or more parties through any medium that best facilitates the transfer and comprehension of the information. Technical writers research and create information products for a target audience, working with product managers, engineers, and subject-matter experts to produce material such as user manuals, help systems, white papers, design specifications, project plans, and software test plans.

Role and responsibilities

Technical writers analyse the needs of their audience and present information in a form that readers can find, understand, and use. Their work commonly involves:

  • Interviewing subject-matter experts and studying products or processes first-hand
  • Organising and structuring information for clarity and retrievability
  • Writing and editing documentation in plain, consistent language
  • Creating or commissioning diagrams, screenshots, and other illustrations
  • Testing documentation against the actual product and revising it through review cycles
  • Publishing and maintaining content, increasingly through docs-as-code toolchains and content management systems

Skills and qualifications

Technical writers typically combine strong writing and editing ability with enough technical aptitude to understand the products they document. Many hold degrees in English, journalism, communications, or a technical field, and professional bodies such as the Society for Technical Communication (STC) offer certification. Familiarity with structured authoring standards such as DITA and XML, topic-based authoring, single-source publishing, and version control systems is common in software documentation roles.

History

Technical writing grew as a distinct profession during and after the Second World War, when the rapid expansion of military and industrial technology created a demand for clear operating and maintenance documentation. The rise of the personal computer in the 1980s greatly expanded the field, as consumer software required user guides and online help for non-specialist audiences. In recent decades the profession has shifted from printed manuals toward online documentation, embedded user assistance, video tutorials, and API documentation.
