Sequoyah (Cherokee: ၐဝပေဓသ, Ssiquoya; c. 1770 – August 1843), also known as George Gist, was a Cherokee silversmith, blacksmith and inventor who in 1821 completed the Cherokee syllabary, a writing system for the Cherokee language. He is remarkable as the only known person from a pre-literate society to single-handedly create an effective script that came into general use by his people.

Creation of the syllabary

Impressed by the "talking leaves" of white settlers, Sequoyah concluded that writing gave them advantages and set out to create a Cherokee equivalent. After early experiments with logographic symbols proved unwieldy, he analysed the sounds of the language and devised a syllabary of 85 characters, each representing a syllable. He taught the system to his daughter Ayoka, and in 1821 demonstrated it to Cherokee leaders, who adopted it officially. Within a few years a large share of the Cherokee Nation had learned to read and write, and in 1828 the bilingual newspaper Cherokee Phoenix began publication using the new type.

Later life and legacy

Sequoyah served as a delegate of the western Cherokee in Washington in 1828 and moved to Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma), where he built a homestead near present-day Sallisaw. In 1842–43 he travelled to Mexico in search of a band of Cherokee who had migrated there, and died on the journey.

His name is commemorated by Sequoyah County, Oklahoma, by a statue in the United States Capitol, and — according to a popular account — by the giant sequoia trees of California, which were named in his honour in the 1840s.
