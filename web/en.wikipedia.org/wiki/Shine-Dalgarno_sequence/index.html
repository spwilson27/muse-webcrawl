Shine–Dalgarno sequence

The Shine–Dalgarno (SD) sequence is the ribosomal binding site in bacterial and archaeal messenger RNA, generally located around 8 bases upstream of the start codon AUG. The RNA sequence helps recruit the ribosome to the messenger RNA to initiate protein synthesis by aligning the ribosome with the start codon. The consensus sequence is the purine-rich AGGAGG. It was proposed by Australian scientists John Shine and Lynn Dalgarno in 1974, based on their sequencing of the 3′ terminus of E. coli 16S rRNA.

Mechanism

The Shine–Dalgarno sequence is complementary to a pyrimidine-rich sequence (the "anti-Shine–Dalgarno" sequence, core CCUCC) near the 3′ end of the 16S ribosomal RNA of the small (30S) ribosomal subunit. Base pairing between the two positions the 30S subunit on the mRNA so that the start codon sits in the ribosomal P site, after which the 50S subunit joins to form the initiation complex. The spacing between the SD sequence and the start codon, and the strength of the pairing, influence the efficiency of translation initiation, and synthetic biologists exploit this using ribosome binding site calculators when designing expression constructs.

Distribution and exceptions

Shine–Dalgarno interactions are characteristic of bacteria and archaea; most eukaryotic cytoplasmic mRNAs instead initiate by scanning from a 5′ cap, using a Kozak sequence context around the start codon. Not all bacterial mRNAs possess an recognisable SD sequence — leaderless mRNAs and transcripts translated in organisms such as some Bacteroidetes use alternative mechanisms — and the importance of the SD varies among species; in E. coli a majority of genes show SD-like motifs. Mitochondria and chloroplasts, descendants of bacteria, retain a reduced version of the system; chloroplast translation still uses Shine–Dalgarno-like pairing for many genes.
