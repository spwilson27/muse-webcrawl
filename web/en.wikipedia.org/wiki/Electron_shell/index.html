Electron shell

An electron shell is a set of atomic orbitals that share the same principal quantum number n and are therefore at roughly the same energy and average distance from the nucleus. The shells are labelled K, L, M, N… for n = 1, 2, 3, 4…, and each shell can hold at most 2n² electrons. The arrangement of electrons in shells and subshells — the electron configuration — largely determines an element's chemical behaviour, and the concept underlies the structure of the periodic table.

Each subshell consists of orbitals of one type: s (1 orbital), p (3), d (5) and f (7), each holding two electrons. In practice the filling order follows the Aufbau principle and the Madelung rule, so, for example, the 4s subshell fills before 3d, which is why the M shell of potassium and calcium contains only 8 electrons in the ground state.

Valence shell

The outermost occupied shell is called the valence shell; its electrons, the valence electrons, take part in chemical bonding. Atoms tend to react so as to achieve a full outer shell, which explains the stability of the noble gases and the group patterns of the periodic table.

History

The shell picture grew out of the Bohr model of the atom (1913) and the X-ray notation of Charles Barkla, who named the K and L shells; it was refined by Arnold Sommerfeld, Edmund Stoner and Wolfgang Pauli into the scheme based on quantum numbers used today.
