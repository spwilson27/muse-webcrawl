The Women (1939 film)

The Women is a 1939 American comedy-drama film directed by George Cukor and produced by Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer. Based on Clare Boothe Luce's 1936 stage play of the same name, the film is famous for its entirely female cast — more than 130 speaking roles, all played by women, with not a single male character appearing on screen (even the animals, the credits joked, were female). It stars Norma Shearer, Joan Crawford and Rosalind Russell, supported by Mary Boland, Paulette Goddard, Joan Fontaine, Virginia Weidler, Marjorie Main and Hedda Hopper.

Plot

Mary Haines (Norma Shearer) is happily married to Stephen Haines — or so she believes, until her gossipy friend Sylvia Fowler (Rosalind Russell) learns from a manicurist that Stephen is having an affair with Crystal Allen (Joan Crawford), a scheming perfume-shop clerk. Egged on by Sylvia and unwilling to confront her husband, Mary follows the gossip circuit to a Reno divorce ranch, accompanied by a parade of friends and frenemies, including the flamboyant Countess de Lage (Mary Boland) and Miriam (Paulette Goddard). Mary divorces Stephen, who promptly marries Crystal. Two years later, learning that Crystal is unfaithful to Stephen and that he is miserable, Mary fights to win him back, finally abandoning her pride and resolving, in the film's famous closing line, to get what she wants with "jungle red" nails unsheathed: "I've had two years to grow claws, Mother. Jungle red."

Production

The screenplay by Anita Loos and Jane Murfin toned down Luce's sharpest lines for the Production Code while preserving the play's acid repartee. The film is in black and white except for a six-minute Technicolor fashion-show sequence, staged by Adrian, MGM's celebrated costume designer, who designed some 300 costumes for the picture. Cukor, known as a "women's director", handled the enormous cast with noted efficiency; the rivalry between Shearer and Crawford on and off screen became Hollywood legend.

Reception and legacy

The film was one of MGM's hits of 1939 and has remained a classic, regularly revived for its wit, its ensemble performances and its all-female conceit. Russell's frantic comic turn as Sylvia is often singled out. It has been remade several times — as the musical The Opposite Sex (1956) and as The Women (2008) — and in 2007 was selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry by the Library of Congress as culturally, historically or aesthetically significant.
