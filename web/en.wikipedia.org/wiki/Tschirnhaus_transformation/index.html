Tschirnhaus transformation

In mathematics, a Tschirnhaus transformation, named after Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus (1651–1708), is a mapping of the roots of a polynomial to the roots of a transformed polynomial via another polynomial substitution. Tschirnhaus introduced the method in 1683 with the hope of reducing any polynomial equation to a simpler form in which most coefficients vanish, and ultimately of solving equations of every degree by radicals.

The method

Given a polynomial of degree n with roots x1, …, xn, one substitutes y = f(x), where f is a polynomial of degree less than n whose coefficients are unknowns. The transformed polynomial, whose roots are f(xi), can be computed with resultants. By choosing f appropriately, one can force selected coefficients of the transformed polynomial to zero, because each vanishing condition is an equation in the unknown parameters.

Applications and limits

For cubics and quartics the method succeeds: the cubic can be reduced to the depressed form y³ + py + q, and the quartic to a biquadratic. For the quintic, Tschirnhaus transformations reduce the general equation to forms such as the Bring–Jerrard form y⁵ + py + q = 0 — a major achievement — but eliminating further terms requires solving auxiliary equations of degree higher than the original, a circularity that anticipated the Abel–Ruffini theorem: no general radical solution exists for degree five and above. The transformation nevertheless remains useful in algebraic geometry and in the theory of resolvents.
