Tied-arch bridge

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tied-arch bridge (also called a bowstring-arch or bowstring-girder bridge) is a type of arch bridge in which the outward horizontal thrust of the arch is resisted not by the abutments but by a horizontal tie member, usually the bridge deck itself, connecting the two ends of the arch. Because the tie carries the horizontal forces internally, the foundations need only resist vertical loads, which makes the tied arch attractive where ground conditions are poor or where the bridge must be erected without falsework over water, railways or roads.

Structure

The arch rises above the deck and is loaded in compression. Vertical hangers (or, in network arch bridges, inclined crossing hangers) transfer the weight of the deck up to the arch, while the deck acts as a tension tie between the arch springings. The result resembles a bow (the arch) and its string (the tie), giving the type its "bowstring" name. Tied-arch bridges may be built of steel or concrete, with ribs in a single plane on each side of the deck or, in some designs, a single central rib.

History and examples

Early bowstring girders in cast and wrought iron date to the mid-19th century. Modern examples include many medium-span river crossings. The network arch variant, with diagonal hangers crossing each other multiple times, was developed by Norwegian engineer Per Tveit in the late 1950s and offers a particularly stiff and economical arrangement. Because the whole span can be assembled beside its site and floated or skidded into place, tied arches are often used where closures of the obstacle below must be minimized.
