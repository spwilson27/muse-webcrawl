Locally unwanted land use

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A locally unwanted land use (LULU) is a land use that is broadly necessary or beneficial to society as a whole but is opposed by the residents who would live near it. The term is used in urban planning, geography, and environmental studies to describe facilities whose costs—noise, traffic, pollution, perceived risk, or stigma—fall on a host community while the benefits are dispersed across a much wider population.

Examples

Classic LULUs include landfills and waste incinerators, sewage treatment plants, prisons, homeless shelters, halfway houses, power plants, quarries, airports, highways, and waste transfer stations. More controversial recent examples include wind turbines, solar farms, supervised injection sites, and large-scale social housing. What counts as "unwanted" depends on local perception: a facility may be opposed because of real environmental impacts, fear of falling property values, or stigma attached to the people it serves.

Relation to NIMBY

LULUs are closely associated with the phenomenon known as NIMBY ("Not In My Back Yard"), in which residents acknowledge a facility is needed but insist it be built somewhere else. Planners distinguish between opposition grounded in legitimate environmental or health concerns and opposition driven by exclusionary motives; in practice the two are often intertwined. The concentration of LULUs in low-income and minority neighbourhoods—whose residents have less political power to resist them—is a central concern of the environmental justice movement.

Policy responses

Governments have tried several strategies to site LULUs fairly: compensation and host-community benefit packages, voluntary siting processes in which communities bid to host facilities, regional fair-share rules that prevent any one area from being overburdened, and improved design and emissions standards that reduce the burdens themselves. Political scientists note that purely technocratic siting tends to fail, and that durable solutions usually require early public participation and a credible distribution of costs and benefits.
