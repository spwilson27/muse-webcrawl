Agricultural aircraft

An agricultural aircraft is an aircraft built or converted for agricultural use, principally the aerial application of pesticides, herbicides, and fertilisers — a practice popularly known as crop dusting — as well as for seeding and hydroseeding. Agricultural aircraft are typically rugged, single-seat, low-wing designs with excellent low-speed handling, a large chemical hopper ahead of the cockpit, and spray booms along the trailing edge of the wing.

Design and operation

Common types include the Air Tractor AT-502 and AT-802, the Thrush Commander, and the earlier Grumman Ag Cat biplane; converted war-surplus trainers were used extensively in the early decades. Pilots fly very low — often just a few metres above the crop — in precise swaths guided by GPS, dispensing liquid or dry material. The work is demanding and hazardous because of obstacles, wires, weather, and chemical exposure. Modern guidance, flow-control systems, and turboprop engines have greatly improved efficiency and safety.

History and uses

The first aerial application experiments took place in the 1920s in the United States and New Zealand, initially for dusting crops against insects and later for topdressing hill-country pasture with superphosphate. Besides crop treatment, agricultural aircraft are used for aerial seeding, mosquito control, firefighting support, and combating locust plagues.
