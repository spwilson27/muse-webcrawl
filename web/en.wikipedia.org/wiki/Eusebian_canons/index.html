Eusebian canons

The Eusebian canons, also known as the Eusebian sections or Eusebian apparatus, are a system of cross-referencing parallel passages in the four Gospels, devised by Eusebius of Caesarea in the early 4th century AD. The system remained in use throughout the Middle Ages and appears in many illuminated manuscripts of the Gospels, making it one of the earliest and most durable tools of biblical scholarship.

Background

Eusebius built on the work of Ammonius of Alexandria, who had divided the Gospels into numbered sections in the 3rd century. In his Letter to Carpianus, which traditionally prefaced the canon tables in manuscripts, Eusebius explained how he had refined Ammonius's system so that readers could find, for any passage in one Gospel, the corresponding passages in the others without destroying the continuous sequence of each text.

The system

Eusebius divided the four Gospels into 1,165 sections in total: 355 in Matthew, 235 in Mark, 343 in Luke and 232 in John (counts vary slightly between manuscripts). Each section was assigned to one of ten canons according to which combination of evangelists contained parallel material:

  • Canon I: passages common to all four Gospels
  • Canon II: Matthew, Mark and Luke
  • Canon III: Matthew, Luke and John
  • Canon IV: Matthew, Mark and John
  • Canon V: Matthew and Luke
  • Canon VI: Matthew and Mark
  • Canon VII: Matthew and John
  • Canon VIII: Luke and Mark
  • Canon IX: Luke and John
  • Canon X: passages unique to each Gospel, listed separately for each evangelist

In the text of a Gospel manuscript, the section number was written in the margin, with the canon number beneath it, often in red ink. A reader could then look up the section number in the appropriate canon table at the front of the book to find the parallel sections in the other Gospels.

Canon tables in art

The canon tables themselves were usually presented in elaborate architectural frames of columns and arches, and became a standard feature of luxury Gospel books in late antiquity and the early Middle Ages. Famous examples appear in the Lindisfarne Gospels, the Book of Kells, the Codex Beneventanus and numerous Byzantine and Armenian manuscripts. The decorated canon-table pages are regarded as important evidence for the transmission of classical architectural ornament into medieval art.

Influence

The Eusebian apparatus was copied into the vast majority of Greek and Latin Gospel manuscripts and influenced later systems of biblical reference. Its function anticipates the chapter-and-verse cross-references of modern printed Bibles, though the chapter divisions themselves were a medieval innovation attributed to Stephen Langton in the 13th century.
