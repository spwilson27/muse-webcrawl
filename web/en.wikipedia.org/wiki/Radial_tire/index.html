Radial tire

A radial tyre (more precisely, a radial-ply tyre) is a particular design of vehicular tyre. In this design, the cord plies are arranged at 90 degrees to the direction of travel, or radially (from the centre of the tyre), in parallel across the tyre. This is in contrast to the diagonal (bias) arrangement of the plies in a cross-ply tyre, where the cords criss-cross at angles of about 35–40 degrees.

Construction

A radial tyre's carcass plies run straight across from bead to bead, and the tread is stabilised by a belt package — usually steel cords laid at a low angle beneath the tread. Because the sidewall and tread function more independently, the sidewall can flex without distorting the contact patch, giving better grip, lower rolling resistance and longer tread life than a comparable cross-ply tyre.

History

Michelin patented the radial design in 1946 and introduced the Michelin X tyre in 1948, first fitted as standard on the Citroën Traction Avant. Adoption in North America was slow until the 1970s fuel crisis, when the radial's roughly 3%-plus fuel saving and much longer life became decisive; by the 1990s radials had almost completely displaced cross-ply tyres on passenger cars. Cross-ply tyres survive mainly in some motorcycle, agricultural, aviation and vintage applications, where their sidewall stiffness or behaviour characteristics are still preferred.

Advantages and disadvantages

Radial tyres offer cooler running, better fuel economy, improved handling and ride comfort, and typically two to three times the tread life of bias tyres. Disadvantages include higher manufacturing cost, a harder ride at very low speeds in early designs, and greater susceptibility to sidewall damage in some heavy off-road uses.
