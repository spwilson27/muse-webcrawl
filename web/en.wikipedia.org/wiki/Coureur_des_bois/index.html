Coureur des bois

A coureur des bois (French for "runner of the woods"; plural coureurs de bois) was an independent French-Canadian fur trader in 17th- and 18th-century North America. Operating in New France, coureurs des bois journeyed into the interior wilderness to trade European goods directly with Indigenous peoples for beaver pelts, often without the trading licences (congés) required by the colonial authorities.

History

From the early 1600s, young French colonists took to the woods, living among Indigenous nations, learning their languages and customs, and serving as interpreters and intermediaries. Étienne Brûlé, who lived with the Huron from about 1610, is often considered the first coureur des bois. The trade was lucrative but disruptive to official policy, and the French crown repeatedly tried to suppress unlicensed trading; an amnesty and licensing system in 1681 brought many coureurs into the legal trade as voyageurs.

Way of life

Coureurs des bois travelled by birchbark canoe along the Great Lakes and river systems, sometimes spending years in the interior. Many formed lasting alliances — including marriages à la façon du pays — with Indigenous communities, and their descendants contributed to the emergence of the Métis people.
