Occultation

An occultation is an event in which one object is hidden by another object that passes between it and the observer. In astronomy the term is used most often for the Moon passing in front of a star or planet, but occultations by planets, asteroids, and distant Kuiper belt objects are also observed. When the nearer object does not completely conceal the farther one, the event is called a transit; a solar eclipse is a special case of occultation of the Sun by the Moon.

Lunar occultations

The Moon, moving eastward against the stars by roughly its own diameter each hour, frequently occults bright stars and occasionally planets. Because the Moon has essentially no atmosphere, a star disappears and reappears almost instantaneously at the lunar limb; precise timing of these events from many locations once served to map the lunar profile and measure the Moon's position, and now helps refine star positions and reveal close double stars whose components vanish in distinct steps.

Rare and spectacular are grazing occultations, in which the star skims the Moon's polar limb, blinking on and off behind mountains and valleys at the edge of the lunar disk.

Occultations by other bodies

Planetary occultations of stars have yielded major discoveries: the rings of Uranus were discovered in 1977 when the planet's passage in front of a star produced brief dips in brightness before and after the main event, and occultation observations from the Kuiper Airborne Observatory and later campaigns probed Pluto's atmosphere. Asteroid occultations, timed by networks of amateur and professional observers, measure asteroid sizes and shapes with high precision; occultations by trans-Neptunian objects such as Quaoar and Haumea have revealed rings and measured diameters of bodies too small to image directly.

Observing

Predictions are computed from star catalogues and ephemerides, and outcomes depend sensitively on the observer's location, since the occulting body's shadow track across the Earth may be only tens of kilometres wide for an asteroid. The International Occultation Timing Association coordinates predictions and collects observations.
