Diệm, first president of the Republic of Vietnam.

Ngô Đình Diệm (3 January 1901 – 2 November 1963) was a Vietnamese politician who served as the first president of South Vietnam (the Republic of Vietnam) from 1955 until his assassination in 1963. Born into a prominent Catholic mandarin family in central Vietnam, Diệm rose through the colonial administration, briefly serving as interior minister under Emperor Bảo Đại in 1933 before resigning in protest at French unwillingness to grant reforms. A devout Catholic nationalist and staunch anti-communist, he lived in exile in the United States and Europe in the early 1950s, building support among American politicians and Catholic networks.

Rise to power

After the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu and the 1954 Geneva Accords partitioning Vietnam at the 17th parallel, Diệm became prime minister of the State of Vietnam. With strong American backing he consolidated power in 1955 — defeating the Bình Xuyên crime syndicate in the Battle for Saigon — and then deposed Bảo Đại in a referendum widely regarded as fraudulent, proclaiming the Republic of Vietnam with himself as president. His government resettled some 900,000 refugees who fled the communist north, many of them Catholics.

Presidency

Diệm's rule was authoritarian and increasingly family-dominated: his brother Ngô Đình Nhu ran the secret police and the Cần Lao party, and Nhu's wife, Madame Nhu, was the regime's de facto first lady. Land reform was limited, opposition was repressed, and elections were manipulated. The regime's favouritism toward the Catholic minority alienated the Buddhist majority. As the communist-led insurgency (the Viet Cong, backed by North Vietnam) grew from 1959, the United States poured in aid and military advisers; the controversial "Strategic Hamlet" programme of 1962 attempted to isolate villagers from the guerrillas but was widely resented and poorly implemented.

The crisis came in 1963, when government troops fired on Buddhist demonstrators in Huế, and the self-immolation of the monk Thích Quảng Đức in Saigon shocked world opinion. Raids by Nhu's Special Forces on Buddhist pagodas in August 1963 destroyed remaining American confidence in the regime. On 1 November 1963 South Vietnamese generals, with the knowledge of the Kennedy administration, launched a coup; Diệm and Nhu were captured the next day and murdered in the back of an armoured personnel carrier. Their deaths ushered in a period of revolving-door military governments and deeper American involvement in the Vietnam War.
