Railroad tie

A railroad tie (North America), railway tie, or railway sleeper (outside North America) is a rectangular support for the rails in railroad tracks. Ties are laid perpendicular to the rails, transfer the loads from the rails to the track ballast and subgrade, hold the rails upright, and keep them spaced to the correct gauge.

Ties hold rails to gauge and distribute axle loads into the ballast

Materials

Traditionally, ties have been made of wood, usually hardwood such as oak, treated with creosote or other preservatives to resist rot and insect attack. Wooden ties are inexpensive and easy to handle but have service lives of roughly 20–30 years.

Prestressed concrete ties are heavier and longer-lived, and are now standard on many busy and high-speed lines. Steel ties and composite ties made from recycled plastic are also used in some applications. Composite ties resist moisture and insects and can be manufactured from mixed waste plastics, though their cost and stiffness differ from wood.

Dimensions and spacing

A typical North American wooden tie is about 8.5 to 9 feet (2.6–2.7 m) long, 9 inches (230 mm) wide, and 7 inches (180 mm) thick. Ties are commonly spaced about 19–24 inches (roughly 500–600 mm) apart, so that a mile of track contains around 3,000 ties.

Fastening

Rails are fastened to wooden ties with rail spikes driven through tie plates, while concrete and steel ties use resilient clips and other elastic fastening systems that grip the rail foot.

History

Early railways used stone blocks or longitudinal timbers as rail supports. The transverse wooden sleeper, embedded in ballast, became standard during the nineteenth century as track mileage expanded, and it remained the dominant form in North America long after concrete ties became common in Europe.

Disposal and reuse

Retired wooden ties are often reused in landscaping, but because of their creosote treatment they are classed as treated wood waste in many jurisdictions and their burning or disposal is regulated.
