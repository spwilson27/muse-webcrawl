Lex Hortensia

The lex Hortensia, also known as the Hortensian law, was a law passed in the Roman Republic in 287 BC that made resolutions passed by the Plebeian Council (plebiscita) binding on all Roman citizens, including the aristocratic patricians. It was named for Quintus Hortensius, the plebeian dictator appointed to resolve a political crisis after the plebeians had seceded from the city to the Janiculum hill — the final episode of the centuries-long Conflict of the Orders.

Background

Earlier statutes — the lex Valeria Horatia of 449 BC and the lex Publilia of 339 BC — had already declared plebiscites binding in principle, but their effect was blunted by the Senate's power of prior approval (patrum auctoritas) and by ambiguous wording covering only the plebeians themselves. Debt and the unequal distribution of conquered public land drove the plebs to one last secession in 287 BC, paralysing the city.

Provisions and effect

Hortensius's law removed the remaining obstacles: plebiscites enacted in the Plebeian Council thereafter had the force of law for the whole community without senatorial ratification. The law also reportedly fixed nundinae (market days) as court days, aiding rural plebeians. With this measure the formal legal inequality between patricians and plebeians in legislation ended; the plebeian tribunes and council became central instruments of Roman lawmaking, though in practice wealthy plebeian and patrician families fused into a single senatorial nobility that steered the assemblies.

Legacy

The lex Hortensia is conventionally treated as the culmination of the Conflict of the Orders (494–287 BC). Plebiscites under it produced much of later Roman private law, including the lex Aquilia on damages (c. 286 BC, often dated immediately after). Its principle — that a popular assembly's enactments bind the entire state — made it a reference point in later European debates about popular sovereignty.
