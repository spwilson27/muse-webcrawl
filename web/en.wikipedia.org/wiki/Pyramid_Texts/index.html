The Pyramid Texts are a collection of ancient Egyptian religious texts from the time of the Old Kingdom, carved in hieroglyphs on the walls of royal burial chambers in the pyramids at Saqqara. They are the oldest known body of religious literature in the world. First appearing in the pyramid of Unas, last king of the Fifth Dynasty (c. 2350 BCE), and in the Sixth-Dynasty pyramids of Teti, Pepi I, Merenre and Pepi II (and several queens), the texts consist of spells — Egyptologists call them "utterances" — intended to protect the dead king and secure his resurrection and ascent to the afterlife among the gods.

Discovery and study

The texts were discovered in 1880–81 when Auguste Mariette's and Gaston Maspero's workmen entered the pyramids of Saqqara; Maspero published the first edition in 1894. The standard reference edition was produced by Kurt Sethe (1908–22), numbering over 700 utterances; later discoveries, including in queens' pyramids and at South Saqqara, have added further material. Major translations were made by Samuel Mercer (1952), Raymond Faulkner (1969) and James P. Allen (2005).

Content

The utterances are not a single composition but a repertoire: offering rituals and the Opening of the Mouth; "resurrection" texts in which the king rises; protective spells against snakes and hostile beings; hymns; and dramatic ascension texts in which the king flies to the sky as a bird, climbs a ladder, or crosses in the sun god's bark to join Ra or become an imperishable star. The notorious "Cannibal Hymn" of Unas and Teti portrays the king devouring the gods to absorb their power. Language and some contents are archaic even for the Old Kingdom, suggesting roots in earlier, unwritten ritual.

Significance and legacy

The Pyramid Texts mark the first large-scale written expression of Egyptian religion and the royal afterlife, centred on the king's identification with Osiris and Ra. In the First Intermediate Period and Middle Kingdom the tradition broadened socially into the Coffin Texts, and later fed into the Book of the Dead — a process often called the "democratisation of the afterlife".
