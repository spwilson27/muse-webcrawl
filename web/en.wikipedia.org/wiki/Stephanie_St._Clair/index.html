Stephanie St. Clair

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stephanie St. Clair (December 24, 1897 – December 1969), also known as "Queenie" and "Madame St. Clair", was a French-born American racketeer who ran a numbers game (an illegal lottery) in Harlem, New York, during the early 20th century. She became one of the most prominent figures in Harlem's underground economy during Prohibition and the Great Depression, and she is remembered for her public resistance to mobsters such as Dutch Schultz who sought to take over her operation.

Early life

St. Clair was born on the Caribbean island of Martinique, then a French colony, around 1897. Little is known with certainty about her early years; she cultivated an air of mystery about her origins, variously claiming education in France and Montreal. She arrived in New York City around 1912 and settled in Harlem, where she learned English and became involved in the neighborhood's thriving illegal gambling economy.

Numbers racket

By the early 1920s, St. Clair had built a profitable policy banking operation — the "numbers game," in which bettors wagered small sums on a three-digit number drawn from published financial figures. Numbers betting was deeply woven into Harlem life, and bankers such as St. Clair employed runners, controllers, and collectors throughout the neighborhood. She was known for paying winners promptly, investing in Harlem businesses and real estate, and providing bail money and legal aid to residents. Her wealth, tailored clothing, and commanding manner made her a local celebrity.

Conflict with Dutch Schultz

During the early 1930s, the Dutch Schultz organization attempted to muscle in on Harlem's numbers rackets. St. Clair refused to submit, denouncing Schultz in newspaper advertisements and cooperating with authorities when it suited her. The feud turned violent, with beatings, murders of her associates, and intimidation of bettors. After Schultz was assassinated in 1935 — reportedly by order of the Mafia Commission — St. Clair is famously said to have sent a telegram to his deathbed reading, "As ye sow, so shall ye reap."

Later life and legacy

St. Clair served prison time on a policy charge and gradually ceded control of her operation to associates, including Ellsworth "Bumpy" Johnson, who had worked as her enforcer and later became a major Harlem crime figure in his own right. She spent her later years in relative quiet and died in 1969. St. Clair's story has been dramatized in film, including a portrayal in the 1997 film Hoodlum, and she remains a symbol of Black women's defiance of the white organized-crime syndicates of the era.
