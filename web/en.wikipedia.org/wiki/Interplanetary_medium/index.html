Interplanetary medium

The interplanetary medium (IPM) or interplanetary space is the material that fills the Solar System, in which the planets, dwarf planets, asteroids and comets move. It consists of the solar wind plasma, the interplanetary magnetic field, cosmic rays, and interplanetary dust.

The solar wind, a stream of charged particles from the Sun, dominates the interplanetary medium.

Composition and properties

Solar wind and magnetic field

The solar wind is a supersonic plasma of mostly protons, electrons, and alpha particles flowing outward from the Sun at roughly 300–800 km/s. Near Earth its density is only about 5 particles per cubic centimetre and its temperature around 105 K. The Sun's magnetic field is carried outward by the wind and wound into the Parker spiral by solar rotation. The interplanetary magnetic field near Earth is typically about 5 nanotesla. Streams of fast and slow wind interact, and coronal mass ejections create transient disturbances that drive geomagnetic activity.

Dust and zodiacal light

Interplanetary dust, produced by comet disintegration and asteroid collisions, scatters sunlight to produce the zodiacal light visible after sunset and before dawn. Dust near Earth has a density of roughly 10−20 g/cm³; particles spiral slowly toward the Sun under Poynting–Robertson drag or are expelled by radiation pressure.

Cosmic rays

Galactic cosmic rays—high-energy nuclei from beyond the Solar System—permeate the medium and are modulated by the solar wind over the 11-year solar cycle; anomalous cosmic rays originate at the termination shock.

Extent

The interplanetary medium extends to the heliopause, where the solar wind is stopped by the interstellar medium, about 120 AU from the Sun in the direction of motion, a boundary crossed by Voyager 1 in 2012 and Voyager 2 in 2018. Within the heliosphere, spacecraft such as Parker Solar Probe and Solar Orbiter sample the medium in situ.
