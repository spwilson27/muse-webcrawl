An arrestor bed, also called a runaway truck ramp, escape lane or emergency escape ramp, is a traffic device placed beside roads on steep downgrades that enables vehicles suffering brake failure to come safely to a stop. It typically consists of a lane filled with deep, loose material — most often rounded gravel — that creates high rolling resistance, sometimes combined with an uphill gradient.

Design

The bed is filled with gravel of uniform, rounded size that does not compact, so a vehicle's wheels sink in and plough through the material, dissipating kinetic energy quickly. Depths of around 30–45 cm (12–18 in) are common at the start, deepening along the bed. Beds must be long enough for the heaviest vehicles at the design speed, and signs, lighting and access lanes for recovery vehicles are standard features.

Types

Common configurations include gravity escape ramps, which simply climb a slope; sand pile ramps; mechanical-arrestor ramps using nets or cables; and the gravel arrestor bed proper, which may be built on the level or on an upgrade. At airports, an analogous system of crushable concrete blocks, known as an engineered materials arresting system (EMAS), is installed at runway ends.

Operation

A driver with failed brakes steers into the ramp, where deceleration is swift but controlled. After use, the vehicle is winched out backwards by a tow truck and the gravel is re-graded. Arrestor beds are credited with preventing many fatal runaway-truck crashes on mountain roads worldwide, and their locations are marked in advance so that drivers know where an escape exists before brakes begin to overheat.
