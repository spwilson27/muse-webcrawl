Wealth tax

A wealth tax (also called a capital tax or equity tax) is a tax on an entity's holdings of assets. This includes the total value of personal assets, including cash, bank deposits, real estate, assets in insurance and pension plans, ownership of unincorporated businesses, financial securities, and personal trusts. Typically, liabilities (primarily mortgages and other loans) are deducted from an individual's wealth, hence it is sometimes called a net wealth tax.

Practice

Many countries have imposed wealth taxes historically; by the 2020s only a few — including Norway, Spain, and Switzerland — levied broad annual net wealth taxes, down from twelve OECD countries in 1990. France replaced its solidarity tax on wealth (ISF) in 2018 with a tax restricted to real estate. Wealth taxes typically include high exemption thresholds so that only the wealthiest households are taxed, with flat or progressive rates often between 0.1% and 2% of net assets above the threshold.

Arguments

Supporters argue wealth taxes reduce inequality, capture economic gains that escape income taxation, and raise revenue from those most able to pay. Critics point to administrative difficulty in valuing illiquid assets, capital flight and tax avoidance, double taxation of savings already taxed as income, and historically modest revenues relative to collection costs. In the United States, proposals by Senator Elizabeth Warren and others in 2019–2020 prompted debate over whether a federal wealth tax would be constitutional under the apportionment clause for direct taxes.

History

Wealth and property taxes are ancient, levied in Athens, Rome, and medieval Europe, often to finance wars. Modern net wealth taxes spread in Europe after the Second World War; most were repealed from the 1990s onward amid concerns about capital mobility, though they remain part of fiscal policy debates.
