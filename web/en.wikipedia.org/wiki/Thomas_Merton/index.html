Thomas Merton (31 January 1915 – 10 December 1968), religious name M. Louis, was an American Trappist monk, writer, theologian, mystic, poet, artist, and scholar of comparative religion. A monk of the Abbey of Gethsemani in Kentucky, he wrote more than 50 books in a 27-year period, most famously his spiritual autobiography The Seven Storey Mountain (1948), one of the most influential religious books of the 20th century.

Early life and conversion

Merton was born in Prades, France, to Owen Merton, a New Zealand artist, and Ruth Jenkins, an American artist. Orphaned of his mother at six and his father at sixteen, he was educated in France and England (Oakham School) and read English at Clare College, Cambridge, before emigrating to the United States. At Columbia University he took bachelor's and master's degrees in English and moved in a circle that included Robert Lax and Edward Rice. After a bohemian youth, a journey to Rome, and deepening reading in Catholic thought — including Étienne Gilson and the autobiography of Thérèse of Lisieux — he converted to Catholicism in 1938, and in December 1941 entered the Trappist Abbey of Gethsemani. He was ordained a priest in 1949.

Writings and thought

The Seven Storey Mountain, published at the urging of his superiors and edited down from a much longer manuscript, became a surprise bestseller and was credited with filling novitiates in the post-war years. Over time Merton's writing broadened from traditional contemplative themes to social criticism: he wrote on war, racism, and nonviolence (his anti-war writings were censored by his order in the early 1960s), corresponded with figures from Dorothy Day and Martin Luther King Jr. to Boris Pasternak and Czesław Miłosz, and in his last years engaged deeply with Zen Buddhism, Taoism, and Sufism, seeking parallels between Christian contemplation and Eastern meditation. His extensive journals and letters, published posthumously, revealed the restless, critical inner life behind the public monk.

Death and legacy

Merton died on 10 December 1968 in Bangkok, where he had travelled — with rare permission to leave the abbey — to attend an interfaith conference of Eastern and Western monastics; hours after giving a talk, he was found dead, apparently accidentally electrocuted by a faulty electric fan. He is buried at Gethsemani. In 2015 Pope Francis named Merton in an address to the U.S. Congress as one of four exemplary Americans, alongside Abraham Lincoln, Martin Luther King Jr., and Dorothy Day. The Thomas Merton Center at Bellarmine University in Louisville preserves his archive.
