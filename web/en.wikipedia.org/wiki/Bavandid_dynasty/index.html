The Bavand dynasty (Persian: باوندیان, Bavandiyan) was an Iranian dynasty that ruled parts of Tabaristan — the mountainous region of what is now Mazandaran province in northern Iran — for nearly seven centuries, from the early Islamic period until 1210 in some reckonings. Claiming descent from the Sasanian prince Kawus, a brother of Khosrow I, the Bavandids were among the longest-lived local dynasties of the post-conquest era, long retaining Zoroastrian traditions in their highland strongholds.

Origins

According to the dynasty's own tradition, preserved by the local historian Ibn Isfandiyar, the founder Bav was a descendant of Kawus who withdrew to the mountains of Tabaristan after the Arab conquest of Persia. The family's stronghold of Firrim in the Shahriyar mountains gave its rulers the style "King of the Mountains" (malik al-jibal).

History

The historical dynasty emerges with Sharwin I in the late eighth century, who resisted Abbasid attempts to subdue Tabaristan. His grandson Qarin I converted to Islam and cooperated with the caliphate. In the tenth and eleventh centuries the Bavandids were variously tributary to the Ziyarids, Buyids and Ghaznavids, and in 1074 the line nearly ended with the assassination of Qarin II.

A cadet branch revived the dynasty under Shahriyar IV and his son Qarin III, and in the twelfth century Shah Ghazi Rustam IV brought the Bavandids to their greatest power, contending with the Seljuks and the Nizari Ismailis of Alamut. The last great ruler, Hasan II, was assassinated in 1173, after which Bavandid rule decayed; the dynasty's remnants were finally extinguished by the Mongol invasions in the early thirteenth century.

Legacy

The Bavandids are chiefly remembered through Ibn Isfandiyar's History of Tabaristan (c. 1216), the principal source for the dynasty and for the legends of pre-Islamic Mazandaran. Their mountain fastnesses preserved Iranian customs long after the surrounding lowlands were subdued.
