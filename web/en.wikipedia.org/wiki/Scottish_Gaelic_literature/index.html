Scottish Gaelic literature is literature written in the Scottish Gaelic language. It has one of the longest vernacular literary traditions in Europe: although the earliest surviving written Gaelic from Scotland is medieval, the oral tradition behind it stretches back much further, and Scotland shared a common classical Gaelic literary language with Ireland until the 17th century.

Early and classical tradition

Professional poets (filidh) composed elaborate syllabic verse in Classical Gaelic for the chiefs of clans. The most important early Scottish compilation is the Book of the Dean of Lismore (compiled c. 1512–1542), which preserves heroic ballads of the Fenian cycle and court poetry in a Scots-influenced orthography. Mary MacLeod (Màiri Nighean Alasdair Ruaidh, c. 1615–1707) is remembered as one of the great Gaelic poets of the 17th century for her panegyrics to the MacLeods of Dunvegan.

The 18th century

The 18th century is considered a golden age. Alasdair mac Mhaighstir Alasdair (Alexander MacDonald, c. 1698–1770) published the first secular book in Scottish Gaelic (1751) and wrote vivid nature and Jacobite poetry. Duncan Ban MacIntyre (1724–1812) composed the celebrated nature poem Moladh Beinn Dòbhrain ("Praise of Ben Dorain"). James Macpherson's English "Ossian" poems (1760s), presented as translations of ancient Gaelic epic, provoked controversy but sparked European interest in Gaelic tradition.

19th and 20th centuries

The 19th century saw religious verse, emigration songs, and the vast collecting work of John Francis Campbell (Popular Tales of the West Highlands, 1860–62). In the 20th century a "Gaelic Renaissance" was led by Sorley MacLean (Somhairle MacGill-Eain, 1911–1996), whose Dàin do Eimhir (1943) transformed Gaelic poetry, alongside Derick Thomson, George Campbell Hay and Iain Crichton Smith, who wrote in both Gaelic and English. Novelists such as Iain Crichton Smith and, more recently, writers like Aonghas MacNeacail have extended Gaelic prose fiction.

Contemporary

Gaelic publishing is supported by bodies such as the Gaelic Books Council, and new poetry, novels and drama continue to appear despite the language's small speaker base, alongside a strong tradition of Gaelic song.
