Atomic force microscopy

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) or scanning force microscopy (SFM) is a very-high-resolution type of scanning probe microscopy (SPM), with demonstrated resolution on the order of fractions of a nanometer, more than 1000 times better than the optical diffraction limit.

Overview

The AFM consists of a cantilever with a sharp tip (probe) at its end that is used to scan the specimen surface. The cantilever is typically silicon or silicon nitride with a tip radius of curvature on the order of nanometers. When the tip is brought into proximity of a sample surface, forces between the tip and the sample lead to a deflection of the cantilever according to Hooke's law.

Depending on the situation, forces that are measured in AFM include mechanical contact force, van der Waals forces, capillary forces, chemical bonding, electrostatic forces, magnetic forces, Casimir forces and solvation forces. Typically, the deflection is measured using a laser spot reflected from the top surface of the cantilever into an array of photodiodes. A feedback loop adjusts the height of the sample or tip to keep the deflection or oscillation amplitude constant as the tip rasters across the surface, and the recorded adjustments build up a topographic image.

History

The AFM was invented by Gerd Binnig, Calvin Quate and Christoph Gerber in 1986, building on Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer's earlier scanning tunneling microscope (STM). Unlike the STM, the AFM works on insulating as well as conducting surfaces, which opened up atomic-scale imaging of polymers, ceramics and biological molecules.

Modes of operation

  • Contact mode — the tip drags across the surface at constant deflection.
  • Tapping (intermittent-contact) mode — the cantilever oscillates and intermittently touches the surface, reducing damage to soft samples.
  • Non-contact mode — the oscillating tip is held above the surface and responds to attractive forces.

Applications

AFM is used in materials science, semiconductor metrology, polymer physics and biology. Variants measure friction, magnetism, electrical potential, conductivity and mechanical properties such as stiffness at the nanoscale. AFM can image in air, vacuum and liquid, making it suitable for observing living cells and dynamic processes.
