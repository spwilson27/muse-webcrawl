Consonance and dissonance

[Hero image placeholder: musical notation showing consonant and dissonant intervals]

In music, consonance and dissonance categorize the relative harmony or discordance of simultaneous tones. A combination of tones is consonant if it sounds stable and complete — as in the octave, the perfect fifth, and major and minor thirds and sixths — and dissonant if it creates a sensation of tension or instability that calls for resolution, as with the tritone, the minor second, and the major seventh.

Physical basis

The perception of consonance relates to the simplicity of the frequency ratio between tones. The Pythagorean tradition held that consonant intervals correspond to simple integer ratios: the octave 2:1, the perfect fifth 3:2, the perfect fourth 4:3. Hermann von Helmholtz explained dissonance acoustically as the "beating" produced when the overtones of two tones interfere. Modern psychoacoustics adds that roughness perception peaks at intervals around a semitone to a minor second and that cultural learning strongly shapes judgments of pleasantness.

History of theory

Medieval music theory accepted only the perfect intervals — octave, fifth, fourth — as perfect consonances. Thirds and sixths, initially classified as dissonances, were gradually accepted as imperfect consonances during the Renaissance as triadic harmony became the norm, a shift codified by theorists such as Zarlino and Ramos de Pareja. By the common-practice period (c. 1600–1900), composers treated dissonance as needing preparation and resolution according to rules of counterpoint.

20th-century developments

In the early 20th century, composers such as Arnold Schoenberg proclaimed the "emancipation of the dissonance," treating dissonant sonorities as structurally equal to consonances in atonal and twelve-tone music. Meanwhile, traditions of drone, jazz, rock, and many non-Western musics rely on different consonance conventions.
