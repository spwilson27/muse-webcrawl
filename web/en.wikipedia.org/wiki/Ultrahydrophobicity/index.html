Ultrahydrophobicity

Ultrahydrophobicity, more commonly called superhydrophobicity, describes surfaces with extreme water repellence: a water droplet forms a contact angle greater than 150° and typically rolls off with very little tilting (low contact-angle hysteresis). Such surfaces combine a hydrophobic surface chemistry with micro- and nanoscale roughness, so that droplets rest largely on a cushion of trapped air rather than on the solid itself—a state described by the Cassie–Baxter model.

A droplet on a lotus leaf: the classic natural example of superhydrophobicity, with a contact angle above 150°.

Natural examples and theory

The best-known natural example is the lotus effect: lotus leaves bear waxy, rough microstructures that make water bead up and roll away, carrying dirt with it and keeping the surface self-cleaning. Similar surfaces occur on water striders' legs, butterfly wings, and some desert plants. Wetting theory distinguishes the Wenzel state, in which liquid fills the roughness, from the Cassie–Baxter state, in which air pockets remain beneath the droplet and produce the extreme repellence.

Artificial surfaces and applications

Engineered superhydrophobic coatings are made by roughening surfaces through etching, deposition, or nanostructuring and then applying low-surface-energy materials such as fluoropolymers or silicones. Applications include self-cleaning glass and solar panels, anti-icing and anti-corrosion coatings, water-repellent textiles, and microfluidic devices. A persistent challenge is durability: the delicate microstructures that create the effect are easily damaged by abrasion or contaminated by oils.
