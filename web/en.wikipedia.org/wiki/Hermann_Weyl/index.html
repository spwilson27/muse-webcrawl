Hermann Weyl

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hermann Klaus Hugo Weyl (9 November 1885 – 8 December 1955) was a German mathematician, theoretical physicist, logician, and philosopher who was among the last great universalists of mathematics. Although much of his career was spent in Zürich and Göttingen, he later moved to the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, becoming one of its first fellows.

Contributions

A student of David Hilbert at Göttingen, Weyl made fundamental contributions across mathematics and physics: he formulated the first gauge theory in an attempt to unify gravity and electromagnetism; his 1918 book Raum, Zeit, Materie (Space, Time, Matter) was a classic exposition of relativity; and his work on compact Lie groups and their representations underpinned quantum mechanics — reflected in his book The Theory of Groups and Quantum Mechanics (1928). Other lasting concepts include the Weyl tensor, Weyl spinors, Weyl's law, and the Weyl chamber.

Later life

Weyl left Göttingen in 1933 as the Nazis rose to power and spent the rest of his career in Princeton, returning to Zürich after retirement. He died in Zürich in 1955. Freeman Dyson called him the last of the great mathematicians in the tradition of Hilbert and Poincaré.
