In re Debs

In re Debs, 158 U.S. 564 (1895)
CourtSupreme Court of the United States
Decided27 May 1895
PetitionerEugene V. Debs
HoldingThe federal government may use injunctions and force to remove obstructions to interstate commerce and the mails

In re Debs, 158 U.S. 564 (1895), was a United States Supreme Court decision arising from the Pullman Strike of 1894. The Court unanimously upheld the power of the federal government to obtain an injunction against the striking railway workers' union and to enforce it by contempt proceedings, affirming the conviction of American Railway Union president Eugene V. Debs.

Background

When workers at the Pullman Palace Car Company struck in 1894 and the American Railway Union boycotted trains carrying Pullman cars, rail traffic across the Midwest was paralysed. The federal government, arguing that the strike obstructed the US mails and interstate commerce, obtained a sweeping injunction under the Sherman Antitrust Act. President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops over the objections of Illinois governor John Peter Altgeld. Debs defied the injunction, was convicted of contempt of court, and served six months in prison; his habeas corpus petition reached the Supreme Court.

Decision

Justice David Brewer's unanimous opinion rested on the federal government's constitutional authority over interstate commerce and the mails, holding that the executive could use "the entire strength of the nation" to remove forcible obstructions, and that equity courts could protect those national interests by injunction. The Court notably grounded its ruling in the commerce power rather than the Sherman Act.

Significance

The decision legitimised the "government by injunction" widely used against organised labour for the next four decades, until the Norris–LaGuardia Act of 1932 sharply restricted labour injunctions. Debs, meanwhile, emerged from prison a committed socialist and went on to run for president five times.
