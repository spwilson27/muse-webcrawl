A broadside ballad is a form of printed song literature that flourished in Britain and its colonies from the 16th to the 19th century. Lyrics treating news, crime, love, religion, politics, and marvels were printed cheaply on one side of a single sheet of paper — often with a crude woodcut illustration and the name of a familiar tune to which the words should be sung — and sold in the streets for a penny or less. Broadside ballads were among the first forms of mass media, conveying news and entertainment to a partly literate population.

Production and sale

Printers in London's publishing districts — and later in provincial towns and in Catnach Press's famous Seven Dials operation — produced broadsides in huge numbers. Ballad sellers sang their wares in the street to attract buyers, a performance practice that made the broadside an audible as well as printed medium. Themes included executions and "last dying speeches" of criminals, murders, battles, shipwrecks, monstrous births, royal events, and comic or romantic songs. Print runs for sensational items could reach tens of thousands; the Catnach press reportedly sold over a million copies relating to the Cato Street conspiracy and other sensations.

Content and survival

Most broadside songs were ephemeral, but collectors preserved thousands: the Pepys collection at Magdalene College, Cambridge, the Roxburghe Ballads, and the collection of the antiquary Francis James Child drew partly on broadsides. Many traditional ballads survived in print before passing into oral tradition — "Barbara Allen" first appeared on a broadside in the 17th century. Execution broadsides, sold at the scaffold, are an important source for historians of crime and popular morality.

Decline

The trade declined in the later 19th century as cheap newspapers, music hall songbooks, and improved literacy changed popular reading habits, though chapbook and broadside printing persisted into the early 20th century. Scholars prize broadsides as evidence for the history of popular culture, literacy, crime reporting, and the early news trade.
