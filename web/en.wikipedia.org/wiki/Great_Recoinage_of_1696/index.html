Great Recoinage of 1696

The Great Recoinage of 1696 was an attempt by the English Government under William III to replace the silver coinage of England, which had suffered from clipping — the practice of shaving precious metal from the edges of coins — and counterfeiting to the extent that the currency's integrity was undermined. By the 1690s, much of the old hammered silver coinage had lost a large fraction of its weight, and the shortage of sound money threatened trade and the financing of the Nine Years' War against France.

Background

Since the reign of Elizabeth I, English silver coins had increasingly been produced by hand hammering, and their irregular edges made clipping easy and hard to detect. Although machine-made "milled" coinage with lettered or grained edges had been introduced under Charles II, the old hammered coins remained in circulation alongside it and were clipped down to a fraction of their face value. By 1695 the silver currency had depreciated severely; the guinea rose in value against the silver shilling, and the public increasingly refused the clipped money.

The recoinage

After debate in Parliament between advocates of devaluation, such as John Locke's opponents in the Treasury, and those (including Locke and Isaac Newton) who favoured recoining at the old standard, the government decided to call in the old money and recoin it at the existing weight and fineness. Locke's Short Observations and related writings argued that the standard should not be altered.

The old clipped money was accepted by tale (at face value) for a limited period if brought in by certain dates, and thereafter only by weight. New milled coins were produced at the Tower Mint in London and at temporary branch mints in Bristol, Chester, Exeter, Norwich and York, to speed production. Isaac Newton became Warden of the Mint in 1696 and later Master, and he supervised the operation with great energy; nearly £7 million of silver was recoined, an immense task that took until about 1699.

Consequences

The recoinage was costly: the loss on the clipped money was borne largely by the state, financed partly by the new window tax. The transition caused a temporary shortage of coin and considerable hardship. In the longer term, however, it stabilised the currency, and the recoinage — together with the fixed mint price for gold — inadvertently helped set England on the path toward the gold standard, as silver was overvalued at the mint and tended to flow abroad in the following decades.
