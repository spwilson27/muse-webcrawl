Manhunt (military)

Manhunt operations
TypeCounterinsurgency / counterterrorism tactic
ObjectiveCapture or killing of high-value targets (HVTs)
Practised bySpecial operations forces, intelligence services

A manhunt in military and intelligence usage is a focused operation to capture or kill a specific individual, usually a high-value target (HVT) such as an insurgent leader, terrorist commander, or fugitive war criminal. Unlike conventional battle, which seeks to defeat enemy forces or take ground, manhunting is intelligence-driven: its core activity is building a picture of the target's network — family, couriers, financiers, safe houses — and exploiting it until the individual can be located.

History

Historical examples include the Allied search for Nazi war criminals after the Second World War, Israel's pursuit of the Munich Olympics attackers (often associated with Operation Wrath of God), and Colombia's long hunt for drug lord Pablo Escobar, assisted by US signals intelligence. After the September 11 attacks, the United States institutionalised manhunting within Joint Special Operations Command (JSOC). Task forces in Iraq and Afghanistan developed the "find, fix, finish, exploit, analyse" (F3EA) cycle, running raids at a tempo of multiple targets per night; journalist and scholars have termed this the "industrial-scale" manhunt. Operations ended in the deaths of Abu Musab al-Zarqawi (2006), Osama bin Laden (Operation Neptune Spear, 2011), and Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi (2019).

Methods

Manhunts combine human intelligence (HUMINT), signals intelligence (SIGINT), and persistent aerial surveillance from drones. Captured documents, phones, and computers ("sensitive site exploitation") seed the next round of targeting. In law-enforcement usage, the term overlaps with fugitive apprehension by units such as the US Marshals Service.

Criticism

Critics argue that an emphasis on killing or capturing leaders can be tactically effective but strategically inconclusive: networks adapt, successors emerge, and night raids and mistaken-identity strikes can alienate local populations. The legality of targeted killings outside recognised battlefields remains debated under international humanitarian and human-rights law.
