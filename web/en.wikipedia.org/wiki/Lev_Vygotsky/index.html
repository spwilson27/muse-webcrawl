Lev Semyonovich Vygotsky (17 November 1896 – 11 June 1934) was a Soviet psychologist and the founder of cultural-historical psychology. In a career cut short by tuberculosis at 37, he argued that higher mental functions originate in social life: children first master skills and concepts in interaction with others, then internalise them. His ideas — suppressed in the USSR after his death and published in the West only decades later — became central to developmental psychology and education theory.

Life

Vygotsky was born in Orsha and raised in Gomel, in a Jewish family; his father was a bank manager. Admitted by lottery under the Jewish quota, he studied law at Moscow University while also attending the unofficial Shanyavsky University, pursuing literature, linguistics and philosophy. After the Revolution he taught in Gomel, published The Psychology of Art (written earlier, published 1965), and in 1924 moved to Moscow's Institute of Psychology. There he gathered a circle of collaborators — Alexander Luria and Alexei Leontiev among them — and worked on child development, education of children with disabilities ("defectology"), and the psychology of language. His works were banned in the Soviet Union from 1936 amid the pedology decree and were not republished until 1956.

Key ideas

  • Social origin of mind — every higher function appears twice: first between people (interpsychological), then within the child (intrapsychological).
  • Mediation by signs and tools — language, writing, number systems and other cultural "psychological tools" restructure thought.
  • Zone of proximal development (ZPD) — the gap between what a learner can do unaided and with guidance from a more knowledgeable other; instruction should target the ZPD. The concept underlies scaffolding and much social constructivist teaching.
  • Inner speech — children's self-directed "private speech" becomes silent verbal thought; analysed in his masterpiece Thought and Language (1934).

Reception

Thought and Language appeared in English in 1962 (abridged) and the collection Mind in Society in 1978, during the cognitive revolution, when Vygotsky's social alternative to Piaget's individual constructivism attracted wide attention in the West. He is sometimes called "the Mozart of psychology" (Stephen Toulmin) for the brilliance and unfinished state of his work.
