Mit'a

The mit'a (Quechua for "turn" or "season") was a form of mandatory public service and labor taxation in the Inca Empire. Under the system, communities were required to provide a portion of their able-bodied male population to work on state projects for a set period each year.

History

The mit'a had roots in earlier Andean traditions of reciprocal labor, including the minka and ayni systems of communal work. The Inca state expanded and formalized the practice as it grew into the largest empire in pre-Columbian America.

Laborers provided through the mit'a built roads, bridges, terraces, temples, and fortresses, and also worked in mines and in the army. In return, the state was expected to provide food, drink, and clothing for workers and to support their families during their absence.

Colonial adaptation

After the Spanish conquest, the colonial administration adapted the mit'a into the mita, a far harsher system of forced labor, most notoriously at the silver mines of Potosí and the mercury mines of Huancavelica. The colonial mita was widely criticized for its brutality and high mortality and was a factor in major rebellions, including the uprising of Túpac Amaru II in 1780.
