Nose art

Nose art is decorative painting or design on the fuselage of an aircraft, usually near the nose. Begun for practical identification purposes, it evolved into a means of expressing individuality, boosting morale and evoking memories of home life and peacetime pursuits; it is most famously associated with the military aircraft of the Second World War.

History

Decorative aircraft markings date to the First World War, when Italian and German pilots painted personal insignia, and personalised markings flourished with the elaborate paintwork of German air units. The golden age of nose art came during the Second World War, especially in the United States Army Air Forces, where crews named their bombers and commissioned artwork — frequently pin-up style women inspired by magazines and Alberto Vargas paintings, cartoon characters, and names such as the famous Memphis Belle.

Themes

Common subjects included pin-up girls, girlfriends' and wives' names, cartoon characters, fierce animals and predators' teeth (as on the "Flying Tigers" P-40s and shark-mouthed fighters), mission and victory markings, and humorous or defiant slogans. The art was typically applied by talented members of ground crews or local civilian artists.

Later use and legacy

Nose art continued in the Korean War and Vietnam War, declined under stricter regulations, and has seen periodic revivals, including on aircraft in the Gulf War and in heritage and low-observable-compatible forms today. Surviving panels are preserved in museums, and the art is widely reproduced in books and on scale models; it has become a valued record of the human side of air warfare.
