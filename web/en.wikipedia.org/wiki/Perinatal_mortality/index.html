Perinatal mortality (PNM) is the death of a fetus or newborn around the time of birth. The perinatal period commences at 22 completed weeks (154 days) of gestation — or, in some definitions, 28 weeks — and ends seven completed days after birth. Perinatal mortality is usually expressed as the number of stillbirths plus deaths in the first week of life per 1,000 total births, and is used worldwide as an indicator of the quality of obstetric and newborn care.

Definitions

Definitions vary between countries, complicating international comparison. The World Health Organization recommends reporting stillbirths from 28 weeks of gestation or 1,000 g birth weight for international comparisons, while many high-income countries register stillbirths from 22 or 24 weeks. The perinatal mortality rate combines late fetal deaths with early neonatal deaths (within the first seven days), distinguishing them from later neonatal and infant mortality.

Causes

Major causes include complications of preterm birth, intrapartum-related events (birth asphyxia), infections such as sepsis and pneumonia, congenital anomalies, placental conditions including abruption and placenta previa, and maternal conditions such as pre-eclampsia, diabetes and hypertension. Most perinatal deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, and many are considered preventable with skilled birth attendance, emergency obstetric care, antenatal screening and newborn resuscitation.

Trends

Global rates have fallen substantially since 1990 thanks to improved antenatal care, wider access to facility-based births and caesarean section, and better neonatal care including kangaroo mother care and antibiotics. Stillbirths — about 2 million per year globally at 28 weeks or more — have declined more slowly than child mortality, and reducing them is a target of the Every Newborn Action Plan and the Sustainable Development Goals.
