Stellar designation

In astronomy, a stellar designation is a label used to identify a star. Many stars have several designations in different catalogues. The brightest stars have traditional proper names, many of them of Arabic origin (such as Betelgeuse and Aldebaran), and systematic designations were later introduced as catalogues grew.

Bayer designations

The Bayer designation, introduced by Johann Bayer in his Uranometria of 1603, assigns each star a Greek letter (or, when these run out, a Latin letter) followed by the genitive form of its constellation's Latin name, as in Alpha Centauri or Beta Lyrae. Roughly speaking, the letters were assigned in order of brightness, though many exceptions exist.

Flamsteed designations

Flamsteed designations, from John Flamsteed's Historia Coelestis Britannica (published posthumously in 1725), use a number followed by the constellation genitive (such as 61 Cygni), generally ordered by right ascension within each constellation.

Catalogue designations

Modern catalogues designate stars by catalogue abbreviation and number: examples include the Henry Draper Catalogue (HD), the Hipparcos Catalogue (HIP), the Gliese catalogue of nearby stars (GJ), and the Guide Star Catalog (GSC). Variable stars receive their own designations (such as R Coronae Borealis), and exoplanet-hosting stars are designated by the star's name plus a lowercase letter for the planet, beginning with "b".
