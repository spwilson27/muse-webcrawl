Oechsle scale

The Oechsle scale measures the density of grape must and hence the ripeness and potential alcohol of the wine.

The Oechsle scale is a hydrometer scale used to measure the density of grape must, indicating the sugar content and therefore the ripeness of grapes and the potential alcohol of the resulting wine. It is named after Ferdinand Oechsle (1774–1852), a German physicist, goldsmith, and inventor from Pforzheim who developed the scale in the 1830s.

Definition

Degrees Oechsle (°Oe) express how many grams heavier one litre of must is than one litre of water at 20 °C. Water is 0 °Oe; a must with a density of 1.085 kg/L reads 85 °Oe. As a rough rule, each degree Oechsle corresponds to about 2–2.5 grams of sugar per litre, and roughly 8 °Oe are needed for one percent of alcohol by volume after fermentation.

Use in wine classification

The scale is central to the German and Swiss wine classification systems, where the Prädikat categories of German wine are defined by minimum must weights: Kabinett wines typically start around 70–85 °Oe, Spätlese around 85–95 °Oe, and the richest Trockenbeerenauslese musts may exceed 150 °Oe. Because sugar levels rise with ripeness and noble rot, the Oechsle reading at harvest largely determines the category under which a German wine may be sold.
