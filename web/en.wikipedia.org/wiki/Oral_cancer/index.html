Oral cancer

A cancerous lesion inside the mouth.

Oral cancer, also known as mouth cancer, is a cancer of the lining of the lips, mouth, or upper throat. In the mouth, it most commonly starts as a painless white patch or red patch, and can develop into an ulcer or lump that does not heal. Oral cancer is one of the several types of cancer grouped as head and neck cancers, and is treated similarly to them. The great majority of oral cancers — around 90 percent — are squamous cell carcinomas arising from the oral mucosa.

Signs and symptoms

Early signs may include a sore in the mouth that does not heal within two weeks, red (erythroplakia) or white (leukoplakia) patches, a lump or thickening of the tissues, difficulty chewing or swallowing, numbness of the tongue, loose teeth, and persistent bad breath. As the disease advances, there may be pain, bleeding, and spread to the lymph nodes of the neck.

Causes and risk factors

The principal risk factors are tobacco use (smoked and smokeless) and heavy alcohol consumption, which act synergistically. Infection with human papillomavirus (HPV), particularly HPV-16, is an increasingly important cause, especially of cancers of the oropharynx. Chewing betel quid and areca nut is a major cause in parts of Asia. Prolonged sun exposure is a risk factor for cancer of the lip.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by physical examination and biopsy of suspicious lesions. Imaging such as CT, MRI, or PET scans is used to determine the extent of spread, and the disease is staged using the TNM system.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment typically involves surgery to remove the tumor, often combined with radiation therapy and chemotherapy for advanced disease. Early-stage oral cancer has a much better prognosis; the overall five-year survival rate is around 60–70 percent, but falls sharply when the cancer has metastasized.

Epidemiology

Worldwide, cancers of the lip and oral cavity account for several hundred thousand new cases each year, and are more common in men than women. In 2018 there were an estimated 355,000 new cases and 177,000 deaths globally.
