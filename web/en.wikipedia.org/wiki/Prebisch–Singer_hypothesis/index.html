Prebisch–Singer hypothesis

The Prebisch–Singer hypothesis (also called the Prebisch–Singer thesis) argues that the price of primary commodities declines relative to the price of manufactured goods over the long term, causing the terms of trade of commodity-exporting developing countries to deteriorate. It was proposed independently around 1950 by the Argentine economist Raúl Prebisch, in a study for the UN Economic Commission for Latin America, and by the German-British economist Hans Singer.

Explanation

Several mechanisms were offered. Demand for primary commodities has a low income elasticity (Engel's law), so demand grows more slowly than demand for manufactures as world income rises. Technical progress in manufacturing is captured by producers in higher wages and profits because of oligopolistic markets and unionised labour, whereas in competitive commodity production it is passed on to consumers as lower prices. Singer also emphasised that commodity exports generate few linkages in the exporting economy, while foreign investment in enclave sectors repatriates profits.

Influence and debate

The hypothesis was foundational for development economics and for import substitution industrialisation policies in Latin America and elsewhere, as well as for demands for a New International Economic Order in the 1970s. Empirical studies have found evidence of a long-run relative decline in many commodity prices punctuated by cycles and sharp reversals, though the size, universality, and policy implications of the trend remain debated.
