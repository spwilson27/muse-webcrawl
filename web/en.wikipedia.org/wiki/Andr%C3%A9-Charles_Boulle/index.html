André-Charles Boulle

Died28 February 1732 (aged 89), Paris

André-Charles Boulle (1642–1732) was a French cabinet-maker, the most famous of the ébénistes who worked for Louis XIV at Versailles. He gave his name to Boulle work, a technique of marquetry in tortoiseshell and brass (and sometimes pewter) veneered onto ebony, which produced the glittering furniture characteristic of the Sun King's court.

Career

Boulle was appointed cabinet-maker to the king in 1672 and given workshops in the Louvre, an exceptional privilege. He produced commodes, bureaux, clocks, chandeliers and inlaid floors for Versailles and the royal residences, and many of his pieces were also bought by aristocratic patrons across Europe. The commodes he made for the king's bedchamber at the Grand Trianon in 1708–09 are regarded as masterpieces and virtually created the commode as a form.

Technique and legacy

In Boulle work, patterns were cut simultaneously through stacked sheets of shell and metal so that one sheet's design formed the background of the other, yielding pairs of panels known as première partie and contre partie. Boulle's workshop was continued by his sons, and his style was revived repeatedly, notably in the nineteenth century. Many pieces were damaged by a fire in his workshop in 1720 that destroyed years of accumulated work and collections.
