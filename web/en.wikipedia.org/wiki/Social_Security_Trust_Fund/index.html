Social Security Trust Fund

The Social Security Trust Funds are two United States government accounts — the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) Trust Fund and the Disability Insurance (DI) Trust Fund — that hold the accumulated surpluses of the Social Security program. They are managed by the Social Security Administration and invested by law exclusively in special-issue United States Treasury securities.

Operation

Payroll taxes collected under the Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA) are credited to the funds; when tax income exceeds benefit payments, the surplus is exchanged for interest-bearing Treasury securities, and when benefits exceed income, securities are redeemed. The combined funds held roughly $2.8 trillion in reserves in the early 2020s.

Solvency projections

The Social Security Board of Trustees reports annually on the funds' long-range actuarial status. Because of demographic ageing, annual costs have exceeded non-interest income since 2010, and recent trustees' reports project depletion of the combined reserves in the mid-2030s, at which point continuing payroll taxes would cover roughly three-quarters to four-fifths of scheduled benefits. Proposals to close the shortfall include raising the payroll tax, lifting the taxable earnings cap, adjusting benefit formulas, or raising the retirement age.

Criticism and debate

Economists debate the economic meaning of the trust funds: the securities are real legal obligations of the federal government, but redeeming them requires future taxation, borrowing or spending cuts, so some argue the funds are largely an accounting device, while others stress that they represent genuine prefunding of baby-boomer retirements.
