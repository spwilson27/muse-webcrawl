Brown trout

The brown trout (Salmo trutta) is a European species of salmonid fish that has been widely introduced into suitable environments globally. Native to Europe, North Africa and western Asia, it now lives on every continent except Antarctica, and is one of the world's most popular freshwater game fish — as well as one of its most successful invasive fish species.

Forms

Three forms are traditionally recognised: the riverine form (S. t. fario), the lake form (S. t. lacustris), and the anadromous sea trout (S. t. trutta), which migrates to sea and returns to spawn. Colour varies greatly with environment, from silvery in sea-run fish to golden-brown with black and red spots in stream residents.

Ecology

Brown trout feed on aquatic and terrestrial insects, crustaceans and — as they grow — other fish. They spawn in late autumn and winter, excavating redds in gravel. Introduced brown trout have displaced native fish in New Zealand, Australia, South America, and North America, where they often outcompete the native brook trout in warmer or degraded streams. Record specimens exceed 20 kg; large lake and sea trout called ferox trout inhabit deep oligotrophic lakes of Scotland and Ireland.

Angling

Revered as wily and challenging, the brown trout is the archetypal quarry of fly fishing, celebrated in literature from Izaak Walton onward. Most famous wild fisheries include the chalk streams of southern England, New Zealand's South Island rivers, and Patagonia.
