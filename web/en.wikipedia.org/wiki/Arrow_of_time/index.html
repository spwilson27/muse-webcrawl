ConceptAsymmetry of time
Coined byArthur Eddington (1927)

The arrow of time, also called time's arrow, is the concept positing the "one-way direction" or "asymmetry" of time. It is developed from the idea that, as microscopes and telescopes showed us ever more and ever less, time appears to flow in a single direction: we remember the past but not the future, causes precede effects, and disorder tends to increase. The term was coined by the British astronomer Arthur Eddington in 1927.

The thermodynamic arrow

The best-known arrow is the thermodynamic arrow: the second law of thermodynamics states that the entropy of an isolated system never decreases, so entropy increase defines the future direction of time. This is puzzling because the underlying microscopic laws of physics are (almost) time-symmetric. The standard resolution appeals to the Past Hypothesis: the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state at the Big Bang, so entropy has been increasing ever since.

Other arrows

  • Psychological arrow — our subjective sense of time's flow and of remembering the past.
  • Causal arrow — causes precede their effects.
  • Radiative arrow — electromagnetic waves diverge (retarded solutions) rather than converge.
  • Cosmological arrow — the direction of the universe's expansion.
  • Particle physics (weak) arrow — CP violation in kaon and B-meson decays entails a microscopic T violation, though far too weak to explain the macroscopic arrow.
  • Quantum arrow — the irreversibility associated with measurement and decoherence.

Whether all these arrows must point the same way, and why, remains an active question in the philosophy and foundations of physics, discussed by authors from Boltzmann and Eddington to Huw Price and Sean Carroll.
