Volatility (chemistry)

In chemistry, volatility is a material quality which describes how readily a substance vaporizes. At a given temperature and pressure, a substance with high volatility is more likely to exist as a vapour, while a substance with low volatility is more likely to be a liquid or solid. Volatility can also be applied to the phase changes of liquids and solids.

Description

Volatility is directly related to a substance's vapor pressure: the higher the vapor pressure at a given temperature, the more volatile the substance and the lower its normal boiling point. A liquid with a high vapor pressure at ordinary temperatures — such as diethyl ether or gasoline — is often described simply as "volatile." Volatility is a consequence of intermolecular forces: substances whose molecules attract one another weakly (small, nonpolar molecules) escape into the gas phase more readily than those with strong hydrogen bonding or ionic attractions, such as water and salts.

Measurement and relative volatility

In distillation engineering, relative volatility quantifies how easily two components of a mixture can be separated: it is the ratio of the vapor pressures (or, more precisely, the ratio of the y/x distribution coefficients) of the more volatile component to the less volatile one. A relative volatility near 1 makes separation by ordinary distillation impractical, prompting methods such as azeotropic or extractive distillation.

Applications

Volatility governs the design of distillation columns in petroleum refining, the formulation of fuels (gasoline is blended for seasonal volatility so engines start in winter without vapor-locking in summer), solvent safety (volatile organic compounds, or VOCs, contribute to air pollution and require ventilation), and perfumery, where top notes are the most volatile components of a fragrance.
