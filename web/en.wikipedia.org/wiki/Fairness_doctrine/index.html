Fairness doctrine

The doctrine applied to licensed US broadcasters

The fairness doctrine of the United States Federal Communications Commission (FCC), introduced in 1949, was a policy that required the holders of broadcast licences both to present controversial issues of public importance and to do so in a manner that fairly reflected differing viewpoints. It was built on the premise that broadcast spectrum was scarce and licensees were public trustees.

Components

The doctrine had two basic elements: broadcasters had to devote some airtime to discussing controversial matters of public interest, and they had to afford reasonable opportunity for contrasting viewpoints. Related corollaries included the personal attack rule and the political editorial rule. The Supreme Court upheld the doctrine in Red Lion Broadcasting Co. v. FCC (1969), while in Miami Herald v. Tornillo (1974) it refused to extend a similar right of reply to newspapers.

Repeal and aftermath

The FCC abolished the doctrine in 1987 during the Reagan administration, concluding that it chilled speech and that the growth of media outlets had undermined the scarcity rationale; the decision was upheld in court. Congress passed a bill to codify the doctrine, but President Reagan vetoed it. The personal attack and political editorial corollaries survived until 2000. Periodic proposals to revive the doctrine recur in American politics, while its repeal is frequently credited (or blamed) for the rise of partisan talk radio.

The doctrine is distinct from the equal-time rule (Section 315 of the Communications Act), which concerns airtime for political candidates and remains in force.
