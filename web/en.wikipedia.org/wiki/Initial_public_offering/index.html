An initial public offering (IPO) is the process through which shares of a privately held company are offered to the public for the first time, allowing the company to raise capital from public investors and giving its shares a listing on a stock exchange. The transition from private to public company subjects the firm to ongoing disclosure and regulatory requirements.

Process

The company appoints investment banks as underwriters, files a registration statement and prospectus with the securities regulator (in the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission), and markets the offering to institutional investors in a "roadshow". The offer price is typically set through bookbuilding, in which underwriters collect indications of demand. Underwriters may stabilize the price after listing and exercise an over-allotment ("greenshoe") option. Lock-up agreements usually bar insiders from selling for about 180 days.

Advantages and disadvantages

An IPO raises growth capital, creates liquid currency for acquisitions, lets early investors and employees realize returns, and enhances prestige and creditworthiness. The costs include underwriting fees (often around 7% of proceeds in the U.S.), substantial compliance burdens, loss of privacy and control, and short-term market pressure. IPOs are frequently underpriced on average — shares often rise on the first trading day — a persistent puzzle in financial economics, while long-run IPO returns have historically lagged the market.

Notable IPOs

The largest IPO to date was Saudi Aramco's 2019 listing in Riyadh, which raised about US$25.6 billion (US$29.4 billion including the greenshoe). Alibaba's 2014 New York listing raised US$25 billion. Other landmark offerings include Visa (2008), Facebook (2012), and the wave of technology listings during the dot-com boom, when first-day price doublings were common.

Alternatives

Companies can also go public through a direct listing (no new shares issued, e.g., Spotify in 2018) or by merging with a special-purpose acquisition company (SPAC), a route that surged in 2020–21 and then receded.
