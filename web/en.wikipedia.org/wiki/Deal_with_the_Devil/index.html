Deal with the Devil

A deal with the Devil (also called a Faustian bargain, or Mephistophelian bargain) is a cultural motif exemplified by the legend of Faust and the figure of Mephistopheles, as well as being elemental to many Christian folk stories. According to traditional Christian belief about witchcraft, the pact is between a person and the Devil or another demon, trading a soul for diabolical favours, which vary by the tale, but tend to include such things as youth, knowledge, wealth, fame and power.

It was also believed that some people made this type of pact just as a sign of recognising the Devil as their master, in exchange for nothing. Nevertheless, the bargain is a dangerous one, as the price of the fiend's service is the wagerer's soul. The tale may have a moralising end, with eternal damnation for the foolhardy venturer. Conversely, it may have a comic twist, in which a wily peasant outwits the devil, characteristically on a technical point. The person making the pact sometimes tries to outwit the devil, but loses in the end (e.g., man sells his soul for eternal life because he will never die to pay his end of the bargain. Eventually immortal, he is sentenced to life in prison).

Synopsis

The bargain usually involves the Devil granting favours — commonly youth, knowledge, wealth, fame or power — in exchange for the person's soul, often to be collected after a fixed term of years. A great number of variations and developments upon the theme exist. The adjective Faustian implies variously a person who is ambitious and cutthroat and the moral choices involved.

In folklore and literature

The motif appears in the legend of Theophilus of Adana in the 6th century, regarded as the earliest known pact story, and in the medieval Faustbuch (1587), which Christopher Marlowe dramatised in Doctor Faustus. Goethe's Faust (1808–1832) gave the theme its most famous treatment. Other notable versions include Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray, the short story "The Devil and Daniel Webster" by Stephen Vincent Benét, and the musical Damn Yankees. Crossroads bargains in blues folklore are famously associated with Robert Johnson.

Themes

Scholars have read the motif as a meditation on ambition, the limits of human knowledge, temptation, and the idea that some desires carry a hidden and ruinous price. In Christian theology, such pacts were also alleged against supposed witches and heretics during the early modern witch trials, where confessions of pacts with the Devil were a common feature of prosecutions.
