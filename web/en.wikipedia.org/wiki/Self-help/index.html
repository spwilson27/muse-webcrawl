Self-help

Self-help is a self-guided improvement — economically, intellectually, or emotionally — often with a substantial psychological basis. It encompasses books, groups, courses and media aimed at helping individuals solve personal problems and achieve goals without, or alongside, professional assistance. The term was popularised by Samuel Smiles's book Self-Help (1859), an early bestseller celebrating industry, thrift and perseverance.

History

Advice literature is ancient, from Egyptian wisdom texts to Marcus Aurelius's Meditations. In the 20th century the modern self-help movement grew with Napoleon Hill's Think and Grow Rich (1937), Dale Carnegie's How to Win Friends and Influence People (1936) and Norman Vincent Peale's The Power of Positive Thinking (1952). Twelve-step programmes, beginning with Alcoholics Anonymous (founded 1935), created a durable model of peer-led self-help groups.

Industry

The self-help industry — books, seminars, coaching, apps and online courses — is worth billions of dollars annually. Bibliotherapy, the structured use of reading materials, has some clinical evidence for mild depression and anxiety when based on cognitive-behavioural methods.

Criticism

Critics argue that much self-help offers anecdote rather than evidence, recycles common sense in proprietary jargon, and locates structural problems within individual attitudes. Others defend it as an accessible, low-stigma route to psychological literacy.
