Cholecystokinin

Cholecystokinin (CCK) is a peptide hormone of the gastrointestinal system responsible for stimulating the digestion of fat and protein. CCK, formerly called pancreozymin, is synthesised and secreted by enteroendocrine I-cells in the duodenum, the first segment of the small intestine. Its presence causes the release of digestive enzymes from the pancreas and bile from the gallbladder, and it acts as a hunger suppressant.

History

Evidence that intestinal extracts stimulate gallbladder contraction was reported by Ivy and Oldberg in 1928, who named the responsible hormone cholecystokinin. A pancreatic enzyme-stimulating factor, pancreozymin, was described by Harper and Raper in 1943; the two were later shown to be the same peptide.

Structure and forms

CCK is produced in several molecular forms differing in the number of amino acids (CCK-58, CCK-33, CCK-22 and CCK-8), all derived from a 115-amino-acid precursor. It shares the same five C-terminal amino acids as the related hormone gastrin.

Functions

  • Contraction of the gallbladder and relaxation of the sphincter of Oddi, releasing bile into the duodenum.
  • Stimulation of pancreatic acinar cells to secrete digestive enzymes.
  • Slowing of gastric emptying and suppression of appetite via vagal afferents and central CCK receptors; CCK in the brain is also implicated in anxiety and panic responses.

Clinical significance

Synthetic CCK (sincalide) is used in diagnostic imaging of the gallbladder (HIDA scan) and pancreatic function testing. CCK receptor antagonists have been investigated for panic disorder and gastrointestinal conditions.
