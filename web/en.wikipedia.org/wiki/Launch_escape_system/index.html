TypeCrew safety system for launch vehicles

A launch escape system (LES) or launch abort system (LAS) is a crew safety capability connected to a space capsule that separates the capsule from its launch vehicle in an emergency, carrying the crew to a safe parachute descent. It can be activated automatically or manually at any time from before launch until the system is jettisoned or the spacecraft reaches a regime where a safe abort is possible by other means.

Designs

The classic configuration is a tower of solid rocket motors mounted above the capsule, which pulls the crew module away from a failing booster — used by Mercury, Gemini (via ejection seats in early flights), Apollo, and Soyuz. "Pusher" designs, used by SpaceX's Crew Dragon (SuperDraco engines) and Boeing's Starliner, integrate the abort motors into the spacecraft itself, allowing them to be carried to orbit and used throughout the ascent. Blue Origin's New Shepard likewise uses a pusher-type escape motor.

History

Escape towers were pioneered under Maxime Faget for Project Mercury. The Apollo LES was never needed in flight but was tested in the Little Joe II programme. The only crewed pad rescue by an escape tower occurred on 26 September 1983, when the Soyuz T-10-1 crew was pulled clear seconds before their booster exploded on the launch pad. In 2018 the Soyuz MS-10 crew survived a booster failure during ascent using the launch abort system and capsule separation. The Space Shuttle notably had no full-envelope escape system, a gap criticised in the investigations following the Challenger disaster.
