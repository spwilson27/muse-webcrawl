Compromise of 1877

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Compromise of 1877, also known as the Wormley Agreement or the Bargain of 1877, was an unwritten political deal that resolved the disputed United States presidential election of 1876 and effectively ended the Reconstruction era. Under the arrangement, Democrats conceded the presidency to Republican Rutherford B. Hayes in exchange for the withdrawal of the remaining federal troops from the former Confederate states.

Background

The 1876 election between Hayes and Democrat Samuel J. Tilden ended with disputed electoral votes from Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina (plus one from Oregon). Tilden won the popular vote and appeared to be one electoral vote short of victory, while both parties claimed the disputed states, raising the spectre of a contested inauguration and renewed civil conflict. Congress created an Electoral Commission of fifteen members — five each from the House, Senate, and Supreme Court — which voted 8–7 along party lines to award all the disputed votes to Hayes.

The bargain

In informal negotiations, culminating in meetings at Wormley's Hotel in Washington in February 1877, Southern Democrats agreed to accept Hayes and end their filibuster of the electoral count. In return they obtained commitments that Hayes would withdraw the remaining federal troops supporting Republican governments in South Carolina and Louisiana, appoint at least one Southerner to his cabinet (Postmaster General David M. Key of Tennessee), and support federal aid for Southern internal improvements such as the Texas and Pacific Railroad, along with a promise of "home rule" in the South.

Consequences

Hayes was inaugurated in March 1877 and withdrew the troops in April. The last Reconstruction governments in the South collapsed, and white Democratic "Redeemer" governments consolidated control. In the following decades, Southern states enacted Jim Crow laws, poll taxes, literacy tests and other measures that disenfranchised African American voters, a settlement that endured until the civil rights movement of the 20th century. Historians continue to debate the precise terms and novelty of the bargain — C. Vann Woodward's Reunion and Reaction (1951) is the classic account — but agree on its decisive effect in ending Reconstruction.
