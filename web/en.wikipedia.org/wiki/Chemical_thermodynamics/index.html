Chemical thermodynamics

Chemical thermodynamics is the study of the interrelation of heat and work with chemical reactions or with physical changes of state within the confines of the laws of thermodynamics. Its central questions are whether a reaction will occur spontaneously, how much energy it will absorb or release, and where its equilibrium lies.

Key quantities

The field is built on state functions such as internal energy (U), enthalpy (H = U + PV), entropy (S) and the Gibbs free energy (G = H − TS). At constant temperature and pressure, a process is spontaneous when ΔG is negative, linking thermodynamics directly to the equilibrium constant through ΔG° = −RT ln K. The subject was largely created by Josiah Willard Gibbs in his 1876 paper "On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances," with further development by Gilbert N. Lewis, Merle Randall and others.

Applications

Chemical thermodynamics underlies the calculation of heats of formation and combustion, phase diagrams, battery voltages (through the Nernst equation), and the prediction of reaction feasibility in industrial processes such as the Haber process. It describes only the endpoints of a process, not its rate — which is the province of chemical kinetics.
