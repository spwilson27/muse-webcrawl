Christopher Marlowe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The anonymous portrait in Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, traditionally identified as Christopher Marlowe]
The anonymous portrait in Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, traditionally identified as Christopher Marlowe

Christopher Marlowe (baptised 26 February 1564 – 30 May 1593) was an English playwright, poet and translator of the Elizabethan era, the foremost tragedian of his age before William Shakespeare, whose work he profoundly influenced. His plays — Tamburlaine, Doctor Faustus, The Jew of Malta and Edward II — forged the mighty line of English blank verse and the overreaching tragic hero. He was killed at 29 in murky circumstances, possibly connected to his work as a government spy.

Life

A Canterbury shoemaker's son, Marlowe won scholarships to the King's School and Corpus Christi College, Cambridge (BA 1584, MA 1587 — the degree nearly withheld until the Privy Council certified his services to the Queen, generally read as espionage). In London he shared the theatrical world of the University Wits; arrests, a fatal street fight in which he was acquitted, accusations of atheism and counterfeiting, and the informer Richard Baines's catalogue of his 'damnable opinions' dogged his final years.

Works

Tamburlaine the Great (c. 1587) made his name — the Scythian shepherd conquering empires in thundering verse; Doctor Faustus (c. 1588–92) turned the German chapbook into the defining treatment of the Faust legend; The Jew of Malta's Barabas anticipates Shylock; Edward II pioneered the intimate history play. His narrative poem Hero and Leander (finished by George Chapman) and lyric 'The Passionate Shepherd to His Love' are classics of Elizabethan verse; he also translated Ovid's Amores and Lucan.

Death

On 30 May 1593, at Eleanor Bull's house in Deptford, Marlowe was stabbed above the eye by Ingram Frizer during a quarrel officially over the bill ('the recknynge'), in the company of two other intelligence-connected men; the coroner ruled self-defence. Theories of assassination — by order of the state, over religion, espionage or a love triangle — have never been provable; nor has the fringe claim that he faked death to write Shakespeare's works, for which there is no evidence.
