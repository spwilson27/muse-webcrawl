Regnal number

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A regnal number is the number assigned to a monarch to distinguish them from other rulers of the same name within the same kingdom or empire. Regnal numbers are typically expressed as Roman numerals, placed after the monarch's regnal name — for example, Elizabeth II, Louis XIV, or Benedict XVI. The number is an example of an ordinal number, although in some traditions, such as Swedish, it is derived from chronicles rather than strict succession.

Usage

Regnal numbers came into common use during the Middle Ages as repeating royal names made disambiguation necessary. A monarch who is the first of their name within a realm usually bears no number, though exceptions exist: some monarchs have adopted "I" retrospectively or by custom. Popes have used regnal numbers for many centuries; the practice among secular rulers spread from the late medieval period onwards.

Variations

Numbering practices differ between countries. In England, numbering of monarchs named Edward counts from Edward the Confessor's successors after the Norman Conquest in a way that includes the pre-Conquest kings Edward the Elder, Edward the Martyr and Edward the Confessor in some lists. Japan's emperors are not numbered, while Chinese emperors are usually known by era or temple names rather than numbers. Some monarchs change or select a new regnal name on accession, and the number follows that name's sequence.
