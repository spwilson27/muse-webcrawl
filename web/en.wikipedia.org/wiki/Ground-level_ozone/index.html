Ground-level ozone

Ground-level ozone (O3), also known as surface-level ozone or tropospheric ozone, is a trace gas in the troposphere (the lowest level of the Earth's atmosphere), with a mean concentration near 35 parts per billion. Unlike the stratospheric ozone layer, which shields the Earth from ultraviolet radiation, ground-level ozone is a harmful air pollutant and a component of smog.

Formation

Ground-level ozone is not emitted directly. It forms through photochemical reactions between oxides of nitrogen (NOx) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in the presence of sunlight and heat. Major sources of the precursor pollutants include motor vehicle exhaust, industrial emissions, gasoline vapours, and chemical solvents. Ozone levels therefore tend to peak on hot, sunny afternoons, especially in and downwind of large cities.

Health effects

Breathing ozone can trigger coughing, throat irritation, chest pain, and worsening of bronchitis, emphysema, and asthma; it can inflame and damage the airways and increase susceptibility to respiratory infections. Children, older adults, and people with lung diseases are at greatest risk.

Environmental effects

Ozone damages sensitive vegetation and ecosystems, reduces crop yields (including corn, wheat, and soybeans), and harms forests. It is also a greenhouse gas, the third most important contributor to human-driven radiative forcing after carbon dioxide and methane.
