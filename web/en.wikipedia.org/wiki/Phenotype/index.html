Phenotype

An organism's phenotype is the set of its observable characteristics or traits, including its morphology, development, biochemical or physiological properties, behaviour, and products of behaviour. An organism's phenotype results from two basic factors: the expression of its genetic code (its genotype) and the influence of environmental factors; the two may interact to produce the final phenotype.

Definition and terminology

The distinction between genotype and phenotype was proposed by the Danish botanist Wilhelm Johannsen in 1911 to clarify the difference between an organism's hereditary material and what that material produces. A well-known summary is that "DNA makes RNA, and RNA makes protein": the polypeptides produced fold and become the enzymes and structural proteins whose activity generates the phenotype.

Phenotypic variation

Variation in phenotypes is a prerequisite for evolution by natural selection. Some phenotypic variation is genetic and heritable; other variation results from environmental influences — the range of phenotypes a single genotype can produce across environments is called its norm of reaction. Phenotypic plasticity describes an organism's ability to change its phenotype in response to environmental conditions, as when a water flea grows defensive spines in the presence of predators.

The concept has been extended beyond genes alone: Richard Dawkins proposed the "extended phenotype", in which the effects of a gene reach outside the organism's body, as a beaver's dam or a parasite's manipulation of its host can be considered phenotypic expressions of genes.
