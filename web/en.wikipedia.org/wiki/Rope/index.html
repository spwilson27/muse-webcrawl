Rope

A rope is a group of yarns, plies, fibres or strands that are twisted or braided together into a larger and stronger form. Ropes have tensile strength and so can be used for dragging and lifting. Rope is thicker and stronger than similarly constructed cord, line, string and twine.

Construction

Rope may be laid (twisted) or braided. In traditional laid rope, fibres are twisted into yarns, yarns into strands, and strands into rope, each level twisting in the opposite direction to the one below so the finished rope resists untwisting. Common constructions use three strands. Braided rope interweaves strands in a tubular pattern and may have a braided core (kernmantle construction, standard for climbing ropes) or a hollow braid. Cable-laid rope is made by twisting already-laid ropes together.

Materials

  • Natural fibres: manila, hemp, linen, cotton, coir, jute and sisal — traditional materials, strong but liable to rot.
  • Synthetic fibres: nylon (strong and elastic), polyester (strong, low stretch, UV-resistant), polypropylene (light and floats) and high-performance fibres such as aramids and HMPE (e.g. Dyneema), which are stronger than steel wire of the same diameter.
  • Wire rope is made of steel strands around a fibre or steel core and is used for cranes, elevators and suspension work.

History

Rope is among humanity's oldest tools: impressions of cordage in fired clay date back some 28,000 years, and the ancient Egyptians were making rope from water reeds by about 4000 BC, using special tools. Rope-making was a major industry in maritime towns, carried out in long narrow buildings called ropewalks. Leonardo da Vinci sketched machines for mechanised rope-making.

Use

Ropes are essential in shipping, construction, climbing, rescue, towing and rigging. Their safe use depends on proper knots, splices and terminations, and on respecting a working load limit well below the breaking strength.
