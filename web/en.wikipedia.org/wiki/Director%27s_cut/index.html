Director's cut

A director's cut is an edited version of a film (or video game, television episode, music video, or commercial) that is supposed to represent the director's own approved edit in contrast to the theatrical release. "Cut" refers to the process of film editing. Director's cuts became widely available with the rise of home video, especially laserdisc and DVD.

Origins

The term emerged in the 1970s and 1980s when directors such as Ridley Scott and James Cameron revisited films that studios had altered. Scott's Blade Runner (1982) is a famous case: the studio-imposed voice-over and ending were removed in the 1992 Director's Cut, and again in the 2007 Final Cut, which Scott called definitive.

Notable examples

Well-known director's cuts include Kingdom of Heaven (2005), widely considered dramatically improved by its longer cut; the extended editions of The Lord of the Rings trilogy; and Zack Snyder's Justice League (2021), released after a sustained fan campaign. Other cuts, such as the "Redux" of Apocalypse Now, have divided critics.

Marketing use

The label is sometimes applied for marketing reasons to versions the director had little involvement in, leading some filmmakers and critics to treat the term with skepticism.
