Facies

In geology, a facies is a body of rock with specified characteristics, such as a particular composition, grain size, sedimentary structures, and fossil content, reflecting the environment in which it formed. The concept was introduced by the Swiss geologist Amanz Gressly in the 1830s from studies of the Jura Mountains.

Types

Lithofacies describe physical and chemical rock characteristics; biofacies describe fossil assemblages. A facies may be named for its lithology (sandstone facies), its environment (fluvial facies, turbidite facies), or geographic/type locality.

Walther's law

Walther's law states that facies found in a conformable vertical succession were originally deposited in laterally adjacent environments, allowing geologists to reconstruct ancient environments from rock sequences. Lateral changes of facies within a time-equivalent layer record shifting environments such as a transgressing sea.

Other uses

The term is also used in medicine for characteristic facial appearances associated with conditions (for example, "moon facies"), and in archaeology and architecture, but the geological sense is primary on this page.
