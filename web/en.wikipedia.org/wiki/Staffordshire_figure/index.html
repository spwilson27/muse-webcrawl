Staffordshire figure

A Staffordshire figure is a type of earthenware figure made in the pottery district of Staffordshire, England — the "Potteries" around Stoke-on-Trent — mainly between about 1780 and 1900, with the great mass of production in the Victorian period (roughly 1840–1900). Cheaply made and brightly painted, the figures decorated the mantelpieces of working- and lower-middle-class homes, and are often called mantelpiece dogs in the case of the most famous type, pairs of spaniels — though the subjects ranged across almost every aspect of Victorian life.

History and manufacture

The figures developed out of earlier Staffordshire earthenware traditions: salt-glazed stoneware, agateware, and the creamware and pearlware figures associated with potters such as Ralph Wood the Elder and John Walton, whose pieces were often marked and of finer quality. By the mid-19th century, production had become a mass industry. Figures were formed in piece molds, usually in two halves joined together, then fired and decorated, most characteristically with bright overglaze enamel colors applied by hand, often by child labor. The flat-back figure, modeled in the round only at the front, was designed to sit against a wall on a mantelpiece.

Subjects

The range of subjects is a portrait gallery of Victorian popular culture: royalty (Queen Victoria and Prince Albert, and later Edward VII), politicians and generals, Crimean War and other military heroes, preachers such as John Wesley and Charles Spurgeon, temperance figures, actors and actresses in costume, boxers and cricketers, murderers and their victims, and scenes from literature and the theater. Animal subjects were especially popular: besides the ubiquitous King Charles spaniels (usually in pairs, often with gilt collars and chains), potters produced lions, zebras, giraffes, deer, and sheep. Many figures were copied from prints, and makers rarely paid much attention to copyright or even to spelling in the titles on the bases.

Collecting

Staffordshire figures were produced in such quantity that they were long considered merely quaint; serious collecting and scholarship grew in the 20th century. Fine early examples by named makers such as Walton fetch much higher prices than the mass-produced Victorian portrait figures, and the field is complicated by 20th-century reproductions and fakes. Major collections are held by the Victoria and Albert Museum and the Potteries Museum & Art Gallery in Stoke-on-Trent.
