Pest control

Pest control is the regulation or management of organisms defined as pests — species that damage crops, livestock, property, stored products or human health. Methods range from ancient practices such as trapping and crop rotation to modern chemical pesticides and engineered biological controls, and are applied in agriculture, forestry, public health and buildings.

Methods

Chemical control uses pesticides — insecticides, herbicides, fungicides and rodenticides — which became dominant after the Second World War with synthetic compounds such as DDT, but which raise problems of resistance, residues and harm to non-target species. Biological control deliberately introduces or conserves natural enemies (predators, parasitoids and pathogens) of the pest, from ladybirds against aphids to the cane toad disaster in Australia, a cautionary example of an introduction gone wrong. Cultural and mechanical controls include crop rotation, resistant varieties, sanitation, barriers, traps and sterile-insect releases. Integrated pest management (IPM), formalised from the 1970s, combines these approaches, using monitoring and economic thresholds so that pesticides are applied only when justified.

History and regulation

Farmers have protected crops since antiquity — Sumerians used sulphur compounds and the Chinese used botanical insecticides and encouraged predatory ants. The twentieth century's heavy reliance on broad-spectrum insecticides, and its ecological effects, were publicised by Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962), which spurred environmental regulation of pesticides in many countries, licensing of professional pest-control operators, and the IPM movement. Urban pest control targets rodents, cockroaches, bed bugs, termites and stored-product insects in homes, hospitals and food premises.
