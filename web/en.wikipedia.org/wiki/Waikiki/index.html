Waikīkī

Location: Honolulu, Oʻahu, Hawaiʻi, United States

Known for: Waikiki Beach, surfing, resorts

Landmark: Diamond Head (Lēʻahi)

Waikīkī (Hawaiian: Waikīkī, "spouting fresh water") is a neighbourhood of Honolulu on the south shore of the island of Oʻahu in Hawaiʻi, United States. It is world-famous for Waikiki Beach and its dense strip of high-rise hotels and resorts, set against the volcanic crater of Diamond Head.

History

Before European contact, Waikīkī was a retreat for Hawaiian royalty, watered by streams and fishponds and prized for its surfing breaks. In the 1800s it became the favoured residence and playground of the Hawaiian monarchy. Tourism began in earnest with the opening of the Moana Hotel (now the Moana Surfrider) in 1901, followed by the Royal Hawaiian Hotel in 1927. The Ala Wai Canal, completed in the 1920s, drained the wetlands behind the beach and enabled the district's intensive development. Olympic swimmer and surfer Duke Kahanamoku popularised Waikīkī's surfing culture in the early 20th century, and his statue stands on Kalākaua Avenue.

Geography and tourism

The neighbourhood extends roughly from the Ala Wai Canal to Diamond Head along Kalākaua and Kūhiō avenues. Waikiki Beach is actually a series of adjoining beaches — including Fort DeRussy, Kūhiō and Queen's beaches — much of it maintained with imported sand because of erosion. Attractions include the Waikiki Aquarium (founded 1904), Honolulu Zoo, Kapiʻolani Park and the International Market Place. Waikīkī remains Hawaiʻi's principal visitor district, hosting a large share of the state's tourists, while grappling with sea-level rise, beach erosion and the pressures of tourism on residents.
