Tidal marsh

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tidal marsh (also called a tidal wetland) is a marsh found along rivers, coastlines and estuaries that is flooded and drained by the astronomical tides of the adjacent estuary, sea or ocean. Tidal marshes occur worldwide, mostly at middle to high latitudes in sheltered areas with gentle slopes, where fine sediments accumulate. Because the frequency and duration of flooding vary with elevation, marsh vegetation typically grows in distinct zones of salt- and flood-tolerant plants.

Types

Tidal marshes are divided by the salinity of their flooding water. Salt marshes are flooded by seawater and dominated by salt-tolerant grasses such as Spartina (cordgrass). Brackish marshes occur where fresh and salt water mix, and tidal freshwater marshes lie farther up estuaries, flooded by tides but with fresh water and a more diverse plant community. Marsh surfaces are cut by tidal creeks and dotted with shallow pools called pannes or ponds.

Ecological importance

Tidal marshes are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth. They provide nursery habitat for fish and shellfish, feeding and breeding grounds for birds, natural water filtration, shoreline stabilization, and flood buffering for coastal communities. Their waterlogged, oxygen-poor soils bury carbon for long periods, making them significant "blue carbon" stores. Despite these values, large areas of tidal marsh have been lost to filling, diking and development, and remaining marshes are threatened by accelerating sea-level rise where they cannot migrate inland or accrete sediment fast enough.
