Current account

[Image: an accountant's balance sheet graphic with credits and debits for a nation's external accounts]

The current account is the part of a country's balance of payments recording exports and imports of goods and services, net investment income and net transfers (such as remittances and foreign aid) with the rest of the world. A surplus means the country is a net lender to the world; a deficit, a net borrower.

Interpretation

The current account equals national saving minus Investment — the identity through which economists analyse it: the large US deficits of the 2000s mirrored low household saving and fiscal deficits (the 'twin deficits' hypothesis) funded by surplus countries' purchases of US assets, an interdependence some called Bretton Woods II. Persistent imbalances — China's surplus peaked near 10% of its GDP in 2007; Germany's ran at 7–8% — have driven policy debate and IMF surveillance. Sudden stops of the capital inflows financing deficits caused the Asian crisis of 1997–98 and several emerging-market crises, making the current account a key variable of open-economy macroeconomics alongside the Exchange rate.
