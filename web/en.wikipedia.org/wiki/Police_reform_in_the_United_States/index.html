Police reform in the United States

Police reform in the United States encompasses a series of movements and legislative efforts, from the Progressive Era to the present, aimed at changing the organisation, accountability and practices of American law enforcement. Because policing in the United States is highly decentralised — there are roughly 18,000 federal, state and local law enforcement agencies — reform has proceeded unevenly at local, state and federal levels.

Key themesAccountability, use of force, training, qualified immunity, civilian oversight
Major wavesProgressive Era; 1960s commissions; post-1991; post-2014; post-2020

History

Professionalisation era

Early twentieth-century reformers such as August Vollmer sought to professionalise policing through education, technology and freedom from political patronage. The Wickersham Commission (1929–1931) documented widespread police lawlessness, including the coercive "third degree".

Civil rights era

The Kerner Commission (1968) blamed police practices in part for urban riots, and the Miranda and Mapp Supreme Court decisions imposed constitutional constraints on interrogation and searches. The Knapp Commission (1970s) exposed corruption in New York City.

1990s–2010s

The beating of Rodney King in 1991 led to the Christopher Commission and renewed scrutiny of the LAPD. The 1994 crime bill authorised the Department of Justice to investigate departments for patterns or practices of unconstitutional policing, resulting in consent decrees in cities including Los Angeles, Cincinnati and Baltimore. Body-worn cameras spread widely after 2014.

After 2020

The murder of George Floyd by a Minneapolis police officer in 2020 triggered nationwide protests and a wave of state legislation: bans or restrictions on chokeholds and no-knock warrants, duty-to-intervene rules, and limits on qualified immunity in states such as Colorado and New Mexico. The proposed George Floyd Justice in Policing Act passed the House of Representatives but stalled in the Senate. Some jurisdictions pursued "defund" or alternative-response programmes, sending mental-health professionals rather than officers to certain calls; several later reversed budget cuts amid rising crime concerns.

Principal reform proposals

  • National use-of-force standards and databases of officer misconduct
  • Reforming or abolishing qualified immunity
  • Civilian review boards with subpoena power
  • Changes to police union contracts and arbitration of discipline
  • De-escalation and procedural-justice training
  • Alternative response models for mental-health crises
