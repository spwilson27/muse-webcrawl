Identification key

In biology, an identification key, also known as a dichotomous key, is a printed or computer-aided device that enables the identification of biological organisms — such as plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, and viruses — by presenting a sequence of choices between pairs of contrasting characteristics. By successively selecting the statement that matches the specimen, the user narrows the possibilities until a name is reached.

Structure

Each step of a dichotomous key consists of a pair of statements called leads, forming a couplet. Each lead points either to another couplet or to the name of a taxon. Keys may be presented in bracketed format (both leads together, with cross-references) or indented format (leads nested by level). Simple keys are used in field guides for amateur naturalists, while exhaustive keys for specialists may distinguish thousands of taxa using microscopic or chemical characters.

History

The dichotomous method is commonly credited to Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, whose Flore française (1778) presented plants through analytical tables, building on earlier hierarchical devices. Dichotomous keys became the standard identification tool in taxonomy throughout the 19th and 20th centuries.

Variants

  • Single-access (sequential) keys — the classic dichotomous format, used in order from a fixed entry point.
  • Multi-access keys — the user chooses which characters to evaluate and in what order; suited to computer implementation, as in DELTA and Lucid software.
  • Synoptic keys — list all taxa exhibiting each character state, allowing comparison at a glance.

Limitations

Keys work only for the taxa and conditions they include; broken specimens, intermediate or atypical individuals, and immature stages can mislead. Modern identification increasingly supplements keys with DNA barcoding and image-recognition tools, but keys remain fundamental to field guides and taxonomy teaching.
