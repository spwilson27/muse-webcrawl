Schubert calculus

A classical enumerative problem: lines meeting four given lines

Schubert calculus is a branch of algebraic geometry concerned with enumerative problems — counting the number of geometric objects (such as lines, planes, or subspaces) satisfying a set of incidence conditions. It is named after Hermann Schubert, whose 1879 book Kalkül der abzählenden Geometrie systematised the subject.

Classical problems

A famous example asks how many lines in three-dimensional space meet four given lines in general position; the answer is two. Another asks for the number of lines tangent to given surfaces or lying on a cubic surface — Cayley and Salmon proved that a smooth cubic surface contains exactly 27 lines. Schubert's "principle of conservation of number" justified computing such counts by specialising the configuration, but the arguments lacked rigour; making them rigorous was the subject of Hilbert's fifteenth problem.

Modern formulation

In modern terms, Schubert calculus is the study of the cohomology (or Chow) ring of Grassmannians and flag varieties. Subspaces satisfying incidence conditions with a fixed flag define Schubert varieties (and their cohomology classes, Schubert classes); the number of solutions to an enumerative problem is computed by multiplying the corresponding classes and extracting the coefficient of the class of a point. Intersection products of Schubert classes on the Grassmannian are governed by the Littlewood–Richardson rule, connecting the subject to representation theory and combinatorics.
