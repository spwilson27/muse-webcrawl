The elk (Cervus canadensis), also known as the wapiti, is the second-largest species within the deer family (Cervidae) — smaller only than the moose — and one of the largest land mammals in its native ranges of North America, Central Asia and East Asia. (In British English, "elk" instead refers to the moose, Alces alces.) Elk are closely related to the red deer of Europe and were long classified as a subspecies of it.

Description

Bulls stand about 1.5 metres (4 ft 11 in) at the shoulder and weigh 320–330 kilograms on average, with large branching antlers — shed and regrown annually — that may exceed a metre in length. The coat is reddish-brown in summer and darker in winter, with a distinctive pale rump patch. During the autumn rut, bulls advertise themselves with a high-pitched, whistling call known as bugling, and compete for harems of cows.

Distribution and behaviour

Elk inhabit forest, forest-edge and alpine meadow habitats, grazing on grasses, sedges and forbs in summer and browsing shrubs and bark in winter. They are highly social, forming herds that may number in the hundreds, and many populations migrate seasonally between high summer ranges and sheltered winter valleys. Wolves, bears, cougars and coyotes prey on them, especially calves and the old.

History and conservation

Once numbering perhaps ten million across North America, elk were reduced by market hunting to fewer than 100,000 by the early twentieth century; reintroductions and regulated hunting restored them to well over a million, including famous herds in Yellowstone and Rocky Mountain national parks, and restored populations in the eastern United States. Chronic wasting disease is an emerging threat in some regions.
