Motion sickness

Motion sickness occurs in response to real or perceived motion and is characterized by nausea, vomiting, dizziness, cold sweats, headache, and fatigue. Common forms include car sickness, seasickness, and airsickness; visually induced motion sickness can also occur with simulators, virtual reality, and some films.

Causes

The most widely accepted explanation is the sensory conflict theory: symptoms arise when signals from the vestibular system of the inner ear conflict with visual input and with the brain's expectations based on past experience. For example, reading in a moving car presents a stationary visual scene while the vestibular system senses motion. An alternative evolutionary account proposes that the brain misinterprets the sensory mismatch as a neurotoxin-induced disturbance and triggers vomiting defensively.

Symptoms and susceptibility

Symptoms typically progress from unease and yawning to nausea and, in severe cases, repeated vomiting. Susceptibility varies widely; it is low in young children, peaks around ages 9–10, and often declines with age and repeated exposure (habituation). Migraine sufferers and women — particularly during hormonal changes — report higher susceptibility.

Treatment and prevention

Behavioral measures include facing the direction of travel, watching the horizon, obtaining fresh air, and avoiding reading. Medications include antihistamines such as dimenhydrinate and meclizine, and the anticholinergic scopolamine, which is considered highly effective but can cause drowsiness and dry mouth. Ginger is sometimes used, with mixed evidence.
