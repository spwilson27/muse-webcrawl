Samuel

Samuel is a figure who, in the narratives of the Hebrew Bible, plays a key role in the transition from the period of the biblical judges to the institution of a kingdom under Saul, and again in the transition from Saul to David. He is venerated as a prophet in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. In addition to his role in the Hebrew Bible, Samuel is mentioned in the New Testament, in rabbinical literature, and in the second chapter of the Qur'an (although not by name).

Sources

Samuel's life and deeds are described in the Books of Samuel, where he appears as the last of the judges, the anointer of the first two Israelite kings, and a prophet of national importance. The narrative of his birth (1 Samuel 1) presents his mother Hannah dedicating him to the sanctuary at Shiloh, where he serves under the priest Eli.

Role in the narrative

Birth and calling

According to the text, Samuel was born to the barren Hannah in answer to prayer and was left at Shiloh to serve in the sanctuary. As a boy he receives a divine call in the night, announcing judgment on the house of Eli. He grows to be recognized throughout Israel as a prophet of Yahweh.

Judge and kingmaker

As an adult Samuel leads Israel against the Philistines, intercedes for the people at Mizpah, and circuits among the towns as a judge. When the elders demand a king, Samuel reluctantly anoints Saul son of Kish. After Saul's failures, Samuel is sent to Bethlehem to anoint David, the youngest son of Jesse, though he continues to mourn for Saul until his death at Ramah. In a famous episode after his death, the Witch of Endor summons his spirit for Saul on the eve of the battle at Mount Gilboa.

Perspectives

Historicity

Scholars debate the historicity of Samuel's career and the sources underlying the Books of Samuel, which appear to weave together pro-monarchic and anti-monarchic traditions. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Samuel (notably 4QSama) that sometimes differ from the Masoretic Text.

Religious views

In Jewish tradition Samuel is counted among the greatest leaders of Israel; rabbinic literature ranks him alongside Moses and Aaron in some respects. Christians regard him as a prophet and judge who prefigures themes of kingship. In Islam, he is generally identified with the unnamed prophet mentioned in the Qur'an who appoints Talut (Saul) as king.
