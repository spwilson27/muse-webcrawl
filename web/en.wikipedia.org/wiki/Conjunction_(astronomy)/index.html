Conjunction (astronomy)

[Image placeholder: planetary conjunction at dusk]

In astronomy, a conjunction occurs when two astronomical objects or spacecraft have either the same right ascension or the same ecliptic longitude, usually as observed from Earth. To a casual observer the two bodies appear close together in the sky. The term is most often used for configurations involving the Sun, Moon and planets.

Types

A superior planet (one outside Earth's orbit) can only be in conjunction with the Sun; an inferior planet (Mercury or Venus) can be in inferior conjunction, between Earth and the Sun — during which a transit may occur if the alignment is exact — or superior conjunction, on the far side of the Sun. When two planets appear very close together it is often called an appulse, and an especially close pairing of bright planets, such as the Jupiter–Saturn "great conjunction" of 21 December 2020 (separation 0.1°), attracts wide public attention. Conjunction with the Sun is also when a planet is lost in the Sun's glare and effectively unobservable.

Conjunctions and occultations

If one body actually passes in front of another as seen from Earth, the event is more precisely an occultation (or a transit for a smaller body crossing a larger one, such as Venus crossing the Sun). Exact conjunctions of the Moon with planets or bright stars are used in traditional calendars and navigation.
