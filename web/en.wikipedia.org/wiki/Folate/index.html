Folate

Folate (vitamin B9)
ClassB vitamin
Synthetic formFolic acid
Main sourcesLeafy green vegetables, legumes, liver, fortified grains
DeficiencyMegaloblastic anaemia; neural tube defects in pregnancy

Folate, also known as vitamin B9 and folacin, is one of the B vitamins. Manufactured folic acid, which is converted into folate by the body, is used as a dietary supplement and in food fortification as it is more stable during processing and storage. Folate is required for the body to make DNA and RNA and to metabolise amino acids necessary for cell division and maturation of blood cells.

As the human body cannot make folate, it is required in the diet, making it an essential nutrient. It occurs naturally in many foods. The recommended adult daily intake varies by country; many governments mandate fortification of flour with folic acid.

Health effects

Deficiency

Folate deficiency can lead to many health problems, most notably neural tube defects in developing embryos (such as spina bifida and anencephaly) and megaloblastic anaemia in adults. Women of childbearing age are widely advised to take folic acid supplements before conception and during early pregnancy, and fortification programmes have substantially reduced neural tube defect rates where implemented.

Biochemistry

Folate coenzymes, principally tetrahydrofolate, carry and transfer one-carbon units in reactions central to the synthesis of purines and thymidylate, and to the remethylation of homocysteine to methionine. Its metabolism is closely linked with vitamin B12; B12 deficiency can produce a functional folate deficiency (the "methyl trap"). Antifolate drugs such as methotrexate exploit this pathway in cancer chemotherapy and immunosuppression.

History

Research in the 1930s by Lucy Wills identified a dietary factor in yeast that corrected macrocytic anaemia of pregnancy; the factor was isolated from spinach leaves in the 1940s and named from the Latin folium (leaf).
