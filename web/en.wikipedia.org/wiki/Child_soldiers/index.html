Child soldiers

Child soldiers are children — persons under the age of 18 — associated with armed forces or armed groups, whether as fighters, porters, cooks, messengers, spies, or for sexual purposes. Tens of thousands of children have been recruited and used in conflicts around the world, in violation of international law. The 2007 Paris Principles define the term broadly as "children associated with an armed force or armed group".

Recruitment

Children are recruited by abduction and force — as by Joseph Kony's Lord's Resistance Army in Uganda — but also through poverty, displacement, ideology, revenge and the search for protection or belonging. Girls constitute a substantial minority of children in armed groups and face particular risks of sexual violence; they are often overlooked in release programmes.

International law

The Convention on the Rights of the Child and its Optional Protocol on children in armed conflict (2000) set 18 as the standard for compulsory recruitment and require states to take all feasible measures to keep under-18s from direct hostilities. The Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court makes conscripting or using children under 15 a war crime; Congolese warlord Thomas Lubanga became the ICC's first convicted person in 2012 on such charges. The UN Secretary-General's annual "list of shame" names parties that recruit children.

Release and reintegration

Disarmament, demobilisation and reintegration (DDR) programmes provide former child soldiers with medical care, psychosocial support, education and family reunification. Long-term consequences can include trauma, stigma and interrupted schooling; reintegration is widely considered a decades-long process rather than a single event.
