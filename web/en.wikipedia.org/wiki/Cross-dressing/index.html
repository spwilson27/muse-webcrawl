Cross-dressing is the act of wearing clothes traditionally or stereotypically associated with a different gender. From as early as the European Middle Ages, cross-dressing has been practiced in various forms and for various purposes: as theatrical convention, as disguise for safety or access, as part of religious and carnival ritual, and in modern times as fashion or personal expression.

Cross-dressing is a behavior, not an identity: it does not by itself indicate that a person is transgender, though the histories overlap. The older clinical term "transvestism" was coined by Magnus Hirschfeld in 1910, and older still, Joan of Arc was famously tried in part for wearing men's clothing.

Terminology

The phenomenon was discussed in ancient times — the Hebrew Bible's Deuteronomy 22:5 prohibits a man from wearing women's apparel and vice versa, one of the earliest written references. Hirschfeld coined "transvestite" in his 1910 work Die Transvestiten; the word "cross-dresser" later became preferred by many communities as a neutral, descriptive term. "Drag" refers specifically to cross-dressing as exaggerated theatrical entertainment, including drag queens and drag kings.

History

Stories of cross-dressing appear in mythology and folklore worldwide: the Norse god Thor disguised as a bride in the Þrymskviða, Chinese heroine Hua Mulan serving in her father's place, and Greek myths of Achilles hidden among women. Throughout the 17th to 19th centuries, numerous women lived and worked as men — as soldiers, sailors, and pirates — either from ambition, economic necessity, or personal inclination; famous cases include Hannah Snell and the pirates Anne Bonny and Mary Read. In Tokugawa Japan, the onnagata tradition formalized male actors' portrayal of women in kabuki.

In theatre and performance

In Shakespeare's England, women were barred from the stage, so female roles were played by boys and young men. Comic stage traditions — British pantomime with its dame and principal boy, music hall, and modern drag performance — made cross-dressing a mainstream entertainment staple. In film and television, cross-dressing has appeared in comedies from Some Like It Hot (1959) to Mrs. Doubtfire (1993), as well as in dramatic portrayals.

Motives and contexts

People cross-dress for many reasons: entertainment and artistry, religious festivals and carnival inversions of hierarchy, espionage and wartime disguise, access to male-only occupations historically, and personal comfort, play, or expression. In the 20th century, women's adoption of trousers moved from transgression to mainstream fashion, illustrating how gendered clothing norms change over time.

Legal and social history

Many jurisdictions once enforced sumptuary laws and anti-cross-dressing ordinances; police raids on gay bars frequently invoked such laws, a grievance in the background of the 1969 Stonewall riots. Most such laws were repealed or invalidated during the later 20th century. Attitudes today vary widely across cultures.
