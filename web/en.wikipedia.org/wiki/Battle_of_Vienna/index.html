The Battle of Vienna took place on 12 September 1683, when the Ottoman army that had besieged Vienna for two months was routed by a combined Imperial and Polish-Lithuanian relief force under the overall command of King John III Sobieski of Poland. It is one of the most famous turning points of early modern Europe: after Vienna, the Ottomans never again seriously threatened central Europe, and the Habsburgs began the reconquest of Hungary.

The siege

Grand Vizier Kara Mustafa led an army of well over 100,000 against Vienna in July 1683. The city's garrison of some 15,000 under Ernst Rüdiger von Starhemberg held the walls against mining and assault while plague and dwindling supplies gnawed at both sides. By early September the defenders were at the end of their strength when the relief army — Imperial troops under Charles of Lorraine and Sobieski's Poles — assembled on the Kahlenberg heights above the city.

The battle

On 12 September the allies descended from the hills in a day-long battle. In the late afternoon Sobieski led some 18,000 cavalry — including 3,000 Polish winged hussars — in one of the largest cavalry charges in history, which broke the Ottoman army and overran its camp. Kara Mustafa fled, leaving his great tent and treasury; he was executed at Belgrade on the sultan's orders that Christmas.

Aftermath

The Holy League pressed its advantage in the Great Turkish War: Buda was retaken in 1686, Hungary recovered, and by the Treaty of Karlowitz (1699) the Ottoman Empire ceded most of Hungary and Transylvania. Viennese legend credits the siege with the croissant and with the city's coffee-house culture, founded — so the story goes — on sacks of coffee beans abandoned in the Ottoman camp.
