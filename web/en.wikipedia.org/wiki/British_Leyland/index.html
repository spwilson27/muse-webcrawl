British Leyland was a British automotive conglomerate formed in 1968 by the merger of British Motor Holdings and the Leyland Motor Corporation. At its peak it was one of the largest car manufacturers in Europe, uniting almost the entire British-owned motor industry — Austin, Morris, Jaguar, Rover, Triumph, MG, Land Rover and the Mini — under a single company. It became a byword for industrial trouble in 1970s Britain and was nationalised in 1975, before its remnants were broken up and sold in the 1980s.

Formation

The merger brought together two groups that were each already the product of earlier consolidations: British Motor Holdings had itself been formed in 1966 from the British Motor Corporation and Jaguar, while Leyland owned Triumph, Rover and the commercial-vehicle brands. The government encouraged the merger as a way of creating a British champion large enough to compete with American and European giants.

Decline

British Leyland was beset by poor management, duplication across plants, chronic strikes and quality problems. The Ryder Report of 1975 led to effective nationalisation, with the government taking control through the National Enterprise Board. The company was renamed BL in 1978, and the 1980s saw the closure of plants at Speke, the sale of Jaguar in 1984 and the disposal of the truck businesses. The car division became the Rover Group in 1986 and was sold to British Aerospace in 1988; BMW later bought it in 1994, selling the Rover marque on in 2000. Its legacy continues through the Mini, Land Rover and MG brands under other owners.

Products

British Leyland produced many of Britain's best-known cars of the era, including the Morris Marina, Austin Allegro, Triumph TR7, Rover SD1, Jaguar XJ and the Mini, which continued in production throughout the period. Its commercial arm built the Leyland National bus and a wide range of trucks.
