Restorative justice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Restorative justice is an approach to justice in which one response to a crime is to organise a meeting between the victim and the offender, sometimes with representatives of the wider community, to repair the harm caused. Rather than asking only what law was broken and what punishment is deserved, it asks who was harmed, what they need, and whose obligation it is to meet those needs — with offenders encouraged to acknowledge wrongdoing and make amends, and victims given voice and restitution.

Origins and practice

Its modern movement dates from 1970s experiments (victim–offender reconciliation in Kitchener, Ontario, 1974) and Howard Zehr's Changing Lenses (1990), drawing on indigenous practices (Māori family group conferences, institutionalised in New Zealand youth justice in 1989; First Nations sentencing circles in Canada) and on religious peace traditions. Formats range from victim–offender mediation and conferencing to truth commissions at societal scale (South Africa's TRC trading amnesty for truth). Evidence reviews find high participant satisfaction and modest recidivism reductions; critics raise concerns about coercion of apologies, power imbalances (especially in domestic violence), net-widening and justice's consistency — debates that shape where restorative programmes sit relative to courts (diversion, sentencing input, post-sentence).
