Protozoa

Protozoa (sg. protozoan) are a group of single-celled eukaryotes, either free-living or parasitic, that feed on organic matter such as other microorganisms or organic tissues and debris. Historically, protozoa were regarded as "one-celled animals", as they often possess animal-like behaviours such as motility and predation, and lack a cell wall, unlike algae and fungi. The term was coined by Georg August Goldfuss in 1818.

Classification

When first introduced, Protozoa was ranked as a phylum or subkingdom. Modern molecular phylogenetics has shown that the old grouping is paraphyletic: the organisms once lumped together are scattered across many distinct branches of the eukaryotic tree, including the Amoebozoa, Excavata, Alveolata (ciliates, dinoflagellates and apicomplexans), Rhizaria and others. Despite this, "protozoa" remains a useful informal term for heterotrophic, motile protists. Tens of thousands of species have been described.

Characteristics

Protozoa range in size from about 1 micrometre to several millimetres. They move by cilia, flagella, or pseudopodia, take in food by phagocytosis, and reproduce mainly by binary or multiple fission, though some have complex life cycles with sexual stages. Many form resistant cysts that survive harsh conditions and aid transmission between hosts.

Ecology and disease

Free-living protozoa are major consumers of bacteria in soils and waters and are central to microbial food webs; foraminifera and radiolarians contribute vast shell deposits to ocean sediments. Parasitic protozoa cause some of the most important human diseases, including malaria (Plasmodium), sleeping sickness (Trypanosoma brucei), Chagas disease, amoebic dysentery (Entamoeba histolytica), giardiasis, leishmaniasis and toxoplasmosis.

History of study

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek observed free-living protozoa in the 1670s, calling them "animalcules". Through the 19th and early 20th centuries they were a central subject of microscopy and cell biology; the discovery that malaria is transmitted by mosquitoes, involving the parasite's complex protozoan life cycle, won Ronald Ross the Nobel Prize in 1902.
