Labour movement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The labour movement is the collective organisation of working people to campaign for better working conditions, wages and political rights. It encompasses trade unions, labour and social-democratic political parties, cooperatives and friendly societies, and the campaigns — strikes, demonstrations, lobbying — through which they act. Its historical achievements include the eight-hour day, the abolition of child labour in industrial countries, workplace safety law, social insurance and universal suffrage in many states.

The movement grew out of industrialisation in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, as factory workers organised despite legal prohibition (Britain's Combination Acts, the French Le Chapelier Law). The founding of the International Workingmen's Association (the First International) in 1864 linked national movements; the Second International (1889) coordinated socialist and labour parties and popularised May Day. In the twentieth century the movement divided between revolutionary communist and reformist social-democratic wings, a split hardened by the Russian Revolution of 1917.

Decline and adaptation

Union density has fallen across most advanced economies since the 1970s, under pressure from deindustrialisation, globalisation, service-sector growth and legal changes; the gig economy and platform work pose new organisational problems. Labour parties have often broadened their appeal beyond manual workers, adopting Third Way programmes in the 1990s. The movement remains a major political force in many countries and continues to campaign on minimum wages, working time and job security.
