Ligand

In coordination chemistry, a ligand is an ion or molecule with a functional group that binds to a central metal atom to form a coordination complex. The bonding typically involves formal donation of one or more of the ligand's electron pairs, though metal–ligand bonds range from highly ionic to covalent in character. The term comes from the Latin ligare, "to bind".

Classification

Ligands are classified by denticity — the number of donor atoms through which they bind a single metal centre:

  • Monodentate ligands bind through one atom: water, ammonia, chloride, carbon monoxide
  • Bidentate ligands bind through two atoms: ethylenediamine, oxalate, bipyridine
  • Polydentate (chelating) ligands bind through several atoms and form ring structures called chelates; EDTA is hexadentate

Ligands are also grouped by the type of bond they form (the CBC — covalent bond classification — method distinguishes L-type two-electron donors, X-type one-electron donors, and Z-type acceptors).

Spectrochemical series

Ligands differ in the strength of the ligand field they produce, ordered in the spectrochemical series from weak-field ligands such as iodide to strong-field ligands such as cyanide and carbon monoxide. Field strength determines the spin state and colour of transition-metal complexes, a relationship explained by crystal field theory and ligand field theory.

Biochemistry

In biochemistry, "ligand" has a broader meaning: any molecule that binds to a target, such as a receptor, enzyme or transport protein, to form a complex and trigger or modify a biological response. Examples include oxygen binding to haemoglobin (via the iron centre of haem), hormones binding to receptors, and substrates binding to enzymes.
