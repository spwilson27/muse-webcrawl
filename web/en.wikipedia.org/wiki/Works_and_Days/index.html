Works and Days (Ancient Greek: Ἔργα καὶ Ἡμέραι, Erga kai Hēmerai) is a didactic poem of some 828 lines written by the Greek poet Hesiod around 700 BC. It is addressed to Hesiod's brother Perses and combines practical instruction on farming and seafaring with myths, fables and moral exhortation on the necessity of honest labour.

Content

The poem opens with an invocation of Zeus and the Muses and a reflection on the two kinds of Strife (eris) in the world — one destructive, the other a wholesome spirit of competition that stirs men to work. Hesiod relates that he and his brother Perses fell into dispute over their inheritance, and that Perses, having bribed the local "gift-devouring" lords, won more than his share; the poem urges Perses to abandon idleness and litigation and earn his living by labour instead.

Myths

Two famous myths explain why human life is hard. In the story of Prometheus and Pandora, mankind once lived free of toil and disease, until Pandora opened a jar (later mistranslated as a "box") releasing all evils into the world, with only Hope remaining inside. The second is the Myth of the Five Ages: Hesiod describes a succession of races of men — Gold, Silver, Bronze, the Heroic age, and the present Iron Age — declining in justice and happiness, his own iron generation condemned to ceaseless labour and sorrow.

Practical instruction

The "Works" portion gives a farmer's calendar keyed to the risings and settings of stars such as the Pleiades: when to plough, sow, prune vines, harvest and thresh; how to build a plough and choose oxen; and advice on marriage, neighbours, sacrifice and avoiding idleness. The poem also contains one of the earliest Greek "fables" — the hawk and the nightingale — and a celebrated description of winter in Boeotia. The concluding "Days" section is a calendar of lucky and unlucky days of the month for various tasks, probably a later addition or a distinct traditional appendix.

Significance

Together with the Theogony, the Works and Days is one of the two surviving complete poems of Hesiod and a foundational text of archaic Greek literature. It is a key source for Greek mythology, early Greek attitudes to justice (dikē) and work, and the agricultural year of early Greece.
