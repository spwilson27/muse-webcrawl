FieldGenocide studies

Genocides in history is a survey of events, from antiquity to the present, that have been characterised as genocide — the deliberate destruction, in whole or in part, of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. The word was coined by the Polish lawyer Raphael Lemkin in 1944 and given legal form in the United Nations Genocide Convention of 1948.

Memorials to genocide victims stand in many countries.

Definition and debate

Scholars dispute which historical events qualify. Lemkin himself applied the concept broadly to colonial violence, while others restrict it to cases meeting the Convention's intent standard. Debated cases include the destruction of Carthage, the Mongol conquests, the European colonisation of the Americas, and the treatment of Indigenous peoples in Australia and elsewhere, where demographic collapse from disease interacted with violent dispossession.

Twentieth century

The twentieth century saw the events that defined modern understanding of the crime: the Herero and Nama genocide in German South West Africa (1904–1908), often called the first genocide of the century; the Armenian genocide in the Ottoman Empire (from 1915), together with massacres of Assyrians and Greeks; the Holodomor in Soviet Ukraine (1932–33), recognised as genocide by many states; the Holocaust, in which Nazi Germany murdered about six million Jews, alongside the genocide of the Roma and other victim groups; the Cambodian genocide under the Khmer Rouge (1975–1979); the Rwandan genocide of 1994, in which some 800,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu were killed in about one hundred days; and the Bosnian genocide, including the Srebrenica massacre of 1995, adjudicated by international tribunals.

Twenty-first century

International courts and governments have applied the term to the Darfur conflict, the Islamic State's campaign against the Yazidis (recognised as genocide by the UN and several states from 2016), and the persecution of the Rohingya in Myanmar, which is the subject of proceedings at the International Court of Justice. Prevention and timely recognition remain central concerns of genocide studies.
