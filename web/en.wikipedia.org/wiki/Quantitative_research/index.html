Quantitative research

Quantitative research is the systematic investigation of phenomena by gathering quantifiable data and performing statistical, mathematical or computational techniques. It aims to develop and employ mathematical models, theories and hypotheses, and to measure variables in a way that supports generalisation from a sample to a wider population. It is contrasted with qualitative research, which explores meaning and experience in context.

Quantitative research expresses observations numerically and analyses them statistically.

Approach

Quantitative research typically follows a structured, deductive cycle: deriving hypotheses from theory, operationalising concepts into measurable variables, collecting data with standardised instruments, and testing relationships with statistics. Measurement is fundamental: variables are assigned numerical values at nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio levels, enabling the computation of descriptive statistics, correlations and effect sizes, and inferential tests of significance.

Methods

Common designs include experiments and quasi-experiments, which manipulate an independent variable to infer causation; cross-sectional and longitudinal surveys using questionnaires; correlational and observational studies of recorded data; and secondary analysis of administrative or census data. Sampling theory and statistical power analysis are used to decide how much data to gather so that results can be generalised with known precision.

Fields and criticism

Quantitative methods dominate the natural sciences and are extensively used in psychology, economics, sociology, political science, education and public health. Critics note that quantification can strip context, embed contested assumptions in measures, and confer a false sense of objectivity; many researchers therefore advocate mixed methods that combine quantitative and qualitative evidence. Concerns about publication bias and questionable research practices have driven reforms such as preregistration and open data.
