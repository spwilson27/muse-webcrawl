Limited equity housing cooperative

A limited equity housing cooperative (LEHC) is a type of housing cooperative in the United States in which residents own shares in a corporation that owns the property, but are restricted by the cooperative's bylaws from selling their shares at market value. The resale price is limited by a formula fixed in advance, in order to keep the housing affordable to low- and moderate-income households in perpetuity.

Structure

As in any housing cooperative, members purchase a share that entitles them to occupy a unit and pay monthly carrying charges covering the building's mortgage, taxes, and maintenance. In a limited equity co-op, the bylaws cap the resale price of the share — often at the original purchase price plus a modest index-based adjustment or a share of principal paid down — so departing members cannot capture speculative gains.

History

The model grew out of union-sponsored cooperative housing in New York City in the early 20th century, including the Amalgamated Housing Cooperative (1927). From the 1950s, New York's Mitchell-Lama program created many limited-equity co-ops. Elsewhere, tenant groups used LEHCs to buy and preserve buildings threatened by gentrification or abandonment, frequently with support from community land trusts and public subsidies whose affordability restrictions the equity limits help enforce.

Advantages and criticisms

Supporters argue the model preserves long-term affordability, gives residents democratic control, and prevents displacement. Critics note that capped equity limits wealth-building for owners, can complicate financing and refinancing, and may leave cooperatives undercapitalized for major repairs if charges are kept too low.
