Xenophanes of Colophon (c. 570 – c. 478 BC) was a Greek philosopher, theologian, poet, and critic of Homeric religion. Born in Ionia, he travelled widely through the Greek world as a wandering poet. Only fragments of his verse survive, quoted by later authors, but they had a lasting influence on Greek thought.

Critique of anthropomorphism

Xenophanes famously attacked the anthropomorphic gods of Homer and Hesiod, who attributed to the gods "all things that are shameful and a reproach among mankind: theft, adultery, and mutual deception." He observed that different peoples picture gods in their own likeness: Ethiopians imagine snub-nosed, black gods, Thracians blue-eyed and red-haired ones — and if cattle and horses could draw, they would draw gods like cattle and horses. Against this, Xenophanes taught that there is "one god, greatest among gods and men, in no way similar to mortals either in body or in thought," who sees, thinks, and hears as a whole and moves all things by thought without toil.

Epistemology and nature

Xenophanes also expressed a strikingly modern fallibilism: no man has seen the clear truth about the gods and the universe; even if someone chanced to speak the whole truth, he would not know it — opinion is allotted to all. He offered naturalistic explanations of phenomena others ascribed to gods, such as rainbows, and reasoned from fossil shells found inland and imprints of sea creatures in stone that earth and sea had once been mixed, a very early geological inference.
