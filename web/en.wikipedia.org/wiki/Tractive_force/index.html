Tractive force

As used in mechanical engineering, the term tractive force can either refer to the total traction a vehicle exerts on a surface, or the amount of the total traction that contributes to motion. In railway engineering, tractive force is most often described as tractive effort, the force exerted by a locomotive to move itself and its train.

Vehicle dynamics

For a road or rail vehicle, the tractive force at the driving wheels is limited by two factors: the torque that the engine and transmission can deliver to the wheels, and the maximum force that friction (adhesion) between wheel and surface can support. If the applied force exceeds the adhesive limit — the product of the coefficient of friction and the normal load on the driven wheels — the wheels slip.

The net tractive force available for acceleration equals the applied force minus resistances such as rolling resistance, aerodynamic drag, and gradient resistance. Power and tractive force are related by P = F·v: at a constant power, the available force decreases as speed increases, which is why locomotives exert their maximum tractive effort at starting and low speeds.
