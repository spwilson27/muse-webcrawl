Lesbian feminism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lesbian feminism is a cultural movement and critical perspective that emerged in the 1970s, largely in North America and Western Europe, encouraging women to direct their energies toward other women rather than men, and often advocating lesbianism as a political choice. It arose in part from dissatisfaction with the second-wave feminist movement and the gay liberation movement, both of which many lesbian feminists felt marginalized lesbian concerns — feminists within the mainstream movement sometimes treated lesbianism as an embarrassment, while gay male organizations often subordinated women's issues.

History

A key moment came in 1970, when members of the group Radicalesbians disrupted the Second Congress to Unite Women in New York, distributing a manifesto titled "The Woman-Identified Woman." The paper argued that lesbianism was not primarily a sexual matter but a political commitment to women, and it became a foundational text of lesbian feminism. That same year, Betty Friedan's reported description of lesbians as a "lavender menace" within the feminist movement prompted a backlash that pushed lesbian issues toward the center of feminist debate.

Key ideas

Lesbian feminist theory examined compulsory heterosexuality, a term popularized by Adrienne Rich in her 1980 essay "Compulsory Heterosexuality and Lesbian Existence," which argued that heterosexuality is imposed on women as a political institution rather than being a natural default. Monique Wittig's essays, including "The Straight Mind" and "One Is Not Born a Woman," argued that lesbians are not women in the conventional sense because the category "woman" is defined by a heterosexist social order.

Some strands of lesbian feminism overlapped with separatist feminism, advocating women-only spaces, communes, and cultural institutions such as women's bookstores, music festivals, and publishing houses. Others criticized separatism as impractical or exclusionary. Lesbian feminism also contributed to debates about pornography, butch/femme roles, and the ethics of representation.

Criticism and later developments

Lesbian feminism was criticized from several directions: for essentialism about women's nature, for underplaying race and class (a critique developed by Black feminists such as those in the Combahee River Collective), and, later, by queer theorists who questioned its fixed categories of sexual identity. Nevertheless, its institutions and concepts — woman-identification, the critique of compulsory heterosexuality, and women-centered cultural production — continue to influence feminist thought.
