Microfluidics

Microfluidics
FieldFluid mechanics, nanotechnology
Typical scalesMicrometres (10−6 m)
Key applicationsLab-on-a-chip, inkjet printheads, DNA chips, point-of-care diagnostics

Microfluidics is the science and technology of systems that process or manipulate small amounts of fluids, using channels with dimensions of tens to hundreds of micrometres. It is a multidisciplinary field at the intersection of physics, chemistry, engineering and biotechnology, with practical applications in the design of systems in which low volumes of fluids are processed to achieve multiplexing, automation and high-throughput screening.

Microfluidics emerged in the beginning of the 1980s and was initially used in the development of inkjet printheads. It was later applied to lab-on-a-chip technology, DNA chips, micro-propulsion and micro-thermal technologies.

Behaviour of fluids at the microscale

At the microscale, factors such as surface tension, energy dissipation and fluidic resistance dominate the system. The Reynolds number becomes extremely low (often less than 1), meaning flow is almost always laminar and viscous forces dominate over inertial forces. Co-flowing fluids therefore do not mix except by diffusion, and molecules can be transported across laminar interfaces in a controlled fashion. Capillary forces and surface roughness also play important roles that are negligible at larger scales.

Applications

Microfluidic structures include micropneumatic systems (micropumps, microvalves and mixers) for off-chip handling of fluids. Major applications include:

  • Lab-on-a-chip devices integrating laboratory functions onto a single integrated circuit of only millimetres to a few square centimetres.
  • Point-of-care diagnostics, including lateral-flow and cartridge-based tests.
  • Droplet-based microfluidics, in which discrete volumes are created using immiscible phases, enabling single-cell analysis and high-throughput screening.
  • Inkjet printheads and continuous-flow systems for chemical synthesis.
  • Organ-on-a-chip models that mimic physiological responses of tissues.

Fabrication

Early microfluidic devices were fabricated in silicon and glass using photolithography borrowed from the semiconductor industry. Most devices today are made from polymers such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) by soft lithography, a rapid-prototyping technique popularised by George M. Whitesides and co-workers. More recently, paper-based microfluidic devices and 3D-printing approaches have lowered costs for diagnostic applications in resource-limited settings.
