Committee of Union and Progress

The Committee of Union and Progress (CUP; Ottoman Turkish: İttihad ve Terakki Cemiyeti) was a revolutionary organization and later political party active in the Ottoman Empire and the early Republic of Turkey. Founded in 1889 by medical students in Constantinople, it became the central organ of the Young Turk movement, which sought constitutional government and the modernization of the empire. After the Young Turk Revolution of 1908 forced Sultan Abdul Hamid II to restore the constitution, the CUP became the dominant political force in the empire.

Road to power

Originally founded as the Committee of Ottoman Union, the organization merged with Ahmed Rıza's Paris-based group and adopted the name Union and Progress in the 1890s. Exiled leaders such as Ahmed Rıza and Mizancı Murat directed propaganda from abroad, while military officers in Macedonia, including Enver Bey and Mustafa Kemal, built networks inside the army. Dissatisfaction with the Sultan's rule and fears of foreign intervention led to the 1908 revolution, after which the CUP governed through the parliament while retaining influence as a party committee.

Rule and the First World War

Following the 1913 Ottoman coup d'état, the CUP ruled the empire in a virtual one-party regime dominated by the "Three Pashas" — Talaat Pasha, Enver Pasha, and Cemal Pasha. They led the Ottoman Empire into the First World War on the side of the Central Powers, signing a secret alliance with Germany in August 1914. During the war, the CUP government implemented a program of Turkification and demographic engineering; its policies were responsible for the Armenian genocide of 1915, as well as genocides and persecution directed against Assyrian and Greek populations. The empire also suffered catastrophic military defeats, including at Sarikamish and in Palestine.

Dissolution

After the Ottoman defeat in 1918, the CUP's leaders fled the country aboard a German vessel, and the party dissolved itself at its final congress in November 1918. Many former members later joined Mustafa Kemal's national movement, and the Turkish War of Independence followed. In 1926, alleged CUP plots against Atatürk led to trials and executions of surviving leaders, ending the organization's direct legacy in Turkish politics.
