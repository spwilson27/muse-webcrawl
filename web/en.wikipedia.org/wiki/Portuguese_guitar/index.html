Portuguese guitar

The Portuguese guitar (Portuguese: guitarra portuguesa) is a plucked string instrument of the cittern family, with twelve steel strings arranged in six courses of two. It is one of the defining instruments of fado music, providing the bright, ornamented accompaniment to the singer alongside the Spanish-style classical guitar (viola de fado).

Models and construction

Two main models exist. The Lisbon guitar has a narrower body, a scroll-shaped headstock carved like a snail shell, and a slightly shorter scale, giving it a brighter sound. The Coimbra guitar, used in the fado of Coimbra, has a larger body, a simpler "fan" headstock, a longer scale and a mellower tone, and is tuned a tone lower. Both use a distinctive "Preston" watch-key tuning mechanism, an 18th-century English invention preserved in Portugal.

Technique

The instrument is played with picks fitted over the fingernails of the thumb and index finger, allowing rapid, glittering runs and arpeggios. Its music is characterised by the dedilho style of intricate ornamentation. Celebrated players include Carlos Paredes, who raised the Coimbra guitar to concert status as a solo instrument.

History

The Portuguese guitar descends from the English guitar popular in the 18th century and from older cittern traditions. By the mid-19th century it was established in Lisbon taverns as the accompaniment to the emerging fado, and it remains inseparable from that genre.
