Faunus

In ancient Roman religion and myth, Faunus was the horned god of the forest, plains, and fields, and the patron of farmers and herdsmen, who made cattle fruitful. One of the oldest Roman deities — among the di indigetes — he was later equated with the Greek god Pan, and in literature often appears in human form with goat's horns, ears, and sometimes legs.

Character and worship

Faunus was a prophetic god: in his sacred groves, notably at Tibur (Tivoli) and on the Aventine Hill at Rome, he revealed the future in dreams to those who slept on the fleeces of sacrificed lambs, a rite called incubation. His oracular voices were imagined speaking in Saturnian verse. In one tradition he was a mortal king of Latium, son of Picus and grandson of Saturn, deified after death for his services to agriculture; Virgil's Aeneid makes him the father of King Latinus, and it is Faunus who warns Latinus not to marry his daughter Lavinia to a Latin suitor. A female counterpart, Fauna — variously his wife, sister, or daughter — was worshipped by women as Bona Dea.

Festivals

Two festivals belonged to Faunus. The Faunalia on 5 December was a rustic celebration in the countryside. The Lupercalia on 15 February, Rome's famous festival of purification and fertility, was celebrated at the Lupercal cave at the foot of the Palatine, where priests called Luperci, clad in goatskins, sacrificed goats and a dog and ran through the streets striking bystanders with leather thongs. Faunus was also associated with the wild and potentially dangerous powers of untamed nature, and with the fauns, half-goat woodland spirits modelled on the Greek satyrs.
