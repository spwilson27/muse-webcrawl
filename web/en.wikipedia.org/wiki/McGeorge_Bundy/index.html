McGeorge "Mac" Bundy (March 30, 1919 – September 16, 1996) was an American academic who served as the US National Security Advisor to Presidents John F. Kennedy and Lyndon B. Johnson from 1961 through 1966. He was president of the Ford Foundation from 1966 through 1979, and later a professor of history at New York University.

Bundy was one of the chief architects of the United States' escalation of involvement in the Vietnam War, and played a major role in the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 and the Bay of Pigs Invasion of 1961. Together with Defense Secretary Robert McNamara, he formed the public face of the "best and the brightest" generation of policymakers.

Early life and career

Born into a wealthy Boston family, Bundy was educated at Yale University, where he graduated with a degree in mathematics in 1940. During World War II he served in US Army intelligence. In 1953, at age 34, he became dean of the Faculty of Arts and Sciences at Harvard University, the youngest dean in the university's history.

National Security Advisor

Appointed by Kennedy in 1961, Bundy reorganised the National Security Council staff into a small, activist body. He advised Kennedy during the Bay of Pigs Invasion, the Cuban Missile Crisis, and the early escalation in Vietnam. Under Johnson he advocated sustained bombing of North Vietnam, before leaving government in 1966.

Later life

As president of the Ford Foundation (1966–1979), Bundy expanded its funding of civil rights and public interest law. In later years he worked on nuclear arms control and co-authored a 1994 appeal for deep reductions in nuclear weapons. He died of a heart attack in 1996.
