Homocysteine is a non-proteinogenic α-amino acid. It is a homologue of the amino acid cysteine, differing by an additional methylene bridge (–CH2–). It is biosynthesised from methionine by the removal of its terminal Cε methyl group. In the body, homocysteine can be recycled into either methionine or converted into cysteine with the aid of vitamin B6, B12, and folate.

Homocysteine is made in the body through the metabolism of methionine. It normally is then either remethylated back to methionine or further metabolised, but it can build up unless vitamins B6 and B12 and folate are present. Elevated levels in the blood (hyperhomocysteinemia) have been associated with atherosclerosis, venous thromboembolism, and other cardiovascular outcomes, though whether homocysteine is a causal factor or merely a marker remains debated.

Structure

Homocysteine exists as a zwitterion at physiological pH. The L enantiomer is the one of biological significance; it is structurally analogous to cysteine but with a four-carbon rather than three-carbon backbone ending in a thiol group.

Biosynthesis and metabolism

Methionine is converted to S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the body's principal methyl donor. After donating its methyl group, SAM becomes S-adenosylhomocysteine, which is hydrolysed to homocysteine. Homocysteine then stands at a metabolic branch point:

  • Remethylation — recycled to methionine by methionine synthase (requiring vitamin B12 and 5-methyltetrahydrofolate) or by betaine-homocysteine methyltransferase in the liver.
  • Transsulfuration — irreversibly converted to cystathionine by cystathionine β-synthase (requiring vitamin B6), onward to cysteine, glutathione, and sulfate.

Clinical significance

Normal fasting plasma homocysteine is roughly 5–15 µmol/L. Severe elevation occurs in the rare genetic disorder homocystinuria, caused by cystathionine β-synthase deficiency, in which homocysteine and methionine accumulate and cause lens dislocation, skeletal abnormalities, intellectual disability, and early thromboembolism. Milder elevations are associated with folate or B12 deficiency, renal impairment, hypothyroidism, and certain drugs. Large trials of B-vitamin supplementation lowered homocysteine but generally did not reduce cardiovascular events, weakening the case that homocysteine itself is a major causal agent.

History

Homocysteine was first described in 1932 by Vincent du Vigneaud, who generated it by treating methionine with sulfuric acid. Its clinical importance was recognised in the 1960s through studies of homocystinuria, and Kilmer McCully proposed the "homocysteine theory of arteriosclerosis" in 1969 after observing advanced arterial disease in affected children.
