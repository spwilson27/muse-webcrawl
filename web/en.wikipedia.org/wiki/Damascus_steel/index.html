Damascus steel

Damascus steel was the forged steel of the blades of swords smithed in the Near East from ingots of wootz steel imported from Southern India and Sri Lanka. These swords are characterised by distinctive patterns of banding and mottling reminiscent of flowing water, sometimes in a "ladder" or "rose" pattern. Such blades were reputed to be tough, resistant to shattering, and capable of being honed to a sharp, resilient edge. The steel is named after Damascus, the capital city of Syria and one of the largest cities in the Levant, though whether the name refers to swords made or sold there, or to the resemblance of the patterns to damask cloth, is debated.

Wootz and manufacture

The original material was wootz, a high-carbon crucible steel (roughly 1–2 per cent carbon) made in India from at least the early centuries CE and traded as small ingots or "cakes". Smiths in Persia, Syria, and elsewhere forged the ingots at relatively low temperatures; careful thermal cycling caused the cementite (iron carbide) particles in the steel to align into bands, producing the characteristic "watered" surface pattern after polishing and etching. Blades of genuine Damascus steel were produced until around the 18th century, after which the craft declined — possibly through loss of suitable ore sources and trade routes as much as through forgotten technique.

Modern research and reproduction

Because surviving blades differ chemically from modern steels, the original process was long described as "lost". Metallurgists such as John Verhoeven and blacksmith Alfred Pendray reported in the 1980s and 1990s that they had reproduced wootz Damascus blades, arguing that trace elements such as vanadium in the original ores were crucial to the banding. Separately, modern "Damascus steel" sold for knives and swords is usually pattern-welded steel — layered billets of two or more steels forge-welded and manipulated to imitate the appearance of the original — and is historically and metallurgically a different material. In 2006 researchers reported carbon nanotubes and cementite nanowires in a genuine historical blade, findings that attracted wide attention.
