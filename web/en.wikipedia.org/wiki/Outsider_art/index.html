Outsider art is art made by self-taught or supposedly naïve artists with little or no contact with the conventional art world. The term is the English equivalent of art brut ("raw art"), coined in 1945 by the French artist Jean Dubuffet for work created outside the boundaries of official culture—particularly by psychiatric patients, prisoners, children and other social outsiders—which he prized for its inventiveness and freedom from artistic convention.

History

Interest in the art of psychiatric patients grew in the early 20th century, notably through Hans Prinzhorn's 1922 study Artistry of the Mentally Ill, based on the collection at Heidelberg. Dubuffet collected such work systematically from 1945, showing it in Paris and founding the Compagnie de l'Art Brut; his collection was installed in Lausanne in 1976 as the Collection de l'Art Brut. The art critic Roger Cardinal introduced "outsider art" in his 1972 book of that title.

Notable artists

Canonical outsider artists include Adolf Wölfli, a Swiss psychiatric patient whose epic illustrated manuscripts fill thousands of pages; Henry Darger, a Chicago janitor whose vast illustrated novel of the "Vivian Girls" was discovered only at his death; and Martín Ramírez, a Mexican immigrant who produced large drawings in a California psychiatric hospital. Their works now command major exhibitions and prices.

Debates

The field has expanded with institutions such as New York's Outsider Art Fair (founded 1993) and increasing museum interest, prompting debate over whether integrating such artists into the market and canon destroys the "outsiderness" that defines the category, and whether the label marginalises the artists it celebrates. Related categories include folk art, visionary art, naïve art and visionary environments.
