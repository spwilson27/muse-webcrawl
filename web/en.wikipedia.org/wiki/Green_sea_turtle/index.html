The green sea turtle (Chelonia mydas), also known as the green turtle, black (sea) turtle or Pacific green turtle, is a species of large sea turtle of the family Cheloniidae. It is the only species in the genus Chelonia. Its range extends throughout tropical and subtropical seas around the world, with distinct Atlantic and Pacific populations. The common name refers to the green colour of the fat and cartilage beneath its carapace, not to the colour of the shell, which is typically brown or olive.

Description

Adults commonly reach 1.1–1.5 m in shell length and weigh 110–190 kg. The carapace is smooth and heart-shaped with four pairs of costal scutes; unlike the hawksbill, the green turtle has a short, unserrated beak and a single pair of prefrontal scales between the eyes. The species is unusual among sea turtles in that adults are largely herbivorous, grazing on seagrasses and algae, which tints their fat green.

Life cycle

Females return to the beaches where they were born to nest, often migrating thousands of kilometres between feeding grounds and nesting sites such as Ascension Island, Tortuguero (Costa Rica) and Raine Island (Australia). A female lays several clutches of around 100 eggs in a season; the sex of hatchlings is determined by nest temperature. Maturity is reached only after 20–50 years.

Conservation

The green turtle has been heavily exploited for meat, eggs and shell, and populations collapsed at many rookeries. It is listed in CITES Appendix I and assessed as Endangered globally, though some regional populations, notably in Hawaii and Florida, have recovered strongly under protection. Threats include bycatch in fisheries, coastal development, plastic ingestion and fibropapillomatosis disease.
