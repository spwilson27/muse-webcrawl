Human factors and ergonomics

Human factors and ergonomics (commonly referred to as human factors, HF, or HF/E) is the application of psychological and physiological principles to the engineering and design of products, processes and systems. Its goal is to reduce human error, increase productivity and enhance safety and comfort by designing for the capabilities and limitations of the people who use a system.

Domains

The International Ergonomics Association divides the discipline into three domains: physical ergonomics, concerned with anatomy, anthropometry and biomechanics in the design of workplaces, tools and controls; cognitive ergonomics, concerned with perception, memory, reasoning and mental workload in interaction with systems — including human–computer interaction; and organizational ergonomics, concerned with the design of work schedules, teams, communication and safety culture.

History

The field took shape during the Second World War, when studies of pilot error and cockpit layout — by researchers such as Alphonse Chapanis and Paul Fitts — showed that many "pilot errors" were design errors. Post-war work spread to aviation safety, nuclear power, medicine and consumer products; the Fitts's law model of aimed movement and Hick's law of decision time remain foundational results. After accidents such as Three Mile Island, human factors analysis became standard in high-hazard industries, and usability engineering extended it throughout software design. Human factors professionals investigate accidents, design interfaces and workstations, and develop standards such as the ISO 9241 series on ergonomics of human–system interaction.
