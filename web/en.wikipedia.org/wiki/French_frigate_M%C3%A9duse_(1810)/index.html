French frigate Méduse (1810)

Launched1 July 1810
ClassPallas-class frigate, 40 guns
FateWrecked on the Arguin Bank, 2 July 1816

Méduse was a 40-gun Pallas-class frigate of the French Navy, launched in 1810. She took part in the Napoleonic Wars and, after the Bourbon Restoration, was assigned in 1816 to ferry French officials and settlers to re-establish the colony of Senegal. Her wreck on the Arguin Bank off the coast of present-day Mauritania, and the ordeal of those aboard her makeshift raft, became one of the most notorious maritime disasters of the 19th century.

The wreck and the raft

Commanded by the inexperienced Captain Hugues Duroy de Chaumareys, the frigate ran aground on 2 July 1816. With too few boats for the roughly 400 people aboard, about 147 passengers and crew were put onto a hastily built raft, intended to be towed to shore. The tow was soon cut, and the raft drifted for 13 days in the Atlantic. Starvation, thirst, exposure, violence and cannibalism reduced the survivors to just 15 men when the brig Argus found them, nearly by chance.

Aftermath

The scandal discredited the Restoration government, which had appointed the captain for political reasons. Survivors Jean-Baptiste Henri Savigny and Alexandre Corréard published an influential account of the disaster. The episode was immortalised by Théodore Géricault's monumental painting The Raft of the Medusa (1818–19), now in the Louvre, which depicts the moment the survivors sighted the Argus.
