Drug dependence

Drug dependence is a state in which an organism functions normally only in the presence of a drug, and manifests a physical withdrawal syndrome when the drug is abruptly discontinued. Dependence differs from addiction: addiction is characterized by compulsive drug-seeking and use despite harmful consequences, whereas dependence refers to the physiological adaptation to a substance. Many drugs taken for legitimate medical purposes, such as opioids, benzodiazepines, corticosteroids and antidepressants, can produce dependence without addiction.

Definitions

Diagnostic systems such as the DSM-5 have largely replaced the term "dependence" with "substance use disorder," graded by severity, partly to avoid confusion between physiological dependence and addiction. Physical dependence involves tolerance — needing more of a drug to achieve the same effect — and withdrawal symptoms on cessation. Psychological dependence involves emotional-motivational withdrawal symptoms such as dysphoria and craving.

Mechanisms

Dependence develops through neuroadaptation: chronic drug exposure alters receptor density, second-messenger systems and gene expression in reward circuits, particularly the mesolimbic dopamine pathway. The brain counter-adapts to the drug's presence, so that in its absence normal functioning is disturbed, producing withdrawal.

Treatment

Management may involve gradual tapering of the drug, substitution therapy (such as methadone or buprenorphine for opioid dependence, or nicotine replacement), and medications to ease withdrawal symptoms. Abrupt discontinuation of some substances, notably alcohol and benzodiazepines, can be medically dangerous.
