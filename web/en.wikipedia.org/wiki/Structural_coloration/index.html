Structural coloration

The iridescent blue of peacock feathers is structural, not pigmentary

Structural coloration in animals and plants is the production of colour by microscopically structured surfaces fine enough to interfere with visible light, often in combination with pigments. The structures create colour through interference, diffraction, or coherent scattering rather than through selective absorption by pigments alone.

Mechanisms

In feathers, butterfly wings and beetle cuticle, nanoscale arrays — multilayers, photonic crystals, or ordered keratin structures — reflect particular wavelengths constructively. The blue of a blue morpho butterfly and of a peacock's neck feathers arises this way; because the colour depends on the geometry of the structure, it often changes with viewing angle, producing iridescence. Non-iridescent structural colours, such as the blue of many bird feathers, come from quasi-ordered spongy keratin structures that scatter coherently.

History and applications

Robert Hooke and Isaac Newton both described aspects of the phenomenon, and Lord Rayleigh explained the blue of the sky and of some feathers by scattering. Structural colours do not fade as pigments do — fossil beetles retain metallic colours for millions of years — which has made them attractive models for biomimetic paints, cosmetics, sensors and anti-counterfeiting features.
