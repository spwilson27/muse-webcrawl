Janus v. AFSCME

Janus v. American Federation of State, County, and Municipal Employees, Council 31, 585 U.S. 878 (2018), abbreviated Janus v. AFSCME, is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States concerning the rights of labor unions and the First Amendment. The Court held, 5–4, that public-sector "agency fees" — payments required of non-union employees to cover the costs of collective bargaining — violate the First Amendment's free speech clause, because they compel nonmembers to subsidise a union's speech on matters of public concern. The decision overruled Abood v. Detroit Board of Education (1977), which had permitted such fees for four decades.

Background

Many state laws authorise public employers to recognise a union as the exclusive bargaining representative of all employees in a unit. Because the union must represent nonmembers fairly, roughly half the states permitted "fair share" or agency fees from nonmembers to prevent free-riding. In Abood (1977) the Supreme Court upheld such fees for collective bargaining costs while barring their use for political activities. That framework survived for decades but was questioned in Knox v. SEIU (2012) and Harris v. Quinn (2014). A direct challenge, Friedrichs v. California Teachers Association, ended in a 4–4 tie in 2016 after the death of Justice Antonin Scalia.

The case

Mark Janus, an Illinois child support specialist, objected to the agency fee (about $45 per month) deducted from his pay to AFSCME Council 31, arguing that public-sector bargaining is inherently political. Represented by the National Right to Work Legal Defense Foundation and the Liberty Justice Center, he sued in federal court; the district court and the Seventh Circuit ruled against him under Abood. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and heard argument on February 26, 2018.

Decision

Writing for the majority, Justice Samuel Alito held that "states and public-sector unions may no longer extract agency fees from nonconsenting employees", reasoning that everything public unions bargain over — wages, benefits, pensions — affects public budgets and is therefore speech on matters of public concern. The Court found Abood poorly reasoned and unworkable, and required clear affirmative consent ("opt-in") before any union payment is taken from a nonmember. Justice Elena Kagan's dissent accused the majority of weaponising the First Amendment to undermine unions and of disregarding stare decisis and the expectations of more than twenty states.

Impact

The ruling immediately ended agency fees for some five million public employees in the 22 states that had allowed them, and unions braced for membership and revenue losses. In practice, public-sector union membership declined only modestly in the following years as unions mounted large internal organising campaigns, though fee-payer revenue was eliminated nationwide and the case spurred follow-on litigation over opt-out windows, dues deduction and exclusive representation.
