Partition of Belgium

Belgium's federal regions: Flanders (Dutch-speaking), Wallonia (French-speaking) and bilingual Brussels.

The partition of Belgium is a hypothetical situation, discussed in Belgian politics and media, in which Belgium splits into two or more independent states, principally along the linguistic divide between Dutch-speaking Flanders in the north and French-speaking Wallonia in the south, with the bilingual Brussels-Capital Region as a major obstacle and prize.

Background

Belgium's internal tensions arise from long-standing linguistic, economic and cultural differences between its communities. A series of state reforms from 1970 onwards transformed unitary Belgium into a federal state of three regions (Flanders, Wallonia, Brussels) and three language communities. Flemish nationalist parties, notably the Nieuw-Vlaamse Alliantie (N-VA) and Vlaams Belang, advocate confederalism or outright Flemish independence, while most Walloon and Brussels politicians favour maintaining the federation.

Scenarios

Proposed scenarios include an independent Flanders with Wallonia either independent, attached to France (rattachism), or remaining as a rump Belgian state with Brussels; Brussels itself might become an independent city-state, remain shared, or join one of the successors. Complications include the division of the national debt, the status of Brussels as seat of the European Union institutions, the German-speaking Community in the east, and the Flemish municipalities with French-speaking minorities around Brussels.

Public opinion

Polls generally find support for outright secession to be a minority position in both Flanders and Wallonia, though support for greater Flemish autonomy is widespread. Long government-formation crises — notably 541 days in 2010–11 — periodically revive speculation about partition, but Belgium has so far always resolved them with coalition agreements and further devolution.
