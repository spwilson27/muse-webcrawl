Social ownership

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Social ownership is an umbrella term for forms of ownership of the means of production in socialist theory and practice, in which the surplus product accrues to society as a whole, to the workers of an enterprise, or to the users of a service, rather than to private capital owners. Its historical forms include state ownership (nationalised industries, dominant in the Soviet model and post-1945 Western Europe), cooperative ownership (worker cooperatives such as the Mondragon federation in the Basque Country), municipal ownership, common ownership of land and resources, and newer proposals (social wealth funds, platform cooperatives, market-socialist share schemes in the Roemer and Schweickart models).

The term marks the point of maximum disagreement within socialism: centralised state ownership was identified by critics (including other socialists) as substituting bureaucratic for social control — the Soviet experience being the standing exhibit — while market socialists and syndicalists argued social ownership must mean decentralised worker and community control to deserve the name. In mixed economies social ownership persists at large scale invisibly (sovereign wealth funds — Norway's fund owns on the order of 1.5 per cent of all listed equities worldwide — public land, infrastructure and pension funds), keeping the conceptual question (who controls the surplus, and how) alive inside capitalism itself.
