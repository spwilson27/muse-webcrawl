The Chicago Manual of Style (abbreviated CMOS or CMS) is a style guide for American English published since 1906 by the University of Chicago Press. It is one of the most widely used and respected style guides in American publishing, governing grammar, usage, punctuation, and the mechanics of scholarly citation for authors, editors, and publishers.

History

The manual began as a single sheet of proofreaders' rules compiled by the university press staff, grew into a 200-page book in its first edition, and expanded steadily through the twentieth century. The fifteenth edition (2003) was the first to be published simultaneously in print and online. The seventeenth edition (2017) ran over 1,100 pages; a substantially reorganized eighteenth edition appeared in 2024.

Citation systems

Chicago style is best known for prescribing two documentation systems: the notes and bibliography system, preferred in literature, history, and the arts, and the author–date system, used in the sciences and social sciences. The manual also covers manuscript preparation, rights and permissions, and (since the 16th edition) production and digital publishing. A simplified derivative, Kate L. Turabian's A Manual for Writers ("Turabian style"), adapts Chicago rules for student papers.

Usage

Many academic journals and trade publishers in the humanities follow Chicago style, and the manual's guidance on spelling, hyphenation, and capitalization is widely treated as authoritative for American book publishing — the reference editors consulted when, for example, preparing editions of works such as The Conquest of Bread for modern readers.
