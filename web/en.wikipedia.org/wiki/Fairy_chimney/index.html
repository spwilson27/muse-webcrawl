A fairy chimney is a tall, thin spire of rock that protrudes from the bottom of an arid basin or badland. Also known as a hoodoo, tent rock or earth pyramid, fairy chimneys consist of relatively soft rock topped by a harder, more resistant stone that protects the column from erosion. They are most famously found in the Cappadocia region of Turkey and in Bryce Canyon National Park in Utah, United States.

Formation

Fairy chimneys form in sedimentary rock sequences where a resistant layer — such as basalt, andesite or well-cemented sandstone — overlies softer material like tuff, mudstone or limestone. Rain, wind and frost weathering erode the soft rock faster wherever it is exposed, while the hard capstone shields the column beneath it, leaving a mushroom- or chimney-shaped pillar. Heights range from about 1.5 metres to more than 40 metres.

Cappadocia

In Cappadocia, eruptions of ancient volcanoes deposited thick layers of tuff, later capped by basalt. Over millions of years erosion carved the plateau into valleys filled with fairy chimneys, particularly around Göreme, Paşabağ and Uçhisar. Because the tuff is soft and easily carved, local people hollowed the pillars and the surrounding cliffs into dwellings, churches and entire underground cities; many carved chimneys are still used as homes, storerooms and cave hotels. The Göreme National Park and Rock Sites of Cappadocia are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Other locations

Similar formations occur at Bryce Canyon, where they are called hoodoos; in the South Tyrol Renon earth pyramids in Italy; at Taiwan's Yehliu Geopark; and in Serbia's Đavolja Varoš ("Devil's Town"). The Turkish name peribacası literally means "fairy chimney".
