Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis

Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) is a laboratory technique used to separate biological macromolecules, usually proteins or nucleic acids, according to their electrophoretic mobility through a gel made of cross-linked polyacrylamide. The gel acts as a molecular sieve: smaller molecules migrate faster through its pores when an electric field is applied.

The gel

The gel is cast by polymerising acrylamide monomer with the cross-linker N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide, initiated by ammonium persulfate and TEMED. Pore size is controlled by the total acrylamide concentration and the proportion of cross-linker, allowing the separating range to be tuned; gradient gels widen that range.

Variants

In the most common protein method, SDS-PAGE, samples are denatured with the detergent sodium dodecyl sulfate, which coats proteins with a uniform negative charge so that separation reflects molecular mass alone. Gels typically consist of a low-percentage stacking gel layered over a resolving gel (the discontinuous Laemmli system). Native PAGE omits the detergent to preserve protein structure and activity, while specialised forms such as blue native PAGE and two-dimensional gel electrophoresis (IEF followed by SDS-PAGE) resolve complex mixtures for proteomics.

After the run, bands are visualised with stains such as Coomassie brilliant blue or silver stain, or transferred to a membrane for Western blotting. DNA sequencing was historically performed by PAGE, and the method remains standard for analysing PCR products and RNA.
