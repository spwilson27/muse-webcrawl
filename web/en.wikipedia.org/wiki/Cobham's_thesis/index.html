Cobham's thesis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cobham's thesis equates feasible computation with polynomial-time computation.

Cobham's thesis, also known as the Cobham–Edmonds thesis, asserts that a computational problem can be feasibly solved on a computer if and only if it can be solved in polynomial time—that is, if it lies in the complexity class P. It is named after Alan Cobham, who discussed the machine-independence of the class P in his 1965 paper "The intrinsic computational difficulty of functions", and Jack Edmonds, who made related remarks the same year.

Significance

The thesis is a cornerstone of computational complexity theory because P is remarkably robust: the set of problems solvable in polynomial time is the same for nearly all reasonable deterministic models of computation (Turing machines, RAM machines, modern programming languages), since one model can simulate another with at most polynomial overhead. Cobham's thesis therefore gives a mathematical definition of "tractable" that does not depend on hardware details.

Limitations and criticism

  • Large exponents and constants: an algorithm running in time n100 is polynomial but useless in practice; conversely, some exponential algorithms with small growth rates work well on practical inputs.
  • Average-case behaviour: some problems (like integer factorization with the number field sieve) are not known to be in P yet are routinely solved for moderate sizes.
  • New models: randomized algorithms (class BPP), quantum computing (BQP), and quantum algorithms such as Shor's algorithm challenge a strictly classical reading of the thesis. The related extended Church–Turing thesis—that any physically realizable computer can be efficiently simulated by a probabilistic Turing machine—is widely doubted because of quantum speedups.

Despite these caveats, Cobham's thesis remains the standard working definition of computational feasibility.
