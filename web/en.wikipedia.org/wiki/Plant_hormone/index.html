Plant hormones
Also calledPhytohormones
FunctionSignal molecules regulating growth and development
Classical fiveAuxins, cytokinins, gibberellins, abscisic acid, ethylene

Plant hormones (or phytohormones) are signal molecules produced within plants that occur in extremely low concentrations and regulate growth, development, reproduction and responses to the environment. Unlike animal hormones, they are not produced in dedicated glands; each cell is generally capable of producing them, and they may act locally or be transported through the plant's vascular tissues or by diffusion.

Classes

HormonePrincipal effects
Auxins (e.g. IAA)Cell elongation, phototropism, apical dominance, root initiation
CytokininsCell division, shoot growth, delay of leaf senescence
GibberellinsStem elongation, seed germination, flowering
Abscisic acid (ABA)Dormancy, stomatal closure, stress responses
EthyleneFruit ripening, leaf abscission, triple response in seedlings
BrassinosteroidsCell expansion and division, vascular differentiation
JasmonatesDefence against herbivores and pathogens, wound responses
Salicylic acidSystemic acquired resistance to pathogens, thermogenesis
StrigolactonesShoot branching control, symbiotic signalling with mycorrhizal fungi

History

The concept grew out of Charles and Francis Darwin's experiments on phototropism in the 1880s; Frits Went isolated auxin in 1926–28. Gibberellins were discovered through the Japanese "foolish seedling" disease of rice caused by the fungus Gibberella fujikuroi. Interactions between hormones — such as the auxin-to-cytokinin ratio used in tissue culture to direct root or shoot formation — are as important as individual effects.

Applications

Synthetic plant hormones and growth regulators are widely used in agriculture and horticulture: auxin herbicides such as 2,4-D, ethylene-releasing ripening agents, gibberellins for seedless grapes and malting, and anti-gibberellins that keep cereals short and lodging-resistant.
