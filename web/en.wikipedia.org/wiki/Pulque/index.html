Pulque

Pulque (Spanish: [ˈpulke]; Classical Nahuatl: metoctli) is an alcoholic beverage made from the fermented sap of the maguey (agave) plant. It is traditional to central Mexico, where it has been produced for millennia. The drink has a characteristic milky-white, slightly viscous appearance and a sour, yeast-like taste, with an alcohol content typically between 2 and 7 percent.

Production

When a mature maguey approaches flowering, its central bud is cut out, leaving a cavity in which the sap, called aguamiel ("honey water"), collects. A worker known as a tlachiquero extracts the sap with a long gourd and transfers it to fermentation vessels, traditionally leather bags and later wooden or plastic barrels. Fermentation, driven by bacteria including Zymomonas mobilis and lactobacilli as well as yeasts, takes from a few hours to several days. Because it spoils quickly, pulque cannot be stored or shipped far and is consumed near where it is made, typically in taverns called pulquerías.

History

Pulque had a sacred role in pre-Hispanic religion, associated with the goddess Mayahuel and the Centzon Totochtin, the "four hundred rabbits" of drunkenness. Its consumption was heavily regulated among the Aztecs and reserved in part for ritual and the elderly. After the Spanish conquest it became a mass popular drink, and in the 19th and early 20th centuries it was Mexico's most popular alcoholic beverage, generating hacienda fortunes in the states of Hidalgo, Tlaxcala, Puebla, and México. It declined sharply in the 20th century under competition from beer and official campaigns portraying it as unhygienic, though it has seen a revival among younger drinkers since the 2000s.

Varieties

Plain pulque is called pulque blanco. Flavored versions, curados, are blended with fruits, nuts, or other ingredients such as pineapple, strawberry, walnut, celery, or oatmeal.
