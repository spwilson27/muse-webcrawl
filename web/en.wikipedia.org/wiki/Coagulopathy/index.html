Coagulopathy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Specialty: Hematology
Symptoms: Excessive bleeding or abnormal clotting
Causes: Inherited disorders, liver disease, vitamin K deficiency, trauma, anticoagulant drugs

Coagulopathy (also called a bleeding disorder) is a condition in which the blood's ability to coagulate (form clots) is impaired. The term can also refer more broadly to disorders of coagulation in either direction, including states of excessive clotting. Coagulopathies may be inherited, such as hemophilia and von Willebrand disease, or acquired, as in liver disease, vitamin K deficiency, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and the effects of anticoagulant medication.

Causes

  • Inherited: hemophilia A and B (factor VIII and IX deficiency), von Willebrand disease, rare factor deficiencies, and platelet function disorders.
  • Acquired: liver failure (reduced synthesis of clotting factors), vitamin K deficiency or warfarin therapy, DIC triggered by sepsis, trauma, or malignancy, dilutional coagulopathy after massive transfusion, and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.
  • Trauma: acute traumatic coagulopathy arises from tissue injury, shock, hypothermia, acidosis, and hemodilution—sometimes called the "lethal triad" when combined with hypothermia and acidosis.

Diagnosis

Laboratory evaluation uses the platelet count, prothrombin time (PT/INR), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), fibrinogen level, and D-dimer. Viscoelastic tests such as thromboelastography (TEG) and ROTEM assess clot formation in whole blood and increasingly guide transfusion in trauma and cardiac surgery.

Treatment

Treatment targets the underlying cause: factor concentrates for hemophilia, vitamin K or plasma for warfarin-related bleeding, tranexamic acid to stabilize clots, and balanced transfusion of red cells, plasma, and platelets in major hemorrhage. Specific reversal agents exist for several anticoagulants, including idarucizumab for dabigatran and andexanet alfa for factor Xa inhibitors.
