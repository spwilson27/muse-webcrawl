Chainring

A crankset with double chainrings.

A chainring is a toothed ring on a bicycle's crankset that engages the drive chain and transfers pedalling power to the rear wheel. Chainrings are bolted to the crank arm via a "spider", traditionally with four or five arms.

Configuration

Bicycles may have one, two (double) or three (triple) chainrings. Together with the rear sprockets (cogset), the chainring sizes determine the available gear ratios: a larger front ring gives a higher (harder, faster) gear for a given rear cog. Typical road doubles pair 53/39 teeth (standard) or 50/34 (compact); mountain bikes historically used triples such as 44/32/22, though modern mountain and gravel bikes favour a single ("1x") narrow-wide chainring that improves chain retention without a front derailleur.

Sizing and standards

Chainring compatibility is defined by the bolt circle diameter (BCD), such as 130 mm or 110 mm for road cranks and 104 mm for many mountain cranks, along with the bolt pattern (symmetric or asymmetric). Teeth are machined with ramps and pins on multi-ring setups to aid front shifting under load. Chainrings were traditionally steel or aluminium alloy; carbon fibre and titanium versions exist for weight saving.

Non-circular rings

Oval or elliptical chainrings (such as Shimano's discontinued Biopace and modern Rotor Q-Rings) vary the effective gear ratio through the pedal stroke, aiming to smooth the rider's power delivery through the "dead spot" at the top and bottom of the revolution.
