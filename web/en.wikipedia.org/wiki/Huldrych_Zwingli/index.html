Huldrych or Ulrich Zwingli (1 January 1484 – 11 October 1531) was a Swiss Christian theologian, musician and parish priest, and a leader of the Reformation in Switzerland. Born during a time of emerging Swiss patriotism and increasing criticism of the Swiss mercenary system, Zwingli attended the University of Vienna and the University of Basel, a scholarly centre of Renaissance humanism. He continued his studies while he served as a pastor in Glarus and later in Einsiedeln, where he was influenced by the writings of Erasmus.

In 1519, Zwingli became the pastor of the Grossmünster in Zurich where he began to preach ideas on reform of the Catholic Church. In his first public controversies — the Affair of the Sausages (1522), in which eating sausage during Lent became a symbolic protest — and the Zurich Disputations of 1523, the city council sided with his program; images were removed from churches, clerical celibacy was abandoned, and Zurich broke with Rome and the bishop of Constance.

Theology

Zwingli's theology centred on the absolute authority of Scripture, an iconoclastic simplicity of worship, and a strongly symbolic ("memorialist") understanding of the Lord's Supper — Christ's words "this is my body" meant "this signifies my body". His disagreement with Luther on the Eucharist proved irreconcilable at the Marburg Colloquy of 1529, splitting the Protestant movement. He also opposed the Anabaptists, whose Zurich leaders he debated from 1525, and defended infant baptism, while persecuting the radicals from the city. His principal works include Of the Clarity and Certainty of the Word of God (1522), the Sixty-Seven Theses (1523), and the Commentary on True and False Religion (1525).

Wars of Kappel and death

Zwingli promoted a Protestant alliance of Swiss cantons and sought a political confederation under Zurich's lead. Conflict with the Catholic Forest Cantons erupted in the First War of Kappel (1529), settled by negotiation, and the Second War of Kappel (1531). Zurich's forces were defeated at the Battle of Kappel on 11 October 1531; Zwingli, serving as a military chaplain, was killed on the field. His body was quartered and burned for heresy. His work was carried forward in Zurich by his successor Heinrich Bullinger, and his theology, mediated through Bullinger and Calvin, helped shape the Reformed tradition worldwide.
