Train operating company

A train operating company (TOC) is a business operating passenger trains on the railway system of Great Britain under the structure created by the privatisation of British Rail in the 1990s. Under that model, infrastructure (track, signalling and major stations) is owned by Network Rail, rolling stock is owned mainly by rolling stock leasing companies (ROSCOs), and TOCs run the trains and most stations under franchises or contracts awarded by government.

History

The Railways Act 1993 broke British Rail's passenger operations into 25 franchises, let to private companies from 1995 to 1997. The original TOCs included companies such as Virgin Trains, Connex and Great North Eastern Railway (GNER). Over time franchises were re-let, merged or taken back into public control; the Association of Train Operating Companies (ATOC), founded in 1993, provided shared services such as National Rail Enquiries and the Rail Settlement Plan, and its functions later passed to the Rail Delivery Group.

Structure

Most TOCs are subsidiaries of transport groups — including FirstGroup, Arriva (Deutsche Bahn), Govia, Abellio (Dutch state railways) and Stagecoach — so much of the network came to be operated by companies owned by foreign state railways. Open-access operators such as Grand Central and Lumo run services outside the franchise system. Franchising was effectively suspended during the COVID-19 pandemic and replaced by management contracts; subsequent legislation under the Labour government elected in 2024 provides for passenger services to return progressively to public ownership as contracts expire, under the planned Great British Railways.

Criticism

The franchising model has been debated throughout its life: supporters credit it with large growth in passenger numbers, while critics cite fragmentation, high costs, short franchise terms discouraging investment, and repeated operator failures on prestigious routes such as the East Coast Main Line.
