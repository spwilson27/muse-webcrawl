Néel temperature

The Néel temperature, denoted TN, is the temperature above which an antiferromagnetic material becomes paramagnetic — that is, the thermal energy becomes large enough to destroy the microscopic magnetic ordering within the material. It is named after Louis Néel (1904–2000), the French physicist who developed the modern theory of antiferromagnetism and ferrimagnetism and received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1970.

Below TN, neighbouring magnetic moments align antiparallel on interpenetrating sublattices.

Explanation

In an antiferromagnet, neighbouring atomic magnetic moments point in opposite directions, arranged on two (or more) sublattices whose magnetisations cancel. Néel's molecular-field theory explained this by postulating a strong negative exchange coupling between sublattices. Above TN the antiparallel order melts and the material behaves as an ordinary paramagnet, with magnetic susceptibility following a Curie–Weiss law:

χ = C / (T + θ)

where the positive constant θ is closely related to the Néel temperature. At TN itself, the susceptibility reaches a characteristic maximum (cusp), and the heat capacity shows an anomaly — the standard experimental signatures used to measure the transition. Because the net magnetisation is zero on both sides of the transition, neutron diffraction is often used to observe the ordering directly.

Examples

Values of the Néel temperature vary enormously between materials. Chromium orders at about 311 K; the transition-metal monoxides MnO (116 K), FeO (~198 K), CoO (291 K), and NiO (523 K) are classic examples studied by Néel and his school. Hematite (α-Fe2O3) has a Néel temperature near 955 K, while some rare-earth and organic antiferromagnets order only below a few kelvin. The Néel temperature is the antiferromagnetic analogue of the Curie temperature for ferromagnets.
