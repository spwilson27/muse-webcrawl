Holism

Holism is the idea that systems — physical, biological, social, mental or linguistic — should be viewed as wholes rather than merely as collections of their parts, and that the properties of a whole cannot be fully explained by the properties of its components alone. The term was coined by the South African statesman and philosopher Jan Smuts in his 1926 book Holism and Evolution, from the Greek holos, "whole".

Origins

The idea is often traced to Aristotle's observation in the Metaphysics that the whole is more than the sum of its parts. Smuts developed holism as a philosophy of nature in which evolution produces ever more integrated wholes, from matter through life to mind and personality. Gestalt psychology, arising in the same period, applied similar principles to perception.

Forms of holism

  • Ontological holism — the claim that wholes are real entities with properties not reducible to their parts.
  • Methodological holism — the position, in social science, that social phenomena must be studied at the level of institutions and cultures rather than individuals alone.
  • Semantic holism — the view that the meaning of a word or sentence depends on its place in a whole language or theory (associated with Quine and Davidson).
  • Holistic medicine — approaches to health that treat the whole person, including lifestyle and environment, rather than isolated symptoms.

Holism and reductionism

Holism is usually contrasted with reductionism, the approach of explaining phenomena by their smallest components. Much modern debate concerns intermediate positions: systems biology, ecology and complex-systems science study how higher-level patterns emerge from interactions, using ideas such as emergence. Critics argue that vague appeals to "the whole" can obstruct testable explanation, while defenders hold that some phenomena, from consciousness to ecosystems, are only intelligible at the level of the whole.
