Prepared piano

A prepared piano is a piano whose sound has been altered by placing objects — including bolts, screws, rubber, coins, and pieces of wood or plastic — on or between its strings, over its dampers, or on its hammers. The practice is closely associated with the American composer John Cage, whose 1940 work Bacchanale is generally credited as the first composition for prepared piano, written because the performance space was too small for the requested percussion ensemble.

History

Cage encountered the need for percussion-like sounds while composing for dancers at the Cornish School in Seattle. Influenced by Henry Cowell, who had pioneered playing directly on piano strings, Cage experimented with objects inserted into the instrument, discovering that a single prepared piano could produce a wide palette of percussive timbres resembling a gamelan or drum ensemble.

Repertoire

Cage's Sonatas and Interludes (1946–48) is the landmark cycle for prepared piano. Later composers, including Lou Harrison, Christian Wolff, and George Crumb, also prepared pianos, and the technique became common in experimental, avant-garde, and some popular music.

Preparation

Preparations are specified in a chart indicating which strings receive objects, their placement, and materials. Because preparations vary by composer and instrument, performances often involve lengthy setup and tuning adjustments.
