Hieratic

Hieratic (from Greek hieratikos, "priestly") is the name given to a cursive writing system used for Ancient Egyptian, and the principal script for writing that language from its development alongside the hieroglyphs in the late fourth millennium BC until it was replaced by the even more cursive Demotic script in the mid-first millennium BC.

Development and use

Hieratic developed in parallel with the monumental hieroglyphic script: while hieroglyphs were carved or painted for temples and tombs, hieratic was written with a reed brush and ink on papyrus, wood, leather, and ostraca, and served as the everyday script of administration, letters, accounts, and literature. Its signs are simplified, ligatured forms of hieroglyphic originals, and, like hieroglyphs, it was normally written from right to left.

The script passed through several phases. Old Kingdom and Middle Kingdom forms gave way to the more stylised hands of the New Kingdom, including a highly abbreviated "abnormal hieratic" used for business documents in Upper Egypt during the Third Intermediate Period. Even after Demotic became the standard secular script from the seventh century BC onward, hieratic remained in use for religious texts — hence Clement of Alexandria's designation of it as the "priestly" script — and the last hieratic texts date to the third century AD.

Reading and study

Because hieratic hands vary greatly by period and by scribe, reading it requires transcription into hieroglyphs, the standard practice in Egyptological publication. Important hieratic documents include the Westcar Papyrus, the Prisse Papyrus with its teachings, the mathematical Rhind and Moscow papyri, and large numbers of New Kingdom literary and magical texts. The decipherment of hieratic advanced together with that of hieroglyphs following Jean-François Champollion's work in the 1820s, and Georg Möller's Hieratische Paläographie remains a foundational sign-list for the script.
