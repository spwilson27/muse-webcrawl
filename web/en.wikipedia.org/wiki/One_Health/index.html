One Health

One Health is an integrated, unifying approach that aims to sustainably balance and optimize the health of people, animals, and ecosystems. It recognizes that the health of humans, domestic and wild animals, plants, and the wider environment (including ecosystems) are closely linked and interdependent. The approach mobilizes multiple sectors, disciplines, and communities at varying levels of society to work together to foster well-being and tackle threats to health and ecosystems, while addressing the collective need for clean water, energy and air, safe and nutritious food, action on climate change, and sustainable development.

Background

The interrelatedness of human and animal medicine has been recognized since the 19th century: Rudolf Virchow coined the term "zoonosis" and observed that there are no dividing lines between animal and human medicine. In the 20th century, veterinary epidemiologist Calvin Schwabe promoted the concept of "One Medicine". The modern One Health movement gained momentum after the emergence of SARS (2003), highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1, and other zoonotic outbreaks in the early 2000s demonstrated the speed with which diseases can cross species and geographic boundaries.

An estimated 60% of known human infectious diseases and up to 75% of emerging infectious diseases are zoonotic — originating in animals. Environmental change, deforestation, intensive farming, wildlife trade, and climate change all increase contact between humans, livestock, and wildlife, raising spillover risks.

Institutional adoption

The One Health approach is promoted by the "Quadripartite" organizations: the World Health Organization (WHO), the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH, formerly OIE), and the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP). In 2021, the One Health High-Level Expert Panel (OHHLEP) adopted the definition quoted above. In October 2022 the four organizations launched the One Health Joint Plan of Action (2022–2026), covering areas including food safety, zoonotic disease control, antimicrobial resistance, and environmental integration.

Key focus areas

  • Zoonotic diseases — surveillance and control of diseases such as rabies, avian influenza, Ebola, and coronaviruses.
  • Antimicrobial resistance — coordinated stewardship of antimicrobials in human medicine, veterinary medicine, and agriculture.
  • Food safety and foodborne disease.
  • Environmental health — vector-borne diseases, water quality, pollution, and climate-change impacts.
  • Laboratory and surveillance systems shared across human and animal health sectors.

Criticism and challenges

Implementation is hampered by institutional silos, uneven funding between human and veterinary sectors, and the difficulty of measuring the added value of integration. Critics also note that environmental and plant-health dimensions were historically under-represented relative to disease surveillance.
