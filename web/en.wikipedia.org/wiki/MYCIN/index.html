MYCIN

MYCIN was an early expert system developed at Stanford University in the 1970s, principally by Edward Shortliffe as his doctoral research. It used artificial intelligence to identify bacteria causing severe infections, such as bacteraemia and meningitis, and to recommend antibiotics, with the dosage adjusted for the patient's body weight.

How it worked

MYCIN operated with a knowledge base of roughly 500–600 production rules, elicited from infectious-disease specialists. It conducted an interactive question-and-answer consultation, reasoning backwards from its goal using an inference engine, and attached "certainty factors" to conclusions to handle uncertain evidence. In evaluations, MYCIN's therapy recommendations were rated as acceptable as those of Stanford infectious-disease specialists, and markedly better than those of non-specialist physicians.

Influence

Despite its performance, MYCIN was never used in clinical practice, owing to legal and ethical questions about responsibility for computer-generated diagnoses and the practical limits of 1970s computing. Its rule-based shell, EMYCIN ("Essential MYCIN"), became a template for later expert systems, and the project helped launch the field of knowledge engineering.
