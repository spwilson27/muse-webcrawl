A sophist (Greek: σοφιστής, sophistēs) was a teacher in ancient Greece in the fifth and fourth centuries BC. Sophists specialised in using philosophy and rhetoric, among other subjects, for the purposes of teaching aretē — "excellence" or "virtue" — predominantly to young statesmen and nobility. They travelled between cities, took fees for instruction, and gave display speeches, practices that were controversial in a culture where aristocratic education had not been commercial.

Teachings

The leading sophists included Protagoras, famous for the dictum "man is the measure of all things"; Gorgias of Leontini, master of elaborate style and author of On Non-Existence and the Encomium of Helen; Hippias of Elis, a polymath; Prodicus, noted for precise distinctions between synonyms; and Thrasymachus, associated in Plato's Republic with the thesis that justice is the advantage of the stronger. Sophists are credited with advancing grammar, argumentation, relativism, and the systematic study of language, and with spreading intellectual culture across the Greek world.

Reputation

Plato's dialogues depict sophists as mercenary purveyors of plausible but empty argument, contrasted with Socrates' search for truth; Aristotle similarly dismissed sophistry as the appearance of wisdom without the reality. This hostile tradition gave English the pejorative "sophistry" — specious reasoning. Modern scholarship has substantially rehabilitated the sophists, recognising their role in developing democratic rhetoric, humanism, and critical reflection on custom (nomos) versus nature (physis).

Second Sophistic

The term "Second Sophistic", coined by Philostratus, describes the flowering of Greek oratory under the Roman Empire from the 1st to 3rd centuries AD, when celebrity rhetors such as Dio Chrysostom, Aelius Aristides, and Herodes Atticus performed declamations on classical themes before large audiences.
