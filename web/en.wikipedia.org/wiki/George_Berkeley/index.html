George Berkeley (1685–1753) was an Anglo-Irish philosopher and Anglican bishop whose theory of subjective idealism holds that material objects exist only as ideas in minds: esse est percipi, 'to be is to be perceived'. In the Principles of Human Knowledge (1710) and the Three Dialogues (1713) he argued that what we perceive are ideas, and that talk of unperceived matter is incoherent; the continued existence of the world is secured by God's perception of it.

Thought and life

Berkeley developed John Locke's empiricism by attacking the primary/secondary quality distinction and abstract ideas. Samuel Johnson famously 'refuted' him by kicking a stone, but philosophers take Berkeley's arguments seriously as a challenge to materialism. He travelled in America planning a college in Bermuda (the city of Berkeley, California, is named for him) and later served as Bishop of Cloyne, writing on economics and public health — including a pamphlet promoting tar-water as a cure-all.
