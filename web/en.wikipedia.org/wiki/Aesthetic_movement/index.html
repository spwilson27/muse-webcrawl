Aesthetic movement

The Aesthetic movement (also known as Aestheticism) was an intellectual and art movement that flourished in Britain in the late 19th century, roughly from the 1860s to the 1890s. It championed "art for art's sake" — the doctrine that art should be valued for its beauty alone, free of moral, didactic, or utilitarian purpose.

Origins and figures

The movement drew on the ideas of Théophile Gautier in France and, in Britain, on the writings of Walter Pater, whose Studies in the History of the Renaissance (1873) urged readers to burn "with a hard, gemlike flame". Its most famous figure was Oscar Wilde, who embodied the Aesthetic persona of the dandy. Other figures included the painters James McNeill Whistler, Dante Gabriel Rossetti, Edward Burne-Jones, and Albert Moore, and the designer Christopher Dresser.

Arts and design

Aesthetic taste favoured peacock feathers, sunflowers, lilies, Japanese art and blue-and-white porcelain, and influenced interior decoration, furniture, and fashion — the movement was both mocked and popularized in Gilbert and Sullivan's opera Patience (1881). Whistler's Peacock Room (1876–77) is a celebrated surviving interior.

Legacy

Aestheticism fed into the Decadent movement and early modernism, and its insistence on artistic autonomy shaped 20th-century art theory.
