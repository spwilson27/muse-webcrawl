Shot noise

Shot noise or Poisson noise is a type of noise arising from the discrete nature of electric charge and light quanta. Because current is carried by individual electrons and light by individual photons arriving at random, statistically independent times, the measured current or intensity fluctuates even when the average rate is constant. The phenomenon was analysed by Walter Schottky in 1918 in studying vacuum tubes.

Statistics

Arrival events follow Poisson statistics: if the mean number of events in an interval is N, the standard deviation is √N, so relative fluctuations shrink as 1/√N as the signal grows. For an average current I measured over a bandwidth Δf, the root-mean-square shot-noise current is in = √(2 e I Δf), where e is the elementary charge.

Where it matters

Shot noise is a fundamental limit in photodetectors, image sensors (photon shot noise in low light), semiconductor junctions, and precision electronics. In optical imaging it typically dominates other noise sources once read noise is small, setting the maximum achievable signal-to-noise ratio. Correlations between carriers can suppress shot noise below the Poisson level (as in mesoscopic conductors and squeezed light), described by the Fano factor.
