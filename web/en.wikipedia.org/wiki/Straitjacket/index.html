Straitjacket

A straitjacket is a garment of strong canvas or similar material with long sleeves that can be crossed over the chest and tied behind the back, used to restrain a person who may otherwise harm themselves or others. It became an emblem of the old asylum system and, in escapology, a classic challenge prop.

Design and use

The jacket fastens at the back with straps or buckles, out of the wearer's reach. The over-long sleeves are crossed and secured behind, immobilising the arms; many designs add a crotch strap to prevent the jacket being worked up over the head and loops through which restraining belts can pass. Straitjackets were widely used in nineteenth- and twentieth-century psychiatric institutions to control violent patients at a time when few effective treatments existed. Their routine use declined with the advent of antipsychotic medication and modern standards governing physical restraint, though restraint jackets remain in limited clinical and custodial use.

Escapology

Escaping from a straitjacket became a signature feat of stage magicians. Harry Houdini made the straitjacket escape famous, performing it suspended upside down from cranes while crowds watched; the escape depends on slack gained during fitting, dislocating or flexing the shoulders, and laboriously freeing an arm. Suspended straitjacket escapes remain a staple of escape artists, and timed escapes are contested as records.

In language

By extension, "straitjacket" is used metaphorically for any rigid set of rules that prevents freedom of action — a usage that reinforces the garment's cultural image of coercive control.
