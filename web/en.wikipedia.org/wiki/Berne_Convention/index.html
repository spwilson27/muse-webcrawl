Berne Convention

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Berne Convention for the Protection of Literary and Artistic Works, adopted in Berne in 1886, is the founding treaty of international copyright law, administered by WIPO and now binding some 180 states. Its principles: national treatment (foreign works protected as domestic ones), automatic protection (no registration or formalities), and minimum standards — protection for life of the author plus 50 years as the floor (many states apply 70), and moral rights of attribution and integrity under Article 6bis.

The Convention has been revised repeatedly (Berlin 1908, Rome 1928, Brussels 1948, Stockholm 1967, Paris 1971) and incorporated by reference into TRIPS Agreement (1994), which added enforcement through the WTO — though TRIPS excludes the moral-rights article. The United States, long the holdout, joined in 1989, adapting its law only minimally (no general moral rights, retained registration incentives for domestic works). Berne sets the architecture within which national copyright law — its terms, exceptions (the three-step test for limitations) and new subject matter (photographs, software debates, databases) — is negotiated.
