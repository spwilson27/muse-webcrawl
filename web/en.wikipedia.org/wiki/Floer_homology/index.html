Floer homology

Floer homology is a tool for studying symplectic geometry and low-dimensional topology, named after its inventor, the German mathematician Andreas Floer, who introduced it in the late 1980s. It is an infinite-dimensional generalization of Morse theory: a chain complex is generated by the critical points of an action functional on an infinite-dimensional space (such as the space of loops or connections), and the differential counts gradient flow lines between them — which correspond to solutions of an elliptic partial differential equation, typically pseudoholomorphic curves.

Varieties

  • Symplectic Floer homology — generated by periodic orbits of a Hamiltonian on a symplectic manifold; used by Floer to prove cases of the Arnold conjecture on fixed points of symplectomorphisms.
  • Lagrangian Floer homology — generated by intersection points of two Lagrangian submanifolds, with the differential counting pseudoholomorphic strips; central to the Fukaya category and homological mirror symmetry.
  • Instanton Floer homology — for 3-manifolds, generated by flat connections, with gradient flow given by anti-self-dual instantons on a cylinder; a tool for studying homology spheres and knot invariants such as Khovanov–Floer theories.
  • Seiberg–Witten Floer homology — built from solutions of the Seiberg–Witten equations; Kronheimer and Mrowka's monopole Floer homology forms a foundation for much of modern gauge-theoretic topology.
  • Heegaard Floer homology — introduced by Ozsváth and Szabó, a combinatorial-geometric invariant of 3-manifolds and knots related to symplectic Floer homology of symmetric products.

Historical development

Floer's ideas grew out of work of Conley and Zehnder, Gromov's theory of pseudoholomorphic curves, and Witten's Morse-theoretic interpretation of supersymmetric quantum mechanics. Despite early analytic difficulties — transversality, compactness and bubbling, and orientation — later work by many mathematicians placed the theories on rigorous foundations, and they became among the most powerful invariants in geometry and topology.
