Botany

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Botany, also called plant science or phytology, is the branch of biology that studies plants — their structure, growth, reproduction, metabolism, development, diseases, chemical properties and evolution, and their relationships with the environment. It spans organisms from microscopic algae to the giant redwoods, and includes fungi in traditional (though now separated) usage.

Plants underpin nearly all life on land: Photosynthesis by plants, algae and cyanobacteria produces the oxygen in the atmosphere and the base of most food chains. Botany's practical branches — agriculture, horticulture and forestry — supply food, medicine, timber and fibres for humanity, and plant science is central to food security and climate responses.

History

Theophrastus (c. 371–287 BC), a student of Aristotle, wrote the first systematic plant treatises. Herbal traditions in China, India and the Islamic world accumulated pharmacological knowledge for millennia. Carl Linnaeus built his classification first on plants; the 19th century added plant physiology and ecology, and the 20th plant genetics (Barbara McClintock's maize work) and molecular biology.

Subdisciplines

Plant anatomy and morphology, physiology, taxonomy and systematics, plant ecology, paleobotany, ethnobotany and economic botany. Modern work uses genomics to breed disease-resistant and climate-tolerant crops and to understand how plants sense and signal — their own hormones, defence chemicals and root–fungus networks.
