Ribose

IUPAC name(2R,3R,4S,5R)-5-(hydroxymethyl)oxolane-2,3,4-triol (cyclic form)
Chemical formulaC5H10O5
Molar mass150.13 g/mol
AppearanceWhite solid
Melting point95 °C (203 °F)
Solubility in waterVery soluble

Ribose is a simple sugar and carbohydrate with the molecular formula C5H10O5. It is a pentose — a monosaccharide with five carbon atoms — and an aldose, bearing an aldehyde group in its open-chain form. The naturally occurring form, D-ribose, is a fundamental component of the ribonucleotides from which RNA is built, and derivatives of ribose occur in ATP, NADH, cyclic AMP and many coenzymes.

Structure

In aqueous solution ribose exists mainly as a five-membered furanose ring, formed when the aldehyde carbon reacts with the hydroxyl group on carbon 4. The ring puckers out of plane, adopting envelope and twist conformations that influence the shape of RNA strands. Ribose differs from its close relative deoxyribose by the presence of a hydroxyl group on the 2′ carbon; deoxyribose, lacking that group, forms the sugar backbone of DNA and confers greater chemical stability on it.

Occurrence and biosynthesis

Ribose is produced in cells through the pentose phosphate pathway, which converts glucose-6-phosphate into ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis. The pathway is especially active in rapidly dividing cells. Free ribose itself is uncommon in nature; it is almost always encountered phosphorylated or as part of nucleotides, nucleosides and RNA.

History

Ribose was first reported in 1891 by Emil Fischer, who obtained it from the hydrolysis of gum arabic components and named it as an anagram-like rearrangement of "arabinose", the sugar from which its chemistry was derived. Phoebus Levene and others later established its role as the sugar of nucleic acids in the early 20th century.

Uses

D-ribose is marketed as a dietary supplement, promoted for athletic performance and recovery and investigated in connection with cardiac energy metabolism, though evidence for performance benefits is limited. The modified sugar D-ribose analogues and ribose derivatives are important in pharmaceutical chemistry; the antiviral drug ribavirin is a ribonucleoside analogue.
