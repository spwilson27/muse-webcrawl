Philosophy of education

Philosophy of education
Key questions: What is education? What are its aims? What should be taught, and how?
Key figures: Plato, Aristotle, Rousseau, Dewey, Freire, Montessori, Nel Noddings
Branches: Analytic philosophy of education; Continental approaches; feminist and critical pedagogy

The philosophy of education is the branch of applied philosophy that investigates the nature, aims and problems of education. It draws on ethics, epistemology, metaphysics and political philosophy to ask what education is, what knowledge is most worth teaching, how learning happens, and what role schools should play in a just society.

History

Educational thinking is as old as philosophy itself: Plato's Republic set out an ideal curriculum for guardians, and Aristotle treated education as essential to the polis. Rousseau's Émile (1762) revolutionised thought by centring education on the child's nature. In the twentieth century John Dewey's pragmatism recast schooling as learning by doing within a democratic community, while Maria Montessori and others developed child-centred methods grounded in observation.

Main themes

Central topics include the aims of education (knowledge for its own sake, vocational preparation, personal autonomy, citizenship, or social transformation); curriculum theory (what counts as knowledge and whose knowledge is included); the nature of teaching and learning; indoctrination versus education; educational equality and justice; and the authority of teachers and the state. Analytic philosophers such as R. S. Peters and Israel Scheffler analysed concepts like education, teaching and learning, while critical theorists — above all Paulo Freire in Pedagogy of the Oppressed (1968) — portrayed traditional "banking" education as an instrument of oppression and advocated dialogical, liberating pedagogy.

Relation to practice

Philosophy of education informs debates over school choice, testing, religious education, sex education, and the purpose of universities. It is studied both as an academic discipline within philosophy departments and as a foundation course in teacher education.
