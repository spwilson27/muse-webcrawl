Nian Rebellion

The Nian Rebellion (1851–1868) was a large armed uprising in northern China against the Qing dynasty, contemporaneous with the Taiping Rebellion in the south. The Nian — the word refers to bands or gangs — were loose confederations of smugglers, peasants, and outlaws from the Huai River region of Anhui, Henan, Jiangsu, and Shandong.

Course

Catastrophic Yellow River flooding in 1851 and 1855, together with Qing neglect, drove the Nian into open revolt under leaders such as Zhang Lexing. Unlike the Taiping, the Nian had no unified ideology or capital; their strength was highly mobile cavalry raiding across the North China Plain. They allied at times with Taiping forces, and after the Taiping fell in 1864 the combined remnants posed a renewed threat.

Suppression

The Qing commander Sengge Rinchen was killed fighting the Nian in 1865. Zeng Guofan and then Li Hongzhang, using modern-armed Huai Army troops and a strategy of fortified lines and river blockades, split the Nian into eastern and western groups and destroyed them by 1868. The rebellion, with the Taiping and other revolts, gravely weakened the Qing state.
