Reasonable doubt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Reasonable doubt is the standard of proof required in criminal trials in common-law jurisdictions: the prosecution must prove the defendant's guilt “beyond reasonable doubt”, the highest standard in law, reflecting the maxim that it is better that ten guilty persons escape than that one innocent suffer (Blackstone's ratio). The standard is usually explained negatively or by comparison (greater than the civil balance of probabilities; less than absolute certainty), and courts have in general been reluctant to define it with precision — attempts at formula (“moral certainty”, “abiding conviction”, the English direction to be “sure”) have themselves generated appellate litigation (in the US, Cage v. Louisiana, 1990, and Victor v. Nebraska, 1994).

Empirical studies asking judges and jurors to quantify the standard typically place it in the range of 85–95 per cent certainty, with wide dispersion — the vagueness being, defenders say, the price of a moral rather than statistical judgment, and, critics say, an invitation to inconsistency. The standard's roots lie in the theology-soaked jury trial of the eighteenth century (Whitman's thesis: it originated to protect jurors' souls when convicting), and its modern form anchors the presumption of innocence in Article 14 of the ICCPR and in national constitutions worldwide.
