Katsushika Hokusai

Katsushika Hokusai (Japanese: 葛飾 北斎; c. 31 October 1760 – 10 May 1849) was a Japanese ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period, celebrated as the creator of The Great Wave off Kanagawa and the print series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji. Working into his late eighties, he was an enormously prolific draughtsman — his output is estimated at some 30,000 works — and changed his art name more than thirty times over his career.

Life and career

Born in the Katsushika district of Edo, Hokusai began painting as a child and was apprenticed to a woodblock engraver before entering the studio of Katsukawa Shunshō, a master of actor prints. After Shunshō's death he was expelled from the school for studying rival styles, an episode he later called the making of his art. Through middle age he produced book illustrations, surimono and the hugely popular sketchbooks known as the Hokusai Manga.

His greatest fame came in his seventies with the landscape series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji (c. 1830–1832), which capitalised on the new imported Prussian blue pigment and on a domestic travel boom. The Great Wave off Kanagawa, showing tiny boats beneath a claw-like wave with Fuji small in the distance, has become one of the most reproduced images in the world. He followed it with other celebrated series, including A Tour of the Waterfalls of the Provinces and Thirty-six Views successors.

Legacy

Hokusai's compositions, transmitted to Europe through imported prints, profoundly influenced the Japonisme of the French Impressionists and Post-Impressionists; Van Gogh and Monet collected his work, and Debussy used the Great Wave on the cover of La mer. Famously, Hokusai declared that everything he made before seventy was not worth counting, and that at 110 "every dot and line will be alive". The Sumida Hokusai Museum in Tokyo, opened in 2016, is devoted to his work.
