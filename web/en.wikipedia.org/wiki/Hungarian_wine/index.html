Hungarian wine

Hungarian wine has a history dating back to the Roman Empire. Hungary has 22 designated wine regions and is best known internationally for the sweet white wine Tokaji (from the Tokaj wine region of northeast Hungary), for the red blend Egri Bikavér ("Bull's Blood of Eger"), and for dry Furmint and other whites from volcanic soils around Lake Balaton and Somló. The Hungarian word for wine, bor, is of Turkic origin, suggesting viticulture was learned from peoples encountered during the Magyar migrations.

Vineyards of the Tokaj region, a UNESCO World Heritage cultural landscape.

History

Vines were cultivated in Roman Pannonia; after the Magyar conquest, monastic orders expanded planting, and Italian and Walloon settlers brought expertise. The Ottoman occupation (1541–1699) depopulated much of the country but Tokaj, outside Ottoman control, rose to fame: its aszú wines, made from botrytis-affected grapes, were protected by one of the world's earliest appellation systems, delimited in 1737. Phylloxera devastated vineyards in the late 19th century; quality then suffered under communist-era mass production. Since 1989, investment, small estates, and renewed attention to indigenous varieties have driven a quality revival.

Regions and styles

  • Tokaj — aszú and dry wines from Furmint and Hárslevelű on volcanic soils.
  • Eger — Bikavér red blends based on Kékfrankos; also the white Egri Leányka.
  • Villány — full-bodied reds, especially Cabernet Franc, in the warm south.
  • Balaton — whites such as Olaszrizling and the volcanic Kéknyelű of Badacsony.
  • Sopron — Kékfrankos reds near the Austrian border; Szekszárd — historic Bikavér rival to Eger; Somló — tiny volcanic region noted for Juhfark.

Grape varieties

Indigenous and regional varieties include Furmint, Hárslevelű, Kékfrankos (Blaufränkisch), Kadarka, Olaszrizling (Welschriesling), Irsai Olivér, Cserszegi Fűszeres, Juhfark, and Kéknyelű, alongside international varieties such as Chardonnay, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, and Pinot Noir.
