Saint Nicholas of Myra — Nicholas the Wonderworker — patron of children and sailors.

Saint Nicholas of Myra (270–343), also known as Nicholas the Wonderworker, was a Christian bishop of Greek descent in the ancient port of Myra in Asia Minor during the Roman Empire. Renowned for his generosity — above all the secret gift of dowries to three impoverished girls — he became one of the most popular saints in Christendom and the historical model for Santa Claus.

Life and legends

Few verifiable facts survive beyond his episcopate at Myra, imprisonment under Diocletian's persecution, and release under Constantine; tradition also places him at the Council of Nicaea (325), where he is said to have struck the heretic Arius. The most famous legend tells how he learned of a poor man who could not afford dowries for his three daughters and so, on three nights, threw bags of gold through their window (in later versions, down the chimney, landing in stockings hung to dry) — the origin of his secret gift-giving and of the three gold balls that became the pawnbrokers' sign.

Other miracles made him patron of sailors: he calmed a storm at sea and saved ships from wreck. Another tale has him resurrecting three boys murdered and pickled by an innkeeper, shown as small figures in a tub at his feet in art.

Veneration and relics

Nicholas's tomb at Myra drew pilgrims from across the Christian world. In 1087 sailors from Bari seized his relics and carried them to Italy, where the Basilica di San Nicola remains a pilgrimage site for Catholics and Orthodox alike. His feast on 6 December is celebrated with gift-giving to children in much of Europe — the Dutch Sinterklaas — a custom carried to America and transformed into Santa Claus.
