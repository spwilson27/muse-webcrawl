Non-coding RNA


Labelled placeholder: tRNA structure

A non-coding RNA (ncRNA) is a functional RNA molecule that is not translated into a protein. The DNA sequence from which a functional non-coding RNA is transcribed is often called an RNA gene. Abundant and functionally important types of non-coding RNAs include transfer RNAs (tRNAs) and ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs), as well as small RNAs such as microRNAs, siRNAs, piRNAs, snoRNAs, snRNAs, exRNAs, scaRNAs and the long ncRNAs such as Xist and HOTAIR.

The number of non-coding RNAs in the human genome is unknown; recent transcriptomics and bioinformatics studies suggest that there are many thousands of them. Many of the newly identified ncRNAs have unknown functions, while others have known regulatory roles.

History and discovery

Nucleic acids were first discovered in 1868 by Friedrich Miescher, and by 1939 RNA had been implicated in protein synthesis. In the 1950s, the adaptor hypothesis predicted transfer RNA, and ribosomal RNA was identified as the RNA component of ribosomes. The first non-coding RNA characterised was an alanine tRNA found in baker's yeast, the structure of which was published in 1965. In the 1990s the first microRNAs (lin-4 in Caenorhabditis elegans, 1993) were described, transforming understanding of gene regulation.

Classes

Transfer RNA and ribosomal RNA

tRNAs deliver amino acids to the ribosome during translation; rRNAs form the core of the ribosome and catalyse peptide bond formation.

Small regulatory RNAs

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) repress translation or promote degradation of target mRNAs; small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) mediate RNA interference; piwi-interacting RNAs (piRNAs) suppress transposons in germline cells.

Small nuclear and nucleolar RNAs

snRNAs are components of the spliceosome; snoRNAs guide chemical modification of other RNAs, principally rRNAs.

Long non-coding RNAs

lncRNAs are transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides that are not translated into protein. They regulate gene expression through chromatin modification, transcriptional interference, and scaffolding of ribonucleoprotein complexes. Notable examples include Xist, which coats the inactive X chromosome, and HOTAIR.

Roles in disease

Mutations in ncRNA genes and dysregulation of ncRNAs contribute to cancers, neurological disorders and cardiovascular disease. For example, abnormalities in the snoRNA region of chromosome 15q11–13 underlie Prader–Willi syndrome.
