Peaceful Revolution

Mass Monday demonstrations in Leipzig helped bring down the East German regime without violence.

The Peaceful Revolution (German: Friedliche Revolution) was the largely non-violent movement of popular protest in East Germany (the German Democratic Republic) in 1989 and early 1990 that ended the rule of the Socialist Unity Party (SED), brought down the Berlin Wall, and opened the way to German reunification on 3 October 1990.

Course of events

Through 1989, thousands of East Germans fled via Hungary, which had opened its border with Austria, and occupied West German embassies in Prague and Warsaw. Inside the country, prayers for peace at Leipzig's Nikolaikirche grew into the Monday demonstrations, which by late October drew hundreds of thousands of people chanting "Wir sind das Volk" ("We are the people"). Crucially, on 9 October 1989 the authorities in Leipzig refrained from a violent crackdown despite preparations for one.

Leader Erich Honecker was forced out on 18 October and replaced by Egon Krenz. On 9 November, after a bungled press conference by Günter Schabowski announcing new travel rules "immediately", crowds overwhelmed the Berlin Wall checkpoints and the Wall fell. The first free Volkskammer elections followed on 18 March 1990, won by the Alliance for Germany, paving the way for rapid unification.

Significance

The Peaceful Revolution is remembered as a model of successful non-violent civil resistance: disciplined demonstrators carried candles and insisted on "Keine Gewalt" ("no violence"), and the regime dissolved without a civil war or massacre — a sharp contrast with the violent suppression of protests in Beijing the same year.
