Long March (rocket family)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Long March rockets (Chinese: 长征系列运载火箭) are a family of expendable launch systems operated by China, named for the Red Army's Long March of 1934–35. Since 1970 they have flown every category of Chinese mission — the Dong Fang Hong I satellite that made China the fifth spacefaring nation, the Shenzhou crewed programme on the human-rated Long March 2F, the Chang'e lunar missions, and the modules of the Tiangong space station on the heavy Long March 5B.

The family spans small solid-fuelled quick-response rockets to the Long March 5, China's most powerful (about 25 tonnes to LEO), which also launched the Tianwen-1 Mars mission. A new generation — the Long March 10 for crewed lunar flight and reusable concepts under development — is intended to carry China's 2030 Moon-landing goal.

Record

With more than 500 launches by the mid-2020s and a high recent flight rate concentrated at four launch centres (Jiuquan, Taiyuan, Xichang, Wenchang), the Long March family is the backbone of the world's second-busiest space programme. Its early export ambitions were curtailed by US export rules in the 1990s, after which China's launch industry developed in near-total independence — the condition that produced today's parallel Chinese space ecosystem under the China Manned Space Agency and CASC.
