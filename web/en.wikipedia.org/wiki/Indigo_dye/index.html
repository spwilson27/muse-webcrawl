Indigo dye

Indigo dye is an organic compound with a distinctive blue colour. Historically, indigo was a natural dye extracted from the leaves of certain plants, most importantly species of Indigofera such as Indigofera tinctoria, and it was traded around the world as a precious commodity. Today the dye is produced synthetically and is best known as the colourant of blue denim jeans.

History

Indigo is one of the oldest dyes known: indigo-dyed textiles have been found in ancient Egyptian tombs and in pre-Columbian Peru. In the colonial era, indigo plantations in India, the Caribbean and the American South made it a major cash crop, often produced by enslaved or coerced labour; the Bengal indigo revolt of 1859–60 was a notable protest against oppressive planter practices. In 1880 Adolf von Baeyer determined the chemical structure of indigo, and BASF brought synthetic indigo to market in 1897, collapsing the natural indigo trade.

Chemistry and use

The indigo molecule (C16H10N2O2) is insoluble in water; for dyeing it is reduced to a soluble leuco form ("indigo white"), applied to fibre, and then oxidises in air back to the blue pigment. Because indigo binds only to the surface of yarn, dyed fabric fades characteristically — the effect prized in denim.
