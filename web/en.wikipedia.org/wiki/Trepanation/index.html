Trepanation

Trepanation, also known as trepanning, is a surgical intervention in which a hole is drilled or scraped into the human skull. The intentional perforation of the cranium exposes the dura mater to treat health problems related to intracranial diseases or to release pressured blood buildup from an injury. It may also refer to any "burr" hole created through other body surfaces, including nail beds.

History

Trepanation is among the oldest surgical procedures for which there is archaeological evidence. Neolithic skulls bearing healed holes — indicating that patients survived — have been found in Europe, and the practice was widespread in pre-Columbian Peru, where large numbers of trepanned skulls show survival rates that some studies estimate at 50–90% in later periods. Ancient Greek and Roman physicians, including Hippocrates and Galen, described the procedure for head injuries, and it remained part of European surgical practice through the Middle Ages and Renaissance.

Reasons

Historically, trepanation was performed for head trauma (to remove bone fragments and relieve pressure), for epilepsy, migraines and mental disorders, and in some cultures for ritual or magical purposes, such as releasing evil spirits. In modern medicine, drilling a burr hole remains a legitimate neurosurgical procedure — for example, to drain a chronic subdural hematoma or to gain access for brain surgery — but it is distinct from voluntary trepanation promoted by a small modern movement, which the medical community considers dangerous and without proven benefit.

Methods

Prehistoric techniques included scraping with flint or obsidian, cutting a rectangular or circular groove, and drilling with a bow drill. Bone regrowth around the edges of excavated holes allows archaeologists to determine whether the patient survived the operation.
