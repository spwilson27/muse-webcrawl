The Five (composers)

Also known asThe Mighty Handful (Russian: Могучая кучка, Moguchaya kuchka)
OriginSt. Petersburg, Russian Empire
Active years1856–1870s (as a circle)
MembersMily Balakirev, César Cui, Modest Mussorgsky, Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov, Alexander Borodin

The Five, also known in Russian as The Mighty Handful (Могучая кучка), were five prominent nineteenth-century Russian composers who worked together from 1856 under the leadership of Mily Balakirev to create a distinctly Russian style of classical music, free of what they saw as stifling Western conservatory conventions. The critic Vladimir Stasov coined the name in 1867 after a concert of Balakirev's music, and popularized the group in his later writings.

Members and formation

The circle formed in St. Petersburg: Balakirev, an accomplished pianist and follower of Glinka, mentored the young officer César Cui (1856) and the teenage naval cadet Modest Mussorgsky (1857); the naval officer Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov joined in 1861 and the chemistry professor Alexander Borodin in 1862. All but Balakirev were essentially self-taught amateurs with careers outside music. They met to play through new works, study scores of Glinka, Schumann, and Liszt, and compose music rooted in Russian folk song, church modes, and national subjects.

Music and legacy

The Five produced much of the core Russian national repertoire: Mussorgsky's opera Boris Godunov and Pictures at an Exhibition; Borodin's Prince Igor and Second Symphony; Rimsky-Korsakov's Scheherazade; and Balakirev's piano fantasy Islamey. Their use of folk melody, modal harmony, whole-tone and octatonic color, and programmatic orchestral music influenced composers from Debussy and Ravel to Stravinsky — a protégé of Rimsky-Korsakov — much as later self-defined movements, down to the G-funk architects of The Chronic, codified a regional school's sound for the world.
