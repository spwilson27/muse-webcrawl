Crystallographic defect

A crystallographic defect is an interruption of the regular periodic arrangement of atoms in a crystalline solid. Real crystals are never perfect, and defects — often present in minute concentrations — can dominate a material's mechanical, electrical and optical properties. Defect engineering is central to metallurgy and to the semiconductor industry, where controlled doping is the deliberate introduction of point defects.

Classification

Defects are classified by dimensionality. Point defects (zero-dimensional) include vacancies, interstitial atoms and substitutional impurities, which occur in pairs such as Frenkel and Schottky defects in ionic crystals. Line defects (one-dimensional) are dislocations — edge, screw and mixed — whose motion underlies plastic deformation. Planar defects (two-dimensional) include grain boundaries, twin boundaries, stacking faults and external surfaces. Volume defects (three-dimensional) include precipitates, voids and inclusions.

Effects

Dislocations explain why metals yield at stresses far below the theoretical strength of a perfect lattice, while impurity atoms enable semiconductor devices and give gemstones their colours — chromium in corundum produces ruby, for example. Thermodynamically, defects are stabilised by entropy, so a truly defect-free crystal cannot exist at any temperature above absolute zero. See also Dislocation and Crystallite.
