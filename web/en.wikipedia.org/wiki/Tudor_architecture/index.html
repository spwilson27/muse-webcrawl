Tudor architecture

The Tudor architectural style is the final development of medieval architecture in England and Wales, during the Tudor period (1485–1603) and even beyond. It followed Perpendicular Gothic and, though superseded by Elizabethan, Jacobean and ultimately Palladian styles at court, remained the vernacular of choice for country houses and Oxbridge colleges for centuries.

Characteristics

Characteristic features include:

  • The four-centred ("Tudor") arch, a flattened pointed arch, used for doorways, windows and arcades.
  • Oriel windows and wide bay windows admitting more light, with rectangular window openings divided by mullions and transoms.
  • Half-timbering (black-and-white work) on upper storeys, often with decorative patterns and jettied overhangs.
  • Tall, ornate brick chimneys, frequently in twisted or clustered stacks, as open fireplaces replaced the central hearth.
  • Long galleries, great halls with hammerbeam roofs, linenfold panelling, and heraldic ornament.
  • Red brick as a prestige material, laid with diaper patterning, as at Hampton Court and St James's Palace.

Examples

The supreme monuments of the style are Henry VII's Chapel at Westminster Abbey and King's College Chapel, Cambridge, with their spectacular fan vaults; secular examples include Compton Wynyates, Layer Marney Tower, Hampton Court, and the merchant houses and guildhalls of towns such as Lavenham and Chester. In the 19th and early 20th centuries the style was revived as Tudorbethan and, in suburbia, as the half-timbered "Mock Tudor" style, which also spread widely in the United States as Tudor Revival architecture.
