Sacred king

In many historical societies, a sacred king is a king who holds a special religious significance for his subjects, serving as an intermediary between the human and divine worlds. The sacred king's person, health and fortune were often believed to be bound up with the fertility of the land and the well-being of the community; in some traditions he might be ritually killed or deposed when his powers waned or after a fixed term.

Frazer's theory

The concept was famously developed by Sir James George Frazer in The Golden Bough (first published 1890), which begins with the priest-king of Nemi — the Rex Nemorensis — who held office until slain by a successor. Frazer gathered parallels from around the world, including divine kings in Africa, to argue that sacred kingship was a widespread early institution in which the king embodied a dying and reviving god of vegetation.

Examples and criticism

Examples often cited include the Pharaoh of ancient Egypt, regarded as divine; the kings of the Shilluk of the Sudan, studied by E. E. Evans-Pritchard; and sacral kingship in early medieval Europe, where kings were anointed and credited with healing powers (the "royal touch"). Later anthropologists have criticised Frazer's sweeping comparative method and questioned whether reported ritual regicides were literal historical practices, but the idea of sacred kingship remains influential in the study of religion and political theology.
