Sirolimus

TypeMacrolide, immunosuppressant
TargetmTOR
SourceStreptomyces hygroscopicus
Also calledRapamycin

Sirolimus, also known as rapamycin, is a macrolide compound produced by the bacterium Streptomyces hygroscopicus, first isolated from soil samples from Easter Island (Rapa Nui). It is used as an immunosuppressant to prevent organ transplant rejection and, in coated coronary stents, to prevent restenosis; its target, mTOR, makes it a subject of research in cancer and ageing.

Mechanism

Sirolimus binds the protein FKBP12, and the resulting complex inhibits the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), a kinase central to cell growth and proliferation. This blocks the response of T and B cells to interleukin-2, suppressing the immune response.

Medical uses

Sirolimus is approved for prophylaxis of kidney transplant rejection and for lymphangioleiomyomatosis, a rare lung disease. Sirolimus-eluting stents reduce re-narrowing of coronary arteries. Related compounds (everolimus, temsirolimus) are used in oncology.

Side effects

Common adverse effects include raised blood lipids, impaired wound healing, mouth ulcers and increased susceptibility to infection. Unlike calcineurin inhibitors it is relatively sparing of the kidneys, though combinations require careful monitoring.
