Lip

The lips are a horizontal pair of soft, mobile, muscular folds surrounding the opening of the mouth in humans and many other animals. In humans the lips serve as a tactile sensory organ, an instrument of articulation in speech, and an opening for food intake; they are also an erogenous zone and a prominent feature of facial expression and aesthetics. Each person has an upper lip (labium superius) and a lower lip (labium inferius). The reddish part of the lips, the vermilion zone, owes its colour to a thin, translucent epidermis over a dense network of capillaries; unlike ordinary skin it has no hair follicles, sweat glands or sebaceous glands, which is why lips dry and chap easily.

Structure

The skin of the lips transitions from thick, hairy facial skin on the outside to the smooth, moist mucous membrane of the oral cavity on the inside; between them lies the vermilion border. Features of the upper lip include the philtrum — the vertical groove running from the nose to the lip — and the central Cupid's bow contour. The core of each lip is the orbicularis oris muscle, into which numerous other facial muscles insert, enabling the fine movements used in speech, eating, whistling and expression. The lips are richly innervated (hence their sensitivity to touch and temperature) and supplied by the labial branches of the facial artery, which can be felt pulsating where they run in the lip.

Functions

Lips are essential to speech articulation — the bilabial sounds /p/, /b/ and /m/ and labiodental sounds /f/ and /v/ are formed with them — and to eating and drinking, sealing the mouth, suckling in infants and manipulating food in many mammals. They contribute to facial expression (smiling, pouting, grimacing) and to social behaviour such as kissing. In other animals lips vary greatly: they are mobile and prehensile in horses and some primates, reduced in many birds and reptiles, and absent as separate structures in most fishes.

Clinical significance

Common conditions affecting the lips include chapping, cold sores (herpes labialis, from herpes simplex virus), angular cheilitis (cracking at the corners), allergic and irritant cheilitis, and actinic cheilitis from sun exposure, which can precede squamous cell carcinoma of the lip — one of the commonest oral cancers, strongly associated with sunlight and pipe smoking. Cleft lip is a common congenital malformation resulting from failure of fusion of the facial processes, affecting about 1 in 700–1,000 births worldwide and repaired surgically in infancy. Bluish discoloration of the lips (central cyanosis) is an important clinical sign of hypoxaemia.

Culture

Lips have been decorated for millennia — lipstick use dates to ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt — and fullness of the lips is widely treated as a feature of attractiveness, influencing fashion and cosmetic procedures such as lip augmentation. The mouth and lips feature prominently in idioms ("read my lips", "tight-lipped") and gestures such as a finger pressed to the lips for silence.
