Earthenware

Earthenware is glazed or unglazed non-vitrified pottery that has normally been fired below 1,200 °C. It is the oldest and most widespread type of pottery, made from common clays that fire to colours ranging from buff and red to brown and black. Because the fired body remains porous, earthenware vessels intended to hold liquids are usually glazed.

Terracotta flowerpots are a familiar form of earthenware.

Characteristics

Earthenware bodies do not vitrify at their relatively low firing temperatures, leaving them comparatively soft, porous and less strong than stoneware or porcelain, but easy to work and cheap to produce. Typical varieties include terracotta (unglazed red ware), tin-glazed earthenware such as maiolica, faience and delftware, creamware and pearlware, and slipware.

History

Pottery vessels first appear in East Asia more than 15,000 years ago, and earthenware dominated world ceramics until the development of high-fired stoneware in China and, later, porcelain. Painted earthenwares such as Greek black- and red-figure pottery, Roman terra sigillata, and Islamic lustreware achieved great refinement. In industrial Britain, potters such as Josiah Wedgwood developed refined earthenware bodies like creamware ("Queen's ware") for the table. Today earthenware remains the standard material for flowerpots, roof tiles, bricks and much studio and folk pottery.
