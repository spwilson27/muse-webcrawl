Paleomagnetism

Paleomagnetism (or palaeomagnetism) is the study of the record of Earth's magnetic field in rocks, sediments or archaeological materials. Magnetic minerals in rocks can lock in a record of the direction and intensity of the magnetic field when they form — a remanent magnetisation — providing a record that extends back billions of years and can be dated using stratigraphic and radiometric methods.

Methods

Rocks acquire magnetisation in several ways: igneous rocks acquire thermoremanent magnetisation as they cool through the Curie temperature of their magnetic minerals; sediments acquire detrital remanence as magnetic grains settle; and chemical changes can add a chemical remanence. Paleomagnetists measure samples with magnetometers and progressively "clean" them by stepwise thermal or alternating-field demagnetisation to isolate the oldest, primary signal.

Applications

Measurements of ancient field direction give the latitude at which a rock formed, and comparisons between continents produce apparent polar wander paths, used to reconstruct past plate motions. The symmetric pattern of marine magnetic anomalies — stripes of normal and reversed polarity on the ocean floor generated by geomagnetic reversals — provided decisive evidence in the 1960s for seafloor spreading and plate tectonics. The reversal record is calibrated into the geomagnetic polarity time scale, widely used to date young rocks and sediments (magnetostratigraphy), and paleointensity studies inform models of the geodynamo.
