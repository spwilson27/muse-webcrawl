Newtonian fluid

A Newtonian fluid is a fluid in which the viscous stresses arising from its flow are at every point linearly correlated to the local strain rate — the rate of change of its deformation over time. Equivalently, the viscosity of a Newtonian fluid depends only on temperature and pressure, not on the forces acting upon it.

Definition

For a simple shear flow, Newton's law of viscosity states that the shear stress τ is proportional to the shear rate:

τ = μ (du/dy)

where μ is the dynamic viscosity, a constant for a given fluid at fixed temperature. Fluids obeying this law are named after Isaac Newton, who first formulated the relationship. In tensor form, the viscous stress is an isotropic linear function of the rate-of-strain tensor.

Examples

  • Water
  • Air and most gases
  • Light oils and petrol
  • Alcohol and glycerine (approximately)

Most common everyday fluids are Newtonian, which makes the Navier–Stokes equations far more tractable. By contrast, non-Newtonian fluids — such as blood, ketchup, paint, cornstarch suspensions and most polymer solutions — have a viscosity that varies with the applied shear rate or shear stress, giving rise to shear-thinning, shear-thickening, thixotropic or viscoelastic behaviour.

Temperature dependence

For liquids, viscosity generally decreases rapidly with temperature; for gases, it increases roughly with the square root of absolute temperature. This difference reflects the different molecular mechanisms of momentum transfer in the two phases.
