Pseudotachylite

Pseudotachylite (also spelled pseudotachylyte) is a dark, dense, glassy or very fine-grained rock that forms veins and networks in fault zones and impact structures. It superficially resembles the volcanic glass tachylite, hence the name, but originates not from magma: the classic interpretation is that it is produced by frictional melting of rock during rapid slip on a fault, typically during an earthquake, and then quenching almost instantaneously.

Formation

During seismic slip, frictional heating along the fault surface can raise temperatures enough to melt a thin layer of the wall rock, sometimes estimated to exceed 1,000 °C. The melt is injected into fractures branching off the main fault surface, forming generation surfaces and injection veins, and solidifies so quickly that it develops a glassy or aphanitic texture, often containing rounded clasts of unmelted wall rock and microlites or spherulites formed during crystallization. Some pseudotachylites may instead form by cataclasis without melting — a distinction debated in the literature — but melting textures are widely documented.

Occurrence and significance

Pseudotachylites are classic indicators of paleo-earthquakes in exhumed fault zones, and they occur in major structures such as the Vredefort impact structure in South Africa, where very large volumes are associated with impact-related faulting and shock, and along large strike-slip and thrust faults worldwide. Because they record the physical conditions of ancient seismic slip, they are studied extensively in earthquake geology and fault mechanics.
