Reformed theology

Reformed theology (Calvinism)
Key figures: Huldrych Zwingli, John Calvin, John Knox, Jonathan Edwards
Confessions: Westminster Confession, Belgic Confession, Heidelberg Catechism
Distinctives: Five solae, five points (TULIP), covenant theology

Reformed theology, also known as Calvinism, is the system of Christian theology associated with the Reformed churches of the Protestant Reformation. Rooted in the work of Huldrych Zwingli in Zürich and systematised by John Calvin in Geneva — above all in his Institutes of the Christian Religion (1536–1559) — it spread to Scotland, the Netherlands, France, Hungary, and New England.

Distinctives

Reformed theology shares the Reformation's five solae (Scripture alone, faith alone, grace alone, Christ alone, to the glory of God alone) and emphasises the absolute sovereignty of God. Its summary in the five points of Calvinism (TULIP), drawn from the Canons of Dort (1619), teaches total depravity, unconditional election, limited (definite) atonement, irresistible grace, and perseverance of the saints.

Covenant and sacraments

Covenant theology reads the Bible through the covenants of works and grace, underpinning practices such as infant baptism. Reformed teaching affirms two sacraments — baptism and the Lord's Supper — and, against Lutheran and Catholic views, holds to a spiritual (pneumatic) presence of Christ in the Supper rather than a bodily one. Church government by elders (presbyterian) or consistories, a regulated approach to worship, and rich confessional documents such as the Westminster Standards and the Three Forms of Unity are further marks.

Legacy

Reformed ideas profoundly shaped Presbyterian, Dutch Reformed, Congregational, and many Baptist churches, and influenced political thought on resistance to tyranny, education, and vocation. Major later figures include Jonathan Edwards, Abraham Kuyper, and Karl Barth.
