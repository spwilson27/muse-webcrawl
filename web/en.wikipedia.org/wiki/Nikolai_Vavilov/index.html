Nikolai Vavilov

Nikolai Ivanovich Vavilov (1887–1943) was a Russian and Soviet biologist whose expeditions and theories transformed the study of crop diversity, and who died in prison as a victim of Stalinism and the pseudoscience of his rival Trofim Lysenko.

Work

Vavilov directed the Institute of Plant Industry (VIR) in Leningrad, organising more than a hundred collecting expeditions to some 64 countries between 1916 and 1940. From the patterns of diversity he found, he formulated the theory of centres of origin of cultivated plants — regions such as the Near East, Ethiopia, Central Asia, China, Mesoamerica and the Andes where crops were first domesticated and where their wild relatives and greatest genetic variety survive. His institute assembled a collection of some 250,000–350,000 seed samples, the largest in the world, and his law of homologous series in variation (1922) predicted the forms new crop varieties would take. During the siege of Leningrad, staff of the institute famously starved rather than eat the collection, preserving it for posterity.

Persecution and death

Vavilov's defence of Mendelian genetics brought him into conflict with Lysenko, whose Lamarckian doctrines won Stalin's favour. Vavilov was dismissed from his posts, arrested in 1940 while collecting in Ukraine, sentenced to death (later commuted) on fabricated espionage charges, and died of starvation in Saratov prison in January 1943. He was posthumously rehabilitated in 1955, and the institute in St Petersburg now bears his name; his centres-of-origin concept still underpins global seed-bank strategy.
