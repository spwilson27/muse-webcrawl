Fore-and-aft rig

A Bermuda-rigged sloop, the most common modern fore-and-aft configuration.

A fore-and-aft rig is a sailing vessel rig with sails set mainly along the line of the keel, rather than across it like the square rig. Such sails are described as fore-and-aft rigged, and can be set on stays (staysails and jibs) or attached to masts and spars (gaff sails, Bermuda sails, lateen sails, spritsails and lug sails).

Fore-and-aft rigs allow a vessel to sail closer to the wind (points of sail "in irons" excluded, a well-trimmed fore-and-aft vessel can sail within roughly 30–45 degrees of the wind direction), and are far easier to handle with a small crew than square rigs, which need many hands to work the yards. For these reasons the fore-and-aft rig dominates modern yachting and most small working craft.

Types

  • Bermuda (Marconi) rig — a tall triangular mainsail laced to the mast and boom; standard on racing and cruising yachts.
  • Gaff rig — a four-sided sail whose head is supported by a spar called a gaff; common on traditional craft.
  • Lateen rig — a triangular sail on a long yard crossing the mast, used for millennia in the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean.
  • Lug rig — a four-sided sail on a yard that crosses the mast, found on many traditional fishing boats.
  • Spritsail rig — a four-sided sail supported by a diagonal spar (the sprit), as on the Thames sailing barge.
  • Junk rig — fully battened sails fanning from the mast, traditional in China.

History

Lateen-rigged vessels plied the Mediterranean in antiquity, and fore-and-aft sails appear in many independent maritime traditions, including the Pacific proa's crab-claw sail. In northern Europe fore-and-aft sails were long used only as auxiliary steering sails (the spritsail under the bowsprit, the mizzen lateen), but by the 17th and 18th centuries they took over on small and medium vessels — sloops, cutters, schooners and brigantines — while large ocean-going ships kept square rigs for downwind ocean passages. The schooner's fore-and-aft rig made it a favourite of fishermen and coasters. By the early 20th century the Bermuda rig had become the standard for yachts.
