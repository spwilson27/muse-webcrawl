Ethnoastronomy

Ethnoastronomy is the anthropological study of the astronomical knowledge, beliefs and practices of contemporary cultures, especially indigenous peoples. Together with archaeoastronomy, which studies the astronomy of past societies through material remains, it forms the field sometimes called cultural astronomy.

Scope

Ethnoastronomers document how living cultures name and group stars and constellations, construct calendars from solar, lunar and stellar cycles, navigate by the sky, and embed celestial phenomena in mythology, ritual and agricultural practice. Because the knowledge is usually oral, the field relies on ethnographic fieldwork with culture-bearers. Well-studied examples include the star lore of Aboriginal Australian peoples — including "dark cloud" constellations such as the Emu in the Sky formed by dust lanes of the Milky Way — Polynesian wayfinding by star compasses, the Maya Venus cycle recorded in the Dresden Codex tradition, and Inuit star names used for seasonal timekeeping.

History of the field

The term came into use in the 1970s as scholars such as Elizabeth Carlson Chesley Baity (whose 1973 survey coined much of the vocabulary) sought a framework for studying folk astronomy systematically. Journals and societies devoted to cultural astronomy, and the inclusion of indigenous sky knowledge in planetarium programming and heritage protection (including UNESCO's Astronomy and World Heritage initiative), have raised the field's profile.

Methods

Researchers combine interviews and participant observation with sky simulation software to test claimed alignments and risings, and increasingly work collaboratively with indigenous communities, observing protocols about restricted knowledge and intellectual property in traditional star lore.
