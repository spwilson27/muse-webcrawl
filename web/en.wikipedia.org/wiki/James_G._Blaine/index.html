Rise in politics

James Gillespie Blaine (31 January 1830 – 27 January 1893) was an American statesman and the dominant Republican of the Gilded Age. Settling in Maine as a newspaper editor, he rose through the Maine legislature to Congress (1863), becoming Speaker of the House (1869–1875) and then senator (1876–1881). Dubbed "the Plumed Knight" by Robert Ingersoll's nominating speech in 1876, Blaine sought the Republican presidential nomination repeatedly; his career was dogged by the Mulligan letters scandal, implicating him in railway favour-trading.

The 1884 campaign

Blaine finally won the nomination in 1884 and lost narrowly to Grover Cleveland in one of the most vitriolic campaigns in U.S. history. Reform Republicans ("Mugwumps") defected over Blaine's corruption taint, and a supporter's "Rum, Romanism, and Rebellion" slur against Democrats — which Blaine failed to disavow — probably cost him New York and the election.

Secretary of State

Briefly Secretary of State under Garfield (1881), Blaine returned under Benjamin Harrison (1889–1892) and pursued an energetic foreign policy: the first Pan-American Conference (1889), reciprocal trade agreements, and assertive expansion of American influence in Latin America and the Pacific — an early architecture of U.S. hemispheric leadership. Failing health forced his resignation in June 1892; he died the following January.
