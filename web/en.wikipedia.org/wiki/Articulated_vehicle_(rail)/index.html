Articulated vehicle (rail)

Articulated rail vehicle
DefinitionRail vehicle whose adjacent body sections share a common bogie (truck)
Key featureJacobs bogie at the joint between sections
Common usesHigh-speed trains (TGV), trams and light rail, freight wagons

An articulated rail vehicle is a railway vehicle in which two adjacent body sections rest on a single shared bogie (truck) at their joint, rather than each section having its own pair of bogies. The shared bogie most commonly used is the Jacobs bogie, named after German railway engineer Wilhelm Jakobs (1858–1942). Articulation is distinct from the articulation of articulated locomotives, whose engine units pivot.

Advantages

  • Fewer bogies: each joint eliminates one bogie, reducing weight, cost, and maintenance.
  • Smoother running: the shared bogie constrains the joint, improving stability and ride quality, especially at high speed.
  • Lower floor and better aerodynamics: fewer bogies and smaller gaps between cars simplify continuous low floors and sealed gangways, reducing drag and noise.
  • Safety: articulated trainsets resist jackknifing in derailments because the cars are semi-permanently coupled.

Disadvantages

The sections of an articulated trainset cannot readily be separated, so the train's length is effectively fixed and the whole set must visit a workshop with suitable facilities for bogie work. A failure in one section can sideline the entire set, and operators cannot easily add or remove cars to match demand.

Applications

France's TGV high-speed trains are the best-known example: their trailer cars share Jacobs bogies, contributing to their smooth high-speed performance and to the remarkable outcome that no passengers have been killed in derailments of in-service TGVs. Articulation is also near-universal in modern multi-section trams and light-rail vehicles, where shared bogies allow low floors and tight curves, and is used in some freight wagons (for example, double-stack container well cars in articulated sets) and in trainsets such as Denmark's IC3 and Germany's Talent multiple units.
