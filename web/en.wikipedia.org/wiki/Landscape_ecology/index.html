Landscape ecology

Landscape ecology is the science of studying and improving relationships between ecological processes in the environment and particular ecosystems. This is done within a variety of landscape scales, development spatial patterns, and organizational levels of research and policy. The term was coined by the German geographer Carl Troll in 1939.

As a discipline, landscape ecology emphasises spatial heterogeneity: how the arrangement of patches, corridors, and the surrounding matrix influences flows of energy, materials, and organisms. The patch-corridor-matrix model, popularised by Richard Forman, is a central framework.

Core concepts

  • Patch — a relatively homogeneous area that differs from its surroundings.
  • Corridor — a linear strip of habitat that connects patches, such as a hedgerow or riparian strip.
  • Matrix — the dominant land-cover type in which patches and corridors are embedded.
  • Scale — the spatial and temporal dimensions at which patterns and processes are observed.
  • Connectivity — the degree to which the landscape facilitates or impedes movement among patches.

Applications

Landscape ecology informs conservation planning, reserve design, habitat restoration, urban planning, and the management of agricultural and forest landscapes. Tools such as geographic information systems (GIS) and remote sensing are used to quantify landscape metrics like patch size, edge density, and fragmentation.
