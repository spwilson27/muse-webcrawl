Edward Earl Hazel (10 April 1950 – 23 December 1992) was an American guitarist and singer in the funk bands Parliament-Funkadelic. He is best known for the ten-minute guitar solo "Maggot Brain" (1971), one of the most celebrated guitar performances in popular music, and is regarded as a pioneer of psychedelic funk guitar, with a style often compared to Jimi Hendrix.

Early life

Hazel was born in Brooklyn and raised in Plainfield, New Jersey. He played guitar from childhood and, with friend Billy "Bass" Nelson, formed the core of the band that became Funkadelic when they joined George Clinton's doo-wop group The Parliaments as its backing band in the late 1960s.

Career

As Funkadelic fused funk with Hendrix-influenced rock, Hazel's playing — emotive, effects-laden, moving between blues feeling and free-form psychedelia — became central to the band's sound on albums such as Funkadelic (1970), Free Your Mind... and Your Ass Will Follow (1970) and Maggot Brain (1971). According to legend, Clinton, recording "Maggot Brain", told Hazel to play as if he had been told his mother had died; the resulting solo was mixed to dominate the track over a subdued band accompaniment.

Hazel's role in the group fluctuated through the 1970s amid contractual disputes and drug problems; he was briefly jailed after an altercation, contributed to The Temptations' recordings, and released one solo album, Game, Dames and Guitar Thangs (1977), which included a noted cover of "California Dreamin'". He returned for later Parliament-Funkadelic projects, including work on the P-Funk All Stars' recordings and Clinton's solo albums.

Death and legacy

Hazel died on 23 December 1992 of internal bleeding and liver failure after a long struggle with stomach problems. "Maggot Brain" is routinely ranked among the greatest guitar solos of all time, and Hazel has been cited as an influence by guitarists across funk, rock and hip-hop; he was posthumously inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1997 as a member of Parliament-Funkadelic.
