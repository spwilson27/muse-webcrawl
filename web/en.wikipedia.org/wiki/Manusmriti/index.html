The Manusmriti (Sanskrit: मनुस्मृति), also known as the Mānava-Dharmaśāstra or "Laws of Manu," is one of the best-known and most influential Sanskrit texts of the Dharmaśāstra tradition — the body of Hindu legal and ethical treatises. Purporting to be the remembered teaching (smṛti) of Manu, the mythical progenitor of humankind, delivered through the sage Bhrigu, it lays down dharma — religious and social duty — covering the varna (class) system, the stages of life (āśrama), kingship, civil and criminal law, marriage, diet, ritual purity, and penances.

Composition

Modern scholarship, notably Patrick Olivelle's critical edition (2005), holds the text to be the work of a single author or a small group working around the 2nd–3rd century CE, though incorporating older material from perhaps the 2nd century BCE. Written in śloka verse across twelve chapters, the Manusmriti became the most cited of the Dharmaśāstras, with at least nine major medieval commentaries, including Medhātithi's.

Influence and colonial use

Sir William Jones's English translation (1794), one of the first Sanskrit works rendered into a European language, led British administrators to treat the Manusmriti as a statutory "law code" for Hindus — a role many scholars consider a distortion of its character as a normative, aspirational treatise rather than a record of actual practice.

Criticism

The text's hierarchical prescriptions — its graded penalties by caste and its restrictive rules for women and Śūdras — have drawn intense criticism. In 1927, B. R. Ambedkar publicly burned a copy of the Manusmriti at Mahad as a protest against caste oppression, an act commemorated by Dalit movements; defenders within the tradition argue over the text's interpretation and historical context. It remains a touchstone in debates about Hindu law, caste, and social reform.
