Health effects of coffee

The health effects of coffee have been studied extensively. In general, moderate coffee consumption — typically three to four cups per day — is associated in large observational studies with neutral or modestly beneficial outcomes, including lower risks of type 2 diabetes, Parkinson's disease, liver disease and overall mortality, though causality is not firmly established.

Effects

Coffee's chief active ingredient is caffeine, a stimulant that increases alertness but can disrupt sleep, raise anxiety and cause dependence with withdrawal symptoms. Unfiltered coffee contains diterpenes (cafestol and kahweol) that can raise LDL cholesterol; filtering largely removes them. Coffee can aggravate reflux and, in pregnancy, high intake is advised against.

Cancer classification

In 2016 the International Agency for Research on Cancer removed coffee from its list of possible carcinogens, concluding the evidence did not show a carcinogenic effect; drinking very hot beverages of any kind was separately classified as probably carcinogenic.

Research limits

Most evidence comes from observational cohorts subject to confounding, and genetic differences in caffeine metabolism modify individual effects.
