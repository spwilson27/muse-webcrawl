Jan Pieterszoon Coen

Jan Pieterszoon Coen ([jɑn ˈpitərzoːn kun]; 8 January 1587 – 21 September 1629) was an officer of the Dutch East India Company (VOC) in the early 17th century, holding two terms as governor-general of the Dutch East Indies. He founded Batavia (now Jakarta) and was the architect, through force, of the VOC's spice monopoly in the East Indies.

Early career

Born in Hoorn and trained in Rome in bookkeeping, Coen joined the VOC in 1607, sailed to the Indies, and in reports urged a ruthless monopoly of the nutmeg, mace and clove trade, writing that trade could not be maintained without war, nor war without trade.

Governor-general

Coen seized Jayakarta in 1619, razed it and built Batavia as the VOC headquarters. In 1621 he conquered the Banda Islands to secure the nutmeg monopoly; most of the Bandanese population — some 14,000 people — were killed, enslaved or deported, an act now described as genocide, with Japanese mercenaries beheading Bandanese leaders. He repopulated the islands with slave-worked nutmeg plantations (perken).

Legacy

Coen died during the Mataram siege of Batavia in 1629. Long honoured in the Netherlands as a national hero ("Daer can in Indiën wat groots verricht worden" — "Something great can be done in the Indies"), his statue in Hoorn has become controversial, and modern assessments stress the violence of Dutch colonisation in Indonesia.
