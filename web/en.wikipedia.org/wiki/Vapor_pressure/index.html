Vapor pressure (or vapour pressure in British English) is the pressure exerted by a vapor in thermodynamic equilibrium with its condensed phases (solid or liquid) at a given temperature in a closed system. It is a measure of a substance's tendency to evaporate: liquids with high vapor pressure at normal temperatures are described as volatile.

Equilibrium vapor pressure

When a liquid is placed in a closed vessel, molecules escape from the surface into the gas phase while others return to the liquid, until the two rates balance. The resulting equilibrium pressure depends almost entirely on temperature, rising steeply — roughly exponentially — as temperature increases. The relationship is described by the Clausius–Clapeyron equation, which links the slope of the vapor-pressure curve to the enthalpy of vaporisation; the simpler Antoine equation is widely used for engineering interpolation.

Boiling point and applications

A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the surrounding atmospheric pressure, which is why water boils at lower temperatures at high altitude. Vapor pressure underlies distillation, the concept of relative humidity (the ratio of the partial pressure of water vapor to its equilibrium vapor pressure), Raoult's law for mixtures, and practical indices such as the Reid vapor pressure used to grade the volatility of petrol.

For solids, the analogous quantity governs sublimation; a solid with appreciable vapor pressure at ordinary temperatures, such as iodine or dry ice, passes directly into the gas phase.
