The Grito de Dolores ("Cry of Dolores"), also known as the Grito de la Independencia, was the call to arms issued by the Roman Catholic priest Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla in the small town of Dolores, near Guanajuato, on the morning of 16 September 1810. It is regarded as the act that began the Mexican War of Independence, and 16 September is celebrated as Mexico's Independence Day.

Events

Hidalgo belonged to a circle of creole conspirators in Querétaro planning a revolt against Spanish rule. When the conspiracy was betrayed, Hidalgo and Ignacio Allende hurried to Dolores, where Hidalgo rang the church bell in the early hours of 16 September to summon his parishioners. From the church steps he delivered a fiery speech — its exact words were not recorded, but it is traditionally held to have ended with cries such as "¡Viva la independencia! ¡Viva la Virgen de Guadalupe! ¡Muera el mal gobierno!" He then raised the banner of the Virgin of Guadalupe and marched with a growing crowd on San Miguel and Guanajuato, igniting an insurgency that, after eleven years of war, achieved Mexican independence in 1821.

Commemoration

Each year on the night of 15 September the President of Mexico re-enacts the Grito from the balcony of the National Palace in Mexico City, ringing the same bell Hidalgo used (transferred to the capital in 1896) and shouting the names of the heroes of independence to a vast crowd in the Zócalo. Governors and mayors perform parallel ceremonies across the country, followed by celebrations on 16 September.
