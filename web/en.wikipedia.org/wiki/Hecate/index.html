Hecate (Ancient Greek: Ἑκάτη, Hekátē) is a goddess in ancient Greek religion and mythology, most often shown holding a pair of torches, a key, or snakes, or accompanied by dogs, and in later periods depicted in triple form. She is variously associated with crossroads, entrance-ways, night, light, magic, witchcraft, the Moon, knowledge of herbs and poisonous plants, graves, ghosts, necromancy, and sorcery.

Origins and early cult

Hecate's origins are debated; her cult may derive from Anatolia (Caria), where theophoric names invoking her are early and where she had a major sanctuary at Lagina. In Hesiod's Theogony she is strikingly honoured by Zeus above all, retaining a share of earth, sea, and sky from the older generation of gods, and bestowing wealth, victory, and wisdom on those she favours — a far more benign figure than her later reputation suggests.

Associations

From the fifth century BC onward Hecate was increasingly linked with the underworld and the restless dead, with Demeter and Persephone (whom she escorted), and with Artemis and Selene as a lunar goddess. Small statues called hekataia stood at crossroads and doorways to avert evil, and food offerings known as "Hecate's suppers" were left at three-way junctions at the dark of the moon. In the Greek Magical Papyri and the Chaldean Oracles she became a supreme cosmic deity and mediator between worlds.

Later influence

Shakespeare made her queen of the witches in Macbeth. In modern times Hecate is a major figure in Wicca and Neopaganism, often venerated as a triple goddess of the crossroads and a patron of witchcraft; the triple-moon symbolism associated with her today partly derives from later reinterpretation.
