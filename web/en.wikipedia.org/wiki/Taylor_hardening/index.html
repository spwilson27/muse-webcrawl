Taylor hardening

Taylor hardening
Field: Materials science, plasticity
Phenomenon: Work hardening of crystals
Named after: G. I. Taylor

Taylor hardening is a model in materials science describing how the strength of a crystalline material increases during plastic deformation because moving dislocations obstruct one another. It was proposed by G. I. Taylor in 1934 as part of his pioneering theory of crystal plasticity.

The model

As a crystal deforms, the dislocation density ρ grows: dislocations multiply through sources such as the Frank–Read mechanism and become entangled, forming "forests" that thread the slip planes. The shear stress τ required to drive a dislocation through this forest rises in proportion to the square root of the density, giving the Taylor equation:

τ = α G b √ρ

where G is the shear modulus, b is the magnitude of the Burgers vector, and α is a dimensionless constant of roughly 0.2–0.5 that captures the strength of dislocation interactions. The square-root dependence on dislocation density is a hallmark of strain hardening in metals and is observed experimentally over wide ranges of strain.

Significance

Taylor hardening connects a measurable macroscopic quantity — the flow stress — to the microscopic evolution of the dislocation substructure, and it forms a basis of modern physically based constitutive models for metals, including descriptions of how dislocation density saturates and how dynamic recovery limits hardening at large strains or elevated temperatures.
