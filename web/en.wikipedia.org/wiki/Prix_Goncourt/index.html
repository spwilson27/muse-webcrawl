The Prix Goncourt (French: Le prix Goncourt) is a prize in French literature, given by the académie Goncourt to the author of "the best and most imaginative prose work of the year". The prize was established in 1903 through the will of Edmond de Goncourt, who wished to create a literary academy and award recognising outstanding French-language prose.

Although the monetary value of the prize is nominal — originally 5,000 francs and today a symbolic cheque of €10 — winning it guarantees the author enormous publicity and a dramatic increase in book sales, typically several hundred thousand copies.

History

Edmond de Goncourt (1822–1896) bequeathed his estate to fund the Académie Goncourt, a society of ten writers. The first prize was awarded in 1903 to John Antoine Nau for his novel Force ennemie. The ten members of the academy, known as les Dix, meet on the first Tuesday of each month at the Restaurant Drouant in Paris, where the winner is traditionally announced in November after a lunch.

Selection

The prize is awarded for a novel published during the current year. Unlike many literary prizes, an author may win the Prix Goncourt only once in a lifetime; an apparent exception was Romain Gary, who won in 1956 for Les Racines du ciel and again in 1975 under the pseudonym Émile Ajar for La Vie devant soi.

Notable winners

Famous recipients include Marcel Proust (1919, À l'ombre des jeunes filles en fleurs), André Malraux (1933, La Condition humaine), Simone de Beauvoir (1954, Les Mandarins), Patrick Modiano (1978, Rue des boutiques obscures), Marguerite Duras (1984, L'Amant), Michel Houellebecq (2010, La Carte et le Territoire), and Leïla Slimani (2016, Chanson douce).

Related prizes

Since 1980, additional Goncourt prizes have been created, including the Prix Goncourt du premier roman (first novel), de la nouvelle (short story), de la poésie and de la biographie. The Prix Renaudot, founded in 1926, is traditionally announced at the same time and place and is often regarded as complementary to the Goncourt.
