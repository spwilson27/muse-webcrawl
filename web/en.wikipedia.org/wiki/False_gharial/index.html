The false gharial (Tomistoma schlegelii), also known by the names Malayan gharial, Sunda gharial, and tomistoma, is a freshwater crocodilian of the family Gavialidae native to Peninsular Malaysia, Borneo, Sumatra, and Java. It is listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List, with the global population estimated at around 2,500 to 10,000 mature individuals.

Description

The false gharial has a characteristically long and slender snout, superficially resembling that of the true gharial, an adaptation for catching fish. Its body is dark brown to blackish in colour, with dark bands and spots on the body and tail, especially in younger animals. Males can grow to lengths of around 5 metres (16 feet), making it among the larger crocodilians, although generally smaller than the saltwater crocodile which shares parts of its range.

Taxonomy

The false gharial was long placed in the family Crocodylidae on morphological grounds, but molecular evidence has consistently shown it to be a close relative of the gharial (Gavialis gangeticus), and it is now generally assigned to Gavialidae. The genus Tomistoma was once more diverse, with fossil species known from Europe, North Africa, and Asia.

Distribution and habitat

The species inhabits freshwater swamps, rivers, and lakes in lowland areas of Southeast Asia, preferring slow-moving waters with dense vegetation. It is shy and reclusive compared with other crocodilians, rarely basking in open areas.

Diet and behaviour

Although its narrow snout suggests a fish specialist, studies of wild individuals have shown a broader diet including small mammals, birds, monkeys, and wild pigs, as well as fish and crustaceans. Females build mound nests of vegetation and lay clutches of roughly 13–35 eggs, which they guard.

Conservation

The main threats to the false gharial are habitat loss through drainage of peat swamp forests, conversion of wetlands to oil palm plantations, and drowning in fishing nets. Captive breeding programmes exist in Malaysia and Indonesia.
