Functor

In mathematics, specifically category theory, a functor is a mapping between categories which preserves their structure: it maps objects to objects and morphisms to morphisms in a way that respects identity morphisms and composition. Functors are the central notion of category theory alongside categories themselves and natural transformations between functors.

Definition

Let C and D be categories. A functor F from C to D assigns to each object X in C an object F(X) in D, and to each morphism f : X → Y in C a morphism F(f) : F(X) → F(Y) in D, subject to two axioms: F preserves identity morphisms, so F(idX) = idF(X), and F preserves composition, so F(g ∘ f) = F(g) ∘ F(f) whenever the composition is defined.

Such a functor is called covariant. A contravariant functor instead reverses the direction of morphisms: F(g ∘ f) = F(f) ∘ F(g). Contravariant functors can equivalently be described as covariant functors from the opposite category.

Examples

The forgetful functor from the category of groups to the category of sets discards the group operation, sending each group to its underlying set. The free functor, left adjoint to it, sends a set to the free group generated by it. In topology, the fundamental group construction is a functor from pointed topological spaces to groups. In linear algebra, the dual space construction is a contravariant functor on vector spaces. The power set construction is a functor on the category of sets in both covariant and contravariant forms.

Properties

Functors may be faithful, full, or fully faithful depending on whether the induced maps on morphism sets are injective, surjective, or bijective. An equivalence of categories is given by a pair of functors that are inverse to one another up to natural isomorphism. Functors that are also equipped with coherent isomorphism data (such as monoidal functors) play a central role in modern algebra, geometry, and theoretical computer science, where functors model type constructors in languages such as Haskell.
