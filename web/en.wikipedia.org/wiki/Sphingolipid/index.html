Sphingolipid

Sphingolipids are a class of lipids defined by the presence of a sphingoid base backbone, most commonly the 18-carbon amino alcohol sphingosine. They are essential components of eukaryotic cell membranes, especially the plasma membrane and the myelin sheaths of nerve cells, and they play important roles in cell signalling, recognition, and apoptosis.

Structure

All sphingolipids share a long-chain sphingoid base. Attachment of a fatty acid to the amino group of sphingosine by an amide bond yields a ceramide, the structural foundation of the family. Addition of different head groups at the primary hydroxyl position gives the main subclasses:

  • Sphingomyelin – head group is phosphocholine (or phosphoethanolamine); abundant in myelin.
  • Glycosphingolipids – head group is one or more sugar residues; includes cerebrosides, globosides, and gangliosides (which bear sialic acid, N-acetylneuraminic acid).
  • Ceramide phosphoinositides – prominent in fungi and plants.

Biosynthesis and metabolism

De novo synthesis begins in the endoplasmic reticulum with the condensation of serine and palmitoyl-CoA by serine palmitoyltransferase, producing 3-ketosphinganine, which is reduced to sphinganine and acylated to dihydroceramide, then desaturated to ceramide. Ceramide can be converted in the Golgi apparatus to sphingomyelin or glycosphingolipids, or phosphorylated to ceramide-1-phosphate. Breakdown occurs in lysosomes through a stepwise series of enzymes, and salvage pathways recycle the sphingosine backbone.

Functions

Sphingolipids cluster with cholesterol in membrane microdomains known as lipid rafts, which organise signalling proteins. Bioactive metabolites such as ceramide, sphingosine, and sphingosine-1-phosphate act as second messengers regulating cell growth, differentiation, senescence, and programmed cell death. Gangliosides on neuronal surfaces mediate cell–cell recognition and serve as receptors for certain toxins and viruses.

Disorders

Defects in lysosomal sphingolipid degradation cause the sphingolipidoses, a group of lysosomal storage diseases that includes Gaucher disease (glucocerebrosidase deficiency), Tay–Sachs disease (hexosaminidase A deficiency), Niemann–Pick disease (sphingomyelinase deficiency), and Fabry disease. Many are treated with enzyme replacement or substrate reduction therapy.
