Jus positivum

Jus positivum (Latin for "positive law") is the body of law laid down or "posited" by human authority, as distinct from jus naturale (natural law), which is understood to be discoverable by reason and to hold universally. Positive law includes enacted statutes, decrees, constitutions and, on many accounts, judge-made law and custom recognised by a legal system.

History of the concept

The distinction between natural and positive law was elaborated by medieval scholastic thinkers. Thomas Aquinas treated human law as deriving from natural law, either by way of conclusions drawn from its principles or by determinatio — the specification of general principles in concrete form, as when natural law requires the punishment of wrongdoers but leaves the particular penalty to human choice. In medieval usage, jus positivum could also refer specifically to law enacted by divine or human authority, such as ceremonial precepts, as opposed to moral precepts grounded in nature.

With the rise of the modern state, the concept became central to theories of sovereignty. In the nineteenth century, John Austin placed positive law — the commands of a sovereign — at the centre of jurisprudence and distinguished it sharply from positive morality. Thinkers such as Hans Kelsen and H. L. A. Hart developed the positivist tradition in different directions, maintaining in various forms that the validity of law depends on social sources rather than on its moral content.

Relationship to legal positivism

Jus positivum is the historical ancestor of the modern doctrine of legal positivism. The two are not identical: jus positivum is a category of law, while legal positivism is a theory about what makes norms legally valid. Nevertheless, both ideas emphasise that law is a human artefact whose existence can be identified by its pedigree — enactment, precedent or recognition — rather than by its conformity to morality.
