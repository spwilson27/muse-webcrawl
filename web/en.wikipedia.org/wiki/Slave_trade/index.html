Slave trade

The slave trade is the capture, transportation, and sale of human beings as slaves. Slave trading existed in many societies from antiquity onward, but its largest and most consequential form was the Atlantic slave trade (sixteenth to nineteenth centuries), in which European merchants and African suppliers transported roughly 12.5 million enslaved Africans across the Atlantic to the Americas, of whom about 10.7 million survived the Middle Passage.

Forms of the trade

Other major systems included the trans-Saharan trade, which carried enslaved people from sub-Saharan Africa to North Africa and the Middle East over more than a millennium; the Indian Ocean and Red Sea trades, supplying East Africa's captives to Arabia, Persia, and India; and internal African and European trades, including the Barbary corsairs' enslavement of Mediterranean and Atlantic Europeans. In antiquity, slavery and slave markets were central to the economies of Greece and Rome.

The Atlantic trade

European demand for plantation labour in the Caribbean, Brazil, and North America drove the triangular trade: manufactured goods to Africa, enslaved people to the Americas, and sugar, tobacco, and cotton back to Europe. Portugal and Brazil carried the largest share, followed by Britain, France, Spain, and the Netherlands. Conditions on slave ships were catastrophic, with mortality commonly between 10 and 20 per cent per voyage.

Abolition

Denmark banned the trade in 1803, Britain and the United States in 1807–1808, and other powers followed, though illegal trading to Cuba and Brazil persisted until the 1860s. Slavery itself was abolished across the nineteenth century, from Haiti (1804) to Brazil (1888). The United Nations recognizes 25 March as a day of remembrance for the victims of the slave trade, and historians continue to document its demographic, economic, and cultural legacy.
