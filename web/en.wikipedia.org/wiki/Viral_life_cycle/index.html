Viral life cycle

The viral life cycle is the sequence of stages by which a virus infects a host cell and produces new virus particles (virions). Because viruses are obligate intracellular parasites, every stage depends on the machinery and resources of the host cell.

Stages

  1. Attachment: viral surface proteins bind specific receptors on the host cell; this determines host range and tissue tropism.
  2. Entry (penetration): the virion or its genome enters the cell, by endocytosis, membrane fusion or, in bacteriophages, injection of the genome through the cell wall.
  3. Uncoating: the capsid is removed or opened, releasing the viral genome.
  4. Replication and gene expression: viral genomes are copied and viral proteins synthesised using host ribosomes; the strategy varies with genome type (see the Baltimore classification).
  5. Assembly: new genomes are packaged into capsids to form progeny virions, sometimes with a lipid envelope acquired from host membranes.
  6. Release: progeny exit by cell lysis, budding or exocytosis, and go on to infect new cells.

Patterns of infection

In a lytic cycle, replication rapidly produces progeny and destroys the cell, as in the lytic bacteriophages. In a lysogenic cycle, the viral genome integrates into the host chromosome (as a prophage, or a provirus in animals) and is copied passively until induction triggers lytic growth. Animal viruses may also cause latent infections (e.g. herpesviruses), persistent infections (e.g. hepatitis B), or contribute to cancer through oncogenes and insertional mutagenesis.
