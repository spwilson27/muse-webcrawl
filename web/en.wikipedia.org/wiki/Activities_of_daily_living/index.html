Activities of daily living

Activities of daily living (ADLs) is a term used in healthcare to refer to the routine activities that people tend to do every day without needing assistance. The concept was developed by Sidney Katz and colleagues in the 1950s and remains a standard measure of a person's functional status, especially for older adults and people with disabilities.

Basic ADLs

The basic activities of daily living consist of self-care tasks:

  • Bathing and personal hygiene
  • Dressing
  • Toileting and continence
  • Transferring — moving in and out of bed or a chair
  • Feeding oneself
  • Ambulating — walking or moving about

Instrumental ADLs

Instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs), described by Lawton and Brody, are more complex tasks needed for independent living in the community: managing finances, shopping, preparing meals, housekeeping, using transportation, managing medications, and communicating by telephone or other devices.

Assessment

Clinicians assess ADLs using instruments such as the Katz Index of Independence in ADLs, the Barthel Index, and the Lawton IADL scale. The ability or inability to perform ADLs is used to determine eligibility for long-term care services, rehabilitation goals, discharge planning, and insurance coverage. Declining ADL performance is associated with aging, dementia, stroke, and other chronic conditions. Occupational therapists specialize in evaluating and improving performance of daily activities.
