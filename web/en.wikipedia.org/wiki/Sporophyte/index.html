Sporophyte

A sporophyte is the diploid multicellular stage in the life cycle of a plant or alga that undergoes alternation of generations. The sporophyte develops from the zygote produced when a haploid egg is fertilised by a haploid sperm, so its cells carry two sets of chromosomes (2n). It produces haploid spores by meiosis in organs called sporangia; each spore can grow into a haploid gametophyte.

Alternation of generations

All land plants alternate between a sporophyte generation and a gametophyte generation. The sporophyte makes spores by meiosis; the gametophyte produces gametes (eggs and sperm) by mitosis; fertilisation restores the diploid condition. Which generation dominates the visible plant differs by group.

In different plant groups

  • Bryophytes (mosses, liverworts, hornworts) – the gametophyte is the dominant, photosynthetic plant; the sporophyte (foot, stalk-like seta, and capsule) grows out of and remains nutritionally dependent on it.
  • Ferns and horsetails – the sporophyte is the familiar leafy plant; the gametophyte is a small, independent heart-shaped prothallus.
  • Seed plants – the sporophyte is overwhelmingly dominant, and the gametophytes are reduced to the pollen grain (male) and the embryo sac within the ovule (female).

Structure and evolution

The earliest land-plant fossils, from the Silurian period, are simple sporophytes consisting of branching axes tipped with sporangia, like those of Cooksonia. Over evolutionary time sporophytes gained roots, leaves, vascular tissue, and secondary growth, reaching their largest forms in the great tree ferns and seed-plant forests. Because the sporophyte is diploid, harmful recessive mutations can be masked, which is one proposed advantage of an extended diploid phase.
