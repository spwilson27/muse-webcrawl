Oneirology (from Greek ὄνειρον, oneiron, "dream", and -λογία, "the study of") is the scientific study of dreams. Modern oneirology combines neuroscience, psychology and sleep medicine, investigating how and when dreams occur, what neural processes underlie them, and what functions — if any — they serve.

History

Dream interpretation is ancient — from Artemidorus's Oneirocritica (2nd century AD) to Freud's The Interpretation of Dreams (1899), which treated dreams as disguised wish-fulfilment. The scientific era began with the discovery of REM sleep by Aserinsky and Kleitman in 1953 and the correlation of REM periods with vivid dreaming. Calvin Hall's content analysis of thousands of dream reports founded an empirical, quantitative approach in the 1950s–60s.

Research findings

Dreams occur chiefly in REM sleep but also in NREM stages. Neuroimaging shows high activity in limbic and visual areas with reduced dorsolateral prefrontal activity during REM, matching dreams' emotional, visual and illogical character. Dream content shows continuity with waking concerns, and lucid dreaming — awareness within the dream — can be studied with pre-agreed eye-movement signals.

Theories of dream function

Proposed functions include memory consolidation and emotional regulation, threat simulation (Revonsuo), social simulation, and the activation-synthesis model of Hobson and McCarley (1977), which treats dream narrative as the cortex's interpretation of brainstem activation. None commands universal assent.
