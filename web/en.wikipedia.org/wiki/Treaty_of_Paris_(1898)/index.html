The Treaty of Paris of 1898 was the peace treaty that ended the Spanish–American War. Signed on 10 December 1898, it ended Spain's colonial empire in the Americas and the western Pacific and marked the emergence of the United States as an overseas colonial power.

Terms

Under the treaty:

• Spain relinquished all claim of sovereignty over Cuba, which came under U.S. occupation, with independence following in 1902.
• Spain ceded Puerto Rico and the island of Guam to the United States.
• Spain ceded the Philippine archipelago to the United States in exchange for a payment of $20 million.
• Spanish troops were withdrawn and prisoners exchanged; civil and property rights of inhabitants in the ceded territories were addressed in the treaty's articles.

Negotiations and ratification

An armistice protocol had been signed in August 1898 following American victories at Manila Bay and Santiago de Cuba. The Paris commission, led on the U.S. side by Secretary of State William R. Day and including William P. Frye, George Gray, William Day and Cushman Davis, negotiated through the autumn. The status of the Philippines was the most contested issue; President William McKinley instructed the commissioners to demand the whole archipelago. The U.S. Senate ratified the treaty on 6 February 1899 by a single vote above the required two-thirds majority, amid fierce debate between imperialists and anti-imperialists.

Consequences

The treaty's transfer of the Philippines—negotiated without Filipino representation—helped trigger the Philippine–American War (1899–1902) after fighting broke out between U.S. forces and the First Philippine Republic of Emilio Aguinaldo in February 1899. In the United States, the acquisitions prompted the Insular Cases in the Supreme Court, which defined the constitutional status of the new unincorporated territories. Spain, meanwhile, turned inward in the period of reflection known as the Desastre del 98.
