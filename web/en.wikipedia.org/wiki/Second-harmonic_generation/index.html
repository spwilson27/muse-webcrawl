Second-harmonic generation

Second-harmonic generation (SHG), also known as frequency doubling, is a nonlinear optical process in which two photons of the same frequency interact with a nonlinear material and combine to form a new photon with twice the energy, and therefore twice the frequency and half the wavelength, of the original photons.

History

SHG was first demonstrated by Peter Franken and colleagues at the University of Michigan in 1961, shortly after the invention of the laser: a ruby laser beam passing through a quartz crystal generated weak ultraviolet light at twice the frequency. It became one of the foundational experiments of nonlinear optics.

Theory

The process requires a medium lacking inversion symmetry, described by a non-zero second-order susceptibility χ(2). Efficient conversion requires phase matching—conservation of momentum between the interacting waves—which is achieved through birefringence or quasi-phase-matching in periodically poled materials such as periodically poled lithium niobate (PPLN).

Applications

Frequency doubling is routinely used to convert infrared laser light to visible light (for example, doubling 1064 nm Nd:YAG output to 532 nm green light). SHG microscopy exploits the sensitivity of the effect to non-centrosymmetric structures such as collagen, enabling label-free biological imaging.
