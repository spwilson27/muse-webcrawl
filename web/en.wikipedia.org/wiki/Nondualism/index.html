Nondualism

Nondualism includes a mature state of consciousness in various religious and spiritual traditions, where one's experience of the world is not fragmented into opposing pairs, and the sense of a separate, individual self distinct from the rest of the universe is transcended. Definitions of nondualism vary, but in general it refers to the view that reality is a unified whole and that distinctions such as self/other, subject/object, and mind/body are ultimately illusory or conventional rather than fundamental.

Indian traditions

In Hinduism, nondualism is most fully developed in Advaita Vedānta, systematised by the 8th-century philosopher Śaṅkara, which teaches that the inner self (Ātman) is identical with ultimate reality (Brahman), and that the appearance of plurality is due to ignorance (avidyā) and superimposition. Kashmir Shaivism and other Śaiva traditions developed their own nondual frameworks in which the world is a real manifestation of consciousness (Śiva). In Buddhism, Mahāyāna teachings on emptiness (śūnyatā), the Yogācāra analysis of experience, and practices such as Zen, Mahāmudrā, and Dzogchen are often described as nondual, pointing to the inseparability of appearance and emptiness and of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa.

Other traditions and modern usage

Nondual themes also appear in Taoism (the unity of the Tao beyond the interplay of yin and yang), in some strands of Sufism and Christian mysticism, and in Neo-Vedanta. In the 20th and 21st centuries, teachers such as Ramana Maharshi and Nisargadatta Maharaj, and later Western teachers, popularised "nonduality" as a standalone spiritual orientation, studied also by psychologists and philosophers of mind interested in the structure of consciousness.
