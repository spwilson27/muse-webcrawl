Political action committee

[Image: Chart of contribution limits for US federal political action committees]

In the United States, a political action committee (PAC) is an organisation that pools campaign contributions from members and donates those funds to campaigns for or against candidates, ballot initiatives, or legislation. PACs are regulated by the Federal Election Commission under the Federal Election Campaign Act.

Types

Traditional PACs may be established by corporations, labour unions, trade associations, or ideological and membership groups; they may solicit contributions only from a restricted class of connected persons (such as employees or union members), and their donations to candidates are capped (historically $5,000 per candidate per election for multicandidate committees). "Leadership PACs" are formed by politicians to support colleagues and build influence. Non-connected PACs may solicit the general public.

Super PACs

Following the Supreme Court's decision in Citizens United v. FEC (2010) and the D.C. Circuit's ruling in SpeechNow.org v. FEC, so-called super PACs (independent expenditure-only committees) may raise unlimited sums from individuals, corporations, and unions, but may spend them only independently of candidates and may not contribute directly to campaigns. Hybrid PACs combine both functions in segregated accounts.

Role and criticism

Thousands of PACs are registered in the United States, and they are a major channel of campaign finance. Critics argue PACs give organised interests disproportionate influence and that super PACs undermine contribution limits; defenders argue they facilitate political speech and association protected by the First Amendment. Related concepts include soft money and 527 organisations.
