Allopathic medicine, or allopathy, is a term historically used to describe what is now more commonly called conventional, mainstream, or modern scientific medicine. The word was coined in the early 19th century by Samuel Hahnemann, the founder of homeopathy, to distinguish the orthodox medical practice of his day — which often relied on treatments such as bloodletting, purging, and large doses of mineral drugs intended to produce effects opposite to the patient's symptoms — from his own system of "like cures like."

Today the term is used mainly in historical and regulatory contexts, and sometimes pejoratively by proponents of alternative medicine. Most physicians and medical organisations avoid it, preferring terms such as "evidence-based medicine" or simply "medicine," since modern practice is defined by scientific method rather than by any single therapeutic doctrine.

Etymology and origin

Hahnemann formed "allopathy" from the Greek allos ("other" or "different") and pathos ("suffering"). In his Organon of the Medical Art (1810) he argued that orthodox physicians treated diseases with remedies that produced effects unrelated to, or opposite to, the symptoms of the disease — in his phrase, contraria contrariis — whereas homeopathy treated with substances that would produce similar symptoms in a healthy person (similia similibus curentur).

Hahnemann applied the label broadly and polemically. Critics have long noted that the medicine of 1800 differs profoundly from the medicine of today, and that grouping modern, scientifically validated practice under a term invented to attack pre-scientific "heroic medicine" is misleading.

Historical development

Through the 19th century, "allopath" became a sectarian label in the competitive medical marketplace of Europe and North America, where regular physicians competed with homeopaths, eclectics, Thomsonians, and later osteopaths and chiropractors. Professional bodies such as the American Medical Association (founded 1847) sometimes used the distinctions in codes of ethics that discouraged consultation with irregular practitioners.

As laboratory science, germ theory, anaesthesia, antisepsis, vaccination, and later antibiotics transformed practice, the old sectarian categories faded. By the mid-20th century, "allopathic" survived chiefly in one institutional niche: the degree title Doctor of Medicine (MD), awarded by "allopathic" medical schools, as distinct from the Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine (DO) awarded by osteopathic schools in the United States.

Modern usage

In contemporary usage the word appears in:

  • United States medical education and licensing, where "allopathic medical schools" denotes MD-granting schools, contrasted with osteopathic (DO-granting) schools — although residency accreditation was unified under a single system completed in 2020.
  • Indian health policy, where "allopathy" is the common term for modern medicine within the AYUSH framework of recognised medical systems.
  • Writing by proponents of alternative medicine, where it is sometimes used critically to suggest that conventional medicine treats only symptoms.

Mainstream medical writers generally regard "allopathy" as an anachronistic and inaccurate descriptor, since modern medicine is not committed to any principle of opposites but to treatments demonstrated effective by clinical evidence, whatever their mechanism.
