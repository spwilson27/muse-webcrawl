Countee Cullen (born Countee LeRoy Porter; May 30, 1903 – January 9, 1946) was an American poet, novelist, children's writer and playwright. He was a leading figure of the Harlem Renaissance, known for poems that joined traditional European forms — the sonnet, the ballad, the Keatsian lyric — to Black American experience, most famously in his first collection, Color (1925).

Life

The facts of Cullen's early life are obscure; orphaned, he was taken in by Reverend Frederick A. Cullen, pastor of Harlem's Salem Methodist Episcopal Church, whose surname he took. A brilliant student at De Witt Clinton High School and New York University, he won poetry prizes while still an undergraduate and took a master's degree at Harvard. In 1928 he married Yolande Du Bois, daughter of W. E. B. Du Bois, in a Harlem wedding of the decade; the marriage soon ended in divorce.

Work

Color (1925) included his best-known poems, "Heritage" ("What is Africa to me?"), "Incident" — recalling a childhood racial slur in Baltimore — and "Yet Do I Marvel", which wonders that God could "make a poet black, and bid him sing". Later collections included Copper Sun (1927) and The Ballad of the Brown Girl (1927); he edited the anthology Caroling Dusk (1927) of Black poets. He wrote one novel, One Way to Heaven (1932), children's books, and, with Arna Bontemps, the play St. Louis Woman (1946). From 1934 until his death he taught French at Frederick Douglass Junior High School in Harlem, where one of his students was the young James Baldwin.

Death and legacy

Cullen died of uremia following high blood pressure in 1946, aged 42. The Countee Cullen branch of the New York Public Library in Harlem bears his name, and his poems remain staples of American anthologies.
