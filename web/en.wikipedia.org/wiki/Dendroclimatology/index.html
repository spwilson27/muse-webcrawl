Dendroclimatology

Dendroclimatology is the science of determining past climates from tree rings. Because trees in temperate regions form one growth ring per year, and because ring width, density and composition vary with growing conditions such as temperature and rainfall, precisely dated ring-width chronologies can be used to reconstruct climate variables centuries to millennia before the start of instrumental weather records.

Method

Researchers sample living trees, dead wood and archaeological timbers, measure ring widths (or maximum latewood density), and cross-date the series so each ring is assigned its exact calendar year. Statistical methods then calibrate the ring record against the instrumental climate record over their period of overlap, and the fitted relationship is applied to earlier rings to estimate past climate. Long chronologies — from bristlecone pines, oaks and other long-lived species — extend reconstructions back thousands of years.

Applications and issues

Dendroclimatic reconstructions have been central to studies of past temperature change, drought history (including North American megadroughts) and the context of recent warming, and featured prominently in IPCC assessments and in debates over "hockey stick" temperature graphs. One methodological concern is the "divergence problem", in which some high-latitude trees' rings stop tracking instrumental warming in the late 20th century.
