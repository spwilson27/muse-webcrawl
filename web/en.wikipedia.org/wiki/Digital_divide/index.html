Digital divide

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Photograph contrasting a teenager with a laptop in a city with children sharing one phone in a rural village]

The digital divide is the unequal distribution of information and communication technologies — in access, quality, skills and outcomes — between different populations, whether between countries, between urban and rural areas of one country, or between demographic groups within a city. The term came into wide use in the mid-1990s, as the Internet commercialised and US policy reports (the NTIA's 'Falling Through the Net' series) documented who was being left offline.

The divide has several layers: the access divide (is there a connection and a device?), the quality divide (fibre broadband versus a prepaid phone on a congested network), the skills divide (digital literacy), and the outcome divide (who converts access into education, income and civic power). Globally, roughly two-thirds of humanity is now online, with near-universal access in rich countries and far lower rates in the least developed ones; within countries, cost, age, disability and rurality are the usual fault lines.

Policy responses range from universal-service funds and community networks to satellite constellations serving remote regions and public access in libraries and schools. The COVID-19 pandemic, which moved work and schooling online overnight, recast the divide from an abstract concern into an emergency measured in children without connections — and 'broadband as utility' from a slogan into legislation in several countries.
