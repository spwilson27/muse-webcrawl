The Aos Sí (pronounced "ees shee"; singular sídhe) are a supernatural people in Irish folklore and mythology, and in Scottish tradition as the daoine sídhe. The name means "people of the mounds": the sídhe are the ancient burial mounds and hollow hills of Ireland in which these beings are said to dwell. In Irish mythology they are generally identified with the Tuatha Dé Danann, the god-like race of the Mythological Cycle, who withdrew underground after their defeat by the Milesians; in folk belief they also merge at times with the fallen angels or the spirits of the dead.

Belief and customs

The Aos Sí were treated with careful respect — and fear. Euphemisms were used instead of their name: "the Good People", "the Fair Folk", "the Gentry", "themselves". They were credited with great beauty, power over music and fate, and a dangerous touchiness: they might bless a household, blight cattle, steal children (leaving a sickly "changeling" in their place), or carry off adults to their own timeless realm, from which a night's dancing might cost a century. Hence a web of protective customs: offerings of milk and food left out, iron kept by the cradle, rowan and holy water used as charms, and strong taboos against building on fairy paths or cutting down lone hawthorn trees ("fairy trees") at ringforts — customs that have influenced Irish land use into the present day.

Kinds of Aos Sí

Folklore distinguishes many members of the fairy host, including the bean sídhe (banshee), the wailing woman whose cry foretells a death in the family; the solitary leprechaun, a cobbler who guards buried treasure; the headless horseman called the dullahan; and the sluagh sídhe, the restless fairy host of the unforgiven dead, said to fly through the night sky. Time in their realm runs differently from the mortal world, a motif shared with the Fenian tale of Oisín in Tír na nÓg.
