Thyroidectomy

A thyroidectomy is an operation that involves the surgical removal of all or part of the thyroid gland. In general surgery, endocrine or head and neck surgeons often perform a thyroidectomy when a patient has thyroid cancer or some other condition of the thyroid gland (such as hyperthyroidism) or an adjacent neck structure. A complete removal is called a total thyroidectomy, while removal of one lobe is a thyroid lobectomy or hemithyroidectomy, and removal of most of the gland is a subtotal thyroidectomy.

Indications

Thyroidectomy is performed for thyroid malignancy, including papillary and follicular thyroid cancer; for large or symptomatic goitres causing compression of the airway or oesophagus; for Graves' disease and toxic multinodular goitre when other treatments fail or are unsuitable; and for thyroid nodules whose biopsy results are suspicious or indeterminate for cancer.

Procedure

The operation is usually performed under general anaesthesia through a transverse incision in the lower neck. The surgeon dissects the thyroid gland free while identifying and preserving the recurrent laryngeal nerves and the parathyroid glands, which regulate calcium. In cancer cases, lymph nodes of the central neck compartment may also be removed. Newer approaches include minimally invasive video-assisted thyroidectomy and robotic surgery through remote-access incisions.

Complications

Possible complications include bleeding and haematoma, injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve causing hoarseness, damage to the parathyroid glands causing temporary or permanent hypocalcaemia, and infection. After total thyroidectomy, patients require lifelong thyroid hormone replacement with levothyroxine. Historically, thyroid surgery carried high mortality until the work of surgeons such as Theodor Kocher in the late nineteenth century reduced operative deaths dramatically; Kocher received the Nobel Prize in 1909 for his work on the thyroid.
