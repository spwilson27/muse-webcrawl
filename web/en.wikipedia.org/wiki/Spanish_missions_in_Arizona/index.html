The Spanish missions in Arizona comprise a series of religious outposts established by Spanish Catholic missionaries — first Jesuits, then Franciscans — in the region then known as the Pimería Alta, in what is now southern Arizona and northern Sonora. Their purpose was to convert the indigenous O'odham and other peoples, teach them Spanish and Christianity, and extend the frontier of New Spain.

Kino's missions

Father Eusebio Francisco Kino, an Italian Jesuit, founded a chain of missions from 1687, including San Xavier del Bac near present-day Tucson and the visitas that later became churches at Tumacácori and Guevavi. Kino also mapped the region and proved that Baja California was a peninsula. After the Jesuits were expelled from Spanish territories in 1767, Franciscans took over the missions.

Architecture and fate

The most famous survivor is Mission San Xavier del Bac, completed in 1797, a dazzling white Moorish-influenced church still serving the Tohono O'odham community and nicknamed the "White Dove of the Desert". San José de Tumacácori and Los Santos Ángeles de Guevavi survive as ruins within Tumacácori National Historical Park. Mission life declined after Mexican independence and secularisation in the 1820s–30s, and Apache raids forced the abandonment of many sites.
