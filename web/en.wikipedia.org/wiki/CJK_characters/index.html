CJK characters

In internationalization, CJK characters is a collective term for the Chinese, Japanese, and Korean languages, all of which use Chinese characters and derivatives in their writing systems, sometimes in combination with other scripts. Collectively, the CJK characters include Chinese characters (hanzi), Japanese kanji, Korean hanja, and Vietnamese chữ Hán (included in the broader CJKV grouping). The term describes the shared repertoire of logographic characters used across East Asia.

Writing systems

Chinese is written almost entirely with hanzi. Japanese mixes kanji with the kana syllabaries (hiragana and katakana). Korean historically used hanja alongside Hangul, though modern Korean is written predominantly in Hangul, with hanja appearing mainly in academic, legal, and disambiguating contexts. Traditional and simplified Chinese character forms differ in many cases, reflecting 20th-century script reforms in mainland China, while Japan adopted its own simplifications (shinjitai).

Unicode and Han unification

In Unicode, CJK characters are encoded largely in the CJK Unified Ideographs blocks. The Han unification process merged character forms considered variants of the same abstract character into single code points, even where their glyphs differ slightly between regions; regional rendering depends on the font used. Tens of thousands of characters are encoded, with extension blocks added over successive Unicode versions, though everyday use requires only a few thousand.

Encoding and input

Before Unicode, East Asian text used encodings such as Big5, GB 2312, Shift JIS, and EUC-KR. Text input on computers relies on input method editors (IMEs) that convert phonetic typing (pinyin, romaji, or Hangul jamo) into candidate characters. Character counts and stroke-order rules are standardized nationally; for example, China's list of frequently used characters covers 3,500 characters.
