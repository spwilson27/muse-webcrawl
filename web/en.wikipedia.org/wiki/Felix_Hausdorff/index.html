Felix Hausdorff (8 November 1868 – 26 January 1942) was a German mathematician, one of the founders of modern topology, who contributed significantly to set theory, descriptive set theory, measure theory, and functional analysis. Born into a wealthy Jewish textile-manufacturing family in Breslau, he studied at Leipzig, where he also wrote literature and philosophy under the pseudonym Paul Mongré.

Life

Hausdorff studied mathematics and astronomy at the University of Leipzig, receiving his doctorate in 1891 with work on the refraction of light in the atmosphere. He later held positions at Leipzig, Greifswald and Bonn. In 1914 he published his masterwork Grundzüge der Mengenlehre (Principles of Set Theory), the first systematic textbook of point-set topology.

Under the Nazi regime, Hausdorff, as a Jew, was progressively excluded from academic life; he was dismissed from Bonn in 1935. Facing deportation, he, his wife Charlotte, and his sister-in-law Edith Pappenheim died by suicide in Bonn on 26 January 1942.

Contributions

Hausdorff introduced the class of spaces now called Hausdorff spaces (separation axiom T2), developed the theory of partially ordered sets, and proved the Hausdorff maximal principle, a form of the axiom of choice. With Stefan Banach he produced the Banach–Tarski paradox's precursor, the Hausdorff paradox (1914). His generalisation of length, area and volume to non-integer dimensions led to the concepts of Hausdorff measure and Hausdorff dimension, central to fractal geometry. He also gave his name to the Hausdorff moment problem and the Young–Hausdorff inequalities.
