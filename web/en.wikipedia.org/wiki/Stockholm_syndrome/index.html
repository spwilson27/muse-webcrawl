Stockholm syndrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stockholm syndrome is a proposed condition or theory that tries to explain why hostages sometimes develop a psychological bond with their captors. It is supposed to result from a specific set of circumstances: powerful imbalances of power in hostage-taking, kidnapping, and abusive relationships. Emotional bonds that may occur between captors and captives during intimate time together are generally considered counterintuitive, and the syndrome is not recognized as a formal diagnosis in major psychiatric classification systems.

History

The term was coined after the Norrmalmstorg robbery in Stockholm, Sweden, in August 1973. Jan-Erik Olsson attempted to rob the Kreditbanken at Norrmalmstorg and took four bank employees hostage for six days. During the standoff, the hostages appeared to develop positive feelings toward their captor and distrust of the police; after their release, some defended Olsson. Criminologist and psychiatrist Nils Bejerot is credited with naming the phenomenon, calling it Norrmalmstorgssyndromet.

Proposed explanations

Explanations offered for such bonding include the victim's perception that small kindnesses from the captor are lifesaving, isolation from perspectives other than the captor's, a perceived inability to escape, and a survival strategy in which cooperation and identification with the aggressor reduce the risk of harm. Critics note that such responses are rare, poorly defined, and may reflect rational survival behaviour rather than a distinct syndrome.

In popular culture

The concept is widely invoked in films, fiction, and commentary about kidnappings and abusive relationships, and is frequently compared with related ideas such as trauma bonding and Lima syndrome, in which captors develop sympathy for their hostages.
