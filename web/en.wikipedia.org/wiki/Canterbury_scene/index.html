Canterbury scene

The Canterbury scene (or Canterbury sound) was a musical scene centred on the city of Canterbury, Kent, England, during the late 1960s and early 1970s, associated with a loosely connected group of progressive rock, psychedelic rock, and jazz fusion bands. Rather than a unified style, the term describes musicians linked by personal connections, whimsical humour, and a shared background in the Wilde Flowers, the band from which the scene's central groups emerged.

Origins

The Wilde Flowers (1964–1967) included Robert Wyatt, Kevin Ayers, Richard Sinclair, and Hugh and Brian Hopper. From this pool came two principal bands: Soft Machine, formed in 1966, which moved from psychedelic pop toward jazz-influenced instrumental rock, and Caravan, formed in 1968, known for melodic, organ-driven progressive rock. The local scene was supported by figures such as Australian beat poet Daevid Allen, who later founded Gong in France.

Associated bands and musicians

Bands and artists commonly counted in the scene include Soft Machine, Caravan, Gong, Hatfield and the North, National Health, Egg, Delivery, Camel, and Matching Mole, along with solo careers by Kevin Ayers, Robert Wyatt, and Steve Hillage. Dave Sinclair, Richard Coughlan, Pye Hastings, and the Hopper brothers provided a web of shared membership. The music typically blends jazz harmony and improvisation with whimsical English lyrics, extended instrumental passages, and fuzz organ.

Legacy

Though none of the bands achieved mass commercial success in Britain at the time, Caravan and Gong built strong followings in continental Europe. The Canterbury scene became a recognized subgenre of progressive rock, celebrated for its eccentricity and musicianship, and rediscovered by later generations through reissues; the term, rarely used by the musicians themselves in the 1970s, was popularized by journalists and fans.
