Siege of Namur (1695)

The siege of Namur of 1695 was a major engagement of the Nine Years' War (1688–1697), in which an Allied army under William III of England retook the strategically vital fortress city of Namur, in the Spanish Netherlands, from the French. Namur, with its citadel perched above the confluence of the Sambre and Meuse rivers, had been captured by Louis XIV in person in 1692 and its defences strengthened by the great engineer Vauban. Its recapture in 1695 was William's most celebrated success of the war and one of the largest sieges of the seventeenth century.

Background

Namur controlled the junction of the Sambre and Meuse and the routes between France and the Spanish Netherlands. Louis XIV had taken it in 1692 after a model siege directed by Vauban, who then modernised its works; the French engineer Coehoorn's Dutch counterpart, Menno van Coehoorn, now served the Allies, setting up a celebrated duel between the two greatest siege engineers of the age.

The siege

William invested Namur in early July 1695 with an army of roughly 58,000 men, while a covering force watched the French field army under Villeroy. The town itself fell after heavy fighting in early August, following a costly assault on the outer works. The garrison under the Duke of Boufflers withdrew into the citadel, whose position on a rocky height made it exceptionally strong.

The assault on the citadel in late August and early September was among the bloodiest episodes of the war. Allied troops stormed the Terra Nova fort and the outer defences in desperate fighting. With his position untenable, Boufflers surrendered the citadel on 4 September (some accounts give the capitulation as 5 September), marching out with the honours of war. In a notorious sequel, the Allies detained Boufflers for a time in retaliation for French treatment of Allied garrisons at Diksmuide and Deynze.

Consequences

Allied casualties were heavy — estimates of total losses on both sides run well into five figures — but the victory was strategically and psychologically significant: it was the first great fortress France had lost in the war and demonstrated that Louis XIV's armies could be beaten. Namur was returned to Spain by the Treaty of Ryswick (1697). The siege was later immortalised in fiction in Laurence Sterne's novel Tristram Shandy, through the character of Uncle Toby, who was wounded at Namur and re-enacted the siege on his bowling green.
