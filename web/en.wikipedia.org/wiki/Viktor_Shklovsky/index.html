Viktor Shklovsky

Viktor Borisovich Shklovsky (Russian: Ви́ктор Бори́сович Шкло́вский; 24 January 1893 – 6 December 1984) was a Russian and Soviet literary theorist, critic, writer, and pamphleteer. He is one of the founders of the Russian Formalist school of literary criticism and a major figure in 20th-century literary theory, best known for the concept of defamiliarization (Russian: ostranenie).

Life

Shklovsky was born in Saint Petersburg to a Jewish father and German-Russian mother. While a student he co-founded OPOJAZ (the Society for the Study of Poetic Language) in 1916, the Petrograd centre of Formalism, alongside Boris Eichenbaum, Yuri Tynyanov, and others. He fought in the First World War, winning the Cross of St. George, and was involved in revolutionary politics with the Socialist Revolutionaries. After fleeing arrest in 1922 — an escape recounted in his memoir A Sentimental Journey — he lived briefly in Berlin before returning to the USSR. Pressured under Stalinism, he publicly recanted Formalism in 1930 ("A Monument to a Scientific Error") but continued writing widely on literature, Tolstoy, Pushkin, Eisenstein, and cinema until his death at 91.

Key ideas

Defamiliarization

In his landmark essay "Art as Device" (1917; often translated "Art as Technique"), Shklovsky argued that habitual perception becomes automatic, and that the purpose of art is to make the familiar strange — to "make the stone stony" — so that it is perceived freshly and with effort. The concept became foundational for Formalism, structuralism, and later literary and film theory.

Fabula and syuzhet

Shklovsky and his colleagues distinguished fabula (the chronological raw material of a story) from syuzhet (the plot as artistically arranged and deformed), a distinction absorbed into narratology worldwide. His Theory of Prose (1925) applied these ideas to Cervantes, Sterne, Dickens, and Tolstoy, celebrating digression, parody, and the "baring of the device".

Legacy

Shklovsky's ideas shaped structuralism, the Prague School, narratology, and defamiliarization remains a standard concept in literary criticism and creative writing. His own fragmented, digressive prose style influenced generations of Russian writers.
