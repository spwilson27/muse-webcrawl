DevelopersAlfred Binet and Théodore Simon
First published1905
PurposeAssessing children's intellectual development
Key conceptMental age

The Binet–Simon scale was the first practical intelligence test, developed by French psychologists Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon. It was published in 1905 after the French government commissioned research into methods for identifying schoolchildren who needed additional educational support. The scale introduced the concept of mental age — the level of intellectual development associated with a typical child of a given chronological age.

Development

In 1904, the French Ministry of Public Instruction appointed a commission to devise a method of distinguishing children who would benefit from special education. Binet and Simon responded with a set of thirty tasks of increasing difficulty, including following simple instructions, naming objects, repeating digits, defining words, and constructing sentences. The scale was revised in 1908 and again in 1911, grouping the tasks by the age at which an average child could pass them.

Mental age

A child's performance on the scale yielded a mental age: if a seven-year-old passed the tasks typical of nine-year-olds, his or her mental age was nine. Binet cautioned that the scale measured a child's current level of functioning for educational purposes, and he did not regard it as measuring a fixed, innate quantity. The German psychologist William Stern later proposed dividing mental age by chronological age to produce an intelligence quotient, or IQ.

Legacy

The Binet–Simon scale was adapted in the United States by Lewis Terman at Stanford University, producing the Stanford–Binet Intelligence Scales in 1916, which became the dominant intelligence test of the twentieth century. Adaptations by Henry Goddard and others were used in schools, immigration screening, and the military — applications that went far beyond Binet's original educational intent and later drew significant criticism.
