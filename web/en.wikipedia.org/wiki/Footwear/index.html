Footwear refers to garments worn on the feet, primarily to protect against ground textures, temperature extremes and injury, but also as items of fashion and social signalling. Footwear includes shoes, boots, sandals, slippers and specialised forms such as athletic trainers, safety boots and orthopaedic shoes.

History

The oldest known footwear is the sagebrush-bark sandals from Fort Rock Cave in Oregon, dated to roughly 10,000 years ago. Ötzi the Iceman, from about 3300 BC, wore sophisticated shoes of bearskin, deer hide and bark netting insulated with grass. Ancient Egyptians wore palm-fibre sandals, Romans developed the hobnailed caliga and enclosed calceus, and medieval Europe produced the long pointed poulaine. Shoemaking was industrialised in the nineteenth century, notably in centres such as Northampton, England, and Massachusetts, and the twentieth century added rubber soles, trainers and mass-produced fashion footwear.

Components and construction

A shoe consists principally of an upper, which covers the foot and is shaped over a last, and a sole — comprising insole, midsole and outsole — attached by stitching, cementing or vulcanisation. Heels, toe boxes, laces, buckles and fasteners vary by style. Sizing systems differ by region (UK, US, European and Mondopoint scales) and are based on foot length.

Industry

Footwear is a global industry worth hundreds of billions of dollars; production is concentrated in Asia, with China and Vietnam the largest producers. Major brands include Nike, Adidas and Puma in athletic footwear. Growing attention is paid to labour conditions in supply chains and to the environmental impact of leather tanning, synthetic materials and shoe waste.

Health

Ill-fitting footwear is associated with bunions, corns and other foot problems, while safety footwear with steel toe caps and puncture-resistant soles is legally required in many industrial workplaces.
