Seaplane

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A yellow de Havilland Beaver floatplane taking off from a lake, spray flying]
A floatplane lifting off the water.

A seaplane is a powered fixed-wing aircraft capable of taking off and landing on water. Two main types exist: floatplanes, with slender pontoons (floats) under the fuselage, and flying boats, whose fuselage itself forms a planing hull. Seaplanes pioneered intercontinental air routes in the 1930s (Pan Am\'s Clippers) because oceans needed no runways, and they remain essential in regions such as Alaska, the Canadian bush, and the Maldives — the world\'s largest seaplane operation (Trans Maldivian Airways).

History

Henri Fabre made the first seaplane flight in 1910; Glenn Curtiss developed practical floatplanes and flying boats for the US Navy, and NC-4 flying boats made the first transatlantic crossing (1919, with stops). Giant boats (Dornier Do X, Short Empire, Boeing 314 Clipper) defined luxury air travel before runways built for the Second World War made landplanes dominant.

Modern use

De Havilland Canada Beavers and Otters on floats serve wilderness and island routes; firefighting \'scoopers\' (Canadair CL-415) refill water tanks by skimming lakes. Japan\'s ShinMaywa US-2 serves search-and-rescue, and China\'s AG600 amphibian (among the largest modern seaplanes) entered development flight testing in the 2010s–20s.

Operation

Water handling differs from land: step taxiing on the hull\'s planing \'step\', sailing in wind with water rudders, glassy-water landing hazards, and corrosion control dominate seaplane technique. Ratings (ASES/AMES) are separate licence endorsements in the US system.
