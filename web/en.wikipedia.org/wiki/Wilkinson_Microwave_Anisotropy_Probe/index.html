The Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe (WMAP), originally the Microwave Anisotropy Probe (MAP), was a NASA spacecraft operating from 2001 to 2010 that measured temperature differences across the sky in the cosmic microwave background (CMB) — the radiant heat remaining from the Big Bang. Led by Professor Charles L. Bennett of Johns Hopkins University, the mission developed a partnership between NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center and Princeton University.

WMAP was named in honour of David Todd Wilkinson (1935–2002), a pioneer in the study of the cosmic microwave background and a member of the mission science team. Its measurements of CMB anisotropies pinned down the age, geometry and composition of the universe with unprecedented precision and helped establish the standard Lambda-CDM model of cosmology.

Mission

Launched aboard a Delta II rocket on 30 June 2001, WMAP travelled to a Lissajous orbit around the Sun–Earth L2 Lagrange point, about 1.5 million kilometres from Earth. From there it scanned the entire sky every six months. Originally planned for two years, the mission was extended several times; after nine years of data collection the spacecraft was retired in 2010 and moved into a heliocentric graveyard orbit.

Spacecraft and instruments

The observatory carried differential microwave radiometers sensitive in five frequency bands (K, Ka, Q, V and W, from 23 to 94 GHz), passively cooled to below 95 K. It produced full-sky maps with angular resolution roughly 33 times sharper than its predecessor, the COBE satellite.

Results

WMAP's nine-year data established that the universe is 13.77 billion years old (later refined to about 13.8 billion), that ordinary atoms make up only about 4.6 percent of its content, with dark matter contributing about 24 percent and dark energy about 71 percent, and that the geometry of space is flat to within about half a percent. The data also constrained the epoch of reionisation and the number of neutrino species, and the mission's papers became among the most highly cited in all of science.

Legacy

WMAP was succeeded by the European Space Agency's Planck mission (2009–2013), which mapped the CMB with still greater sensitivity. Bennett and the WMAP team received numerous honours, including the 2018 Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics.
