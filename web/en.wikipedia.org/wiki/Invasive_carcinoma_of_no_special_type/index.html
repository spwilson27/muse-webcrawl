Invasive carcinoma of no special type


Specialty: Oncology, pathology
Former name: Invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC)

Invasive carcinoma of no special type (NST), formerly called invasive ductal carcinoma, is the most common type of invasive breast cancer, accounting for roughly 70–80% of all invasive cases. The name reflects that, unlike special-type tumours such as invasive lobular carcinoma, its microscopic appearance does not show specific differentiating features sufficient to classify it as a distinct special type.

Pathology

Tumours are typically firm, grey-white masses with an irregular, stellate outline. Microscopically, cancer cells form cords, nests, and tubules in a dense fibrous (desmoplastic) stroma. Grading follows the Nottingham system, assessing tubule formation, nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic count. Immunohistochemistry for oestrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2 guides treatment.

Diagnosis and treatment

Most cases are detected by screening mammography or as a palpable lump, and diagnosed by core-needle biopsy. Treatment combines surgery (breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy, with sentinel node biopsy), radiotherapy, and systemic therapy — endocrine therapy for hormone-receptor-positive disease, anti-HER2 therapy for HER2-positive disease, and chemotherapy depending on stage and biology. Prognosis depends on stage, grade, and receptor status.

Terminology

The World Health Organization's classification of breast tumours adopted "invasive carcinoma of no special type" to replace "invasive ductal carcinoma, not otherwise specified", recognising that the tumours are not necessarily of ductal origin.
