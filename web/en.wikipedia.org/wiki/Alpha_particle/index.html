An alpha particle (symbol α) is a type of ionising radiation consisting of two protons and two neutrons bound together — identical to the nucleus of a helium-4 atom. Alpha particles are emitted in the radioactive decay of heavy nuclei (alpha decay), are produced in cosmic radiation and particle accelerators, and were historically pivotal in establishing the structure of the atom.

Properties

With a charge of +2e and a mass of about 4 atomic mass units, an alpha particle is heavy and slow compared with other common radiations: typical decay energies of 4–9 MeV correspond to speeds around 5% of the speed of light. Because of its large mass and double charge it interacts strongly with matter, ionising densely over a very short path — a few centimetres of air, or a sheet of paper, stops it entirely. The human skin's outer dead layer likewise absorbs external alpha radiation, but alpha emitters are highly hazardous when inhaled or ingested, because the dense ionisation is then deposited in living tissue; this is the danger of radon gas and its decay products in the lung, and of internal contamination by isotopes such as polonium-210.

History

Ernest Rutherford distinguished alpha and beta rays in 1899 and by 1907, with Thomas Royds, showed spectroscopically that alpha particles are helium nuclei. Alpha particles fired at gold foil in the Geiger–Marsden experiment (1909) revealed the tiny, dense atomic nucleus and refuted the plum-pudding model, founding nuclear physics. In 1919 Rutherford used alpha bombardment to achieve the first artificial nuclear transmutation (nitrogen to oxygen), and in 1932 Chadwick's alpha-on-beryllium experiments led to the discovery of the neutron.

Sources and uses

Natural alpha emitters include uranium, radium, radon, and polonium isotopes. Americium-241's alpha emission powers most household smoke detectors (by ionising air in a chamber); alpha sources such as plutonium-238 serve as heat sources in radioisotope thermoelectric generators aboard deep-space probes. In medicine, targeted alpha therapy uses alpha emitters to deliver short-range, high-energy doses to cancer cells. Helium nuclei are also the dominant heavy component of cosmic rays and are produced copiously in stellar fusion chains.
