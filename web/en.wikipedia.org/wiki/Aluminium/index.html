Aluminium (aluminum in American English) is the chemical element with the symbol Al and atomic number 13. It is a silvery, lightweight, corrosion-resistant metal — the most abundant metal in Earth's crust — used in aircraft, cans, foil, power lines and building materials.

Aluminium resists corrosion because it instantly forms a thin, tough oxide layer. Extracting it from bauxite ore by the Hall–Héroult electrolytic process takes large amounts of electricity, which is why recycling aluminium saves about 95% of that energy.

[Image: Aluminium ingots and a roll of aluminium foil]

History

Hans Christian Ørsted first produced impure aluminium in 1825, and Friedrich Wöhler developed the process further. For decades aluminium was a precious metal — a 100-gram pyramid of it capped the Washington Monument in 1884 — until the Hall–Héroult and Bayer processes (1886–88) made it cheap.

Properties

Aluminium is non-magnetic, a good conductor of heat and electricity, and highly ductile. It is the third most abundant element in the crust overall, after oxygen and silicon.
