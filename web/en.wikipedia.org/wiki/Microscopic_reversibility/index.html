Microscopic reversibility

At equilibrium, each forward process is balanced by its reverse

The principle of microscopic reversibility states that, in a system at equilibrium, every elementary molecular process occurs with exactly the same average rate as the reverse of that process. It is a consequence of the time-reversal symmetry of the fundamental equations of motion (both classical and quantum mechanical), and it connects kinetics with thermodynamics.

Statement and consequences

Richard Tolman formulated the principle in the 1920s. A direct consequence is the principle of detailed balance: at equilibrium, the net rate of every elementary step is zero, not merely the net rate of the overall reaction. Detailed balance implies that the ratio of the forward and reverse rate constants of an elementary step equals its equilibrium constant, and it places restrictions on possible reaction mechanisms — for example, in a cyclic sequence of reactions the product of forward rate constants must equal the product of reverse ones (the Wegscheider conditions).

Applications

The principle underlies the theory of transition state theory derivations, the Onsager reciprocal relations in irreversible thermodynamics, and the analysis of enzyme mechanisms. It is also used to argue that a catalyst accelerates the forward and reverse reactions equally and therefore cannot shift an equilibrium. Extensions of microscopic reversibility reasoning lead to modern fluctuation theorems, such as the Crooks and Jarzynski relations, which apply to small systems driven away from equilibrium.
