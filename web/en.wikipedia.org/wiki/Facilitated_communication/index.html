Facilitated communication

Facilitated communication (FC), or supported typing, is a scientifically discredited technique that attempts to aid communication by people with autism or other disabilities to communicate. A facilitator physically supports the person's hand or arm while they point at letters on a keyboard or letterboard.

History

The method was developed in Australia by Rosemary Crossley in the 1970s–1980s and popularised in the United States by Douglas Biklen at Syracuse University around 1989–1990, generating considerable media attention and hope among families.

Scientific evaluation

Controlled studies, including message-passing tests in which facilitator and communicator are shown different stimuli, consistently demonstrate that the typed messages originate with the facilitator, not the person with disabilities — an ideomotor effect analogous to the Ouija board. Professional bodies including the American Psychological Association, the American Academy of Pediatrics, and the American Speech–Language–Hearing Association have issued statements that FC is invalid and potentially harmful.

Harms and related techniques

FC has produced false allegations of abuse and diverted resources from evidence-based augmentative and alternative communication (AAC). Related techniques, including Rapid Prompting Method and Spelling to Communicate, raise similar concerns about facilitator authorship. Genuine independent typing by autistic people, achieved without physical support, is a distinct and valid form of AAC.
