Chilkat weaving

Chilkat weaving is a traditional form of weaving practised by the Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian and other Indigenous peoples of the Northwest Coast of Alaska and British Columbia. Its most famous product is the Chilkat blanket — a ceremonial robe woven from mountain goat wool and shredded cedar bark, decorated with crest designs in the formline style.

Technique

Chilkat robes are woven on an upright loom with a circular warp and no shuttle; the weaver works entirely with her fingers, using a twining technique that allows curvilinear formline designs — nearly impossible on most looms. Designs were traditionally painted onto pattern boards by men, often the weaver's husband, while women did the weaving. Characteristic colours are natural white and black, yellow dyed with wolf moss, and blue-green obtained from copper minerals or trade pigments. A single robe could take a year or more to complete.

History

The technique is believed to have been developed among the Tsimshian and spread to the Chilkat group of the Tlingit near present-day Klukwan, Alaska, from which it takes its name. Chilkat robes were high-status regalia worn by chiefs and clan leaders at potlatches and ceremonies, and were traded widely along the coast. Production declined sharply under colonial pressure in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, but the art has been revived by weavers such as Jennie Thlunaut (1881–1986) and her student Clarissa Rizal (1956–2016).
