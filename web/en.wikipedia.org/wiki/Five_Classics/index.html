The Five Classics (Chinese: 五經, Wǔjīng) are five pre-Qin Chinese books that form part of the traditional Confucian canon. Since the reign of Emperor Wu of Han, when Confucianism became state orthodoxy in 136 BC, the Five Classics formed the core curriculum of Chinese education and, later, of the imperial civil-service examinations.

The five texts

  • I Ching (易經, Classic of Changes) — a divination manual built on hexagrams, with layers of philosophical commentary (the "Ten Wings").
  • Book of Documents (書經, Shūjīng) — speeches and proclamations attributed to sage-kings and early Zhou rulers; some chapters were shown to be later forgeries.
  • Book of Poetry (詩經, Shījīng) — an anthology of 305 poems, songs, and hymns dating from the 11th–7th centuries BC.
  • Book of Rites (禮經, Lǐjīng) — texts on ritual, ceremony, and social norms, principally the Liji compiled in the Han dynasty.
  • Spring and Autumn Annals (春秋, Chūnqiū) — a terse chronicle of the state of Lu (722–481 BC), traditionally read as Confucius's moral judgements in coded language, with three surviving commentaries including the Zuo Zhuan.

History

Tradition credits Confucius (551–479 BC) with compiling or editing all five. The Qin dynasty's biblioclasm of 213 BC destroyed many copies; Han scholars reconstructed the texts, sparking the Old Text/New Text controversy over which versions were authentic. Stone-carved editions (such as the Xiping Stone Classics, AD 175) fixed authoritative versions. From the Song dynasty, the Five Classics were studied alongside the Four Books, chosen by Zhu Xi, and mastery of them defined the scholar-official class until the examination system was abolished in 1905.

The lost sixth classic

Earlier sources sometimes speak of Six Classics, the sixth being a Classic of Music (樂經) said to have been lost by the Han dynasty; some scholars doubt it ever existed as a separate book.

Influence

The Five Classics shaped East Asian political thought, historiography, literature, and ritual practice for two millennia, and were transmitted to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, where they anchored analogous examination systems and Confucian academies.
