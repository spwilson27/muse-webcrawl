Confirmational holism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Quine's metaphor: beliefs form a web that meets experience only at the edges

In the philosophy of science, confirmational holism (also called the Duhem–Quine thesis) is the view that a scientific theory cannot be tested in isolation; an empirical test of a theory always involves a background of auxiliary assumptions and other theories. A failed prediction therefore shows that something in the whole web of belief is wrong, but does not by itself identify which part.

Origins

The idea was developed by Pierre Duhem, who argued in The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory (1906) that a physical experiment can never condemn an isolated hypothesis, only a whole theoretical group. W. V. O. Quine generalized the thesis in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) to all knowledge: "our statements about the external world face the tribunal of sense experience not individually but only as a corporate body."

Consequences

Confirmational holism challenges simple falsificationism: when a prediction fails, scientists may rationally preserve a favored theory by revising auxiliary hypotheses instead (for instance, by postulating an unseen planet to explain anomalies in Uranus's orbit — a move vindicated by the discovery of Neptune). Critics respond that holism threatens to make theory choice unconstrained; defenders reply that pragmatic virtues and the structure of the web — with logic and mathematics at its protected center and observation sentences at its periphery — guide reasonable revision.
