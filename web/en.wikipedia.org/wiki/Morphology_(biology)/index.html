Morphology (biology)

Morphology
FieldBiology
StudiesForm and structure of organisms and their parts
Term coined byJohann Wolfgang von Goethe (1790s); Karl Friedrich Burdach (1800)

In biology, morphology is the study of the form and structure of organisms and their specific structural features. It encompasses the outward appearance (shape, structure, colour, pattern, size) — termed external morphology or eidonomy — and the form and structure of internal parts such as bones and organs — internal morphology or anatomy. The term, from the Greek morphē ("form") and logos ("study"), stands in contrast to physiology, which deals primarily with function.

Divisions

  • Comparative morphology analyses patterns of structure within the body's plan of related organisms, identifying homologous and analogous features.
  • Functional morphology investigates the relationship between structure and function.
  • Experimental morphology studies the effects of external factors on form under controlled conditions.
  • Molecular morphology is sometimes used for the structure of composite molecules such as proteins.

History

Goethe developed morphology as a way of understanding the unity underlying plant diversity, proposing that all plant organs are modifications of the leaf. In the 19th century, comparative morphology underpinned theories of evolution: Richard Owen's definitions of homology and analogy, and later Ernst Haeckel's work, framed form as evidence of descent with modification. In the 20th century, quantification arrived with D'Arcy Thompson's On Growth and Form (1917) and, later, geometric morphometrics, which uses landmarks and statistical shape analysis.

Importance

Morphological characters remain fundamental to taxonomy and systematics, to reading the fossil record, and to evolutionary developmental biology ("evo-devo"), which asks how developmental processes generate morphological diversity.
