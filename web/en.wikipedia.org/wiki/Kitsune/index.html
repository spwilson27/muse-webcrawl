Kitsune

Kitsune (狐, きつね, IPA: [kʲi̥t͡sɯne̞]) is the Japanese word for fox, and in Japanese folklore the fox is a being of intelligence and magical power, closely associated with the Shinto deity Inari. Beliefs about foxes developed from close contact with the animals in agricultural Japan and from Chinese fox lore; stories depict kitsune as tricksters, lovers, wives, guardians, and messengers of the gods, able to grow up to nine tails as their age, wisdom, and power increase.

Types

Folklore distinguishes zenko (善狐, "good foxes") — the celestial, benevolent messengers of Inari, whose shrines across Japan are guarded by fox statues — from yako (野狐, "field foxes"), the wild, mischievous or malevolent foxes of popular tale. Local traditions recognise further types, and foxes are broadly grouped with other animal spirits such as tanuki and mujina. A fox that lives long acquires additional tails; the nine-tailed fox (kyūbi no kitsune) is the most powerful and wise.

Powers and behaviour

The most famous kitsune power is shapeshifting into human form, usually learned at a certain age (often said to be 50 or 100 years), typically to trick humans — though many tales tell of foxes taking the form of women who marry men and live faithfully until discovered, the fox-wife fleeing when her true nature is exposed (a famous example is Kuzunoha, mother of the onmyōji Abe no Seimei, who left the poem "Koishiku ba..." behind her). Kitsune are also credited with possessing humans (kitsunetsuki, fox possession, once a recognised explanation of mental illness in Japan), bending time and space, appearing in dreams, breathing fire or lightning (kitsunebi, "fox-fire"), and carrying a round white ball (hoshi no tama) said to hold their power or soul. They are famously fond of fried tofu — hence the dishes kitsune udon and inarizushi.

History and origins

Fox lore entered Japan from China (where the huli jing could be auspicious or dangerous) by at least the eighth century; the Nihon Ryōiki (c. 822) already contains fox-wife tales. In medieval Japan the identification of foxes with Inari's messengers made them revered at the thousands of Inari shrines — Fushimi Inari-taisha in Kyoto being the head shrine — while village belief also held foxes dangerous. The Edo period produced both sceptical essays and punishment of supposed fox-owners (kitsune-mochi), a stigma that persisted into modern times. Kitsune remain ubiquitous in modern Japanese popular culture, from Noh and kabuki plays such as Yoshitsune Senbon Zakura to anime, manga, and video games.
