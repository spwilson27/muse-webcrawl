The Khanate of Khiva was a Central Asian khanate centred on the oasis of Khwarazm, south of the Aral Sea, from 1511 to 1920. Ruled by Uzbek dynasties—the Arabshahids and later the Qonghrats—from the city of Khiva, it was one of the three Uzbek khanates (with Kokand and the Emirate of Bukhara) that divided Central Asia until the Russian conquest.

History

The khanate was founded when the Uzbek prince Ilbars took Khwarazm from the Timurids in 1511. Its early capitals included Konye-Urgench, devastated when the Amu Darya changed course; the court moved to Khiva in the early 17th century. The khans' authority often barely extended beyond the oasis, and Turkmen tribes raided the frontiers while Khivan markets became notorious for trading in Persian and Russian slaves captured by the Turkmens. In the 19th century the Qonghrat dynasty consolidated power under khans such as Muhammad Rahim I and his successors, building the palaces, madrasas, and minarets that make the walled city of Itchan Kala a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Russian domination and fall

The khanate became a focus of the "Great Game" between Russia and Britain; the British officer James Abbott's mission to Khiva in 1840 to negotiate the release of Russian captives became a celebrated episode. Russian forces under Konstantin von Kaufman captured Khiva in 1873; by the Treaty of Gandamian the khanate became a Russian protectorate, ceding territory on the right bank of the Amu Darya. After the Russian Revolution, Red Army forces deposed the last khan, Said Abdullah, in 1920, replacing the khanate with the Khorezm People's Soviet Republic, which was absorbed into the Uzbek and Turkmen SSRs in 1924.

Economy and culture

The oasis economy depended on irrigation from the Amu Darya for cotton, grain, and fruit; Khiva exported silk, cotton, and slaves along caravan routes to Russia and Persia. Its court patronised chronicles in Khorezmian Turkic, notably the works of the historian-khan Abu'l-Ghazi Bahadur in the 17th century.
