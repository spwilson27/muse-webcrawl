George Smith Patton Jr. (November 11, 1885 – December 21, 1945) was a general in the United States Army who commanded the Seventh Army in the Mediterranean Theater and the Third Army in France and Germany after the Allied invasion of Normandy in June 1944. He is widely regarded as one of the most successful American field commanders of World War II.

Early life and career

Born into a wealthy California family with a strong military tradition, Patton graduated from the United States Military Academy at West Point in 1909. He competed in the modern pentathlon at the 1912 Stockholm Olympics, finishing fifth. He served in the Pancho Villa Expedition (1916), then in World War I commanded troops in the new U.S. Tank Corps, was wounded at the Meuse-Argonne offensive, and between the wars became a leading advocate of armored warfare.

World War II

Patton led the Western Task Force in the invasion of North Africa (Operation Torch, 1942) and took command of II Corps after the American defeat at Kasserine Pass, restoring discipline. In 1943 he commanded the Seventh Army in the invasion of Sicily, racing Bernard Montgomery to Messina — a campaign marred by incidents in which Patton slapped two hospitalized soldiers suffering from combat stress, for which he was forced to apologize and temporarily sidelined.

Given command of the Third Army in 1944, Patton led its famous breakout and rapid advance across France after Operation Cobra, relieved Bastogne during the Battle of the Bulge by pivoting his army north in winter conditions, and drove into Germany and Czechoslovakia in 1945. His aggressive, offensive-minded style and flamboyant persona — including ivory-handled revolvers — made him famous and controversial. After the war he served briefly as military governor of Bavaria. He died in December 1945 from injuries sustained in a car accident near Mannheim.

Legacy

Patton is commemorated by the award-winning 1970 film Patton, starring George C. Scott, and by numerous books and the Patton Museum at Fort Knox. His emphasis on speed, initiative, and offensive action influenced American armored doctrine.
