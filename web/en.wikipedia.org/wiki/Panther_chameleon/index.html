The panther chameleon (Furcifer pardalis) is a species of chameleon found in the eastern and northern parts of Madagascar, with introduced populations in Florida and Hawaii. It is among the most colourful of all reptiles and one of the most popular chameleons in the pet trade.

Description

Males grow to about 50 cm (20 in) in length and display striking colour patterns that vary dramatically by locality — populations from different regions ("locales") show characteristic combinations of turquoise, green, red, orange and blue, usually with a pale lateral stripe. Females are smaller and more uniformly coloured, typically green, tan or peachy, brightening with contrasting patterns when gravid or signalling receptivity. Like other chameleons, panther chameleons change colour mainly for communication and thermoregulation, swivel their eyes independently, and snatch insects with a ballistic tongue longer than their body.

Habitat and behaviour

They inhabit lowland rainforest and drier forest and scrub, particularly along forest edges, river banks and roadside vegetation. Males are territorial and court females with vivid displays; females dig burrows in sandy soil to lay clutches of 10–46 eggs, which incubate for many months.

In captivity

Panther chameleons are widely bred in captivity, with named locales such as Ambanja, Ambilobe, Nosy Be and Sambava prized by keepers. Wild populations are regulated under CITES Appendix II.
