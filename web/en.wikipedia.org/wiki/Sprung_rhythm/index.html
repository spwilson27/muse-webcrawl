Sprung rhythm


Hopkins marked stresses in his manuscripts of sprung rhythm verse.

Sprung rhythm is a poetic meter invented by Gerard Manley Hopkins (1844–1889). Unlike conventional English meters, which count both stressed and unstressed syllables, sprung rhythm counts only the stresses in a line: each foot consists of one stressed syllable plus a variable number (from zero to four) of unstressed syllables. The result imitates the rhythms of natural speech, nursery rhymes, and Anglo-Saxon verse while permitting great metrical variety within a fixed stress pattern.

Technique

Hopkins argued that sprung rhythm was not new but had always existed in English — in the poetry of Shakespeare and Milton and in common speech — buried under syllable-counting convention. Feet can begin with the stress and "fall away" in slack syllables; Hopkins also allowed extra-metrical syllables and marked his poems with stress signs. Poems in sprung rhythm include "God's Grandeur", "The Windhover", and the long shipwreck ode "The Wreck of the Deutschland".

Influence

Though Hopkins's work was published only in 1918, decades after his death, sprung rhythm influenced twentieth-century poets seeking an escape from strict iambic regularity, and anticipates some techniques of free verse.
