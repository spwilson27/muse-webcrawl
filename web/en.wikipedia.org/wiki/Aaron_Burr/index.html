Aaron Burr Jr. (February 6, 1756 – September 14, 1836) was an American politician and lawyer who served as the third vice president of the United States from 1801 to 1805 under President Thomas Jefferson. A Revolutionary War officer and leading Democratic-Republican in New York, he is remembered chiefly for killing Alexander Hamilton in a duel and for his subsequent trial for treason.

Early life and career

Burr was the grandson of theologian Jonathan Edwards and son of Aaron Burr Sr., second president of Princeton, from which the younger Burr graduated at sixteen. He served with distinction in the Continental Army, fighting at Quebec, Monmouth and elsewhere, and rose to lieutenant colonel before resigning in 1779. As a New York lawyer he rivalled Hamilton professionally and politically; he served as state attorney general and defeated Hamilton's father-in-law Philip Schuyler for a U.S. Senate seat in 1791.

Vice presidency and the duel

In the election of 1800, Burr and Jefferson tied in the Electoral College; the House of Representatives chose Jefferson after a deadlock in which Hamilton lobbied against Burr. Dropped from the 1804 ticket, Burr ran for governor of New York and lost, blaming Hamilton's opposition. After Hamilton allegedly made a "despicable opinion" of him at a dinner, Burr challenged him to a duel. On July 11, 1804, at Weehawken, New Jersey, Burr mortally wounded Hamilton, who died the next day. Burr was indicted for murder in New York and New Jersey but never tried, and he completed his term as vice president.

The Burr conspiracy and treason trial

After 1805 Burr travelled west and became involved in schemes — variously interpreted as plans to seize Spanish territory, detach western lands, or invade Mexico — with General James Wilkinson, who betrayed him. Arrested in 1807, Burr was tried for treason in Richmond, Virginia, before Chief Justice John Marshall, whose narrow definition of treason (requiring an overt act of levying war, proved by two witnesses) led to acquittal. Disgraced, Burr spent years in Europe before quietly returning to legal practice in New York.

Later life and legacy

Burr's beloved daughter Theodosia was lost at sea in 1813. He practised law in New York, married the wealthy widow Eliza Jumel in 1833 in a brief, unhappy union, and died in 1836. His reputation has been reassessed by some historians, and he was famously reimagined in Gore Vidal's novel Burr (1973) and in the musical Hamilton (2015), where he serves as narrator and antagonist.
