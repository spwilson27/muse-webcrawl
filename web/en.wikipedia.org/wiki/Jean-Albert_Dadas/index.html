Jean-Albert Dadas

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Portrait of Jean-Albert Dadas

Jean-Albert Dadas (1860–1899) was a French gas fitter from Bordeaux who became famous in nineteenth-century medicine as the first diagnosed "fugueur"—a compulsive pathological traveller. His case, described by his physician Philippe Tissié in the 1887 thesis Les Aliénés voyageurs, gave its name to the diagnosis of "dadas" or ambulatory automatism.

The case

Dadas would periodically abandon his work and family and walk compulsively for days or weeks, covering great distances with little awareness of his actions and sometimes waking, exhausted, in far-off cities; his travels reputedly took him as far as Algiers, Moscow and Constantinople, where he was arrested as a vagrant and deserter from military service. Tissié treated him with hypnosis and attempted to control the fugues.

Legacy

The case became a cornerstone of the brief epidemic of fugue diagnoses in late nineteenth-century France, later discussed by Ian Hacking in Mad Travelers (1998) as an example of a "transient mental illness" shaped by its medical and cultural niche. Dissociative fugue survives in modern classification as a specifier of dissociative amnesia.
