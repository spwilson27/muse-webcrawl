Four-day workweek

A four-day workweek is a working arrangement in which employees work four days per week instead of the conventional five. Models vary: the "100-80-100" model popularised by the organisation 4 Day Week Global keeps 100% of pay for 80% of the time in exchange for 100% of output, while other models compress the same total hours into longer days, or simply reduce both hours and pay proportionally.

History

The five-day week itself was a twentieth-century achievement, standardised after Henry Ford adopted it in 1926 and the United States amended the Fair Labor Standards Act in 1940 to define a 40-hour week. Proposals for further reductions go back decades, and economist John Maynard Keynes famously predicted in 1930 that productivity gains might eventually allow a 15-hour week.

Trials and evidence

Large-scale trials in the 2010s and 2020s revived interest. Microsoft Japan reported a roughly 40% productivity increase in a 2019 trial. The New Zealand firm Perpetual Guardian's 2018 trial led founder Andrew Barnes to co-found 4 Day Week Global, which coordinated pilot programmes in the United Kingdom, the United States, Ireland, and elsewhere. The 2022 UK pilot, involving 61 companies and about 2,900 workers, found that revenue broadly held steady, staff turnover and burnout fell, and most participating companies chose to continue the schedule after the trial. Iceland's public-sector trials between 2015 and 2019, covering about 1% of the workforce, were reported as an "overwhelming success" and led to reduced hours for most workers through union agreements.

Criticism

Critics note that results may reflect selection effects, that compressed schedules can increase work intensity, and that four-day weeks are difficult to apply in sectors requiring continuous coverage such as healthcare and hospitality.
