Banjo clock

The banjo clock is a type of American wall clock invented by the clockmaker Simon Willard of Roxbury, Massachusetts, who received a United States patent for the design in 1802. Willard called it his "Improved Timepiece" or patent timepiece; the name "banjo clock" is a later collector's term, taken from the case's resemblance to the musical instrument — a round dial head above a long, tapering trunk.

Design

The case is typically about 33–36 inches tall and consists of three parts: a circular or octagonal head housing an 8-inch painted iron dial; a narrow waisted throat, usually fronted by a reverse-painted glass (églomisé) tablet; and a rectangular box below with a second painted glass panel, often depicting a landscape, naval battle, or patriotic scene. Decorative brass side arms and a giltwood eagle or ball finial complete the silhouette. The weight-driven, eight-day brass movement is small and of high quality, with the pendulum concealed inside the case; time-only versions are commonest, though striking examples exist.

Production and legacy

Willard's design was widely imitated by his brothers and by makers across New England, and it became one of the first distinctively American clock forms. Original Federal-period examples by the Willard family are highly prized by collectors and museums. Simplified, spring-driven versions remained in production through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and the form became a fixture of American schoolrooms, railway stations, and ships — the related "schoolhouse" and marine clocks share its heritage.
