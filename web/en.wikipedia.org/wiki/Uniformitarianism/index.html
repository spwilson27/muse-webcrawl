Uniformitarianism

Uniformitarianism, also known as the Doctrine of Uniformity or the uniformitarian principle, is the assumption that the same natural laws and processes that operate in our present-day scientific observations have always operated in the universe in the past and apply everywhere in the universe. It has been summarised as "the present is the key to the past". Uniformitarianism was a founding principle of modern geology, advanced in opposition to catastrophism, which attributed Earth's features to sudden, violent events.

History

The Scottish geologist James Hutton proposed in his Theory of the Earth (1788, expanded 1795) that Earth's landscapes were shaped by slow processes operating over immense time — his observation of the angular unconformity at Siccar Point showed rock sequences requiring far more time than biblical chronologies allowed. Hutton's friend John Playfair clarified the theory in 1802. Charles Lyell popularised and extended the doctrine in his Principles of Geology (1830–1833), which profoundly influenced Charles Darwin's thinking about gradual change in biology.

Modern interpretation

The historian William Whewell coined the term "uniformitarianism" in 1832. Modern geology distinguishes methodological uniformitarianism — the assumption that natural laws are invariant in space and time, indispensable to all science — from Lyell's stronger substantive claims that rates and conditions on Earth have always resembled today's. The latter has been softened: rare catastrophic events such as asteroid impacts and megafloods are now accepted as part of Earth's history, a synthesis sometimes called "actualism".
