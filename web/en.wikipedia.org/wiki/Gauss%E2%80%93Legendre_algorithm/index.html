Gauss–Legendre algorithm

The Gauss–Legendre algorithm is an iterative algorithm for computing the digits of π. It is notable for its rapid convergence: the number of correct digits roughly doubles with each iteration. The algorithm is based on the arithmetic–geometric mean and ideas of Carl Friedrich Gauss, and was used in several record computations of π in the computer age.

Algorithm

Start with a₀ = 1, b₀ = 1/√2, t₀ = 1/4 and p₀ = 1. At each step, set aₙ₊₁ to the arithmetic mean of aₙ and bₙ, bₙ₊₁ to the geometric mean √(aₙbₙ), adjust t by subtracting pₙ(aₙ − aₙ₊₁)², and double p. After a few iterations, π ≈ (a + b)² / (4t), with a and b the converged means. Only about 25 iterations are needed for more than 100 million correct digits.

History

Gauss developed the underlying arithmetic–geometric mean iteration in the early nineteenth century; Adrien-Marie Legendre also worked on related elliptic-integral methods. Richard Brent and Eugene Salamin independently rediscovered the algorithm in 1975–76, and it is consequently also called the Brent–Salamin algorithm. Yasumasa Kanada used it for record computations of π in the 1980s and 1990s.
