Normal play

In combinatorial game theory, normal play is the convention under which the player who makes the last move wins the game. It is contrasted with misère play, in which the player who makes the last move loses. Normal play is the standard convention for impartial games such as Nim and Treblecross, and the theory of such games under normal play is governed by the Sprague–Grundy theorem, which assigns every impartial game position a Grundy value (nimber), with a position being a first-player win exactly when its Grundy value is non-zero.

Background

The terminology was standardised in the foundational texts of combinatorial game theory, notably On Numbers and Games (1976) by John Horton Conway and Winning Ways for your Mathematical Plays (1982) by Berlekamp, Conway and Guy. Under normal play, the disjunctive sum of impartial games is equivalent to a Nim heap whose size is the nim-sum of the components' Grundy values, which makes the analysis of many apparently unrelated games uniform.

Misère play

Under misère play the theory is more complicated: for Nim itself only the endgame strategy changes (leaving heaps so that the opponent is forced to take the last object), but for general impartial games the misère quotient theory developed by Thane Plambeck and Aaron Siegel is needed. A few games, such as Dawson's Kayles and Treblecross, are traditionally analysed in both conventions.
