The Ban of Croatia (Croatian: Hrvatski ban) was the title borne by the viceroy or royal governor of Croatia from the medieval kingdom through the Habsburg period until 1918. The ban was the highest Croatian dignitary after the king, presiding over the Croatian Sabor (diet) and commanding the kingdom's military forces. The title, of debated origin, is shared with the bans who governed Bosnia and other territories of the Hungarian-Croatian crown.

Medieval period

Bans are recorded in the Kingdom of Croatia from the 10th century. After the personal union with Hungary in 1102, the king appointed the ban of "the whole of Slavonia" and the ban of Croatia and Dalmatia, posts sometimes united. Notable medieval bans include the Šubić family, whose member Paul I Šubić of Bribir (ban 1275–1312) ruled almost as an independent prince.

Habsburg era

Under Habsburg rule the ban remained the head of the Croatian civil administration and president of the Sabor, though his authority was curtailed by the Military Frontier administered directly from Vienna. Celebrated bans include Nikola Zrinski (ban 1647–1664), a hero of the Ottoman wars; Josip Jelačić (1848–1859), who abolished serfdom in Croatia and led Croatian forces in the revolutions of 1848; and Ivan Mažuranić (1873–1880), a reformer and poet known as the "people's ban". The office disappeared with the collapse of Austria-Hungary in 1918.

Functions

The ban convened and presided over the Sabor, promulgated its decisions, headed the judiciary through the banski stol (ban's court), and served as commander-in-chief of the banal regiments. From 1868, under the Croatian–Hungarian Settlement, the ban was appointed by the king on the proposal of the Hungarian prime minister but was responsible to the Sabor.
