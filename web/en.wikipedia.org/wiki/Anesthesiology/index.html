Anesthesiology

Anesthesiologists manage anaesthesia, vital functions and pain during surgery.

Anesthesiology, anaesthesiology, or anaesthesia is the medical specialty concerned with the total perioperative care of patients before, during and after surgery. It encompasses anaesthesia, intensive care medicine, critical emergency medicine, and pain medicine. A physician specialised in this field of medicine is called an anesthesiologist, anaesthesiologist, or anaesthetist, depending on the country.

Scope

The core element of the specialty is the practice of anaesthesia: the use of drugs to produce a reversible state of unconsciousness (general anaesthesia), regional numbness (such as spinal or epidural blocks), or sedation, while protecting the patient's airway, breathing, circulation, and other vital functions. Beyond the operating room, anesthesiologists staff intensive care units, run acute and chronic pain services, provide labour analgesia, and lead resuscitation teams.

History

The modern era began with the first public demonstration of ether anaesthesia by William T. G. Morton at Massachusetts General Hospital on 16 October 1846. Chloroform, nitrous oxide, and local anaesthetics followed in the 19th century; the 20th century added muscle relaxants, intravenous agents such as thiopental and propofol, sophisticated monitoring, and standards that made anaesthesia dramatically safer — anaesthesia-related mortality fell from roughly one in a few thousand cases to on the order of one in several hundred thousand for healthy patients.

Training

Training typically requires completion of medical school followed by a residency of four or more years in anesthesiology, with optional fellowships in cardiac, paediatric, neuro-, obstetric, pain, or critical-care anaesthesia.
