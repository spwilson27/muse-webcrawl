Search and matching theory (economics)

The matching process between job seekers and vacancies

Search and matching theory is a mathematical framework in labour economics describing the formation of mutually beneficial relationships over time, most famously applied to the creation of jobs. Because trade in labour markets is decentralised and takes time, workers and firms must search for one another; unemployment and vacancies therefore coexist even in equilibrium. The theory's developers, Peter Diamond, Dale Mortensen and Christopher Pissarides, shared the 2010 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.

The matching function

The centrepiece of the framework is a matching function, usually written m = m(u, v), which gives the number of new matches (jobs) formed per period as a function of the number of unemployed workers u and job vacancies v. The function is typically assumed to be increasing in both arguments, concave, and homogeneous of degree one, implying constant returns to scale in matching. A common specification is the Cobb–Douglas form m = A uη v1−η.

The probability that a vacancy is filled, q(θ), and the probability that an unemployed worker finds a job, θq(θ), depend on labour market tightness θ = v/u. When the market is tight (many vacancies per job seeker), firms find it harder to hire and workers find it easier to find work.

The Diamond–Mortensen–Pissarides model

In the canonical DMP model, jobs are destroyed at an exogenous rate, firms post vacancies at a flow cost, and wages are determined by Nash bargaining once a worker and firm meet, splitting the surplus of the match. Free entry of firms drives the value of a vacancy to zero, yielding a job creation condition linking wages, hiring costs and market tightness. The model generates equilibrium unemployment determined by flows into and out of work rather than by wage rigidity alone.

The Beveridge curve and efficiency

The model predicts a downward-sloping relationship between unemployment and vacancies — the Beveridge curve — as shocks shift the economy along it, while changes in matching efficiency shift the curve itself. The Hosios condition characterises the bargaining power under which the decentralised equilibrium is socially efficient: efficiency requires workers' bargaining share to equal the elasticity of the matching function with respect to unemployment.

Applications

Search and matching models have been applied to labour market policy (unemployment insurance, hiring subsidies, minimum wages), to monetary economics, to housing and marriage markets, and to the study of business cycle fluctuations in unemployment, vacancies and job flows. The framework was extended by Mortensen and Pissarides to include endogenous job destruction, producing realistic patterns of job creation and destruction over the cycle.
