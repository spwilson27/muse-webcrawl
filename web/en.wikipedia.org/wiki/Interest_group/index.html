Interest group

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An interest group, also called a special interest group, advocacy group or pressure group, is an organisation that seeks to influence public policy in the interest of its members or cause, without seeking to govern itself (the distinction from a political party). Groups range from business associations, trade unions and professional bodies to single-issue movements (environmental, gun rights, pro- and anti-abortion), think tanks, charities and ad-hoc campaigns.

Political science distinguishes insider groups (consulted routinely by government) from outsider groups (relying on public protest), and analyses their resources: membership size, money, expertise, and the selective incentives (Olson's Logic of Collective Action, 1965) that overcome free-riding — large latent groups are hard to organise, small concentrated ones easy, which biases representation toward producers over diffuse consumers and taxpayers. Pluralists (Truman, Dahl) saw group competition as democracy's engine; critics point to unequal access (“the heavenly chorus sings with a strong upper-class accent” — Schattschneider) and to capture of the state by the organised.
