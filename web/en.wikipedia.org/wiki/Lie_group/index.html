In mathematics, a Lie group (pronounced /liː/) is a group that is also a differentiable manifold, with the group operations of multiplication and inversion being smooth maps. Lie groups — named after the Norwegian mathematician Sophus Lie, who developed their theory in the 1870s and 1880s to study the symmetries of differential equations — combine algebraic and geometric structure and are fundamental to modern mathematics and physics.

Examples

The simplest examples are the real numbers under addition; the circle group U(1) of complex numbers of modulus 1; the general linear groups GL(n, ℝ) and GL(n, ℂ) of invertible matrices; the rotation group SO(3); the unitary groups U(n) and SU(n); and the Heisenberg group. Finite groups are zero-dimensional Lie groups.

Lie algebras

To each Lie group is associated a Lie algebra — its tangent space at the identity, equipped with a bracket operation — which captures the group's local structure. The exponential map connects the two. Simple Lie groups and algebras were classified by Wilhelm Killing and Élie Cartan into the four infinite families An, Bn, Cn, Dn and five exceptional types G2, F4, E6, E7, E8.

Applications

Lie groups model continuous symmetries: rotations and translations in mechanics, the Poincaré group of spacetime, and the gauge groups U(1) × SU(2) × SU(3) of the Standard Model of particle physics. Their representation theory, developed with characters and weights, underlies much of quantum mechanics and the classification of elementary particles.
