Hugo Steinhaus.

Władysław Hugo Dionizy Steinhaus (14 January 1887 – 25 February 1972) was a Polish mathematician and educator, a central figure of the Lwów School of Mathematics. He studied in Lwów and Göttingen, receiving his doctorate in 1911 under David Hilbert.

Career and contributions

Steinhaus helped found the Lwów school around Stefan Banach, whose talent he famously "discovered" by overhearing a park conversation about Lebesgue integration. Jointly with Banach he proved the Banach–Steinhaus theorem (uniform boundedness principle), a cornerstone of functional analysis. His interests were remarkably broad: Fourier series, probability theory, measure theory, game theory and applications of mathematics. He proposed the "one long, three short" notation for very large numbers known as Steinhaus–Moser notation, posed the influential cake-cutting ("fair division") problems — asking for envy-free divisions among three and more people — and formulated the ham sandwich theorem's popular form. He also originated an early formulation of what became the Steinhaus paradox on spheres.

Steinhaus survived the Second World War in hiding under a false identity after the German occupation of Lwów. Afterwards he rebuilt mathematics at the University of Wrocław, founded the journal Colloquium Mathematicum, and wrote the beloved popular book Mathematical Snapshots (1938), translated into many languages. He supervised numerous doctoral students, including Mark Kac.
