Organophosphate

General structure of an organophosphate

In organic chemistry, an organophosphate is a class of organophosphorus compound — generally the esters of phosphoric acid — that includes many of the most widely used insecticides as well as chemical-warfare nerve agents. Organophosphates are characterized by a central phosphorus atom bonded to oxygen (or sulfur) and to organic groups via ester linkages.

Mode of action

Organophosphate insecticides and nerve agents act by irreversibly inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, the enzyme that breaks down the neurotransmitter acetylcholine. The resulting accumulation of acetylcholine causes continuous stimulation of muscles and glands, producing the cholinergic symptoms of organophosphate poisoning: salivation, lacrimation, convulsions, respiratory failure, and potentially death. Atropine and oximes such as pralidoxime are used as antidotes.

Uses

Compounds such as parathion, malathion, chlorpyrifos, and diazinon have been used extensively in agriculture since the 1940s, replacing organochlorines such as DDT. The same chemistry yielded the G-series and V-series nerve agents (sarin, soman, VX), whose military use is banned under the Chemical Weapons Convention. Less toxic organophosphates also serve as plasticizers, flame retardants, and lubricants.

Toxicity and regulation

Because of their acute toxicity to humans and wildlife and evidence linking chronic low-level exposure to neurodevelopmental effects, many organophosphate pesticides have been progressively restricted or banned in the European Union, United States, and elsewhere, though they remain in use in much of the world.
