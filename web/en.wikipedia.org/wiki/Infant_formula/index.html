Infant formula

Infant formula, also called baby formula, simply formula (American English), or baby milk, is an ultra-processed food designed and marketed for feeding babies and infants, usually as a substitute for human breast milk when breastfeeding is not possible, not sufficient, or not desired. Most formula is based on cow's milk modified to approximate the nutritional composition of mature human milk, though soy-based and extensively hydrolysed formulas exist for infants with allergies or metabolic disorders.

Composition and regulation

Modern formulas are standardised to provide roughly 67 kilocalories per 100 ml, with adjusted whey-to-casein ratios, vegetable oil blends as the fat source, lactose as the principal carbohydrate, and added vitamins and minerals including iron, vitamin D, and increasingly long-chain fatty acids such as DHA. Composition is tightly regulated — for example by the US Food and Drug Administration under the Infant Formula Act of 1980 (enacted after a chloride-deficient formula harmed infants) and by Codex Alimentarius standards internationally.

Health guidance

Health authorities including the World Health Organization recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life where possible, while recognising properly prepared formula as a safe alternative. Powdered formula is not sterile and has been associated with rare infections such as Cronobacter sakazakii, which led to guidance on careful preparation with hot water. A factory shutdown in the United States in 2022 precipitated a nationwide formula shortage, exposing the concentration of the market.

Marketing controversy

Aggressive marketing of formula in low-income countries prompted the WHO's International Code of Marketing of Breast-milk Substitutes (1981), following the Nestlé boycott of the late 1970s. The Code restricts advertising and free samples, though it is implemented unevenly across countries.
