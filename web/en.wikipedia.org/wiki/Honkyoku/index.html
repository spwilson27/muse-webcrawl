Honkyoku

Honkyoku
OriginJapan, Edo period (Fuke sect of Zen Buddhism)
InstrumentShakuhachi
PurposeSuizen ("blowing Zen") meditation and alms-seeking

Honkyoku (Japanese: 本曲, "original pieces") are the pieces of shakuhachi music that were handed down within the Fuke sect of Japanese Zen Buddhism, whose mendicant monks, the komusō, played the shakuhachi as a form of meditation known as suizen ("blowing Zen") and as a means of soliciting alms.

History

The Fuke sect traced its lineage to the Chinese Zen master Puhua (Fuke in Japanese). During the Edo period, the komusō — often masterless samurai (rōnin) — were granted distinctive privileges, including travelling freely and wearing the concealing tengai basket hat. Their playing was a spiritual discipline rather than entertainment, and pieces were transmitted orally within temples such as Myōan-ji in Kyoto and Icchoken and Reihō-ji.

In the 18th century the komusō Kinko Kurosawa, associated with the Kinko-ryū school, collected and codified 36 honkyoku, which remain the best-known repertoire. When the Fuke sect was abolished in 1871 during the Meiji Restoration, honkyoku survived through secular shakuhachi schools and temple preservation societies, and is today performed both as meditation and in concert.

Characteristics

Honkyoku are solo, unmetred pieces following the player's breath, with phrases shaped by breathing pauses. They exploit the shakuhachi's full expressive range: meri and kari pitch bending, muraiki "wind" sounds, and wide dynamic contrasts. Many pieces evoke nature or temple bells — the famous Shika no Tōne ("The Distant Call of the Deer") depicts two deer calling across a valley, and pieces such as Tsuru no Sugomori imitate nesting cranes.

Terminology

The word contrasts honkyoku with gaikyoku (outside pieces — arrangements of music from other genres) and shinkyoku (new pieces, composed from the late 19th century onward).
