Cocos Plate

The Cocos Plate, off the Pacific coast of Central America

Type: Minor tectonic plate (oceanic)

Area: approximately 2.9 million km²

Movement: North-east, roughly 67 mm/year

Features: Cocos Ridge, Galápagos hotspot track

The Cocos Plate is a young oceanic tectonic plate beneath the Pacific Ocean off the west coast of Central America, named for Cocos Island, which lies upon it. It is bordered by the Pacific Plate to the west, the Nazca Plate to the south, the Caribbean Plate to the east and the North American Plate to the north. The Cocos Plate was created, along with the Nazca Plate, by the breakup of the ancient Farallon Plate around 23 million years ago as subduction patterns off western North America changed.

Geology

The plate is being subducted beneath both the North American Plate (off southern Mexico) and the Caribbean Plate (off Central America) along the Middle America Trench, producing the volcanic arc of Central America and frequent large earthquakes. Its north-eastern motion is among the fastest of any plate relative to its neighbours. The northeastern boundary includes the Motagua fault zone of Guatemala, site of a destructive magnitude 7.5 earthquake in 1976.

The plate carries the Cocos Ridge, a broad submarine volcanic ridge running from the Galápagos region to Costa Rica, believed to record the track of the Galápagos hotspot. Subduction of this buoyant ridge beneath Costa Rica is associated with the uplift of the Talamanca range and a gap in active volcanism. Off Mexico, subduction of the Cocos Plate produced the great 1985 Michoacán earthquake that devastated parts of Mexico City, and more recently the 2017 Chiapas and Puebla earthquakes.

Boundaries

To the west, the plate is spreading away from the Pacific Plate along the East Pacific Rise; to the south it diverges from the Nazca Plate along the Galápagos spreading centre (Cocos–Nazca Ridge). The Panama fracture zone forms a transform boundary with the Caribbean Plate in the south-east.
