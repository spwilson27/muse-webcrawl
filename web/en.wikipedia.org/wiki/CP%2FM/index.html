CP/M

CP/M
Developer: Gary Kildall, Digital Research
Released: 1974
Name: "Control Program for Microcomputers"
Platforms: Intel 8080, Zilog Z80 and compatibles
Successor lineage: CP/M-86, DR-DOS; influenced MS-DOS

CP/M (Control Program for Microcomputers) is a mass-market operating system created in 1974 for Intel 8080/85-based microcomputers by Gary Kildall of Digital Research, Inc. Initially confined to single-tasking on 8-bit processors and no more than 64 kilobytes of memory, CP/M became the dominant operating system of the late 1970s and early 1980s business microcomputer market.

Architecture

CP/M's portability came from splitting the system into a hardware-dependent BIOS (Basic Input/Output System), a hardware-independent BDOS (Basic Disk Operating System), and a console command processor (CCP). Only the BIOS had to be rewritten for each new machine, which let hundreds of manufacturers ship compatible computers. The system standardized the 8-inch floppy disk format and a large library of business software, including WordStar and dBase.

Decline and influence

When IBM chose Microsoft's PC DOS for the IBM PC in 1981 — after talks with Digital Research famously failed to produce a timely deal — CP/M's 16-bit version, CP/M-86, lost the standard-setting market. MS-DOS borrowed much of CP/M's API and command structure. Digital Research later produced DR-DOS. In 2014, source code for early CP/M versions was released for non-commercial study.
