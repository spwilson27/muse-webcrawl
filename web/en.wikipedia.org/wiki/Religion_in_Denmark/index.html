Religion in Denmark

Religion in Denmark is predominantly Lutheran Christianity. The Evangelical Lutheran Church of Denmark (Folkekirken, "the People's Church") is the state church, supported by the Constitution of Denmark, and its head is the reigning monarch. As of recent years roughly 72–74% of Danes are members of the Church of Denmark, although weekly attendance is low, and membership has been declining steadily.

History

Denmark was Christianised in the 10th century; Harald Bluetooth's Jelling stone, raised around 965, proclaims that he "made the Danes Christian". Catholicism prevailed until the Reformation of 1536, when Christian III established Lutheranism as the state religion. Other faiths were restricted for centuries: Jews were admitted in the 17th century, and full religious freedom was granted by the Constitution of 1849, though the Lutheran Church retained its established status.

Church of Denmark

The Folkekirken is governed by the Ministry of Ecclesiastical Affairs and the Folketing; its bishops oversee ten dioceses in Denmark and one in Greenland. Members pay a church tax collected by the state. Most Danes use the church for baptisms, confirmations, weddings and funerals while describing themselves as only weakly religious — a pattern sometimes summarised as "belonging without believing", or the reverse.

Other religions

Roman Catholics form a small minority (under 1%). Islam is the second-largest religion, practised mainly by immigrants and their descendants from Turkey, Iraq, Pakistan, Bosnia and Somalia, at roughly 4–5% of the population. Other communities include Jews, Jehovah's Witnesses, Mormons, Buddhists, Hindus, and revived Norse paganism (Forn Siðr), which was officially recognised in 2003.

Irreligion

Denmark is among the most secular societies in the world; a large share of the population reports no belief in God, and "no religion" is the fastest-growing category.
