Photovoltaics (PV) is the conversion of light into electricity at the atomic level using semiconducting materials that exhibit the photovoltaic effect. A photovoltaic system employs solar modules, each made of a number of solar cells, which generate electrical power; installations range from small rooftop systems to multi-gigawatt solar parks, and photovoltaics has become one of the cheapest and fastest-growing sources of new electricity generation worldwide.

History and technology

The photovoltaic effect was first observed by Edmond Becquerel in 1839, and the first practical silicon solar cell was demonstrated at Bell Telephone Laboratories in 1954 with about 6% efficiency. Spacecraft from Vanguard 1 (1958) onward relied on PV power. In a typical crystalline silicon cell, photons absorbed in a p–n junction free electrons that are separated by the junction's electric field, producing direct current. Commercial modules today exceed 20% efficiency; perovskite and multi-junction cells hold laboratory records well above that. Inverters convert DC output to AC for grid use, and systems may include batteries, trackers and maximum-power-point tracking electronics.

Deployment

Falling costs — driven by mass manufacturing, especially in China — have made utility-scale solar the cheapest new source of electricity in much of the world. Global installed PV capacity passed one terawatt in 2022 and has continued to grow rapidly. Applications include grid-connected solar farms, residential and commercial rooftops, off-grid power, water pumping, and spacecraft. Challenges include intermittency (addressed with storage and grid integration), land use, and recycling of end-of-life modules.
