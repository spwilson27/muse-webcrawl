The Q code is a standardised collection of three-letter codes, all beginning with the letter Q, used in radio communications. Originally developed around 1909 by the British Post Office for use by ships and coast stations, the codes were adopted internationally and remain in use in amateur radio, aviation and some military contexts. Each code can express a statement or a question depending on context; for example, QTH means "my location is…", while QRM denotes interference from other stations and QRN interference from static.

History and ranges

The first Q codes appeared in official lists of abbreviations prepared for radiotelegraph operators, and the series was expanded by international radiotelegraph conventions from 1912 onward. Different letter ranges were allocated to different services: QAA–QNZ for aeronautical use (many now expressed as QNH, QFE and QNE in altimeter settings), QOA–QQZ for maritime use, and QRA–QUZ for general services, defined by the ITU Radio Regulations. Amateur radio operators adopted a subset — including QSL ("I acknowledge receipt", also the name of the confirmation postcards operators exchange), QSO (a contact), QRP (low power) and QSY (change frequency) — and many have passed into spoken ham slang.
