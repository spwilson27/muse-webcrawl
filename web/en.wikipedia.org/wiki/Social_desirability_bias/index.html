Social desirability bias

In social science research, social desirability bias is a type of response bias in which survey respondents answer questions in a manner that will be viewed favorably by others. It can take the form of over-reporting "good behavior" or under-reporting "bad" or undesirable behavior. The bias poses a serious problem with conducting research involving self-reports, especially in studies of individual characteristics such as personality.

Individual differences

People differ in their tendency to give socially desirable responses. Douglas P. Crowne and David Marlowe developed the Marlowe–Crowne Social Desirability Scale (1960), a personality inventory measuring this tendency. Research distinguishes two components:

  • Self-deceptive positivity — an honest but overly positive self-presentation;
  • Impression management — deliberate tailoring of responses to present oneself favorably to others.

Topics prone to bias

The bias is especially marked for sensitive questions, including voting behavior, religious practice, income, charitable giving, drug use, sexual behavior, and compliance with medical advice. It is implicated in the Bradley effect in opinion polling and inflates self-reported turnout in elections.

Countermeasures

Researchers reduce social desirability bias by ensuring anonymity and confidentiality, using indirect questioning (such as the randomized response technique or list experiments), asking about third parties, using forced-choice formats matched on desirability, and supplementing self-reports with behavioral or administrative data.
