Leonard Bloomfield (April 1, 1887 – April 18, 1949) was an American linguist who led the development of structural linguistics in the United States during the 1930s and 1940s. His influential textbook Language (1933) is often regarded as the founding document of American descriptive linguistics, and his behaviorist, rigorously empirical approach dominated the field for a generation.

Life and career

Bloomfield was born in Chicago and studied at Harvard University, the University of Wisconsin–Madison, and the University of Chicago, where he earned his doctorate in Germanic philology in 1909. He pursued further study at Leipzig and Göttingen with leading Indo-Europeanists. He taught at the University of Illinois, Ohio State University, the University of Chicago (1927–1940), and finally Yale University, where he held the Sterling Professorship until his death. He was a founding member and president of the Linguistic Society of America.

Work

Bloomfield's early work was in Germanic historical linguistics and in the Indo-European field. In the Philippines in 1917–1920-related research he produced a grammar of Tagalog. His most consequential fieldwork concerned Algonquian languages—Fox, Ojibwe, Menominee, and Cree—on which he produced landmark descriptive and comparative studies, including the posthumously published The Menomini Language (1962).

His 1914 book An Introduction to the Study of Language took a psychological approach, but under the influence of behaviorism he recast the discipline in Language (1933), insisting that linguistic analysis proceed from observable speech forms and defer questions of meaning, which he considered not yet scientifically tractable. The "Bloomfieldian" school that followed elevated phonemics and distributional analysis, and its methods were applied to language teaching during the Second World War. His approach was later challenged by the generative grammar of Noam Chomsky, whose teacher Zellig Harris had been Bloomfield's student.
