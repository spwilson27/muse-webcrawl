Mass culture

Mass culture is the set of ideas, perspectives, attitudes, images, and other cultural products that are consumed by large numbers of people within a society, principally those produced and distributed by mass media and the culture industry. The term emerged in the twentieth century alongside mass production, urbanisation, and technologies such as cinema, radio, television, and later the internet.

Critiques

Twentieth-century critics, notably Theodor W. Adorno and Max Horkheimer of the Frankfurt School, attacked mass culture as a standardised "culture industry" that pacifies audiences, manufactures false needs, and displaces authentic folk and high culture. Dwight Macdonald distinguished "Masscult" from genuine art and folk culture. Other thinkers, such as Walter Benjamin, saw emancipatory potential in mechanical reproduction, and later cultural studies scholars emphasised that audiences actively interpret, adapt, and resist mass cultural texts rather than absorbing them passively.

Characteristics

Mass culture is typically described as commercially produced, widely distributed, standardised yet constantly novel, and addressed to the tastes of a broad middle audience. Its forms include popular music, blockbuster films, television formats, bestsellers, advertising, and professional sport. Debates over mass culture intertwine with questions about democracy, taste, cultural homogenisation, and globalisation.
