Trust (business)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

For the legal arrangement, see Trust law. For other uses, see Trust (disambiguation).

A trust or corporate trust is a large grouping of business interests with significant market power, which may be embodied as a legal entity (a "joint-stock trust") or as an arrangement under which the shareholders of several companies transfer their shares to a small group of trustees in exchange for trust certificates, giving the trustees unified control over the companies. Trusts first became significant in late 19th-century American business, and their name became synonymous with large monopolistic enterprises.

History

The prototypical trust was the Standard Oil Trust, formed in 1882 under an agreement by which the shareholders of Standard Oil and its affiliated companies transferred their stock to nine trustees. Similar devices were adopted in the sugar, whisky, lead, cottonseed oil and linseed oil industries. Because trusts could dominate entire industries and restrain trade, they provoked a political backlash that produced the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 and the era of "trust-busting" in the United States.

Trusts and antitrust law

Although the holding company largely replaced the trust as an organizational form after New Jersey liberalized its incorporation laws in 1888–1889, the word "trust" survives in the vocabulary of competition law: bodies of law directed at monopolies and cartels are still known as antitrust law. In modern usage, "trust" in business reporting often loosely denotes any very large corporation or combination of firms.
