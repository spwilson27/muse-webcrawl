Organometallic chemistry

Ferrocene, a landmark organometallic compound discovered in 1951.

Organometallic chemistry is the study of organometallic compounds, chemical compounds containing at least one bond between a carbon atom of an organic molecule and a metal, including alkaline, alkaline earth, and transition metals, and sometimes broadened to include metalloids like boron, silicon, and selenium, as well. Aside from bonds to organyl fragments or molecules, bonds to 'inorganic' carbon, such as carbon monoxide (metal carbonyls), cyanide, or carbide, are generally considered to be organometallic as well. Some related compounds such as transition metal hydrides and metal phosphine complexes are often included in discussions of organometallic compounds, though in strict terms, they may not be necessarily organometallic. The related but distinct term "metalorganic compound" refers to metal-containing compounds lacking direct metal-carbon bonds but which contain organic ligands. Metal β-diketonates, alkoxides, dialkylamides, and metal phosphine complexes are representative members of this class. The field of organometallic chemistry combines aspects of traditional inorganic and organic chemistry.

Organometallic compounds are widely used both stoichiometrically in research and industrial chemical reactions, as well as in the role of catalysts to increase the rates of such reactions (e.g., as in uses of homogeneous catalysis), where target molecules include polymers, pharmaceuticals, and many other types of practical products.

History

Early developments in organometallic chemistry include Louis-Étienne Ménard's synthesis of tetramethyllead in 1847, Edward Frankland's discovery of diethylzinc and dimethylzinc in 1849, and Ludwig Mond's discovery of nickel tetracarbonyl in 1888. The field expanded dramatically in the 20th century with the development of Grignard reagents (Victor Grignard, Nobel Prize 1912), Karl Ziegler and Giulio Natta's catalysts for olefin polymerization (Nobel Prize 1963), and the discovery of ferrocene in 1951, whose sandwich structure was elucidated by Geoffrey Wilkinson and Ernst Otto Fischer (Nobel Prize 1973). William Knowles, Ryōji Noyori, and K. Barry Sharpless received the Nobel Prize in 2001 for work on asymmetric catalysis with organometallic complexes.

Concepts

The 18-electron rule

Many organometallic compounds of the transition metals obey the 18-electron rule, which states that thermodynamically stable complexes tend to have a total of 18 valence electrons around the metal centre. The rule is analogous to the octet rule for main-group elements and is useful for predicting the stability and stoichiometry of metal carbonyls, metallocenes, and related species.

Counting electrons

Two conventions are used to count electrons in organometallic complexes: the neutral (covalent) method, in which ligands are treated as neutral radicals, and the ionic method, in which ligands are treated as closed-shell ions and the metal is assigned an oxidation state. Hapticity, denoted by the symbol η, describes the number of contiguous atoms of a ligand that bind to the metal.

Important compound classes

  • Metal carbonyls — compounds such as Ni(CO)4 and Fe(CO)5, featuring carbon monoxide ligands bound through carbon.
  • Metallocenes — sandwich compounds such as ferrocene and cobaltocene, with cyclopentadienyl ligands.
  • Grignard reagents — organomagnesium halides (RMgX) used extensively in carbon–carbon bond formation.
  • Organolithium compounds — strong bases and nucleophiles such as n-butyllithium.
  • Metal alkyls and hydrides — compounds with metal–carbon sigma bonds or metal–hydrogen bonds.
  • Metal carbenes and carbynes — compounds with metal–carbon double and triple bonds, central to olefin metathesis.

Catalysis

Organometallic complexes are the workhorses of homogeneous catalysis. Industrially important processes include hydroformylation (the oxo process), the Monsanto and Cativa processes for acetic acid production, Ziegler–Natta polymerization of olefins, olefin metathesis, and cross-coupling reactions such as the Suzuki, Heck, and Negishi couplings, which were recognized by the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2010. Key elementary steps in these catalytic cycles include oxidative addition, reductive elimination, migratory insertion, and β-hydride elimination.
