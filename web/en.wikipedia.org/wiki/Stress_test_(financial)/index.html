A stress test, in financial terminology, is an analysis or simulation designed to determine the ability of a given financial instrument or financial institution to deal with an economic crisis. Rather than projecting the most likely outcome, regulators and institutions model severely adverse but plausible scenarios—deep recession, market crashes, spikes in unemployment or defaults—and estimate resulting losses and capital levels.

Bank stress tests

Supervisory stress testing became a central regulatory tool after the 2007–2008 financial crisis. The US Supervisory Capital Assessment Program of 2009 tested the 19 largest bank holding companies; its published results are widely credited with restoring market confidence. Since then the Federal Reserve has run annual tests under the Dodd–Frank Act (DFAST) and the Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review (CCAR), which can restrict dividends and share buybacks at banks whose capital plans fail. In Europe, the European Banking Authority (EBA) coordinates EU-wide exercises, and the Bank of England runs annual tests for major UK banks.

Methodology

A stress test applies a common macroeconomic scenario (and sometimes institution-specific or exploratory scenarios) to balance-sheet and income models to project loan losses, trading losses, revenues, and capital ratios over a horizon of about two to three years. Reverse stress testing works backwards, asking what scenario would make an institution fail. Results feed into capital requirements, contingency planning and disclosure.

Criticism

Critics argue that published scenarios can be too mild, that models rely on historical correlations that break down in crises, and that passing a test may induce false confidence. Tests conducted before the 2011 European debt crisis, for example, passed institutions that failed shortly afterwards, such as the Franco-Belgian bank Dexia.
