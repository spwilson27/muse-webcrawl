Descriptive linguistics is the work of analysing and describing how language is actually spoken (or signed) by a group of people, as opposed to prescriptive linguistics, which sets out rules for how language should be used. A descriptive linguist describes the grammar of a language — its phonology, morphology, syntax and lexicon — based on evidence from speakers, without judging some usages "correct" and others "wrong".

Approach

The descriptive approach became dominant in linguistics in the early 20th century, particularly in American structuralism under Leonard Bloomfield and his successors, who documented many Indigenous languages of the Americas. Description proceeds level by level: the sound system (phonetics and phonology), word structure (morphology), sentence structure (syntax), and meaning (semantics and pragmatics). The goal is an explicit grammar and dictionary adequate to the data — traditionally summarised as a grammar, a dictionary and texts.

Descriptivism and prescriptivism

In popular usage "descriptivism" and "prescriptivism" describe opposing attitudes to usage debates: descriptivists hold that dictionaries and grammars should record how people actually write and speak, including stigmatised forms, while prescriptivists defend standard rules. Professional linguists are descriptivists in method, though they recognise that standard varieties have social functions. The distinction parallels the linguistic study of language endangerment and documentation, where describing a language accurately is a precondition for any revitalisation.
