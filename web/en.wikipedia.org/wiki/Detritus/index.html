Detritus

In biology, detritus is dead particulate organic material, as distinguished from dissolved organic material. Detritus typically includes the bodies or fragments of dead organisms, and faecal material. It typically hosts communities of microorganisms that colonise and decompose (remineralise) it. In terrestrial ecosystems it is present as leaf litter and other soil organic matter; in aquatic ecosystems as suspended or settled "marine snow" and lake snow.

Leaf litter — terrestrial detritus — fuels soil food webs as it decomposes.

Role in ecosystems

Detritus is a major energy pathway: many ecosystems receive more energy through the detrital food chain than through grazing on living plants. Organisms that feed on detritus — detritivores such as earthworms, millipedes, dung beetles, sea cucumbers and many fungi and bacteria — break it down and release nutrients that plants and algae can reuse. Decomposers thus drive nutrient cycling, soil formation, and much of the productivity of oceans, where marine snow carries organic matter from surface waters to the deep sea.

In geology

In geology, detritus refers to rock fragments and mineral grains eroded and transported from their source — the raw material of sedimentary rocks such as sandstone and conglomerate. Geologists classify sedimentary particles as detrital (transported) versus chemical (precipitated in place).
