Magic (illusion)

Performing artIllusion, prestidigitation, conjuring
GenresStage magic, close-up magic, mentalism, escapology
Core techniquesSleight of hand, misdirection, gimmicked apparatus

Magic, also known as illusion, prestidigitation and conjuring, is a performing art in which audiences are entertained by tricks that appear to achieve impossible or supernatural feats. Magicians create these effects using natural means — sleight of hand, misdirection, mechanical devices, psychology and showmanship — while presenting them as if they were genuinely magical. The art is distinguished from "magic" in the occult or religious sense by its openly theatrical, entertainment purpose.

Categories

Magic is commonly divided by scale and setting. Stage magic presents large illusions — productions, vanishings, levitations, sawing a person in half — to a theatre audience. Close-up magic (or table magic) is performed inches from spectators with cards, coins and everyday objects. Parlour magic sits between the two. Specialised branches include mentalism, simulating psychic powers; escapology, the art of escape made famous by Harry Houdini; bizarre magic; children's magic; and street magic, popularised on television by performers such as David Blaine.

History

Conjuring is ancient: the Westcar Papyrus describes a performance for the Egyptian pharaoh Khufu around 2700 BCE, and the cups and balls routine is depicted in Egyptian tomb paintings and was known in Rome. The modern art took shape in the 19th century with Jean Eugène Robert-Houdin, who moved magic from fairs into theatres and evening dress, and reached a golden age with Maskelyne, Devant, Thurston and Houdini. In the 20th and 21st centuries, television and the internet transformed the craft, with figures such as David Copperfield, Penn & Teller and Dynamo reaching global audiences, while organisations like the Magic Circle (founded 1905) guard the profession's secrecy ethic — its motto is indocilis privata loqui, "not apt to disclose secrets".

Principles

Magicians classify effects into categories such as production, vanish, transformation, restoration, teleportation, levitation, penetration and prediction. Underlying them are principles of misdirection — controlling audience attention — and the exploitation of assumptions about objects, time and causality. Exposure of methods is contentious within the community, though some performers, notably Penn & Teller, build acts around apparent revelation.
