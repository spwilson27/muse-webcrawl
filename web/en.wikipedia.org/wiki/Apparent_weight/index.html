Apparent weight

In physics, the apparent weight of an object is the weight of the object as measured by a weighing device such as a spring scale, as opposed to its true weight — the force of gravity acting on it. Apparent weight is equal in magnitude to the normal force (or other supporting force) exerted on the object by its support. Whenever the object accelerates, or other forces such as buoyancy act on it, its apparent weight differs from its true weight.

A scale in an accelerating elevator reads the normal force, which changes with acceleration.

Definition and examples

For an object at rest on Earth's surface, apparent weight approximately equals true weight, mg. In an elevator accelerating upward with acceleration a, the floor must push up with force m(g + a), so a passenger feels heavier and a scale reads more; accelerating downward, the reading falls. If the elevator cable breaks and the cabin falls freely (a = g), the normal force drops to zero: the passenger is weightless in the apparent sense, even though gravity still acts with full strength. This is the same condition experienced continuously by astronauts in orbit, who are in perpetual free fall around the Earth.

Buoyancy

An object immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the displaced fluid (Archimedes' principle). Its apparent weight is therefore the true weight minus the buoyant force. A swimmer or a submerged stone feels lighter; objects whose average density is less than the fluid's have zero apparent weight while resting against a support and instead float, partly submerged, with buoyancy exactly balancing gravity.

Centrifugal effects

Because Earth rotates, objects at the equator experience a small outward centrifugal acceleration (about 0.034 m/s²) that reduces apparent weight by roughly 0.3 percent compared with the poles, an effect compounded by Earth's equatorial bulge. Apparent weight also varies locally with altitude and geological density anomalies, variations that sensitive gravimeters exploit in geophysical surveying.

Relation to g-force

The ratio of apparent weight to true weight is commonly expressed as a g-force. Pilots in tight turns, roller-coaster riders at the bottom of a dip, and astronauts during launch experience apparent weights of several g; at the top of a vertical loop or in parabolic flight, apparent weight can momentarily reach zero. Einstein's equivalence principle rests on the local indistinguishability of gravity and acceleration effects on apparent weight.
