Yiddish (ייִדיש, yidish, literally "Jewish") is a High German-derived language historically spoken by Ashkenazi Jews. It developed in the Rhineland in the early Middle Ages as the vernacular of Jewish communities, fusing German dialects with elements of Hebrew and Aramaic, and later absorbing substantial Slavic vocabulary as its speakers moved eastward into Poland and Russia. It is written in an adaptation of the Hebrew alphabet.

History

By the early 20th century Yiddish was the mother tongue of some 11–13 million Jews worldwide and supported a rich literature, press and theatre. The classical triumvirate of modern Yiddish literature — Mendele Mocher Sforim, Sholem Aleichem and I. L. Peretz — turned a folk vernacular into a literary language; Isaac Bashevis Singer later won the Nobel Prize in Literature (1978) writing in Yiddish. The language was devastated by the Holocaust, which murdered most of its speakers in Europe, and by assimilation and the dominance of Hebrew in Israel and English in America.

Present status

Today Yiddish survives and is growing as the everyday language of Hasidic and Haredi communities in New York, Jerusalem, Antwerp, London and elsewhere, whose high birth rates are reversing the decline among native speakers. Secular Yiddish culture persists through organisations such as YIVO (founded 1925 in Vilnius, now in New York), university programmes, klezmer music and festivals.

Features

Yiddish grammar is fundamentally Germanic, with three genders, case marking and verb-second tendencies, but it incorporates Hebrew-Aramaic vocabulary for religious and communal concepts and Slavic influences in vocabulary, diminutives and aspect. Yiddish words that entered English include chutzpah, schmooze, bagel, glitch and kvetch. The linguist Max Weinreich famously quoted the saying that "a language is a dialect with an army and navy" in defence of Yiddish's status.
