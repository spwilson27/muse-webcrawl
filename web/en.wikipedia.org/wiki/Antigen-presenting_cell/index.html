Antigen-presenting cell

An antigen-presenting cell (APC) is a cell that displays antigen bound by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) proteins on its surface, a process known as antigen presentation. T cells recognise these complexes using their T cell receptors, linking the innate and adaptive immune systems.

Types

The "professional" APCs are dendritic cells, macrophages and B cells, which constitutively express MHC class II molecules and co-stimulatory signals. Dendritic cells are the most potent activators of naive T cells. Nearly all nucleated cells can present endogenous antigens on MHC class I to CD8+ T cells, and are sometimes called non-professional APCs.

Function

APCs internalise pathogens or proteins by phagocytosis or endocytosis, degrade them into peptide fragments, and load the peptides onto MHC molecules. After migrating to lymph nodes, dendritic cells present antigen to T cells, initiating clonal expansion and adaptive immunity. APCs are central targets in vaccine design and cancer immunotherapy, including dendritic-cell vaccines and CAR-T approaches.
