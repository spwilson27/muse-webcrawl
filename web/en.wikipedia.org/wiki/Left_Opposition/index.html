Left Opposition

The Left Opposition was a faction within the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks) from 1923 to 1927, headed de facto by Leon Trotsky. It was formed in opposition to the policies of the triumvirate of Joseph Stalin, Grigory Zinoviev and Lev Kamenev, who dominated the party after Lenin's incapacitation and death. The Left Opposition criticised the bureaucratisation of the party and state, the theory of "socialism in one country", and the leadership's economic policies.

Formation and programme

The faction emerged in October 1923 with the "Declaration of the 46", a letter signed by leading Bolsheviks warning of a crisis of internal party democracy. Trotsky's pamphlet The New Course (1924) set out the opposition's critique. Economically the Left Opposition, advised by Yevgeni Preobrazhensky, advocated of "primitive socialist accumulation", demanded faster industrialisation financed at the expense of the peasantry, and opposed concessions to the richer peasants (kulaks) and private traders (NEPmen) under the New Economic Policy.

United Opposition and defeat

In 1926 Trotsky's supporters formed the United Opposition with the former Zinoviev–Kamenev faction. After a brief campaign, its leaders were expelled from the Central Committee in October 1927 and from the party at the Fifteenth Congress in December 1927. Trotsky was exiled to Alma-Ata in 1928 and deported from the Soviet Union in 1929. Most leading oppositionists later capitulated to Stalin; nearly all were executed in the Great Purge of 1936–38.

Legacy

From exile Trotsky continued to lead an International Left Opposition, which in 1938 founded the Fourth International. Trotskyist movements worldwide trace their origins to the Left Opposition's struggle against Stalinism.
