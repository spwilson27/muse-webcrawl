Biogeography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Biogeography is the study of the geographic distribution of species and ecosystems, and of the processes — evolution, extinction, dispersal and continental drift — that produce those patterns. It asks why kangaroos live in Australia, why islands breed flightless birds, and why the tropics hold so many more species than the poles.

Foundations

The field was founded in the nineteenth century by naturalists such as Alexander von Humboldt, Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace — whose "Wallace Line" through Indonesia separates Asian from Australasian faunas to this day. The theory of island biogeography (MacArthur and Wilson, 1967) modelled species richness as a balance of immigration and extinction set by island size and isolation, becoming a cornerstone of conservation biology.

Modern biogeography

Plate tectonics (see mountain range formation and continental drift) explained the southern distributions of marsupials, ratite birds and southern beech trees as relics of Gondwana. Molecular phylogenies now let biogeographers date and locate the divergences of lineages, while climate change makes the field urgently practical: species' ranges are shifting poleward, and reserve design uses biogeographic models to plan for a moving map, tracked against baselines such as the IUCN Red List.
