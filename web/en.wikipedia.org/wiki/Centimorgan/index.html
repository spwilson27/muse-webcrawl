Centimorgan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In genetics, a centimorgan (abbreviated cM) or map unit (m.u.) is a unit for measuring genetic linkage. It is defined as the distance between chromosome positions (also termed loci or markers) for which the expected average number of intervening chromosomal crossovers in a single generation is 0.01. It is often used to infer distance along a chromosome, although it is not a fixed physical distance: the number of base pairs to which it corresponds varies across the genome and between the sexes, because recombination is not uniform and recombination rates differ in male and female meiosis.

Etymology and history

The unit is named after the American geneticist Thomas Hunt Morgan. Its parent unit, the morgan, is rarely used today. The concept descends from the work of Alfred Sturtevant, who as an undergraduate in Morgan's laboratory constructed the first genetic map in 1913, reasoning that the frequency of recombination between genes could order them along a chromosome.

Relation to recombination frequency

For short distances, the centimorgan and the recombination percentage are approximately equal: loci separated by 1 cM show recombination in about 1% of meioses. Over longer distances, multiple crossovers make the relationship non-linear, and mapping functions such as those of Haldane and Kosambi convert recombination frequencies into map distances.

Physical correspondence

In humans, 1 centimorgan corresponds on average to about 1 million base pairs (1 megabase), but the correspondence ranges widely — from well under 100 kilobases in recombination hotspots to several megabases in recombination deserts, such as regions near centromeres. The human genome totals roughly 3,600 cM in length on sex-averaged maps, with the female map notably longer than the male map.

Uses

Centimorgans are the standard unit in linkage analysis and in consumer genetic genealogy, where the length of shared DNA segments measured in cM is used to estimate how closely two people are related.
