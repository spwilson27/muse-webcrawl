Jason and the Argonauts is a 1963 independent mythological fantasy film distributed by Columbia Pictures. Directed by Don Chaffey and produced by Charles H. Schneer, it retells the Greek myth of Jason's quest for the Golden Fleece. The film is celebrated for the stop-motion animation of Ray Harryhausen, its associate producer, whose "Dynamation" creatures — the bronze giant Talos, the Hydra, the harpies and an army of living skeletons — remain landmarks of visual effects.

Plot

Pelias usurps the throne of Thessaly, killing the family of King Aristo. The infant Jason survives and, grown, sets out to claim his kingdom; Pelias directs him instead to win the Golden Fleece from distant Colchis. Jason assembles the crew of the Argo, the Argonauts, aided and hindered by the gods of Olympus — Hera befriends him, Zeus remains aloof. Adventures en route include rescuing the blind prophet Phineas from the harpies, surviving the clashing rocks (Symplegades) with the aid of a dove and the sea god Triton, and fighting the bronze giant Talos on the Isle of Bronze, defeated when Jason loosens the plug in his ankle. In Colchis, Jason must sow the Hydra's teeth, which sprout into armed skeleton warriors; aided by the priestess Medea, he seizes the Fleece from beneath the sleepless Hydra and escapes with her.

Production and effects

Filming took place in Italy and England with a largely British cast; Jason's voice was dubbed by Tim Turner. Harryhausen spent months animating the seven-minute skeleton battle, in which each skeleton was modelled on a human armature and fought three live actors. The score by Bernard Herrmann, written for brass and percussion without strings, is among his most praised.

Reception and legacy

The film performed only moderately on release but grew into a classic through television screenings. Tom Hanks called it the film that most influenced him, and modern effects artists routinely cite the skeleton sequence as foundational. In 2021 it was frequently ranked among the greatest fantasy films ever made, and Harryhausen himself considered it his finest work.
