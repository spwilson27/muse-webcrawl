Mediumship

[Image: A séance of the kind held in the heyday of Spiritualism]
A séance, c. late nineteenth century

Mediumship is the practice of purportedly mediating communication between familiar spirits or spirits of the dead and living human beings. Practitioners are known as "mediums" or "spirit mediums". There are different types of mediumship, including spirit channeling, automatic writing, and ouija-board sessions maintained by believers.

History

Belief in spirit communication is ancient and widespread — from the Witch of Endor in the Hebrew Bible to Greek oracles and shamanic practice worldwide — but modern mediumship is usually dated to the rise of Spiritualism in the United States in 1848, when the Fox sisters of Hydesville, New York, claimed to communicate with a spirit through rapping sounds. Séances became a mass phenomenon in the US and Britain, combining religion, entertainment and grief, especially after the American Civil War and the First World War. Famous mediums included Daniel Dunglas Home, Florence Cook, Eusapia Palladino and Leonora Piper, who was investigated at length by William James and the Society for Psychical Research.

Claims and criticism

Mediumship is considered a pseudoscience by the scientific community. Investigators from the nineteenth century onward repeatedly caught prominent mediums using fraud (hidden accomplices, concealed objects, dark-room tricks), and the Fox sisters themselves confessed to faking their raps in 1888 (later partially retracting). Modern sceptics attribute apparently accurate readings to cold reading — high-probability statements, fishing questions and feedback from sitters — together with the Barnum effect and subjective validation. Nevertheless, mediumship remains popular: television mediums draw large audiences, Spiritualist churches practise it as religion, and bereavement researchers note that sittings can provide comfort to the grieving regardless of the claims' truth.
