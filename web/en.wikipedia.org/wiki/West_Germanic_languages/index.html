The West Germanic languages constitute the largest of the three branches of the Germanic family of languages, the others being the North Germanic and the extinct East Germanic languages. The West Germanic branch includes English, the most widely spoken Germanic language, as well as German, Dutch, Afrikaans, the Frisian languages and Low German.

History

West Germanic is thought to have developed from Proto-Germanic dialects spoken around the southern North Sea coast and the lower Elbe in the last centuries BCE, sometimes associated with the Jastorf culture. During the Migration Period, West Germanic-speaking groups spread widely; Angles, Saxons and Jutes carried their dialects to Britain, where they developed into Old English.

A defining event in the branch's history is the High German consonant shift, which affected the southern (High German) dialects in the early Middle Ages and distinguishes German from the Low German, Dutch and English varieties that did not undergo the full shift.

Classification

The branch is usually divided into the Anglo-Frisian group (English, Scots and Frisian), the Low German group, the Low Franconian group (Dutch and Afrikaans), and the High German group (German, Yiddish, Luxembourgish and others). The internal relationships among these groups and the status of a single Proto-West Germanic ancestor remain debated among linguists.
