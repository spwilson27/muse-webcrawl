Abdullah I bin Al-Hussein (2 February 1882 – 20 July 1951) was the ruler of Jordan from 11 April 1921 until his assassination in 1951. He was the Emir of Transjordan, a British protectorate, until 25 May 1946, after which he became the first King of the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan. A member of the Hashemite dynasty, he was a leader of the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire during the First World War.

Early life and Arab Revolt

Abdullah was the second son of Hussein bin Ali, the Sharif of Mecca. Educated in Constantinople, he served as deputy for Mecca in the Ottoman parliament. During the Arab Revolt (1916–1918), launched by his father with British support, Abdullah commanded forces in the Hejaz, while his younger brother Faisal led the northern campaign alongside T. E. Lawrence.

Emir of Transjordan

After France expelled Faisal from Syria in 1920, Abdullah moved north to assert Hashemite claims; Britain instead recognised him as Emir of Transjordan in 1921 under the British Mandate for Palestine. He built the emirate's administration and its Arab Legion, commanded by Glubb Pasha. Transjordan gained full independence in 1946 and Abdullah was proclaimed king.

Reign and assassination

In the 1948 Arab–Israeli War, Jordan's Arab Legion captured the West Bank and East Jerusalem, which Abdullah annexed in 1950 — a move recognised by few states. His secret contacts with Jewish Agency and later Israeli leaders, and his perceived moderation, made him enemies among Palestinian nationalists. On 20 July 1951 he was assassinated at the entrance to Al-Aqsa Mosque in Jerusalem by a Palestinian gunman, Mustafa Shukri Ashu. He was succeeded briefly by his son Talal, then by his grandson Hussein.
