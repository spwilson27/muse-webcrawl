Comparison of audio coding formats

The following tables compare general and technical information for a variety of audio coding formats. Audio coding formats are used to encode digital audio for storage or transmission, trading sound quality against bit rate, computational complexity and delay. Formats may be lossless, preserving the original audio data exactly, or lossy, discarding information judged inaudible using psychoacoustic models.

Lossy formats achieve far lower bit rates than uncompressed PCM by exploiting psychoacoustic masking.

Technical details

Lossy encoders differ in the bit rates they support, their maximum sampling rates and channel counts, and their algorithmic delay. MP3 supports bit rates from 32 to 320 kbit/s and two channels in its common form. AAC offers better quality than MP3 at the same bit rate and supports up to 48 channels and sampling rates to 96 kHz. Vorbis is a patent-free format with variable bit rate encoding across a wide range. Opus, standardised by the IETF, is designed for interactive applications: it spans bit rates from 6 kbit/s to 510 kbit/s, sampling rates from 8 to 48 kHz, and frame sizes as small as 2.5 ms, combining the speech-oriented SILK codec with the music-oriented CELT codec. Lossless formats such as FLAC typically compress CD audio to roughly 50–60% of its original size while remaining bit-exact.

Operating system and hardware support also differ widely: MP3 and WAV are nearly universal; AAC is supported by almost all modern devices; Opus is supported by web browsers and messaging applications; FLAC support is common in software but more variable in portable hardware.
