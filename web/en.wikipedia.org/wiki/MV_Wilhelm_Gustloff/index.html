MV Wilhelm Gustloff was a German military transport ship that was sunk on 30 January 1945 by the Soviet submarine S-13 in the Baltic Sea. She was evacuating German civilians, Nazi officials and military personnel from East Prussia as the Red Army advanced during Operation Hannibal. An estimated 9,400 people died, making it the largest loss of life in a single ship sinking in history.

Construction and early service

The ship was built by the Blohm & Voss shipyard in Hamburg and launched on 5 May 1937. Named after Wilhelm Gustloff, the assassinated leader of the Nazi Party's Swiss branch, she was originally intended as a cruise ship for the Strength Through Joy (Kraft durch Freude) organisation, which provided subsidised leisure activities for German workers. Her maiden voyage took place in April 1938.

Wartime service

At the outbreak of the Second World War the ship was requisitioned by the Kriegsmarine. She served as a hospital ship in 1939–40 and was then moored as a floating barracks for the 2nd Submarine Training Division in Gotenhafen (Gdynia).

Sinking

On 30 January 1945 the ship left Gotenhafen carrying refugees and wounded soldiers fleeing the Soviet advance, with perhaps more than 10,000 people aboard despite an official capacity of under 2,000. At about 21:00 the Soviet submarine S-13, commanded by Alexander Marinesko, fired four torpedoes; three struck the ship. She sank in under an hour in freezing waters. German rescue vessels saved roughly 1,200 survivors. The death toll of around 9,400 makes the sinking the worst maritime disaster ever recorded.

Legacy

The disaster was long little discussed in post-war Germany and the Soviet Union. Günter Grass's 2002 novel Crabwalk (Im Krebsgang) brought the sinking back into public memory. The wreck lies in Polish waters and is protected as a war grave.
