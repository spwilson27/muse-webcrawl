Antigen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An antigen is any substance that can bind to a specific antibody or T-cell receptor and often provoke an immune response. The name reflects its function: antibody generator. Antigens are typically proteins or polysaccharides on the surfaces of pathogens — bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites — but can also be pollen, transplanted tissue, or the body's own molecules in autoimmune disease.

Role in immunity

Immune cells distinguish self from non-self largely by antigens. B cells produce antibodies shaped to bind one antigen; T cells recognise antigen fragments presented on cell surfaces by MHC molecules. The part of an antigen that an antibody actually grips is called an epitope. After an infection or vaccination, memory cells persist so the response to the same antigen is faster and stronger next time — the basis of vaccines, which present antigens (whole inactivated microbes, subunits, or instructions to make them, as in mRNA vaccines) without causing the disease.

Uses

Antigens are central to diagnostics (antigen tests, including the rapid lateral-flow tests used in the COVID-19 pandemic), blood typing (the A and B antigens of the ABO system), allergy, and cancer immunotherapy, which seeks to direct the immune system against tumour antigens.
