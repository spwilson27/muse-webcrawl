Pyroptosis is a highly inflammatory form of lytic programmed cell death that occurs most frequently upon infection with intracellular pathogens and is an important part of the innate immune response. Unlike apoptosis, which is immunologically quiet, pyroptosis ruptures the plasma membrane and releases pro-inflammatory cytokines, alerting neighbouring cells to danger.

Mechanism

Pyroptosis is driven by inflammatory caspases. In the canonical pathway, pattern-recognition receptors assemble an inflammasome that activates caspase-1. In the non-canonical pathway, cytosolic lipopolysaccharide from Gram-negative bacteria directly activates caspase-4 and caspase-5 in humans (caspase-11 in mice). These caspases cleave gasdermin D; its N-terminal fragment inserts into the plasma membrane and forms large pores. The pores collapse ionic gradients, water rushes in, the cell swells and bursts, and mature interleukin-1β and interleukin-18 — also processed by caspase-1 — escape to drive inflammation and fever (the name comes from the Greek pyro, fire/fever, and ptosis, falling).

Discovery and significance

Lytic death of infected macrophages was described in the 1990s in Salmonella and Shigella infections, and the term pyroptosis was coined in 2001. The identification of gasdermin D as the executioner protein in 2015 clarified the pathway. Pyroptosis helps the host by destroying the replicative niche of intracellular bacteria and exposing them to neutrophils, but excessive pyroptosis contributes to sepsis, inflammatory bowel disease and other disorders, making the pathway a drug target.
