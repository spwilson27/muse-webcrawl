Build–operate–transfer

A toll bridge financed under a BOT concession

Build–operate–transfer (BOT) is a form of project financing and a type of public–private partnership in which a private entity receives a concession from the public sector to finance, design, construct, own and operate a facility stated in the concession contract. This enables the project proponent to recover its investment and operating and maintenance expenses in the project.

How it works

Under a BOT contract, a government grants a concessionaire the right to build and operate a facility — typically a road, bridge, airport, power plant or water treatment works — for a fixed period, often 20 to 30 years. The concessionaire finances construction, operates the asset and collects revenues (tolls, user fees or availability payments) during the concession. At the end of the period, ownership transfers to the public authority, usually at no additional cost.

Advantages and risks

BOT lets governments deliver infrastructure without upfront public capital and transfers construction and operating risk to the private partner, which is incentivised to build efficiently because its return depends on the facility operating well. Against this, financing costs are usually higher than public borrowing, contracts are complex and long-lasting, and poorly structured concessions can leave the public bearing demand risk or paying excessive fees.

Variants

Related models include build–own–operate–transfer (BOOT), build–lease–transfer (BLT), design–build–finance–operate (DBFO), and build–transfer–operate (BTO), in which ownership passes to the state immediately after construction but operation remains private.
