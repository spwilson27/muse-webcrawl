The Herbert Hoover Dike is a great earthen embankment, about 143 miles (230 km) long, that encircles Lake Okeechobee in south Florida. Built by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers to protect the farming communities around the lake from hurricane-driven flooding, it is named for President Herbert Hoover, who ordered its construction after touring the flood devastation of 1928.

Background

In 1926 the Great Miami Hurricane caused the lake to overflow locally raised muck dikes, killing hundreds. Two years later, the 1928 Okeechobee hurricane drove a storm surge over the lake's southern shore, drowning an estimated 2,500 or more people, most of them Black farmworkers around Belle Glade and Pahokee — one of the deadliest natural disasters in American history. Hoover, then president-elect and formerly Secretary of Commerce, visited the area and championed a federal flood-control programme.

Construction and rehabilitation

Under the Rivers and Harbors Act of 1930 and later flood-control legislation, the Corps raised a much larger dike around most of the lake and excavated the St. Lucie and Caloosahatchee waterways to discharge excess lake water to the coasts. The system made possible the rich sugar-growing Everglades Agricultural Area south of the lake but fundamentally altered the natural flow of water into the Everglades.

From the 1990s engineers found that the dike, built of permeable sand and shell, suffered dangerous seepage and piping at high lake levels, putting some 40,000 residents at risk. A massive rehabilitation programme, begun in the 2000s and largely completed in the early 2020s, strengthened the embankment with cutoff walls, replaced water-control structures, and removed problem sections of the old dike.
