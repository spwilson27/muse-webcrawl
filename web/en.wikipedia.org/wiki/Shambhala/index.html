Shambhala

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Shambhala (Sanskrit: शम्भल, Tibetan: བདེ་འབྱུང) is a mythical kingdom in Tibetan Buddhist tradition, described in the Kalachakra tantra and associated texts as a hidden land where the teachings of Buddhism are preserved in purity. It is said to lie north of the Sita River, to be shaped like an eight-petalled lotus, and to be ruled by a line of Kalki kings, the last of whom will ride forth to defeat the forces of barbarism and inaugurate a golden age.

In Buddhist tradition

According to the Kalachakra literature, the Buddha taught the tantra to King Suchandra of Shambhala, who carried it back to his kingdom. The prophecy of Shambhala foretells that when the world degenerates, the twenty-fifth Kalki king, Raudra Chakrin, will lead an army from Shambhala in the year 2424 (by one common reckoning) to destroy the invading forces of evil. Interpretations differ: some teachers treat Shambhala as a real geographical place, others as a visionary realm accessible in meditation, and still others as an allegory of the practitioner's inner path.

Western reception

Shambhala entered Western imagination in the late nineteenth century through Theosophical writings, and it inspired expeditions to Central Asia by figures such as Nicholas Roerich in the 1920s, who sought the kingdom in the Altai and Himalayas. James Hilton's 1933 novel Lost Horizon transformed the legend into the utopia of Shangri-La. In the twentieth century, the Tibetan teacher Chögyam Trungpa founded the Shambhala International organisation, presenting "Shambhala" as a secular vision of enlightened society.
