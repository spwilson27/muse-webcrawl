Small for gestational age

Small for gestational age (SGA) describes babies with a birth weight below the 10th percentile for their gestational age, meaning they are smaller than most other babies of the same gestational age. Many SGA babies have intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), which refers to poor growth of the fetus in the womb, though the two terms are not identical: some SGA infants are simply constitutionally small.

Causes

Risk factors and causes are commonly grouped into maternal, placental and fetal factors:

  • Maternal: hypertension and pre-eclampsia, chronic disease, malnutrition, smoking, alcohol and drug use, multiple pregnancy.
  • Placental: placental insufficiency, abruption, infarction or abnormal cord insertion limiting nutrient and oxygen transfer.
  • Fetal: chromosomal abnormalities such as trisomy 21, congenital infections (for example cytomegalovirus), and structural anomalies.

Classification

SGA infants may be described as symmetrical, when weight, length and head circumference are all reduced proportionally (suggesting an early-pregnancy insult), or asymmetrical, when weight is reduced but head growth is relatively spared ("head sparing"), typically reflecting late-pregnancy placental insufficiency.

Complications and management

At birth, SGA babies are at increased risk of hypoglycaemia, hypothermia, polycythaemia and feeding difficulties. Management focuses on early feeding, monitoring blood glucose and temperature, and developmental follow-up. Most SGA infants show catch-up growth in the first two years of life; those who do not may be considered for growth hormone therapy. In the long term, being born SGA is associated with increased risk of short stature, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease in adulthood — associations explored by the developmental origins of health and disease (Barker) hypothesis.
