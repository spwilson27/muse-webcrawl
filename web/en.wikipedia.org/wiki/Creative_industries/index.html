Creative industries

The creative industries span advertising, film, design, publishing, music and software.

The creative industries refers to a range of economic activities which are concerned with the generation or exploitation of knowledge and information. They may variously also be referred to as the cultural industries (especially in Europe) or the creative economy, and most recently they have been denominated as the Orange Economy in Latin America and the Caribbean.

Definition

The United Kingdom's Department for Culture, Media and Sport (DCMS) provided an influential definition in 1998, describing the creative industries as those "which have their origin in individual creativity, skill and talent and which have a potential for wealth and job creation through the generation and exploitation of intellectual property". The DCMS list comprised thirteen sectors: advertising, architecture, the art and antiques market, crafts, design, designer fashion, film and video, interactive leisure software, music, the performing arts, publishing, software and computer services, and television and radio.

Economic significance

Governments around the world have adopted the concept in economic policy, mapping the size of the sector and promoting "creative clusters" and quarters in cities. Richard Florida's work on the "creative class" popularised the idea that concentrations of creative workers drive urban growth, though critics question the breadth of the definitions used and the precarious, freelance-heavy nature of much creative work.

Criticism

Scholars have criticised the creative industries concept for blurring the distinct economics of subsidised arts and commercial entertainment, for overstating growth figures by including general software employment, and for legitimising weak intellectual-property-based business models. Others defend the framework as a useful recognition that symbolic and cultural production is a major part of modern economies.
