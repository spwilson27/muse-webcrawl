Lesser capybara

The lesser capybara (Hydrochoerus isthmius) is a large semi-aquatic rodent of Central America and northwestern South America. Long treated as a subspecies of the greater capybara (Hydrochoerus hydrochaeris), it was elevated to full species status on the basis of its smaller size, distinct range, and karyotype differences. Even so, it is among the largest living rodents, second in its genus only to its better-known relative.

Description

The lesser capybara closely resembles the greater capybara, with coarse brownish fur, a blunt muzzle, partially webbed toes, and eyes and ears set high on the head — adaptations to its life in and around water. Adults are typically smaller than greater capybaras, generally weighing up to about 28 kilograms (62 lb), with a stocky, barrel-shaped body and short limbs.

Distribution and habitat

The species ranges from Panama through northern Colombia to western Venezuela, inhabiting marshes, swamps, estuaries, and the margins of rivers and ponds, usually in lowland areas near permanent water. It is closely tied to aquatic vegetation for food and cover.

Behaviour and ecology

Lesser capybaras are herbivores, grazing on grasses and aquatic plants. They are social animals living in groups, though group sizes are generally smaller than those of the greater capybara, and they may be more solitary or nocturnal where hunted. They are strong swimmers and can remain submerged for several minutes to avoid predators. Natural predators include caimans, large snakes, and big cats.

Conservation

The IUCN has listed the species as Data Deficient because of limited information on its population and threats. It is hunted for meat in parts of its range and affected by wetland drainage, and it is considered rare or declining in some localities.
