Parallel communication

In data transmission, parallel communication is a method of conveying multiple binary digits (bits) simultaneously over separate physical channels. It contrasts with serial communication, in which bits are sent one after another over a single channel. Parallel links are typically unidirectional or bidirectional collections of wires — often bundled in a ribbon cable — with additional lines for clock or strobe and handshaking signals.

Examples

Classic examples include the IEEE 1284 printer port (see parallel port), the PATA/IDE disk interface, SCSI buses, and the internal buses of computers, where processor, memory and peripherals historically exchanged data 8, 16, 32 or 64 bits at a time.

Advantages and drawbacks

At a given clock rate, a parallel link can move many times more data than a serial link. However, over longer distances and at higher frequencies parallel buses suffer from clock skew (bits arriving at slightly different times), crosstalk between adjacent conductors, and the cost and bulk of many wires. For these reasons, many former parallel standards — PCI, PATA, parallel SCSI — have been replaced by fast serial standards such as PCI Express, SATA and SAS, which achieve higher throughput with differential signalling and embedded clocks. Parallel communication remains dominant inside chips and over very short board-level distances, such as memory interfaces (for example DDR).
