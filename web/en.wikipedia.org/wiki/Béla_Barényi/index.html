Béla Barényi

Placeholder image: Béla Barényi, pioneer of passive car safety.

Béla Barényi (1 March 1907 – 30 May 1997) was an Austro-Hungarian-born German engineer regarded as the father of passive safety in automotive design. Working for Daimler-Benz from 1939 to 1972, he registered more than 2,500 patents, many of them fundamental to modern vehicle crashworthiness.

Career

Barényi studied engineering in Vienna and early in his career designed a small people's car concept with a rear engine and central-tube frame — a design later at the centre of controversy over his claim to have anticipated elements of the Volkswagen Beetle, a claim upheld by a court in the 1950s. Joining Daimler-Benz in 1939, he built up the company's safety development department.

Safety innovations

His most influential invention was the principle of the crumple zone: a rigid passenger safety cell flanked by front and rear zones designed to deform progressively and absorb crash energy, patented in 1952 and first built into a production car in the 1959 Mercedes-Benz W111 ("Fintail"). He also developed the safety steering column and wheel that deflect away from the driver, recessed windscreen wipers, safety door locks and the concept of the detachable, hinged hardtop. He was inducted into the Automotive Hall of Fame in 1994.
