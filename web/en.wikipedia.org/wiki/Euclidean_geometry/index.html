Euclidean geometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A page of Euclid's Elements with geometric diagrams]

Euclidean geometry is a mathematical system attributed to the Alexandrian mathematician Euclid, who described it in his textbook on geometry, the Elements (around 300 BC). It proceeds from a small set of axioms (Euclid's postulates) and deduces the geometry of the flat plane and solid space.

For more than two thousand years Euclidean geometry was the only geometry, synonymous with the geometry of physical space, taught as the model of rigorous reasoning.

The postulates

Euclid's five postulates include: a straight line can be drawn between any two points; a circle can be drawn with any centre and radius; all right angles are equal; and the parallel postulate — through a point not on a given line, exactly one parallel line can be drawn (in Playfair's formulation).

The Pythagorean theorem, the angle sum of triangles (180°), and the theory of similar figures and solid geometry are all developed in the Elements.

After Euclid

Repeated attempts to prove the parallel postulate from the others failed; in the 19th century Bolyai, Lobachevsky and Riemann constructed consistent non-Euclidean geometries by altering it, and Einstein's general relativity later showed physical space is (slightly) non-Euclidean. Hilbert's Foundations of Geometry (1899) gave Euclidean geometry a fully rigorous modern axiomatisation.
