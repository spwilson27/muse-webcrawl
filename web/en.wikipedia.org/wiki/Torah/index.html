The Torah (Hebrew: תּוֹרָה, "instruction" or "teaching") is the compilation of the first five books of the Hebrew Bible: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy — collectively known in Greek as the Pentateuch. It is the central reference of the religious Judaic tradition and the foundation of Jewish law and theology.

Content

The Torah begins with the creation of the world, the primeval history and the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. Exodus recounts the enslavement of the Israelites in Egypt, their liberation under Moses, the revelation at Mount Sinai and the giving of the commandments. Leviticus is largely priestly legislation; Numbers narrates the wilderness wanderings; Deuteronomy ("second law") presents Moses' farewell speeches restating the law before the entry into the Promised Land.

Composition

Traditional belief holds that Moses received the Torah at Sinai. Modern scholarship, through the documentary hypothesis and its successors, generally regards the text as the product of multiple sources and editors over several centuries of the first millennium BCE, reaching substantially its present form in the Persian period.

The Torah scroll and reading

For liturgical use the Torah is handwritten by a trained scribe (sofer) on parchment (klaf) without vowels, rolled on wooden rollers. Portions are read publicly on a weekly cycle completing the whole scroll each year, culminating in the festival of Simchat Torah. Rabbinic Judaism also speaks of an Oral Torah, codified in the Mishnah and Talmud, interpreting the written text.
