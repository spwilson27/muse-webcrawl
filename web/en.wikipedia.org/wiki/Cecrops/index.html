Cecrops

Cecrops (Greek: Κέκροψ, Kékrops) was a mythical king of Attica in ancient Greek mythology. He was said to be autochthonous (born from the earth itself) and was depicted with the upper body of a man and the lower body of a serpent or fish-tail. Cecrops was a culture hero, credited with founding Athens, instituting marriage and burial customs, and teaching reading and writing.

Mythology

According to the tradition preserved in Apollodorus and Pausanias, Cecrops was the first king of Attica, which was earlier called Acte but was renamed Cecropia after him. During his reign, the contest between Athena and Poseidon for patronage of the city took place: Poseidon struck the rock of the Acropolis with his trident and produced a salt spring, while Athena planted an olive tree. Cecrops, called as witness or judge, testified that Athena had first planted the olive, and the gods awarded the city to her; the city was named Athens in her honour.

Cecrops married Aglaurus, daughter of Actaeus, and had a son, Erysichthon, and three daughters: Aglaurus, Herse, and Pandrosus. He was said to have divided Attica into twelve communities and to have introduced the worship of Zeus and Athena. The citadel of Athens, or part of it, was called Cecropia in his memory, and his tomb was shown on the Acropolis.

Legacy

The Athenian tribe Cecropis was named after him, and the figure of Cecrops appears in Athenian art, including the west pediment of the Parthenon, where he is shown witnessing the contest of Athena and Poseidon. The half-serpent form emphasised his earth-born nature and the Athenians' claim to have sprung from their own soil.
