Hypodermis

The hypodermis (also called the subcutaneous layer, subcutaneous tissue, or superficial fascia) is the lowermost layer of the integumentary system in vertebrates. It lies below the dermis and is not, strictly speaking, part of the skin, though the boundary between the hypodermis and dermis can be difficult to distinguish.

The hypodermis consists primarily of loose connective tissue and lobules of fat. It contains larger blood vessels and nerves than those found in the dermis, as well as the bases of hair follicles, sensory receptors such as Pacinian corpuscles, and in many mammals a layer of striated muscle (the panniculus carnosus).

Functions

  • Energy storage: its adipose tissue stores fat as triglycerides, the body's main energy reserve.
  • Insulation: subcutaneous fat helps to conserve body heat.
  • Protection: the layer acts as a shock absorber, cushioning underlying muscle and bone against mechanical injury.
  • Attachment: it connects the skin to the underlying muscle and bone without limiting movement.

The thickness of the hypodermis varies greatly over the body — thick over the abdomen and buttocks, thin or absent in the eyelids. The amount and distribution of subcutaneous fat differ between the sexes and are influenced by hormones, nutrition, and genetics. Clinically, the hypodermis is the target layer for subcutaneous injections, such as those used for insulin, because it is less richly supplied with blood vessels than muscle and absorption is correspondingly slower and more sustained.
