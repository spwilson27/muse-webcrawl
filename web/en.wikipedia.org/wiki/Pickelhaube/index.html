Pickelhaube


A Prussian infantry Pickelhaube

The Pickelhaube (pl. Pickelhauben; from German Pickel, "point" or "pickaxe", and Haube, "bonnet", a general word for headgear) was a spiked leather or metal helmet worn in the 19th and early 20th centuries by Prussian and German soldiers of all ranks, as well as firefighters and police. It became a widely recognized symbol of Imperial Germany and, during the First World War, of German militarism in Allied propaganda.

History

The Pickelhaube was designed by King Frederick William IV of Prussia in 1842, drawing inspiration from earlier Russian and Swedish spiked helmets, and was introduced for Prussian infantry in 1843. Other German states adopted it over the following decades, and it was copied or adapted by the armed forces and police of many countries, including Britain (in the form of the Home Service helmet), the United States, Sweden, Chile, and others.

Originally made of boiled leather with metal fittings, the helmet's spike was intended, in theory, to deflect sword blows. During the First World War, shortages of leather led to substitutes made of felt, tin, and even papier-mâché. The Pickelhaube offered little protection against modern firepower and shrapnel, and in 1916 it was largely replaced at the front by the steel Stahlhelm, though it remained in ceremonial use.

Design

The classic infantry model featured a polished leather body, a metal spike, a front plate (Wappen) bearing the arms of the state, a round rosette holding a chinstrap of metal scales, and national cockades. Artillery units wore a ball in place of the spike; elite and mounted units had more elaborate versions, and parade dress could add a horsehair plume.
