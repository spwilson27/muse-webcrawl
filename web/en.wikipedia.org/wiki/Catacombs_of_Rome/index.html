Catacombs of Rome

The Catacombs of Rome (Italian: Catacombe di Roma) are ancient catacombs — underground burial places — in and around Rome, Italy, of which there are at least forty, some discovered only in recent decades. Though best known for Christian burials undertaken mainly in the 3rd and 4th centuries AD, people of all the religions then present in the city were buried in them, whether in separate catacombs or in mixed ones. They were cut into the soft volcanic tuff rock outside the walls of the city, since Roman law forbade burial within the city limits.

Structure

The catacombs consist of vast networks of narrow galleries (gallerie) on several levels, with burial niches (loculi) carved in rows into the walls, larger family chambers (cubicula) decorated with frescoes, and arched tombs (arcosolia). The most famous complexes include the Catacombs of Callixtus on the Appian Way, which contain the Crypt of the Popes; the Catacombs of Domitilla, the largest; and the Catacombs of Priscilla, notable for some of the earliest known Christian paintings, including early images of the Virgin and Child.

History

Christian burial in the catacombs expanded during periods of persecution, though scholars note the catacombs were burial places rather than hiding places or regular places of worship. After Christianity became legal in 313 and later the state religion, burials continued, and the tombs of martyrs became pilgrimage sites. As Rome declined and was repeatedly sacked, the relics were largely translated to churches within the city during the 8th and 9th centuries, and the catacombs fell into neglect until their scholarly rediscovery, notably by Antonio Bosio in the late 16th century and the archaeologist Giovanni Battista de Rossi in the 19th century.

Art and inscriptions

The catacombs preserve a rich corpus of early Christian art and epigraphy: frescoes of biblical scenes such as Jonah and the Good Shepherd, symbols like the fish (ichthys), the anchor, and the chi-rho, and thousands of inscriptions recording the names, ages, and hopes of ordinary believers.
