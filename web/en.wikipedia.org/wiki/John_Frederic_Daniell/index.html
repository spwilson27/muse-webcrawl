John Frederic Daniell

John Frederic Daniell
Born: 12 March 1790, London, England
Died: 13 March 1845 (aged 55), London, England
Known for: Daniell cell; dew-point hygrometer

John Frederic Daniell FRS (12 March 1790 – 13 March 1845) was a British chemist and physicist. He is best remembered for inventing the Daniell cell, an improved form of electric battery that provided a steadier current than earlier voltaic piles.

Life and career

Daniell was born in London and worked initially in his family's business before pursuing science. He became the first professor of chemistry at King's College London in 1831 and lectured widely. He was a friend of Michael Faraday, to whom he dedicated his influential textbook An Introduction to the Study of Chemical Philosophy (1839), and he played a role in founding and promoting scientific institutions in London.

Contributions

The Daniell cell, introduced in 1836, used copper and zinc electrodes in separate sulfate solutions connected by a salt bridge or porous barrier, producing a reliable voltage of about 1.1 volts. It became a standard source of electrical power for telegraphy and laboratory work for decades.

Daniell also invented a dew-point hygrometer (1820) for measuring atmospheric humidity, wrote on meteorology and horticulture — including early advocacy of greenhouses for tropical plants and the role of humidity in plant growth — and investigated electrolysis and the behaviour of gases. He received the Royal Society's Copley Medal in 1837, its highest honour.
