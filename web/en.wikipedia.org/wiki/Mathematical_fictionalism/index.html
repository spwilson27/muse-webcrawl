Mathematical fictionalism

Mathematical fictionalism is a position in the philosophy of mathematics according to which mathematical statements, taken at face value, are not literally true, because they purport to refer to abstract objects — numbers, sets, functions — that do not exist. Instead, mathematics is compared to a useful fiction, like a story or a game: statements such as "2 + 2 = 4" are true within the fiction of mathematics, in much the same way that "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" is true within the Holmes stories.

Development

The view is most closely associated with Hartry Field, whose book Science Without Numbers (1980) argued that physics could in principle be reformulated without quantifying over mathematical objects, so that mathematics is a conservative and dispensable aid to science rather than a body of truths. Field's programme answered the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument, which holds that because mathematics is indispensable to our best science, we should believe in mathematical objects just as we believe in electrons.

Variants and criticism

Fictionalists differ over whether ordinary mathematical talk should be reinterpreted (revolutionary fictionalism) or treated as a pretence we knowingly engage in. Critics object that nominalising all of science is impractical, that fictionalism struggles to explain the applicability and objectivity of mathematics, or that the distinction between truth and truth-in-a-fiction collapses for necessary truths. Fictionalism is a form of mathematical nominalism and is opposed to Platonism (mathematical realism).
