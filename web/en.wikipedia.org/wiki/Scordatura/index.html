Scordatura

Scordatura ([skordaˈtuːra]; Italian for "mistuning") is a tuning of a string instrument that is different from the normal, standard tuning. It typically attempts to allow special effects or unusual chords or timbres, or to make certain passages easier to play, by altering the pitch of one or more strings.

History

Scordatura was widely used in the 17th and early 18th centuries for lute, guitar and especially violin music. Heinrich Ignaz Franz Biber's Rosary Sonatas (c. 1676) are the most famous example: each sonata uses a different tuning of the violin, one sonata even requiring the middle strings to be crossed, and the tunings are depicted in the manuscript. Other Baroque composers using scordatura include Johann Pachelbel, Antonio Vivaldi and Georg Philipp Telemann. J. S. Bach used it in his Fifth Cello Suite (the A string tuned down to G), and it appears occasionally in later music, for example in Mozart's Sinfonia Concertante and Saint-Saëns's Danse macabre, in which the violin's top string is tuned down a semitone to suggest the Devil.

Later and modern use

In the 20th and 21st centuries composers such as Igor Stravinsky and Béla Bartók used scordatura for colouristic effects, and it is commonplace in folk fiddling (cross-tuning), blues and rock guitar (drop D and open tunings) and heavy metal, where drop tunings facilitate power chords and extend the instrument's low range.

Notation

Scordatura parts may be written at sounding pitch, or — especially in Baroque violin music — notated as if the standard tuning applied, so that the player's fingering reads normally while the sounding pitches differ.
