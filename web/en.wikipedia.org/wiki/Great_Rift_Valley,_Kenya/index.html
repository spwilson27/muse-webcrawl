Great Rift Valley, Kenya

The Rift Valley escarpment near Lake Naivasha.

The Great Rift Valley is part of an intra-continental ridge system that runs through Kenya from north to south. It is part of the Gregory Rift, the eastern branch of the East African Rift, which is slowly splitting the African Plate into the Nubian and Somali plates. In Kenya the valley runs for some 700 kilometres (430 miles), from Lake Turkana in the north to the Tanzanian border in the south, and is flanked by escarpments rising thousands of metres above its floor.

Geology

Rifting began in the Miocene as the crust stretched and thinned, producing a chain of downfaulted troughs (grabens) separated by step faults, accompanied by extensive volcanism. Volcanoes on or near the valley floor include Mount Longonot, Mount Suswa and Menengai Crater. The valley is still active: geothermal fields at Olkaria supply a large share of Kenya's electricity, and hot springs and geysers occur around Lake Bogoria.

Lakes

The Kenyan Rift is famous for its lakes, many of them alkaline "soda lakes" formed because the enclosed basins have no outlet and evaporation concentrates salts. From north to south they include:

  • Lake Turkana — the world's largest permanent desert lake and largest alkaline lake.
  • Lake Baringo — a freshwater lake noted for its birds and hippos.
  • Lake Bogoria — a soda lake and national reserve famed for geysers and vast flocks of lesser flamingos.
  • Lake Nakuru — a national park once famous for millions of flamingos feeding on its algae.
  • Lake Elmenteita — a shallow soda lake between Nakuru and Naivasha.
  • Lake Naivasha — a freshwater lake supporting fisheries, horticulture and abundant birdlife.
  • Lake Magadi — an intensely saline lake mined for soda ash (trona).

The Kenya Lake System in the Great Rift Valley — lakes Bogoria, Nakuru and Elmenteita — was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2011 for its outstanding biodiversity, including globally important populations of lesser flamingos.

Human origins

The sediments of the Kenyan and Ethiopian rifts have yielded some of the most important fossils in human evolution, because volcanic ash layers allow precise dating. Although the most famous hominin sites (Olduvai Gorge and Laetoli) lie in Tanzania and the Afar sites in Ethiopia, Kenya's Lake Turkana basin — with finds such as the near-complete Homo erectus skeleton "Turkana Boy" (Nariokotome Boy, discovered 1984) — is central to the study of early Homo. The valley is often called part of the "Cradle of Humankind".

Towns and economy

Major settlements in or overlooking the valley include Nakuru (Kenya's fourth-largest city), Naivasha, Narok and Eldoret on the western highlands. Fertile volcanic soils support intensive farming; Naivasha's flower farms are among the world's largest exporters of cut roses. The valley is a major tourist destination, with national parks and reserves including Hell's Gate and Lake Nakuru.
