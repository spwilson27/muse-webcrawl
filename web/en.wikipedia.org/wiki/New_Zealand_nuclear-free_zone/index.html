New Zealand nuclear-free zone

LegislationNew Zealand Nuclear Free Zone, Disarmament, and Arms Control Act 1987
ScopeTerritorial land, sea, and airspace of New Zealand
ProhibitsNuclear explosive devices; nuclear-powered or nuclear-armed vessels

The New Zealand nuclear-free zone is a policy and legal regime under which nuclear explosive devices are prohibited in the land, internal waters, territorial sea, and airspace of New Zealand, and nuclear-powered or nuclear-armed ships are barred from the country's ports. It was enacted in the New Zealand Nuclear Free Zone, Disarmament, and Arms Control Act 1987 and remains a defining feature of New Zealand's independent foreign policy.

History

Opposition to nuclear testing in the Pacific grew from the 1960s as France tested weapons at Mururoa Atoll; in 1973 New Zealand and Australia took France to the International Court of Justice and New Zealand sent naval frigates into the test zone in protest. A mass anti-nuclear movement, including protest flotillas contesting visits by U.S. nuclear-capable warships, propelled the issue into electoral politics. The Labour government of David Lange, elected in 1984, refused port entry to the destroyer USS Buchanan in 1985 because the United States would neither confirm nor deny the presence of nuclear weapons. The United States responded by suspending its ANZUS security obligations to New Zealand in 1986. The bombing of the Greenpeace vessel Rainbow Warrior by French agents in Auckland Harbour in 1985, killing a photographer, further consolidated public support for the policy.

Legislation and effects

The 1987 Act bans the manufacture, acquisition, possession, and testing of nuclear explosive devices, prohibits dumping of radioactive waste at sea within the zone, and empowers the Prime Minister to admit foreign warships only if satisfied they are neither nuclear-armed nor nuclear-powered. The policy survived changes of government, strained but did not end defence relations with the United States — restrictions were gradually eased from 2010, and a U.S. Navy ship visited in 2016 for the first time in 33 years — and became a potent symbol of national identity, often cited in New Zealand's "clean, green" international image.
