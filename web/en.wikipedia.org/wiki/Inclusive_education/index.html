Inclusive education

Inclusive education is an approach to schooling in which children with and without disabilities and other additional needs learn together in mainstream classrooms, with appropriate support. Inclusion differs from earlier models of integration and mainstreaming in that it requires the school system itself to adapt to the learner, rather than expecting the learner to fit the system.

History

The modern inclusion movement grew out of disability rights activism and landmark legislation such as the United States' Education for All Handicapped Children Act of 1975 (later the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act). Internationally, the UNESCO Salamanca Statement of 1994 called on governments to adopt inclusive education as the norm, and the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006) affirmed a right to inclusive education at all levels.

Principles and practice

Inclusive practice involves differentiated instruction, universal design for learning, individual education plans, and collaboration between classroom teachers, special educators, and support staff. Advocates argue that inclusion benefits all students by fostering diversity and reducing stigma, while critics raise concerns about resources, teacher training, and the needs of students whose difficulties may require specialised settings.

Implementation worldwide

Countries vary widely in implementation: some, such as Italy and Canada (New Brunswick), have near-fully inclusive systems, while others retain parallel networks of special schools. Research generally finds comparable or better academic and social outcomes for included students when adequate support is provided.
