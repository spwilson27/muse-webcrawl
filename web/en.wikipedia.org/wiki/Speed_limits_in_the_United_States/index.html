Speed limits in the United States

Speed limits in the United States are set by each state or territory, rather than by the federal government, though federal involvement in highway funding has historically influenced state limits. Speed limits vary widely: rural freeway limits range from 55 mph (89 km/h) in Hawaii to 85 mph (137 km/h) on a toll road in Texas, the highest posted limit in the country.

Overview

Typical limits are:

  • 25 mph (40 km/h) in residential districts and school zones
  • 35–45 mph (56–72 km/h) on urban arterials
  • 55 mph (89 km/h) on two-lane rural highways
  • 65–75 mph (105–121 km/h) on rural freeways in most states
  • 80 mph (129 km/h) on rural freeways in parts of Idaho, Montana, Nevada, South Dakota, Texas, Utah, and Wyoming; 85 mph on Texas State Highway 130

Many states set lower maximum limits for trucks, and most states apply "basic speed laws" under which driving at an unsafe speed for conditions can be an offence even below the posted limit.

History

The first US speed limits applied to automobiles date to 1901 in Connecticut. Limits rose with highway development in the mid-20th century, but in 1974, in response to the 1973 oil crisis, Congress enacted the National Maximum Speed Law, effectively imposing a nationwide 55 mph limit by withholding highway funds from states with higher limits. The limit was relaxed to 65 mph on rural Interstates in 1987 and repealed entirely in 1995, returning full authority to the states. Since then, many states — particularly in the West — have progressively raised rural freeway limits to 70, 75, or 80 mph.

Montana is notable for having had no daytime numerical speed limit on its highways between 1995 and 1999, relying instead on a "reasonable and prudent" standard, before reinstating a 75 mph limit (later 80 mph on Interstates).

Safety debate

Speed limit policy involves a long-running debate. Safety advocates cite the strong relationship between impact speed and crash severity, while opponents of low limits argue that limits set below the 85th-percentile speed of free-flowing traffic are widely ignored, produce speed differentials, and divert enforcement resources. Research findings on the effects of the 1995 repeal remain contested, with studies variously attributing increases in fatalities on affected roads to higher limits.
