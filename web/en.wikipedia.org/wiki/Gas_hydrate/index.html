Gas hydrate

Gas hydrates, also called clathrate hydrates, are crystalline, ice-like solids in which gas molecules (the "guests") are trapped inside cage-like structures of hydrogen-bonded water molecules (the "host"). The most abundant natural gas hydrate is methane clathrate, in which methane occupies the cages. Gas hydrates form under conditions of high pressure and low temperature, and they occur widely in deep-ocean sediments along continental margins and in and beneath permafrost.

Structure

Hydrates crystallize in several structures, the commonest being structure I and structure II, built from pentagonal dodecahedral and larger cages. A unit volume of fully saturated methane hydrate can contain roughly 164 volumes of methane gas at standard conditions, which accounts for both the energy potential and the hazards of these deposits.

Occurrence

The gas hydrate stability zone occurs where water is cold and pressures are high: typically in ocean sediments below a few hundred metres of water depth, and in Arctic permafrost regions. Hydrates are commonly detected on seismic profiles by a bottom-simulating reflector (BSR) that marks the base of the stability zone. Global estimates of the methane stored in hydrates are enormous — on the order of thousands of gigatonnes of carbon — exceeding the carbon in all other fossil fuels combined, though much of it is in low concentrations unlikely to be recoverable.

Significance

Energy resource

Japan, China, India, South Korea, and the United States have national programmes exploring hydrate production. Japan's MH21 programme conducted the first offshore production tests in the Nankai Trough in 2013 and 2017, and China reported sustained production tests in the South China Sea in 2017 and 2020. No commercial production yet exists.

Climate and hazards

Because methane is a potent greenhouse gas, sudden destabilization of hydrates has been hypothesized as a driver of past rapid warming events — the "clathrate gun hypothesis" — notably at the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum. Hydrate dissociation can also weaken continental slopes, potentially triggering submarine landslides, and hydrates forming inside gas pipelines ("plugs") are a major flow-assurance problem in oil and gas engineering.
