Modest Mussorgsky

Modest Petrovich Mussorgsky (21 March 1839 – 28 March 1881) was a Russian composer, one of the group known as "The Five". He was an innovator of Russian music in the Romantic period, striving to achieve a uniquely Russian musical identity through folk themes, realistic depiction of Russian speech in vocal music, and unconventional harmony.

Life

Born into a landowning family in Karevo, Pskov Governorate, Mussorgsky was commissioned in the Preobrazhensky Regiment and worked as a civil servant while composing. In 1857 he met Mily Balakirev and joined the circle of nationalist composers later called The Five. Financial troubles after the emancipation of the serfs in 1861 forced him to support himself with clerical work. Plagued by alcoholism, he died in poverty in a St Petersburg hospital at the age of 42, leaving many works unfinished.

Works

  • Boris Godunov (1869, revised 1872) — his opera masterpiece, based on Pushkin's drama, depicting the troubled Tsar Boris; famous for its crowd scenes and psychological depth.
  • Pictures at an Exhibition (1874) — a piano suite inspired by an exhibition of his late friend Viktor Hartmann's pictures and designs; best known today in Maurice Ravel's 1922 orchestration.
  • Night on Bald Mountain (1867) — an orchestral tone poem depicting a witches' sabbath.
  • Songs and Dances of Death (1875–77) and other song cycles, noted for their stark realism.
  • The unfinished operas Khovanshchina and The Fair at Sorochyntsi.

Legacy

Many of Mussorgsky's works were completed, edited or re-orchestrated after his death by Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov, whose smoother versions long dominated performance; 20th-century scholarship restored much of the composer's original, rougher idiom. His music influenced composers from Debussy and Ravel to Shostakovich.
