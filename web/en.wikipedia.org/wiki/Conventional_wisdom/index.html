Conventional wisdom

Conventional wisdom (CW) is a body of ideas or explanations generally accepted as true by the general public or by experts and practitioners within a field. The term often implies that the ideas are widely held but not necessarily subjected to rigorous critical examination, and it is frequently used in a slightly pejorative sense for unquestioned assumptions.

Origin of the term

The economist John Kenneth Galbraith popularized the expression in his 1958 book The Affluent Society, arguing that ideas are accepted not because they are true but because they are familiar, respectable, and convenient — what he called their "acceptability". Galbraith contrasted the conventional wisdom with the actual course of events, which often renders it obsolete.

Characteristics

Conventional wisdom is characterized by social reinforcement: beliefs persist because many people hold them and because holding them carries no social cost. It can lag behind new evidence, since revising an accepted view requires effort and may conflict with institutional interests.

Relation to science and politics

In science, paradigms described by Thomas Kuhn resemble a disciplined form of conventional wisdom that can resist anomalous findings. In politics and journalism, the "conventional wisdom" describes the prevailing narrative about elections and policy, which pundits often converge upon collectively.
