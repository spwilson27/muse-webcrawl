Internet addiction disorder

Internet addiction disorder
Other namesProblematic internet use; compulsive internet use
SpecialtyPsychiatry, psychology
SymptomsPreoccupation with internet use, loss of control, withdrawal-like symptoms, continued use despite harm
StatusNot a standalone diagnosis in DSM-5 or ICD-11

Internet addiction disorder (IAD), also known as problematic internet use, refers to excessive or poorly controlled preoccupations, urges or behaviours regarding internet use that lead to impairment or distress. The concept was proposed satirically by psychiatrist Ivan Goldberg in 1995, modelling criteria on pathological gambling, but was taken up seriously by researchers such as Kimberly Young, who developed the Internet Addiction Test (1998), and Mark Griffiths. Whether it constitutes a distinct disorder remains debated; neither the DSM-5 nor ICD-11 recognises "internet addiction" as a standalone diagnosis, though ICD-11 includes gaming disorder, and the DSM-5 lists Internet Gaming Disorder in its section for conditions requiring further study.

Signs and proposed criteria

Proposed diagnostic features include salience (internet use dominates thinking and behaviour), tolerance (needing increasing time online), withdrawal symptoms such as irritability when offline, unsuccessful attempts to cut down, loss of interest in other activities, lying about use, use to escape negative moods, and jeopardising relationships, education or work. Related constructs include problematic smartphone use, social media addiction, online gambling and compulsive online shopping or pornography use, which some researchers argue should be classified separately rather than under a single umbrella diagnosis.

Prevalence and comorbidity

Estimates vary widely with instruments and cut-offs, from around 1–2% in some European samples to much higher figures in some East Asian adolescent surveys. Problematic use is frequently comorbid with depression, anxiety disorders, ADHD and substance use disorders, and the direction of causality is contested. China and South Korea were among the first countries to treat severe cases clinically, including dedicated treatment camps and clinics, and China's 2008 clinical definition was an early attempt at formal criteria.

Treatment

Interventions studied include cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for internet addiction (CBT-IA), family therapy, motivational interviewing, digital-detox programmes, and treatment of comorbid conditions. Prevention efforts focus on parental mediation, school programmes and platform design features such as usage dashboards; several jurisdictions have imposed limits on minors' gaming time, notably China's restrictions on online gaming for under-18s.

Debate

Critics argue that heavy internet use is usually a coping behaviour or symptom of underlying disorders rather than an addiction in itself, that the medium is not the activity (one is addicted to gambling or gaming, not "the internet"), and that pathologising ordinary behaviour risks moral panic. Supporters point to neuroimaging similarities with substance and gambling addictions and to clear cases of severe functional impairment.
