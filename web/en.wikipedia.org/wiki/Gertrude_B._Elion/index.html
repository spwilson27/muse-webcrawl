Gertrude B. Elion

Born: 23 January 1918, New York City
Died: 21 February 1999, Chapel Hill, North Carolina
Fields: Biochemistry, pharmacology
Known for: Rational drug design; azathioprine, acyclovir, allopurinol, 6-mercaptopurine
Awards: Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1988)

Gertrude Belle Elion (January 23, 1918 – February 21, 1999) was an American biochemist and pharmacologist who shared the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with George H. Hitchings and Sir James Black "for their discoveries of important principles for drug treatment." Working for over four decades at Burroughs Wellcome, she helped develop a remarkable series of drugs by a new method of rational, targeted drug design rather than trial and error.

Career and discoveries

After graduating from Hunter College in 1937, Elion struggled to find laboratory work because of her gender, working as a teacher and food analyst before joining Burroughs Wellcome in 1944 as assistant to George Hitchings. Together they exploited differences in nucleic acid metabolism between human cells and pathogens to design selective drugs. Their compounds included 6-mercaptopurine (the first effective treatment for childhood leukaemia), azathioprine (the immunosuppressant that made organ transplantation practical), allopurinol (for gout), pyrimethamine (for malaria), trimethoprim (an antibacterial), and acyclovir (the first effective antiviral, for herpes).

Later life and honours

Elion never formally earned a doctorate — she received many honorary degrees instead — and became head of the Department of Experimental Therapy in 1967, later a research professor at Duke University. She was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 1991, the first woman so honoured, and received the National Medal of Science the same year.
