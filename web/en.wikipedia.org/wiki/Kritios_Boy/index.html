Yearc. 480 BC
MediumMarble

The Kritios Boy or Critius Boy belongs to the Early Classical period of ancient Greek sculpture and is a landmark in the development of Greek art. It is the first statue from classical antiquity known to use contrapposto, a realistic shifting of the weight onto one leg that imparts a sense of imbalance and potential movement to the figure. It is named after the sculptor Kritios and is exhibited in the Acropolis Museum in Athens.

Discovery

The marble trunk of the statue was found in 1865 during excavations on the Acropolis of Athens, in the debris (the so-called Perserschutt) left by the Persian sack of Athens in 480 BC; the head was recovered in 1888. The circumstances of its destruction and burial help date the work to just before the Persian invasion.

Description and significance

The figure, slightly under life-size and carved from Parian marble, depicts a nude youth. Unlike the rigid, front-facing kouroi of the earlier Archaic period — with their patterned hair, symmetrical stance, and "Archaic smile" — the Kritios Boy stands relaxed: the weight rests on the left leg, the right knee is bent and the pelvis tilted accordingly, producing a lifelike chain of adjustments through the torso. The head turns slightly and the severe, contemplative expression replaces the fixed smile. The eyes, once inlaid, are now lost.

Art historians regard the statue as a watershed between Archaic and Classical sculpture, influencing later masters such as Polykleitos, whose canon of proportions built on the naturalism first demonstrated here.

Attribution

The statue's name follows its stylistic association with Kritios, who with Nesiotes made the replacement Tyrannicides group in 477/476 BC after the original was carried off by the Persians. The attribution is conventional rather than documented.
