H2 receptor antagonists (also called H2 blockers) are a class of drugs that block the action of histamine at the histamine H2 receptors of the parietal cells of the stomach, thereby reducing the production of gastric acid. They are used in the treatment of peptic ulcer disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and dyspepsia. Their discovery in the 1970s was a landmark of rational drug design, and the first of them, cimetidine, revolutionised the treatment of ulcers and became the world's first blockbuster drug.

Mechanism of action

Gastric acid is secreted by parietal cells in response to three main stimuli: histamine, gastrin and acetylcholine. Histamine, released from enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells, acts on H2 receptors coupled to adenylate cyclase; the resulting rise in cyclic AMP activates the proton pump (H+/K+-ATPase) at the cell's secretory surface. H2 antagonists competitively and reversibly occupy the H2 receptor, suppressing both basal and meal-stimulated acid secretion, and are particularly effective at suppressing nocturnal acid secretion. They are less potent than proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), which block the pump itself, and tolerance (tachyphylaxis) can develop with continuous use.

History

The class grew out of work at Smith, Kline & French in the 1960s led by pharmacologist James Black, who reasoned that if histamine stimulated acid through a receptor distinct from the H1 receptor blocked by antihistamines, a selective antagonist should be discoverable. Systematic chemical modification of the histamine molecule produced burimamide, then metiamide, and finally cimetidine, launched in the United Kingdom in 1976 under the brand name Tagamet. Cimetidine made elective ulcer surgery largely unnecessary for many patients and was followed by ranitidine (Zantac, 1981), which became the best-selling prescription drug in the world, and later famotidine and nizatidine. Black shared the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in part for this work.

Clinical use and adverse effects

H2 blockers heal duodenal and gastric ulcers, relieve heartburn, and are used for stress-ulcer prophylaxis in hospital patients and as adjuncts in anaphylaxis (for the cutaneous H2-mediated effects). They are generally well tolerated. Reported adverse effects include headache, diarrhoea and dizziness; cimetidine at high doses has antiandrogen effects (gynaecomastia, impotence) and inhibits several cytochrome P450 enzymes, causing clinically important interactions with drugs such as warfarin and theophylline. Long-term acid suppression from any cause may impair vitamin B12 and magnesium absorption. Since the 1990s, PPIs have displaced H2 blockers as first-line therapy for most acid-related diseases, though the older drugs remain in wide over-the-counter use for intermittent heartburn.
