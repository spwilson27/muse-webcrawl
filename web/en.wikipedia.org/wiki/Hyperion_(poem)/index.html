Hyperion (poem)

Hyperion is an abandoned epic poem by the English Romantic poet John Keats (1795–1821). Begun in late 1818 and published in unfinished form in 1820, it recounts the fall of the Titans, the elder gods of Greek mythology, and the rise of the Olympians, focusing on the grief of the fallen sun-god Hyperion and the birth of Apollo as god of poetry.

Background

Keats began the poem in the autumn of 1818, working in blank verse under the strong influence of Milton's Paradise Lost. He abandoned it in the spring of 1819, dissatisfied with its Miltonic style, and recast the material as The Fall of Hyperion: A Dream, a first-person vision poem that he also left unfinished.

Summary

The poem opens with the fallen Saturn in despair among the defeated Titans. The sea-goddess Clymene and the Titan Oceanus debate their fall, with Oceanus accepting that the Titans must yield to a greater beauty. In Book III, Apollo, after an agonizing transformation guided by Mnemosyne, becomes the god of poetry, and the poem breaks off.

Reception

Published against Keats's wishes alongside Lamia and The Eve of St. Agnes, Hyperion was praised by Shelley and later critics for passages of great power, and is often considered the most ambitious of Keats's works despite its unfinished state.
