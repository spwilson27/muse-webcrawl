Burette

Burette
Type: Laboratory glassware
Used for: Titration; precise liquid dispensing
Typical capacity: 10–100 mL

A burette (or buret) is a graduated glass tube with a tap (stopcock) at one end, used in analytical chemistry to dispense measured volumes of liquid with high accuracy, most commonly in titrations. Typical burettes hold 25 or 50 mL and are graduated to 0.1 mL, allowing volumes to be read to about ±0.05 mL or better.

Use

The burette is filled above the zero mark with the titrant, the meniscus is adjusted and read, and the solution is then run into a flask containing the analyte until an indicator signals the endpoint; the volume delivered is the difference between initial and final readings. Care is taken to remove air bubbles from the tip and to read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level to avoid parallax error.

History and variants

Early forms were described in the late 18th and early 19th centuries by French chemists including François Descroizilles and Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac; Karl Friedrich Mohr introduced improvements such as the pinchcock and the Schellbach stripe for easier reading. Modern variants include piston (digital) burettes, automatic zero burettes, and amber glass burettes for light-sensitive reagents such as silver nitrate.
