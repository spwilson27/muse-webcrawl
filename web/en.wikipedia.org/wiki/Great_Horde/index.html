The Great Horde (also the Great Orda) was the central remnant of the Golden Horde on the Pontic–Volga steppe following that khanate's disintegration in the fifteenth century. Centred on Sarai on the lower Volga, it claimed the succession to the Golden Horde's authority, including tribute from the Russian principalities, but was steadily weakened by rival successor states — the khanates of Kazan, Crimea and Astrakhan, and the Nogai Horde — and by the rising power of the Grand Duchy of Moscow.

History

The Golden Horde fragmented after the civil wars of the early fifteenth century. Kazan seceded in 1438 and Crimea in 1441–49. The remaining central horde, ruling the steppe between the Dnieper and the Volga from about 1466 under Küchük Muhammad's descendants, styled itself simply "the Horde" and is known to historians as the Great Horde to distinguish it from its rivals.

Its most energetic ruler, Akhmad Khan (r. 1465–1481), sought to restore the old tributary order over Rus' and allied with Casimir IV of Poland–Lithuania against Ivan III of Moscow. In 1480 Akhmad marched to the Ugra River, where his army faced the Muscovite host across the river for several weeks in the Great Stand on the Ugra River. With winter approaching, his Lithuanian allies absent, and his rear threatened, Akhmad withdrew — an event traditionally taken to mark the end of the "Tatar yoke" over Russia. The following year Akhmad was killed by Siberian and Nogai forces.

Under his sons, the Great Horde raided Poland–Lithuania and Moscow but was overtaken by the Crimean khan Mengli I Giray, who had allied with Moscow. In 1502 Mengli Giray destroyed Sarai and defeated the last khan, Shaykh Ahmad, ending the Great Horde. Its territory and population were absorbed chiefly by Crimea and the Nogais.
