Metonymy

Metonymy (/mɪˈtɒnɪmi/) is a figure of speech in which a concept is referred to by the name of something closely associated with that concept. For example, the Crown for monarchy, the White House for the United States presidency and its administration, Hollywood for the American film industry, and the bench for judges. Metonymy works by contiguity — real-world association — rather than by the resemblance that underlies metaphor.

Etymology

The word derives from the Greek metōnumía (μετωνυμία), "a change of name", from metá "after, beyond" and ónoma "name". Classical rhetoric treated metonymy as one of the master tropes, catalogued by Quintilian among the principal figures of speech.

Types

Common metonymic relationships include: container for contents ("the kettle is boiling"), place for institution ("Wall Street panicked"), author for work ("reading Shakespeare"), instrument for agent ("the pen is mightier than the sword"), cause for effect, and object for user ("the buses are on strike"). Synecdoche, in which a part stands for the whole or the whole for a part ("all hands on deck"), is often treated as a subtype of metonymy.

Metonymy and metaphor

Roman Jakobson famously argued that metaphor and metonymy are the two poles of language: metaphor operates on the axis of selection and similarity, metonymy on the axis of combination and contiguity. He associated metaphor with Romanticism and poetry, metonymy with Realism and prose. Cognitive linguists George Lakoff and Mark Johnson later argued in Metaphors We Live By (1980) that metonymy, like metaphor, is not merely rhetorical decoration but a fundamental structure of thought, as in the conceptual pattern THE PART FOR THE WHOLE.

Examples

  • "The Crown" — the monarch and royal authority
  • "Downing Street" — the British prime minister's office
  • "Silicon Valley" — the technology industry of the San Francisco Bay Area
  • "A dish" — the food served in it
  • "Prose and verse" — literature (in older usage)
