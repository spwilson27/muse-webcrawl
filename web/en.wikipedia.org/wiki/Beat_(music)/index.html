Beat (music)

In music and music theory, the beat is the basic unit of time, the pulse with which listeners entrain — tapping a foot or clapping along — and against which rhythm is organised. Beats are grouped into recurring cycles called metre, in which some beats are felt as stronger (downbeats) and others weaker, defining time signatures such as 4/4 or 3/4. The speed of the beat is the tempo, conventionally measured in beats per minute (BPM).

Beat and metre

Musicologists distinguish the beat from rhythm: rhythm is the pattern of durations actually sounded, while the beat is the inferred, regular pulse underlying it. Beats may be subdivided (each beat into two or three parts) or grouped at higher levels into measures and hypermeasures. In much Western popular music the beat is made explicit by drums and bass, with the snare typically accenting the backbeat — beats two and four in 4/4 — a defining feature of rock, pop and R&B. Syncopation places accents off the beat or on weak beats, creating rhythmic tension against the steady pulse.

Perception

Humans perceive and synchronise to a beat spontaneously, a capacity studied in music cognition and shared in limited form with a few other species. Listeners infer a beat even when some beats are silent, and performers characteristically deviate from strict metronomic time by tiny expressive amounts. Conductors communicate the beat with standardised hand patterns, and the metronome, patented by Johann Maelzel in 1815, provides a mechanical reference.

Related senses

In popular music production, "a beat" also denotes the instrumental backing track, especially in hip hop. In acoustics, "beats" are the periodic swelling heard when two tones of slightly different frequency sound together — a distinct phenomenon arising from interference.
