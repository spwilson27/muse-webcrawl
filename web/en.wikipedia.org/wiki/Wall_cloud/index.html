Wall cloud

A wall cloud (murus) is a large, localised, persistent lowering of cloud from the base of a cumulonimbus, often in the rain-free base region of a supercell thunderstorm. Wall clouds are typically a few kilometres across and are associated with the rotating updraft — the mesocyclone — of a supercell.

Formation

A wall cloud forms when warm, moist air is drawn upward into a supercell's rotating updraft. As the air rises and cools, water vapour condenses at a lower level than the surrounding cloud base, producing a visible lowering. Rotation of the updraft gives the wall cloud a visibly turning appearance, and rising motion is often evident as cloud fragments scud upward into it.

Relation to tornadoes

Wall clouds are one of the most important visual indicators used by storm spotters to assess tornado potential. A large majority of significant tornadoes form from wall clouds, and a tornado is generally considered to have touched down when a funnel cloud extends from a wall cloud to the ground. However, most wall clouds never produce a tornado; only a minority of supercells generate tornadoes, and many wall clouds dissipate without doing so.

Identification

Not every lowering beneath a thunderstorm is a wall cloud. Shelf clouds, associated with gust fronts, are arc-shaped and move away from the storm's precipitation, while scud clouds are ragged, transient fragments. A true wall cloud persists for tens of minutes, remains attached to the storm's rain-free base, and often shows sustained rotation.

History

The term "wall cloud" came into widespread use among storm researchers and chasers in the mid-20th century, particularly through the work of the National Severe Storms Laboratory and the spotter networks that developed in the United States in the 1950s and 1960s. The rotating wall cloud was identified as the precursor structure of most tornadoes in the landmark studies of supercell structure in the 1970s.
