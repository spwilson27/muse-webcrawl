Languedoc wine

Languedoc wine, produced in the Languedoc region of southern France along the Mediterranean coast, comes from one of the largest wine-producing areas in the world. Together with neighbouring Roussillon, the region is often referred to as Languedoc-Roussillon. For much of the twentieth century the area was known principally for high-volume, inexpensive table wine, but since the 1980s a quality revolution has brought its appellation wines wide critical attention.

History

Viticulture in the Languedoc dates to the ancient Greeks, who founded settlements such as Agde around the 5th century BC, and was expanded under the Romans along the Via Domitia. In the 19th century the completion of the Canal du Midi and rail links to northern France let the region supply cheap red wine in enormous quantities to industrial workers. The phylloxera crisis, overproduction scandals, and violent winegrowers' revolts in 1907 marked the region's turbulent modern history.

Geography and grapes

The vineyards stretch from the Spanish border to the Rhône delta, backed by the Cévennes and Pyrenean foothills. The climate is Mediterranean, with hot, dry summers and the drying tramontane wind. Traditional varieties include Carignan, Grenache, Syrah, Mourvèdre, and Cinsault for reds and rosés, and Picpoul, Grenache blanc, Marsanne, Roussanne, and Viognier for whites. Bordeaux and other international varieties such as Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon, and Chardonnay are widely planted for Vin de Pays (now IGP) wines, of which the region produces the majority of France's output.

Appellations

Important Appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC) areas include Corbières, Minervois, Saint-Chinian, Faugères, Picpoul de Pinet, and the sparkling wines of Limoux, including Blanquette de Limoux, which local tradition claims predates Champagne. The fortified sweet wines (vins doux naturels) of Banyuls and Maury, made just to the south in Roussillon, are closely associated.
