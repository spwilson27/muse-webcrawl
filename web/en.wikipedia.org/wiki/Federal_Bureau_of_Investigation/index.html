Federal Bureau of Investigation

The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) is the domestic intelligence and security service of the United States and its principal federal investigative agency. Operating under the Department of Justice, it investigates federal crimes, counterterrorism, counterintelligence, cybercrime and public corruption, and maintains the national fingerprint and forensic systems.

History

Attorney General Charles Bonaparte created the Bureau of Investigation in 1908. J. Edgar Hoover, director from 1924 until his death in 1972, professionalised it — fingerprint files, the FBI Laboratory, the Uniform Crime Reports and the Ten Most Wanted list — while also running controversial surveillance such as COINTELPRO, exposed in the 1970s and investigated by the Church Committee.

Organisation

The Director, appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for a ten-year term, oversees 56 field offices, the FBI Academy at Quantico, Virginia, and divisions for counterterrorism, criminal investigations, cyber, intelligence and science and technology. Directors have included Robert Mueller, James Comey and Christopher Wray.

Jurisdiction and priorities

The FBI investigates violations of more than 200 categories of federal law, protects civil rights, runs joint terrorism task forces, and provides forensic and identification services to state and local police. Post-9/11 reforms made intelligence and national security its top priorities.
