Motto

Motto
OriginFrom Italian motto, "a saying", from Latin muttum, "a grunt/murmur"
Used byStates, families, schools, military units, organisations

A motto (derived from the Latin muttum, "mutter", by way of Italian motto, "word" or "sentence") is a sentence or phrase expressing the beliefs or ideals of an individual, family, social group or organisation. Mottos are typically found in written form and may be in any language, although Latin was long favoured in the Western tradition.

Heraldry

In heraldry a motto is often depicted on a scroll beneath the shield in a coat of arms, or above the crest in Scottish heraldry. Heraldic mottos frequently pun on the bearer's name (canting mottoes) or allude to family history. In English heraldry the motto is not officially part of the grant of arms and may be changed at will, whereas in Scotland it forms part of the grant.

National and institutional mottoes

Many states adopt official mottoes: the United States uses "In God We Trust" (and unofficially E pluribus unum), France "Liberté, égalité, fraternité", and the European Union "United in diversity". Schools and universities often preserve Latin mottoes — Harvard's Veritas, Yale's Lux et Veritas, Oxford's Dominus Illuminatio Mea — while military units, scout movements ("Be Prepared") and companies use mottoes as rallying phrases and slogans.

Literature

In literature, an epigraph at the head of a chapter or book functions like a motto, setting the theme; the term "motto" was used in this sense for the short quotations Scott placed at the heads of chapters in the Waverley novels.
