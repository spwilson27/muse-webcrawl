Lipid nanoparticle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are nanoparticles composed of lipids, typically 10–1000 nanometres in diameter, used as pharmaceutical drug delivery vehicles. They encapsulate nucleic acids such as mRNA or siRNA, protecting them from degradation and ferrying them across cell membranes. LNPs became globally prominent as the delivery system for the Pfizer–BioNTech and Moderna COVID-19 mRNA vaccines.

Composition

A typical nucleic-acid LNP has four components: an ionisable cationic lipid (the largest fraction, which binds the negatively charged RNA at acidic pH and becomes near-neutral in the bloodstream), a phospholipid such as DSPC that supports the bilayer structure, cholesterol for stability and membrane fusion, and a PEG-lipid that prolongs circulation time and controls particle size. Particles are assembled by rapid microfluidic mixing of an ethanolic lipid solution with an aqueous RNA solution.

Mechanism

After injection, LNPs are taken up by cells via endocytosis. As the endosome acidifies, the ionisable lipid becomes protonated, disrupts the endosomal membrane and releases the RNA cargo into the cytosol — the "endosomal escape" step that largely determines potency. In intramuscular vaccination, LNPs drain to lymph nodes and also reach the liver via apolipoprotein E adsorption.

History

LNP technology grew out of liposome research and the work of Pieter Cullis and colleagues at the University of British Columbia. The first approved LNP drug was patisiran (Onpattro, 2018), an siRNA therapy for hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis, followed in 2020 by the mRNA vaccines. Research continues into LNPs that target tissues beyond the liver, and into inhaled and oral formulations.
