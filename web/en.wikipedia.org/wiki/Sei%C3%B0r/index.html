Seiðr

Seiðr (Old Norse: seiðr) is a form of magic described in Old Norse literature and associated with the pre-Christian religion of the Norse peoples. Practitioners, often women known as vǫlur (singular vǫlva) or seiðkonur, were said to be able to foresee the future, influence fate, affect the weather, and harm or help people and animals, sometimes through trance, chanting, and ritual performed on a raised platform or seat.

In the sources

Seiðr appears in sagas and mythological texts. The goddess Freyja is said in Ynglinga saga to have taught seiðr to the Æsir, and Odin himself is described as practicing it — a fact Loki uses to taunt him in Lokasenna, since seiðr was considered unmanly (ergi) for men. A detailed account of a seiðr ritual performed by a vǫlva appears in the Saga of Erik the Red, set in Norse Greenland.

Practice

Descriptions suggest rituals involving a high seat, special garments, chanting of spells (galdrar) by assistants, and altered states of consciousness through which the practitioner's spirit was believed to travel. Archaeological finds of staff-like objects in some female burials have been interpreted as the ritual staffs of vǫlur.

Modern practice

Seiðr has been revived in various forms within modern Norse paganism (Heathenry), where trance-based oracular practices are sometimes modeled on the saga accounts.
