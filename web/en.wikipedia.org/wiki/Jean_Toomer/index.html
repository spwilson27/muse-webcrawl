Jean Toomer (born Nathan Pinchback Toomer; December 26, 1894 – March 30, 1967) was an American poet and novelist commonly associated with the Harlem Renaissance, though he resisted that label, and with modernism. His reputation rests on his book Cane (1923), a modernist mosaic of prose, poetry and drama portraying Black life in the rural South and urban North, now regarded as one of the masterpieces of American modernism.

Life

Toomer was born in Washington, D.C., grandson of P. B. S. Pinchback, the first Black governor of a U.S. state (Louisiana). Of mixed ancestry and able to pass for white, he attended several universities without graduating. In 1921 he worked briefly as a school principal in Sparta, Georgia; the encounter with Southern Black folk culture inspired the writing of Cane, published in 1923 with an introduction by Waldo Frank.

Later years

Though hailed by critics, Cane sold poorly, and Toomer distanced himself from being classified as a "Negro writer", insisting he was simply American. He became a follower of the mystic George Gurdjieff, led Gurdjieff groups, experimented with utopian communities, and later joined the Quakers. He published little of lasting note after Cane and fell into obscurity.

Revival

A 1969 reissue of Cane restored Toomer's reputation; Alice Walker and others praised its influence. Its stream-of-consciousness lyrics, such as "Georgia Dusk" and "Song of the Son", and its fragmented form influenced later Black writers, including Zora Neale Hurston's generation and beyond.
