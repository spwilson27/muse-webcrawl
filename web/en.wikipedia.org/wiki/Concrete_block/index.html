A concrete block, also called a concrete masonry unit (CMU), cinder block, or breeze block, is a large rectangular brick-like unit used in building construction. Blocks are made from cast concrete — usually Portland cement and aggregate — formed in moulds and cured, and are laid in courses with mortar to build walls.

Description

Most concrete blocks have one or more hollow cavities, and their sides may be cast smooth or with a decorative design. The voids reduce weight and material use, improve insulation, and can be filled with concrete and reinforcing steel to form strong, reinforced walls. In the United States, the most common nominal size is 16 inches long by 8 inches high by 8 inches deep (the "8-inch block"); units are also made in 4-, 6-, 10-, and 12-inch widths. Blocks made with cinder (coal ash) aggregate are strictly "cinder blocks", a term still common in the US, while "breeze block" is used in Britain — where it also refers to decorative screen blocks popular in mid-20th-century architecture.

History

Concrete blocks date to the 19th century; Harmon Palmer patented a block-making machine in the United States in 1900, and by the early 20th century mechanised production made blocks a cheap, fire-resistant, and durable material for foundations, factories, and houses. Their low cost and speed of construction drove widespread adoption after the Second World War.

Uses and properties

Concrete blocks are used for load-bearing and non-load-bearing walls, foundations, retaining walls, and partitions. They offer good compressive strength, fire resistance, and thermal mass, though modest thermal insulation unless cores are filled with insulation. Walls may be reinforced vertically and horizontally to resist wind and earthquake loads. Finishes include stucco, paint, or exposed architectural block with split-face or ground-face textures.
