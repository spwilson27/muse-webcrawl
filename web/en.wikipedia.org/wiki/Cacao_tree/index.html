Cacao tree

Theobroma cacao (cacao tree or cocoa tree) is a small (6–12 m (20–39 ft) tall) evergreen tree in the family Malvaceae. Its seeds, cocoa beans, are used to make chocolate liquor, cocoa solids, cocoa butter and chocolate. Native to the tropics of the Americas, the largest producers are now in West Africa; the Ivory Coast is the world's largest producer of cocoa beans, followed by Ghana.

Description

Unusually, the tree's small pink flowers and large pods grow directly on the trunk and older branches (cauliflory); pollination is carried out by tiny midges. The fruit, a cacao pod, is ovoid, 15–30 cm long, ripening yellow to orange, and contains 20 to 60 seeds ("beans") embedded in a sweet mucilaginous pulp. After harvest the beans are fermented and dried, processes essential to developing chocolate flavour and reducing bitterness and astringency.

History

Cacao was domesticated in the Amazon basin and cultivated by pre-Columbian cultures including the Olmec, Maya and Aztec, who used the beans for a bitter beverage (xocolātl) and as currency. Introduced to Europe by the Spanish in the 16th century, sweetened chocolate became a luxury drink before 19th-century innovations — the cocoa press of van Houten and solid eating chocolate — made it a mass product.

Cultivation and issues

Cacao grows only in hot, humid climates within roughly 20° of the equator, usually under shade canopy. Production is dominated by smallholder farms; the industry faces persistent criticism over child labour in West Africa, deforestation and low farm incomes, alongside major crop threats such as witches' broom, frosty pod rot and swollen shoot virus.
