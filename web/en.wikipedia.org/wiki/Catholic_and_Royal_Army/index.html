The Catholic and Royal Army (French: Armée catholique et royale) was the principal royalist army of the War in the Vendée during the French Revolution. Raised from the peasant insurgents of the Vendée region in 1793, it fought the armies of the French Republic in defence of the monarchy and the Catholic Church.

Origins

The uprising began in March 1793, provoked by the Republic's conscription decree, its persecution of refractory clergy, and the execution of Louis XVI. Peasant bands chose local leaders — the wagoner Jacques Cathelineau, the gamekeeper Jean-Nicolas Stofflet, and young nobles such as Henri de La Rochejaquelein and Charles de Bonchamps — and in May 1793 united their forces as the Catholic and Royal Army. Its soldiers wore the Sacred Heart of Jesus as a badge and fought under white flags bearing royalist and religious emblems.

Campaigns

The army won striking early victories, capturing Thouars, Saumur and Angers in spring 1793 and briefly fielding tens of thousands of men. Cathelineau, elected generalissimo, was mortally wounded at Nantes in June. After defeat at Cholet in October 1793, the army crossed the Loire in the Virée de Galerne, attempting to seize a port and link with British support, but was destroyed at Le Mans and Savenay in December. Guerrilla warfare continued under Stofflet and Charette until their capture and execution in 1796; further royalist risings occurred in 1799 and 1815.

Legacy

The brutal republican repression of the Vendée, including the "infernal columns" of General Turreau, made the army a lasting symbol of counter-revolutionary resistance in French memory and a subject of continuing historical debate about the character and scale of the repression.
