Apostrophe

’

The apostrophe (’ or ') is a punctuation mark, and sometimes a diacritical mark, in languages that use the Latin alphabet and some other alphabets. In English, the apostrophe is used for three main purposes: marking the omission of one or more letters (contractions, as in don't for do not), marking the possessive case of nouns (as in the cat's whiskers), and, in some conventions, indicating plurals of certain letters and symbols.

History

The apostrophe entered English printing in the 16th century, borrowed from French usage, initially to mark elided letters. Possessive use developed during the 17th and 18th centuries, and the modern distinction between singular possessive (cat's) and plural possessive (cats') became standard in the 19th century.

Possessive apostrophes

Standard English usage places the apostrophe before the s for singular possessives and after the s for regular plural possessives. Its misuse — the so-called "greengrocer's apostrophe," as in apple's for a simple plural — is a common subject of usage commentary.

In other languages and computing

Apostrophes mark elision in French and Italian, glottal sounds in transliterations of Arabic and Hebrew, and letters in Ukrainian and Hawaiian (the ʻokina). In the Unicode standard, the typographic apostrophe is U+2019 (right single quotation mark), distinct from the ASCII apostrophe U+0027, which doubles as a typewriter quote and a prime-like character in computing.
