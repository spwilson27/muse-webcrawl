Cinema of Sweden

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A still-style image of a chess game with Death on a rocky beach, after The Seventh Seal]
A still-style image of a chess game with Death on a rocky beach, after The Seventh Seal

The cinema of Sweden is internationally renowned out of proportion to the country's size, above all for Ingmar Bergman — one of the most influential filmmakers in history — and for the silent-era classics of Victor Sjöström and Mauritz Stiller, who took Swedish film's naturalist style to Hollywood. Swedish cinema sustains a strong public-funding model through the Swedish Film Institute (founded 1963), and its contemporary exports range from the Millennium thrillers to arthouse prizewinners.

Golden ages

The Svenska Bio/Svensk Filmindustri era (1917–1924) produced Sjöström's The Phantom Carriage (1921) and Stiller's films with the young Greta Garbo, establishing landscape-driven psychological drama. After the war, Bergman's run — Smiles of a Summer Night (1955), The Seventh Seal and Wild Strawberries (1957), Persona (1966), Fanny and Alexander (1982) — plus Arne Sucksdorff's documentaries and Alf Sjöberg made Sweden synonymous with serious cinema; the Swedish Film Institute's quality-support system then industrialised the auteur model.

Modern industry

Sweden maintains high per-capita production and cinema attendance, a robust regional-fund system (Film i Väst 'Trollywood'), and global successes in popular genres: Let the Right One In (2008), the Millennium films and their Hollywood remake, Ruben Östlund's Palme d'Or winners The Square (2017) and Triangle of Sadness (2022), and the worldwide noir wave that Swedish television fed.
