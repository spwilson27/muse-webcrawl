Sortition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sortition is the selection of public officials or decision-makers by lottery (random sampling), used in Athenian democracy and some Italian republics, and revived in modern citizens' assemblies. Athens allotted most magistrates, the Council of 500 and jurors, using devices such as the kleroterion; election was reserved for posts requiring expertise, like generals. Supporters then and now argue that lot prevents faction, campaigning and the capture of office by wealth, and produces a descriptively representative body — a “portrait in miniature” of the people.

Modern sortition is used chiefly for advisory or deliberative bodies: Ireland's citizens' assemblies (2012–2018), the British Columbia Citizens' Assembly on electoral reform (2004), deliberative polls, and citizens' juries in planning and ethics. Political theorists propose stronger roles — an allotted second chamber, for example — while critics object that randomly chosen citizens lack accountability and expertise, and that lotteries cannot confer the mandate elections provide.

History

Venice combined lot and election in choosing its doge in an elaborate multi-stage procedure designed to frustrate faction. Jury selection by lot in common-law countries is the most familiar surviving use. The modern revival dates from the 1970s–80s (citizens' juries in Germany and the US) and has grown with dissatisfaction about electoral politics.
