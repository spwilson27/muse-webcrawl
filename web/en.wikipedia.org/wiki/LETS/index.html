Local exchange trading system

A local exchange trading system (also local employment and trading system or local exchange and trading system, generally abbreviated LETS) is a locally initiated, democratically organised, not-for-profit community enterprise that provides a community information service and record of transactions for its members, who exchange goods and services using a locally created currency. LETS are a form of complementary currency and mutual credit system.

How a LETS works

Members list offers and wants in a directory. When one member provides a service to another, the transaction is recorded centrally in the scheme's own unit of account — often pegged in value to the national currency or to an hour of labour — with the buyer's account debited and the seller's credited. No physical currency need exist, and, unlike barter, no direct reciprocity is required: credits earned from one member can be spent with any other. Balances can go negative as a commitment to reciprocate the community in the future; typically no interest is charged on either credit or debit balances.

History

The modern LETS model was developed by Michael Linton in the Comox Valley, British Columbia, Canada, in 1983, amid a local economic downturn following the closure of an air-force base. The idea spread rapidly: by the 1990s there were hundreds of schemes in the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, France (where they are called SEL), Germany (Tauschringe) and elsewhere. Numbers declined from their peak as the novelty faded and administration proved burdensome, but schemes persist, and the mutual-credit concept has influenced timebanking and digital community-currency platforms.

Criticism and evaluation

Supporters credit LETS with building social capital, valuing unpaid and informal work, and keeping economic activity local during cash shortages. Critics note small scale, uneven coverage of skilled trades, difficulties taxing transactions, and the free-rider problem of members who accumulate debits and leave. Academic studies generally find modest economic but significant social benefits for participants.
