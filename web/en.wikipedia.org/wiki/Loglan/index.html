Loglan is a constructed language originally designed by James Cooke Brown (1921–2000) beginning in 1955. Its name derives from "logical language". Brown created Loglan as a test of the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, the idea that the structure of a language affects how its speakers think. If the hypothesis held, speakers of a language based on predicate logic would think more logically.

History

Brown described the project in a 1960 article in Scientific American. The language was developed at the University of Florida, and Brown founded the Loglan Institute to promote it. The definitive description appeared in his book Loglan 1 in 1975. In the late 1980s a schism in the community led some supporters to develop a derivative language, Lojban, beginning in 1987 under the Logical Language Group, after disputes over copyright and control of the vocabulary.

Features

Loglan's grammar is based on predicate logic: every sentence can be analysed as arguments applied to predicates. Its phonology and morphology were designed so that words are unambiguous and so that sentences have exactly one parse. The basic vocabulary ("predicates") consists of short words of the form CCVCV or CVCCV built from roots drawn from the world's most widely spoken languages, chosen for recognisability. Loglan has no definite or indefinite articles, and grammatical structure words ("little words") indicate case-like relations between arguments and predicates.

Influence

Robert A. Heinlein mentioned a language based on the same principles in his novels The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress (1966) and The Number of the Beast (1980). Loglan and its offshoot Lojban remain among the best-known "logical languages" (loglangs) in the constructed-language community.
