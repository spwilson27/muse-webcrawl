Oar

An oar is a long implement with a flat blade at one end, used to propel a boat through the water by leverage. The rower holds the handle (grip), the shaft passes through an oarlock or rests against a thole pin on the boat's gunwale — which acts as a fulcrum — and the blade is pulled through the water. Oars are distinguished from paddles in that they are supported by the boat rather than held freely in both hands.

Parts and types

An oar consists of the grip, the shaft (or loom), the sleeve and button that bear against the oarlock, and the blade, which may be flat, spoon-shaped, or cleaver ("hatchet") shaped in modern sport rowing. In sweep rowing each rower handles one long oar; in sculling each rower uses two shorter oars. Ancient and historical craft — from Egyptian river boats to Greek triremes and Viking longships — were propelled by banks of oars, and steering oars mounted on the quarter preceded the invention of the rudder.

Mechanics

Because the oarlock is the fulcrum and the rower's hands apply force between the fulcrum and the handle's end, the oar acts as a second-class lever against the water: the blade's resistance converts the pulling force into thrust transmitted to the boat at the oarlock. The rowing stroke divides into the catch, drive, finish, and recovery.
