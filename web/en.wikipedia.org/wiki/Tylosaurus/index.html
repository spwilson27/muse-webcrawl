Tylosaurus

Tylosaurus is a genus of mosasaur, a large predatory marine reptile of the Late Cretaceous period (about 86 to 75 million years ago). Among the largest mosasaurs, the biggest species reached lengths of roughly 12–14 metres (39–46 ft). It is characterised by a distinctive cylindrical, bony rostrum (snout tip) that gives the genus its name, meaning "knob lizard".

Discovery

The first specimens were collected from the chalk deposits of Kansas in the 1860s and 1870s during the "Bone Wars" era; Edward Drinker Cope described early material, and Othniel Charles Marsh erected the name Tylosaurus in 1872. The famous "fish within a lizard" specimen described by Cope involved a misidentification later corrected. Fossils are abundant in the Western Interior Seaway deposits of North America, with additional finds in Europe and New Zealand.

Palaeobiology

Tylosaurus was an apex predator feeding on fish, sharks, smaller mosasaurs, plesiosaurs, flightless diving birds such as Hesperornis and turtles; stomach contents preserved in fossils confirm this varied diet. The toothless bony rostrum may have been used to ram or stun prey, though this remains debated. Like other mosasaurs it swam with lateral undulation of a crescent-fluked tail and gave birth to live young at sea.

Species

Recognised species include T. proriger, T. nepaeolicus, T. pembinensis, T. kansasensis and the large T. bernardi. Tylosaurus is the state marine fossil of Kansas.
