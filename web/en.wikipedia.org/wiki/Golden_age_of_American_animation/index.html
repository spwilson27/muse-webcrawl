The golden age of American animation was a period in the history of American animation that began with the popularization of sound cartoons in 1928 and gradually declined through the 1960s as theatrical short subjects gave way to television animation. During this era, animated cartoons became a staple of cinema programs and American studios led the world in the art form.

Disney and the rise of the feature film

Walt Disney's Steamboat Willie (1928) synchronized sound with animation and made Mickey Mouse a star. Disney followed with the Silly Symphonies, pioneering Technicolor, and in 1937 released Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs, the first cel-animated feature film, whose success established animation as a serious cinematic medium. The studio's early features — Pinocchio, Fantasia, Dumbo and Bambi — remain canonical.

Competition

Fleischer Studios answered with Betty Boop and Popeye; Warner Bros.' Termite Terrace unit created Bugs Bunny, Daffy Duck and Porky Pig under directors such as Tex Avery, Chuck Jones and Bob Clampett; MGM fielded Tom and Jerry and Avery's Droopy shorts. In the 1950s UPA introduced a stylized, limited-animation aesthetic exemplified by Mr. Magoo and Gerald McBoing-Boing.

Decline

Rising costs, the Paramount Decrees' restructuring of film distribution, and the rise of television eroded the theatrical shorts market. Studios closed their cartoon units through the 1950s and 1960s, and production shifted to cheaper TV animation led by Hanna-Barbera, ending the golden age by about 1969.
