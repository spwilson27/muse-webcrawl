Third law of thermodynamics

The third law of thermodynamics states that the entropy of a closed system at thermodynamic equilibrium approaches a constant value when its temperature approaches absolute zero. This constant value cannot depend on other parameters; for a perfect crystal it is zero.

Nernst heat theorem

Walther Nernst formulated the theorem in 1905–1906: as temperature approaches 0 K, entropy changes in any isothermal process approach zero. Max Planck's stronger form sets the entropy of a perfect crystal at absolute zero to exactly zero, fixing an absolute scale for entropy measurements.

Consequences

The law implies the unattainability of absolute zero: no finite sequence of processes can cool a system to 0 K, though temperatures within microkelvin and nanokelvin ranges are achieved in cryogenics and laser cooling. Heat capacities of all substances must also vanish as T → 0, and thermal expansion coefficients go to zero.

Relation to other laws

Together with the first and second laws and the zeroth law, the third law completes the classical statement of thermodynamics, enabling absolute entropy tables used in chemistry.
