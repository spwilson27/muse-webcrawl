Big Five personality traits

The Big Five personality traits, also known as the five-factor model (FFM) or by the mnemonic OCEAN, are a taxonomy of five broad dimensions of human personality: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism. The model emerged from factor-analytic studies of trait language and questionnaire data beginning in the mid-twentieth century and consolidated by researchers including Lewis Goldberg, Robert McCrae and Paul Costa in the 1980s–1990s. It is the most widely used framework in personality psychology research.

Development of the model

The approach descends from the lexical hypothesis — that important individual differences become encoded in language — pursued by Allport and Odbert (1936), who catalogued some 4,500 trait terms, and by Raymond Cattell, who reduced these to 16 factors. Independent lines of work by Tupes and Christal (1961) and later Goldberg repeatedly recovered five robust factors. McCrae and Costa developed the NEO inventories to measure them, identifying six facets under each dimension. The Big Five structure has been replicated across many cultures and languages, though not uniformly in small-scale societies.

Findings and criticism

Twin studies estimate the heritability of the traits at roughly 40–60%. Scores show moderate rank-order stability in adulthood and predict outcomes including job performance (conscientiousness), well-being and mental health (neuroticism), and longevity. Critics argue the model is descriptive rather than explanatory, omits traits such as honesty–humility (added in the rival HEXACO model), and derives factors partly from the structure of trait vocabularies rather than human nature itself. Concerns have also been raised about its use in high-stakes decisions such as employment screening.
