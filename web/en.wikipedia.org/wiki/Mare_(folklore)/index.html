Mare (folklore)

For other uses, see Mare (disambiguation).

A mare (Old English: mære, Old Dutch: mare, Proto-Slavic: *mara) is a malicious goblin or spirit in Germanic and Slavic folklore which rides on people's chests while they sleep, bringing on nightmares. The word "nightmare" itself derives from this creature's name, though its original meaning was not a bad dream in general but specifically the sensation of a heavy weight pressing down on the sleeper.

Etymology

The word "mare" comes from the Old English feminine noun mære, which is related to Old Norse mara, Old High German mara, and Dutch mare. The Slavic languages preserve similar forms, such as Polish zmora and Czech můra. The term is thought to derive from the Proto-Indo-European root *mer-, meaning "to rub away" or "to harm".

Folklore

According to folklore, the mare would visit people in their sleep, sitting upon their chests and causing bad dreams and a sense of suffocation. Victims would awaken exhausted, sometimes gasping for breath. This phenomenon is now associated with sleep paralysis and sleep apnea. The mare was also said to ride horses at night, leaving them exhausted and covered in sweat by morning, and to tangle the manes of horses into so-called "mare's knots".

In Swedish folklore, the mare is called maran; in Norwegian and Danish, mare; in German, Mahr or Nachtmahr. The mare was typically described as a woman, sometimes one cursed, or a creature that could transform into animals such as a cat, dog, or horse.

Protection

Various remedies were used against the mare: placing a pair of shoes with the toes pointing outward near the bed, hanging a "mare stone" (a flint with a natural hole, also called a hag stone) above the bed or stable, or placing a broom under the pillow. In some traditions, reciting a specific rhyme or prayers could ward off the creature.

In popular culture

The mare has appeared in numerous works of art, most famously in Henry Fuseli's 1781 painting The Nightmare, which depicts a demonic creature crouching on a sleeping woman's chest. The goblin-like figure in the painting is often taken to represent the mare of folklore.
