Ergot

Ergot (Claviceps purpurea) is a fungus that grows on rye and related plants, producing alkaloids that cause the disease ergotism in humans and livestock.

Ergot is a group of fungi of the genus Claviceps, the best known of which is Claviceps purpurea. The fungus parasitises the flowers of grasses and cereals, especially rye, replacing the grain with a dark, hardened mass of mycelium called a sclerotium — the "ergot" itself, named for its resemblance to a cock's spur (French argot). When flour made from contaminated grain is eaten, the ergot alkaloids it contains cause ergotism, historically known as St Anthony's fire.

Life cycle

The fungus infects a flowering grass head through spores carried by wind or insects. It grows in the ovary, first producing a sugary "honeydew" rich in conidia that attracts insects and spreads the infection, then hardening into a purple-black sclerotium that survives the winter in soil or among harvested grain. In spring the sclerotia germinate, producing small mushroom-like stromata that release ascospores to begin the cycle again.

Effects on humans and animals

Ergot alkaloids, including ergotamine and the lysergic acid derivatives, are powerful vasoconstrictors and act on serotonin, dopamine and adrenergic receptors. Ergotism takes two main forms: gangrenous, in which loss of blood supply causes burning pain and gangrene of the limbs, and convulsive, marked by hallucinations, spasms and seizures. Epidemics devastated medieval Europe, killing tens of thousands; victims often sought relief at shrines of St Anthony, giving the disease its popular name. Poisoning from a contaminated batch of grain is still occasionally reported, and ergoty feed remains a hazard to livestock, causing abortion and gangrene.

Historical speculations

Ergot poisoning has been proposed, controversially, as an explanation for episodes of mass hysteria and for some of the behaviour attributed to witchcraft, including in connection with the Salem witch trials of 1692–93, though historians dispute the hypothesis. What is certain is that ergot's pharmacology has long been exploited in folk medicine, where midwives used it to hasten labour.

Medical uses

Purified ergot alkaloids became important drugs. Ergotamine is used against migraine; ergometrine (ergonovine) controls postpartum haemorrhage; and bromocriptine, a synthetic derivative, treats Parkinson's disease and prolactinoma. In 1938 Albert Hofmann, working on lysergic acid derivatives from ergot at Sandoz, synthesised LSD, whose psychoactive properties he discovered in 1943.
