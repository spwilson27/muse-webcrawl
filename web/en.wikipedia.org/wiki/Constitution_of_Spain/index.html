The Spanish Constitution of 1978

The Spanish Constitution (Spanish: Constitución Española) is the supreme law of the Kingdom of Spain. It was enacted after Spain's transition to democracy following the death of Francisco Franco, approved by the Cortes Generales on 31 October 1978 and ratified by referendum on 6 December 1978 — now celebrated as Constitution Day — before being signed by King Juan Carlos I and entering into force on 29 December 1978. It is the culmination of the Spanish transition to democracy and one of the few constitutions of its era achieved by broad consensus among former regime figures and former opposition alike.

Drafting

The text was prepared by a seven-member committee of the Congress of Deputies — remembered as the Padres de la Constitución ("Fathers of the Constitution") — including figures from the governing centrists, socialists, communists, Catalan nationalists and the right (among them Gabriel Cisneros, José Pedro Pérez-Llorca, Miguel Herrero de Miñón, Miquel Roca, Manuel Fraga, Gregorio Peces-Barba and Jordi Solé Tura).

Contents

The Constitution declares Spain a social and democratic state under the rule of law, a parliamentary monarchy with the King as head of state and the Cortes Generales (Congress of Deputies and Senate) as legislature. A preamble and 169 articles cover: fundamental rights and duties (including abolition of the death penalty except in wartime military law, later fully abolished in 1995); the Crown; government and administration; the judiciary, headed in constitutional matters by the Constitutional Court; and the territorial organisation of the state. Article 2 famously proclaims "the indissoluble unity of the Spanish Nation" while recognising the right to autonomy of "the nationalities and regions" — the basis of the State of Autonomies, under which 17 autonomous communities (and two autonomous cities) were created.

Amendments and debates

The Constitution has been amended only twice: in 1992, to allow EU citizens to stand in municipal elections (Maastricht Treaty), and in 2011, to constitutionalise budget-stability rules during the euro crisis. Recurring debates concern reform of the Senate, the succession rules of the Crown, and the territorial model, most sharply during the Catalan independence crisis and the unilateral referendum and declaration of 2017, after which the government invoked Article 155.
