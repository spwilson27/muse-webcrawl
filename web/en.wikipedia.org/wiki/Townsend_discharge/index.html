Townsend discharge

The Townsend discharge or Townsend avalanche is a gas ionisation process where free electrons are accelerated by an electric field, collide with gas molecules, and consequently free additional electrons. Those electrons are in turn accelerated and free additional electrons. The result is an avalanche multiplication that permits electrical conduction through the gas. It requires a source of free electrons and a significant electric field; without both, the phenomenon does not occur.

Description

The discharge is named after John Sealy Townsend, who discovered the fundamental ionisation mechanism in his work between about 1897 and 1901 at the Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge. The basic setup is a planar parallel plate ionisation chamber filled with a gas that can be ionised by incident radiation, such as ultraviolet light or X-rays, illuminating the cathode.

When a potential difference is applied between the electrodes, a field is created in the gas: free electrons drift toward the anode and ions toward the cathode. If the field is strong enough, electrons gain sufficient energy between collisions to ionise gas molecules on impact, liberating further electrons. Each generation of electrons doubles (approximately) the charge arriving at the anode.

Townsend coefficients

The multiplication is described by the first Townsend coefficient, α, defined as the number of ionising collisions made by an electron per unit distance travelled. If n0 electrons leave the cathode, the number arriving at the anode after travelling a distance d is given by n = n0 eαd.

The second Townsend coefficient, γ, accounts for secondary electron emission at the cathode caused by positive ions striking it. When the combined action of the two coefficients is large enough, the discharge becomes self-sustaining; the threshold condition γ(eαd − 1) = 1 marks the transition to breakdown, described by Paschen's law.

Applications

The Townsend avalanche is the working principle of gaseous ionisation detectors such as the Geiger–Müller tube and proportional counters, used to detect ionising radiation. It also underlies gas discharge lamps, spark gaps, and the early stages of lightning leader formation. At higher multiplications, space charge distorts the field and the avalanche transitions into a streamer, as described by the Raether–Meek criterion.
