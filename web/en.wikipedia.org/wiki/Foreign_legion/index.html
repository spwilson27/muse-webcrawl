Foreign legion

A foreign legion is a military unit composed of volunteers from foreign countries who enlist to serve in the armed forces of a nation other than their own. The term is most famously associated with the French Foreign Legion (Légion étrangère), founded in 1831, but similar formations have existed in many countries and eras, from the Roman Empire's auxiliary troops to modern units in Spain, Ukraine, and elsewhere.

History

The concept of foreigners serving under arms for another state is ancient. Greek mercenaries served Egyptian pharaohs, Carthage relied heavily on foreign troops, and Rome recruited auxiliaries from across and beyond its frontiers. In the early modern period, Swiss, German, Irish, and Scottish regiments served in the armies of France, Spain, and other powers. The modern foreign legion as a distinct institution emerged in the 19th century, when European states sought manpower for colonial campaigns while limiting the political cost of casualties among their own citizens.

The French Foreign Legion

King Louis Philippe created the French Foreign Legion by royal ordinance on 10 March 1831, drawing in part on foreign regiments disbanded after the Bourbon Restoration. Its first major deployment was to Algeria during the French conquest. Legionnaires traditionally enlist under a declared identity and may apply for French citizenship after three years of service, or immediately if wounded in action — a provision known as Français par le sang versé ("French by spilled blood"). The Legion's motto is Legio Patria Nostra ("The Legion is our Fatherland"), and its regimental song is "Le Boudin". Its headquarters is at Aubagne, near Marseille, and its best-known units include the 2nd Foreign Parachute Regiment (2e REP) and the 2nd Foreign Infantry Regiment (2e REI).

Other foreign legions

The Spanish Legion (Legión Española), founded in 1920 and modelled on the French unit, was originally called the Tercio de Extranjeros. During the Russo-Ukrainian War, Ukraine established the International Legion of Territorial Defence of Ukraine in February 2022 to accept foreign volunteers. Other historical examples include the British German Legion of the Crimean War era, the International Brigades of the Spanish Civil War (volunteer units rather than state legions in the strict sense), and various foreign volunteer formations of the Second World War.

Recruitment and service

Foreign legions typically recruit volunteers regardless of nationality, subject to medical, physical, and background checks. Service is usually for a fixed initial term — five years in the French Legion — and recruits serve under the command structure and discipline of the host army. Historically, legions attracted adventurers, exiles, and men seeking a fresh start, which contributed to their romanticised image in literature and film.

In popular culture

Foreign legions, particularly the French, have been depicted in novels such as P. C. Wren's Beau Geste (1924) and in numerous films, cementing the image of the legionnaire in a white kepi in the North African desert.
