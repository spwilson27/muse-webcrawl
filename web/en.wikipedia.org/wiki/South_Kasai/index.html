South Kasai
French: Sud-Kasaï
Capital: Bakwanga (Mbuji-Mayi)
Existed: 1960–1962
Leader: Albert Kalonji

South Kasai (French: Sud-Kasaï) was a short-lived secessionist state in the south-central region of the Republic of the Congo (now the Democratic Republic of the Congo). It declared its autonomy on 8 August 1960, during the Congo Crisis, and existed until its reintegration into the Congolese state in 1962. Its territory roughly corresponds to the present-day Kasaï-Oriental province and parts of neighbouring provinces, with its capital at Bakwanga (now Mbuji-Mayi), the centre of the region's diamond mining industry.

Background

South Kasai was the homeland of the Baluba people. Ethnic tensions between Baluba and the rival Lulua, sharpened by competition over colonial administrative posts, turned violent in 1959. Following Congolese independence on 30 June 1960 and the mutiny of the Force Publique, Baluba leader Albert Kalonji withdrew his faction of the Mouvement National Congolais to Kasai and proclaimed the "Mining State of South Kasai", with himself as head of government and later, controversially, as king (Mulopwe) of the Baluba.

History

The secession was sustained by revenues from the Société minière de Bakwanga diamond operations and attracted tacit Belgian support. In August–September 1960, Congolese national army (ANC) troops sent by Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba invaded South Kasai, committing massacres of Baluba civilians — events that contributed to the international crisis that led to Lumumba's dismissal. South Kasai forces, reorganized as a gendarmerie, resisted, and Kalonji's regime persisted after Lumumba's fall. In December 1961 Kalonji was arrested by central government forces; he escaped in September 1962, but his government collapsed and South Kasai was formally reintegrated before the end of 1962.
