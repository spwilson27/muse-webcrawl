Free trade area

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A free trade area is a region in which a group of countries has signed a free trade agreement eliminating tariffs, quotas, and other trade barriers on most goods and services traded among them. Unlike a customs union, a free trade area's members keep their own independent external tariffs and trade policies toward non-members. Because external tariffs differ, free trade agreements use rules of origin to prevent goods from entering the area through the member with the lowest external tariff — a practice known as trade deflection.

Characteristics

Free trade areas are one stage in the theory of economic integration associated with Béla Balassa, which runs from preferential trading area, to free trade area, to customs union, to common market, to economic and monetary union. An FTA removes internal barriers but requires customs checks at internal borders to enforce rules of origin — product-specific requirements that a good be wholly obtained or substantially transformed within the area to qualify for duty-free treatment.

Major examples

The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA, 1994) between Canada, Mexico, and the United States was replaced in 2020 by the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA). Other major areas include the ASEAN Free Trade Area (AFTA), the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA, operational from 2021 and the largest FTA by number of member states), the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) in Asia-Pacific, and the European Free Trade Association (EFTA), whose members also participate in the European Economic Area. Many bilateral FTAs criss-cross these regional blocs in what economists call a "spaghetti bowl" of overlapping agreements.

Economics

Following Jacob Viner's analysis in The Customs Union Issue (1950), an FTA can create trade — shifting production to more efficient member producers — or divert trade from more efficient outside suppliers to less efficient member ones because of the tariff preference. Modern agreements typically extend beyond tariffs to services, investment, intellectual property, government procurement, and regulatory cooperation, which complicates welfare analysis. Under WTO rules, free trade agreements are permitted as an exception to the most-favoured-nation principle under GATT Article XXIV, provided they cover substantially all trade among the parties.
