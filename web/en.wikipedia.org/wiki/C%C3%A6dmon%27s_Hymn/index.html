Cædmon's Hymn

Cædmon's Hymn is a short Old English poem attributed to Cædmon, a supposedly illiterate and unmusical cowherd who, according to the Northumbrian monk Bede, was miraculously empowered to sing in honour of God the Creator. The poem was composed between 658 and 680 and is the oldest recorded Old English poem, and arguably the oldest recorded Germanic poem.

Text and transmission

The nine-line hymn praises God as creator of heaven and earth. It survives in Northumbrian and West Saxon versions in 21 manuscripts, often copied into manuscripts of Bede's Ecclesiastical History of the English People, which tells Cædmon's story. The opening lines, in Old English, run: "Nū sculon herigean heofonrīces weard, / meotodes meahte and his mōdgeþanc…" ("Now we must praise the Guardian of the heavenly kingdom, the might of the Creator and his purpose…").

Significance

The hymn is a key witness to early English Christian poetry, blending traditional Germanic alliterative verse and formulaic diction with Christian subject matter. Its manuscript tradition has been extensively studied, notably in Daniel Paul O'Donnell's 2005 edition of all witnesses.
