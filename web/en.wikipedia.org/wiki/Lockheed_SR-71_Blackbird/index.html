The Lockheed SR-71 "Blackbird" is a long-range, high-altitude, Mach 3+ strategic reconnaissance aircraft developed and manufactured by Lockheed's Skunk Works under Kelly Johnson. It was operated by the United States Air Force and NASA from 1966 until the late 1990s and remains the fastest air-breathing manned aircraft ever flown operationally.

Design

The SR-71 was developed from the CIA's A-12 Oxcart and the YF-12 interceptor prototype. Its airframe was built overwhelmingly of titanium to withstand skin temperatures exceeding 230 °C (450 °F) generated by aerodynamic heating at Mach 3.2. Two Pratt & Whitney J58 engines — effectively turbojet-ramjet hybrids — used a special low-volatility fuel, JP-7, and the aircraft was refuelled in flight by KC-135Q tankers. Panels were deliberately loose-fitting on the ground and expanded to seal at operating temperature; the aircraft famously leaked fuel while parked. Speed and altitude were its defence: no SR-71 was ever shot down, the standard evasion being simply to accelerate away from missiles.

Operational history

Thirty-two aircraft were built; twelve were lost in accidents, none to enemy action. SR-71s flew reconnaissance missions over Vietnam, Libya (notably during and after the 1986 El Dorado Canyon operation), the Middle East and the periphery of the Soviet Union, operating from bases including Beale AFB, Kadena and RAF Mildenhall. On 28 July 1976 an SR-71 set the world airspeed record of 2,193.2 mph (3,529.6 km/h) and an altitude record of 85,069 ft, marks that stand for its class. The type was retired by the USAF in 1989, briefly reactivated in the mid-1990s, and finally retired in 1998; NASA flew its aircraft until 1999. Most surviving Blackbirds are on museum display.

Specifications

  • Crew: 2 (pilot and reconnaissance systems officer)
  • Length: 107 ft 5 in (32.74 m)
  • Powerplant: 2 × Pratt & Whitney J58 afterburning turbojets, 32,500 lbf each
  • Maximum speed: Mach 3.2+ (over 2,200 mph)
  • Service ceiling: 85,000 ft (25,900 m)
  • Range: about 2,900 nautical miles unrefuelled
