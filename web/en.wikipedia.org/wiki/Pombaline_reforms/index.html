The Pombaline reforms were a sweeping programme of social, economic, educational, and administrative modernisation carried out in Portugal and its empire between 1750 and 1777 under Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo, later Marquis of Pombal, chief minister to King Joseph I. They are a leading example of enlightened absolutism — and of its authoritarian methods.

The Marquis of Pombal, architect of the reforms.

Reconstruction of Lisbon

After the catastrophic 1755 Lisbon earthquake, Pombal directed the rebuilding of the capital's Baixa district on a grid plan with earthquake-resistant timber-frame construction (the gaiola pombalina) — the "Pombaline style" — one of the earliest examples of modern urban planning and seismic engineering.

Economy and empire

The reforms created chartered trading companies for Brazil (Grão-Pará and Maranhão; Pernambuco), abolished the legal distinction between Portuguese settlers and indigenous Brazilians, expelled the Jesuits from Portugal and its colonies in 1759 (seizing their missions and schools), weakened the Inquisition, and curtailed the privileges of the high nobility — brutally, in the Távora affair of 1758–59. Slavery was abolished in mainland Portugal in 1761 and restrictions placed on the import of enslaved Africans into Portugal, though slavery continued in the colonies.

Education and administration

Pombal secularised education, reformed the University of Coimbra (1772), created state primary schools and the Royal College of Nobles, modernised the army with the help of the Count of Lippe, and centralised tax and colonial administration.

Legacy

When Joseph I died in 1777, Pombal fell from power and many reforms were reversed in the Viradeira reaction under Queen Maria I. Historians remain divided between viewing Pombal as a farsighted moderniser and as a ruthless despot.
