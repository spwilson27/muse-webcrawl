Indian classical music

Indian classical music is the classical music of the Indian subcontinent, one of the world's oldest continuous musical traditions. It has two major branches: Hindustani music, practised in northern India, Pakistan, Nepal and Bangladesh, and Carnatic music, practised in southern India and Sri Lanka. Both systems are based on the twin foundations of raga (melodic framework) and tala (rhythmic cycle), and both place improvisation at their centre.

History

The roots of Indian classical music lie in the chanting of the Samaveda and in treatises such as Bharata's Natya Shastra (c. 200 BCE–200 CE), which systematised music, dance and drama. Medieval texts like Sarngadeva's Sangita Ratnakara (13th century) codified theory. From roughly the 13th–16th centuries, the tradition diverged into northern and southern systems: the north absorbed Persian and Central Asian influences under the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughals — associated with figures such as Amir Khusrau and Tansen — while the south developed along devotional lines shaped by the bhakti movement and composers such as Purandara Dasa and the Carnatic trinity (Tyagaraja, Muthuswami Dikshitar and Syama Sastri).

Concepts

A raga is not merely a scale but a melodic personality with characteristic ascending and descending patterns (aroha/avaroha), emphasized notes (vadi/samvadi) and phrases; each raga is traditionally associated with a time of day, season and mood (rasa). Tala organises rhythm into cycles of beats — such as tintal (16 beats) in Hindustani music and adi tala (8 beats) in Carnatic music — marked by claps, waves and improvisation by percussionists on tabla, pakhawaj or mridangam. Performances typically begin with a slow, unmetered alap exploring the raga, followed by composed pieces with increasing rhythmic intensity.

Instruments and transmission

Principal instruments include the sitar, sarod, bansuri and harmonium in the north; the veena, violin and nadaswaram in the south; and the voice, regarded as the primary instrument in both traditions. Music is traditionally transmitted orally from teacher (guru) to disciple (shishya) in the guru–shishya parampara, though notation systems, universities and digital media now play larger roles. Leading performers such as Ravi Shankar, Ali Akbar Khan, M. S. Subbulakshmi and Bhimsen Joshi brought the music to global audiences in the 20th century.
