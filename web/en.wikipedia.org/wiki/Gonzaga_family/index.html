House of Gonzaga

The House of Gonzaga was an Italian princely family that ruled Mantua in Lombardy from 1328 to 1708, first as capitani del popolo, then as marquesses and finally as dukes. They also ruled Monferrato in Piedmont from 1536 to 1708, and the cadet branches of Guastalla, Nevers, Rethel and Mayenne in France. The Gonzaga were among the most prominent patrons of Renaissance art and learning, transforming Mantua into a leading cultural centre.

History

The family rose to power when Luigi (Ludovico) Gonzaga overthrew the Bonacolsi in Mantua in 1328 with the support of Cangrande I della Scala of Verona. His descendants consolidated the family's position as hereditary imperial vicars. Gianfrancesco I obtained the title of Marquis of Mantua in 1433 from Emperor Sigismund. Under Ludovico III (ruled 1444–1478) the court flourished: Leon Battista Alberti designed churches for Mantua, and Andrea Mantegna served as court painter from 1460, producing the celebrated frescoes of the Camera dei Sposi.

Federico II Gonzaga became the first Duke of Mantua in 1530 by grant of Emperor Charles V, and acquired Monferrato by marriage in 1536. Vincenzo I (1587–1612) employed Claudio Monteverdi, whose opera L'Orfeo premiered at the Mantuan court in 1607, and the painter Peter Paul Rubens. The dukes amassed a famous art collection, much of which was sold to Charles I of England in the 1620s.

Decline

The main line died out in 1627, provoking the War of the Mantuan Succession (1628–1631), during which Imperial troops sacked Mantua in 1630. The French cadet line of Gonzaga-Nevers succeeded but never recovered the duchy's former standing. The last duke, Ferdinando Carlo, allied with France in the War of the Spanish Succession; he was deposed in 1708 and Mantua passed to the Austrian Habsburgs.

Saints and cardinals

The family produced Saint Aloysius Gonzaga (1568–1591), patron saint of youth and students, as well as numerous cardinals and bishops.
