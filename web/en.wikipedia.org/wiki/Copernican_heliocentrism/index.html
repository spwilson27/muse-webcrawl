Copernican heliocentrism

Copernican heliocentrism is the astronomical model developed by Nicolaus Copernicus and published in his De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres) in 1543, the year of his death. It placed the Sun, rather than Earth, near the center of the universe, with Earth and the other planets revolving around it in circular orbits modified by epicycles.

The model

Copernicus retained much of the apparatus of Ptolemaic astronomy — uniform circular motion and epicycles — but his arrangement explained retrograde motion of the planets as a natural consequence of Earth's own annual motion, ordered the planets by their orbital periods, and fixed the relative distances of the planets unambiguously for the first time. A short early exposition, the Commentariolus, circulated in manuscript from about 1514.

Reception

Initially used by many astronomers as a calculating device — the astronomer Andreas Osiander's unsigned preface presented it as a mathematical hypothesis — the model gradually came to be accepted as physical truth through the work of Kepler, who replaced circles with ellipses, and Galileo, whose telescopic observations undermined the Ptolemaic system. Opposition from religious authorities, crystallized in the Galileo affair of 1633, made heliocentrism a symbol of the Scientific Revolution.

Legacy

The displacement of Earth from the center is often generalized as the "Copernican principle": the idea that observers on Earth occupy no privileged position in the universe, a notion with wide influence in cosmology and philosophy.
