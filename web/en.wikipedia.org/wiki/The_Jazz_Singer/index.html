The Jazz Singer

Directed by: Alan Crosland
Starring: Al Jolson, May McAvoy, Warner Oland
Released: October 6, 1927
Studio: Warner Bros.

The Jazz Singer is a 1927 American part-talkie musical drama film directed by Alan Crosland and produced by Warner Bros. Starring Al Jolson, it is famous as the first feature-length motion picture with synchronized dialogue sequences, whose commercial success effectively ended the silent film era. It was produced with Vitaphone, a sound-on-disc system, and is based on Samson Raphaelson's 1925 play of the same name, itself adapted from his short story "The Day of Atonement."

Plot

Jakie Rabinowitz, the son of a Jewish cantor on New York's Lower East Side, defies his father's wish that he follow five generations of cantors and instead performs jazz in blackface under the name Jack Robin. On the eve of his Broadway debut — which coincides with Yom Kippur — his father falls ill, and Jakie must choose between stardom and singing Kol Nidre in his father's place. He chooses the synagogue; his father dies reconciled, and the film closes with Jakie back on stage, singing "Mammy" to his mother in the audience.

Dialogue and impact

Most of the film is silent with a synchronized score, but Jolson's musical numbers and roughly two minutes of improvised-sounding speech — including the iconic line "Wait a minute, wait a minute. You ain't heard nothin' yet!" — electrified audiences. Its success led Warner Bros. and the other studios to convert rapidly to sound production. The film also remains controversial for its use of blackface, a mainstream theatrical convention of the time that modern scholars examine as part of its themes of assimilation. It was selected for the National Film Registry in 1996 and remade several times, most notably in 1980 with Neil Diamond.
