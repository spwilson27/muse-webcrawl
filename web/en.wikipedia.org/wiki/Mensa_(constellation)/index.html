Mensa (constellation)

Mensa, the Table, lies close to the south celestial pole.

Mensa is a constellation in the Southern Celestial Hemisphere near the south celestial pole, one of fourteen constellations drawn up in the 18th century by the French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille. Its name is Latin for table, though it originally commemorated Table Mountain in South Africa — the flat-topped mountain overlooking Cape Town, from whose observatory Lacaille surveyed the southern skies.

History

Lacaille created the constellation in 1756 from stars he catalogued during his expedition to the Cape of Good Hope in 1750–1754. He originally named it Mons Mensae, the only constellation named after a terrestrial feature. John Herschel suggested shortening the name to Mensa in 1844, and the International Astronomical Union adopted the four-letter form among the 88 modern constellations.

Characteristics

Mensa is the faintest of the 88 modern constellations: it contains no star brighter than fifth magnitude, and its brightest member, Alpha Mensae, has an apparent magnitude of only 5.09. Alpha Mensae is a Sun-like yellow dwarf roughly 33 light-years away, and in 2020 was found to host an exoplanet. The constellation is circumpolar as seen from most of the southern hemisphere and is never visible from mid-northern latitudes.

Notable features

Mensa's chief claim to fame is that part of the Large Magellanic Cloud, a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way, straddles its border with neighbouring Dorado. The constellation also contains the globular cluster NGC 1987 and several distant galaxies, but no Messier objects and no bright deep-sky targets for amateur observers.
