Chymosin

Chymosin, also known as rennin (not to be confused with renin or rennet in general), is a protease enzyme (EC 3.4.23.4) found in the stomachs of ruminant animals, where it is responsible for curdling milk. It is an aspartic endopeptidase belonging to the MEROPS A01 family, and is a key enzyme used commercially in cheese production.

Biological role

Chymosin is produced by the chief cells of the abomasum (the fourth stomach chamber) in newborn ruminants such as calves, lambs and kids. It is secreted as an inactive precursor, prochymosin, which is activated by the acidic environment of the stomach. The enzyme specifically cleaves the Phe105–Met106 peptide bond of κ-casein, destabilising the casein micelles in milk and causing it to clot. This curdling slows the passage of milk through the stomach, allowing the young animal to absorb nutrients more efficiently.

Structure and mechanism

Bovine chymosin is a single-chain protein of 323 amino acid residues with a molecular weight of about 35.6 kDa. Like other aspartic proteases, it uses two aspartate residues in its active site to catalyse peptide-bond hydrolysis. It exists in several genetic variants, of which chymosin A and chymosin B are the best known; chymosin A has somewhat higher specific activity.

Industrial use

Chymosin is the principal active component of traditional animal rennet, extracted from the stomachs of slaughtered calves. Because supply is limited and demand from the cheese industry is large, most chymosin used today is fermentation-produced chymosin (FPC): the bovine chymosin gene is expressed in microorganisms such as the fungus Aspergillus niger var. awamori or the yeast Kluyveromyces marxianus var. lactis. Chymosin was one of the first food enzymes produced by recombinant DNA technology, approved for food use in the United States in 1990. Camel chymosin, cloned from the Arabian camel, is used for camel milk and some bovine milk applications.
