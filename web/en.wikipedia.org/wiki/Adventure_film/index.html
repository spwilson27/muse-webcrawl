Adventure film

Adventure films are a genre of film whose plots feature elements such as quests, journeys, treasure hunts, exploration, and exotic locales. Unlike action films, which focus primarily on violence and physical feats, adventure films emphasize the journey itself, the discovery of new worlds, and the triumph of the protagonist over the forces of nature or villainy. The genre is one of the oldest in cinema, with roots in the swashbuckler pictures of the silent era.

History

The adventure film emerged in the early 20th century, drawing on popular literature such as the novels of Alexandre Dumas, Robert Louis Stevenson, and H. Rider Haggard. Douglas Fairbanks became the genre's first great star with films such as The Mark of Zorro (1920) and The Thief of Bagdad (1924). Errol Flynn later defined the swashbuckler with Captain Blood (1935) and The Adventures of Robin Hood (1938).

After a lull in the 1960s and 1970s, George Lucas and Steven Spielberg revived the genre with Star Wars (1977) and Raiders of the Lost Ark (1981), the latter introducing the archaeologist hero Indiana Jones. Modern adventure films often blend with fantasy, science fiction, and action, as in the Pirates of the Caribbean series.

Characteristics

Common features include a charismatic hero, a clear quest or goal, exotic or fantastical settings, traps and perils, and a tone of excitement and escapism. Adventure films frequently take place in historical settings, jungles, deserts, or lost worlds, and often involve the search for lost treasure or a mythical artifact.

Subgenres

Subgenres include the swashbuckler film, the pirate film, the survival film, the treasure hunt film, and the disaster film. Many adventure films overlap with fantasy, as in The Lord of the Rings trilogy.
