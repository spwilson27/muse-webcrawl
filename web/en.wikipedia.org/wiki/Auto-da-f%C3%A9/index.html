Auto-da-fé

An auto-da-fé (from Portuguese auto da fé, meaning "act of faith") was a public ceremony held under the Spanish, Portuguese, and Mexican Inquisitions in which the sentences of those accused of heresy were announced and punishments carried out. The ceremony typically involved a solemn procession of the condemned, a Mass, speeches, and the pronouncement of sentences ranging from penance and imprisonment to relaxation to the secular arm — that is, execution, usually by burning at the stake.

History

Autos-da-fé became a hallmark of the Spanish Inquisition after its founding in 1478. The first great auto in Seville in 1481 targeted converted Jews (conversos) accused of secretly practicing Judaism. Elaborate public autos continued through the 16th and 17th centuries, including the famous Logroño trial of Basque "witches" in 1610. The last auto in Spain took place in Valencia in 1826, when the schoolteacher Cayetano Ripoll was hanged as the final victim of the Inquisition, which was abolished in 1834.

Ceremony

The accused wore distinctive garments called sanbenitos painted with flames or crosses depending on their sentence, and walked in procession to a public square where scaffolding had been erected. Sentences were read aloud over many hours; those reconciled received penances, while the obdurate and relapsed were handed to secular authorities for punishment outside the ceremony site.

In culture

The auto-da-fé has become an enduring symbol of religious persecution, depicted by artists such as Francisco Goya and referenced in literature from Voltaire's Candide to Edgar Allan Poe's "The Pit and the Pendulum".
