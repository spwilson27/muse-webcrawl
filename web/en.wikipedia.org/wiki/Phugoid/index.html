Phugoid

A phugoid oscillation: a long-period exchange between altitude and airspeed.

In aviation, a phugoid or fugoid is an aircraft motion in which the vehicle pitches up and climbs, decelerating as it climbs, then pitches down and descends, accelerating as it descends, in a long, slow oscillation. It is one of the basic flight dynamics modes of an aircraft, paired with the short-period pitching oscillation mode.

Characteristics

The phugoid is a lightly damped (and in some aircraft slightly unstable) oscillation with a period typically between 20 and 100 seconds, depending on the aircraft and its speed. During the cycle the angle of attack stays nearly constant while airspeed and altitude trade off against each other: kinetic and potential energy are exchanged as the aeroplane repeatedly climbs and dives.

The mode was analysed by the British engineer Frederick Lanchester, who coined the term from the Greek word for "flight" (as in "fleeing"). In most aircraft the phugoid can be controlled simply with the elevator trim, and in training aircraft it can be excited by deliberately disturbing the pitch and releasing the controls.

Accidents

Uncontrolled phugoid oscillations have been a factor in several accidents and incidents. In the crash of Japan Airlines Flight 123 in 1985, the crew controlled the damaged aircraft for over 30 minutes largely through a phugoid cycle using engine thrust. United Airlines Flight 232 (1989) similarly relied on differential thrust after a total hydraulic failure, with the phugoid behaviour of the DC-10 central to the crew's difficulties in landing.
