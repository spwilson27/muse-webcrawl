Xun (instrument)

Xun
Chinese塤 (simplified: 埙)
ClassificationAerophone, vessel flute
MaterialFired clay or pottery (also stone, bone historically)
OriginChina, Neolithic (examples up to ~7,000 years old)

The xun is a globular vessel flute from China, one of the oldest surviving Chinese musical instruments. Made of fired clay, it is an egg-shaped, hollow wind instrument with a blowing hole on top and finger holes on the sides, producing a soft, mellow, somewhat melancholic tone. It is the Chinese counterpart of the ocarina-type vessel flutes found worldwide, but the xun's history is exceptionally long: the earliest examples, made of stone and bone, date back some 7,000 years.

History and use

Early xun had a single finger hole; over the Zhou and Han dynasties the number of holes grew, and by the later tradition standardised at five finger holes (three front, two back). The xun was one of the principal instruments of Chinese court ritual music (yayue), where its earthy sound — the element earth in the bayin classification of instruments by material — symbolised humility and harmony. The instrument was traditionally paired in poetry with the chi (a transverse bamboo flute), in the expression of brotherly concord: "the elder plays the xun, the younger the chi." After declining in the court repertoire, the xun was revived in the 20th century by performers and archaeologists, and modern makers produce large concert xun with expanded ranges.
