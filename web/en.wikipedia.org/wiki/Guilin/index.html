Guilin (桂林, "forest of sweet osmanthus") is a prefecture-level city in the northeast of China's Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, situated on the west bank of the Li River. It is one of China's most famous tourist destinations, celebrated for a landscape of steep limestone karst towers, winding rivers, and rice paddies that has inspired Chinese poetry and painting for over a thousand years. The saying "Guilin's scenery is the finest under heaven" (桂林山水甲天下) dates to the Song dynasty.

History

The area was first incorporated into China under the Qin dynasty, when the Lingqu Canal — one of the oldest canals in the world — was dug nearby in 214 BC to link the Yangtze and Pearl River systems. Guilin became the provincial capital of Guangxi under the Ming and Qing dynasties and remained so until 1914. It served as a wartime refuge and cultural centre during the Second Sino-Japanese War before being largely destroyed in 1944.

Geography and sights

Karst peaks such as Elephant Trunk Hill, Reed Flute Cave, Fubo Hill, and the Solitary Beauty Peak rise within the city itself. The classic tourist route is a cruise down the Li River to Yangshuo, passing scenery depicted on the 20-yuan banknote. The surrounding region includes the Longji rice terraces and the karst country around Xingping. Guilin's climate is humid subtropical, and the city is a major transport hub by rail, air, and expressway for northern Guangxi.
