Inuktitut (ᐃᓄᒃᑎᑐᑦ, "like the Inuit") is an Inuit language spoken across the Canadian Arctic — in Nunavut, Nunavik in northern Quebec, the Inuvialuit region of the Northwest Territories, and Nunatsiavut in northern Labrador. Closely related varieties form a dialect continuum stretching from Alaska (Iñupiaq) through Canada to Greenland (Kalaallisut). Inuktitut is one of the official languages of Nunavut and has official status in the Northwest Territories.

Features

Inuktitut is a polysynthetic language: complex words are built by chaining suffixes onto a root, so that a single word can express what requires a whole sentence in English — a famous example is tusaatsiarunnanngittualuujunga ("I can't hear very well"). The language has three vowels (i, u, a, each short or long) and around fifteen consonants, and it distinguishes singular, dual, and plural number. Nouns inflect for eight cases, and verbs encode subject and object agreement as well as mood.

Writing systems

In most of Nunavut and Nunavik, Inuktitut is written with a syllabic script adapted in the nineteenth century from Cree syllabics (itself developed from James Evans' system for Ojibwe) by missionaries including John Horden and E. A. Watkins, and standardized for Inuit use in the 1970s. In the western Arctic and Labrador, Latin-based alphabets are used. Debates over standardization and a unified orthography (such as the proposed Inuktut standard "Inuit Nunangat" script reforms) continue among language authorities.

Status and revitalization

Although Inuktitut is among the most widely spoken Indigenous languages in Canada — with a majority of Inuit in Nunavut reporting it as a mother tongue — its use has declined in some regions under pressure from English. Language laws such as Nunavut's Inuit Language Protection Act (2008) and the federal Indigenous Languages Act (2019) support its use in government, education, and media; Inuktitut-language media include the broadcaster Inuit Broadcasting Corporation and APTN programming. The language features in cultural life including Inuit music and film, such as the 2001 feature Atanarjuat: The Fast Runner, filmed entirely in Inuktitut.
