Conjoined twins, popularly known as Siamese twins after the famous pair Chang and Eng Bunker of Siam, are identical twins whose bodies are joined in utero. The phenomenon occurs in roughly 1 in 50,000 to 200,000 births, more often in females. Two competing theories explain it: incomplete fission of a single fertilised egg about 13–15 days after conception, or fusion of two initially separate embryos.

Classification

Twins are classified by the site of union, with the suffix -pagus (Greek for "that which is fixed"): thoracopagus (chest — the most common, often sharing a heart), omphalopagus (abdomen), craniopagus (skull), ischiopagus (pelvis), pygopagus (sacrum, back to back), and parapagus (side by side). Asymmetric cases, in which one twin is incomplete and dependent (a "parasitic" twin), also occur.

Prognosis and surgery

Many conjoined twins are stillborn or die shortly after birth; overall survival is roughly a quarter to a third of cases. Surgical separation, first achieved successfully in various forms from the 17th century onward, is possible when the twins do not share a heart or critical brain structures, but is among the most complex operations in paediatric surgery and raises profound ethical questions where separation may kill or disable one twin.

Famous cases

Chang and Eng Bunker (1811–1874) emigrated to the United States, became farmers in North Carolina, married sisters and fathered 21 children between them. Modern well-known cases include Ladan and Laleh Bijani, who died during separation surgery in 2003, and Abigail and Brittany Hensel, dicephalic twins who live unseparated.
