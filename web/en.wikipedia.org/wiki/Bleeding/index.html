Bleeding, also called haemorrhage, is blood escaping from the circulatory system from damaged blood vessels. Bleeding can occur internally, where blood leaks into body cavities, or externally, through a natural opening or a break in the skin. Loss of a large fraction of the blood volume — hypovolemia — can lead to shock, organ failure, and death; bleeding is the leading preventable cause of death after trauma.

Classification

Bleeding is classified by its source: arterial bleeding is bright red and spurts with the pulse; venous bleeding is darker and flows steadily; capillary bleeding oozes slowly. Clinicians also grade haemorrhage by the proportion of blood volume lost, from class I (under 15 percent, usually well tolerated) to class IV (over 40 percent, immediately life-threatening). Internal bleeding may be concealed, revealed only by pain, swelling, falling blood pressure, or blood appearing in vomit, urine, or stool.

Causes

Causes include traumatic injury; ruptured aneurysms; gastrointestinal ulceration; obstetric complications such as postpartum haemorrhage; surgical complications; and disorders of clotting including haemophilia, thrombocytopenia, vitamin K deficiency, and anticoagulant medication.

Management

First aid for external bleeding is direct pressure on the wound, elevation where practical, pressure dressings, and, for severe limb bleeding, a tourniquet or haemostatic dressing. Hospital care focuses on stopping the source — by surgery, endoscopy, or embolisation — while restoring blood volume with intravenous fluids and transfusion, guided by the principles of damage control resuscitation. Drugs such as tranexamic acid reduce mortality when given promptly after major trauma or childbirth.
