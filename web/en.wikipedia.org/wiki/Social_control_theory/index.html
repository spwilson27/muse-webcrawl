Social control theory

In criminology, social control theory explains delinquency and crime by the strength of an individual's bonds to conventional society: rather than asking why people offend, it asks why most people conform. The best-known version is Travis Hirschi's social bond theory, set out in Causes of Delinquency (1969).

The four bonds

Hirschi proposed four elements of the social bond: attachment — emotional ties to parents, teachers and peers; commitment — investment in conventional goals such as education and career, which offending would jeopardise; involvement — time spent in conventional activities, leaving less opportunity for delinquency; and belief — acceptance of the moral validity of society's rules. Weak bonds free individuals to deviate.

Development and criticism

Earlier control theories include Walter Reckless's containment theory and the work of Albert Reiss. Hirschi later, with Michael Gottfredson, restated the idea as the general theory of crime (1990), attributing offending to low self-control established in childhood. Critics question the theory's handling of serious and white-collar crime and the causal direction between weak bonds and delinquency, but the theory remains influential in research and policy on families, schools and communities.
