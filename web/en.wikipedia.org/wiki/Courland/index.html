Courland (Latvian: Kurzeme; German: Kurland) is one of the historical and cultural regions of Latvia, in the western part of the country, between the Baltic Sea, the Gulf of Riga and the borders with Lithuania and the other Latvian regions of Semigallia, Vidzeme and Latgale. It takes its name from the Curonians, a Baltic tribe who inhabited the coast in the early Middle Ages.

History

In the 13th century Courland was conquered by German crusading orders and incorporated into Livonia, and its native population was gradually enserfed under a Baltic German landowning nobility. When the Livonian Order collapsed in 1561, its last master, Gotthard Kettler, became the first Duke of Courland and Semigallia, a vassal duchy of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth with its capital at Jelgava (Mitau). Remarkably, the small duchy under Duke Jacob Kettler built a merchant fleet and founded short-lived colonies on Tobago in the Caribbean and on the Gambia River in West Africa. Courland was annexed by the Russian Empire in 1795 in the Third Partition of Poland, becoming the Courland Governorate.

Modern Courland

After Latvian independence (1918) Courland formed part of the new republic; it was the last part of Latvia held by German forces in the "Courland Pocket" of 1944–45. Today the region is known for the port cities of Liepāja and Ventspils, the picturesque old town and brick bridge of Kuldīga, wide sandy beaches, and the Livonian Coast (Līvānu krasts) in the north, home of the Livonian people, a Finnic minority with its own language and culture.
