Seville

Seville (Spanish: Sevilla) is the capital and largest city of the autonomous community of Andalusia and of its own province, lying on the Guadalquivir river in southern Spain. With about 680,000 inhabitants in the city and 1.5 million in the metropolitan area, it is Spain's fourth-largest city. Its old town contains three UNESCO World Heritage Sites — the Alcázar palace, the Cathedral (the largest Gothic cathedral in the world, with the Giralda bell tower, originally a minaret) and the Archive of the Indies — and it is famed for flamenco, Holy Week processions, the April Fair (Feria de Abril), and ferocious summer heat.

History

Roman Hispalis gave way to Visigothic and then Muslim rule: as Ishbiliya it flourished under the Abbadids and Almohads, who built the great mosque whose minaret survives as the Giralda. Ferdinand III of Castile captured Seville in 1248. After 1492 it held the monopoly of Spain's American trade through the Casa de Contratación (1503) — Magellan's expedition departed from here in 1519 — and the wealth of the Indies made it Golden Age Spain's great port and artistic capital (Velázquez, Murillo and Zurbarán were born or formed here). As the Guadalquivir silted, trade passed to Cádiz in the eighteenth century. In the Spanish Civil War Seville was seized early by Queipo de Llano and saw notorious repression.

The city

The Santa Cruz quarter — the old Jewish quarter of white lanes and patios — adjoins the cathedral and Alcázar. Across the river lies Triana, historic home of sailors, potters and flamenco. The Plaza de España, built for the 1929 exposition in the Maria Luisa Park, is the city's set-piece; Expo '92 modernised the island of La Cartuja. Seville's Holy Week, with its hooded penitents and candlelit processions, and the week-long Feria that follows, define its calendar.
