Stellar black hole

Artist's impression of Cygnus X-1.

A stellar black hole (or stellar-mass black hole) is a black hole formed by the gravitational collapse of a star. They have masses ranging from about 5 to several tens of solar masses. The process of collapse is observed as a supernova explosion or a gamma-ray burst. Stellar black holes are the most numerous class of black holes known, and the Milky Way alone is estimated to contain tens to hundreds of millions of them.

Formation

When a massive star (initially more than about 20 solar masses) exhausts its nuclear fuel, its core collapses. If the remnant core exceeds the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit — roughly 2–3 solar masses — no known pressure can support it: electron and neutron degeneracy pressure are overcome, and collapse continues past the event horizon to form a black hole. Some of the most massive stars may collapse directly to black holes with little or no supernova display, producing "failed supernovae."

Observations

Because black holes emit no light of their own, stellar black holes are detected through their effects on their surroundings, most often in X-ray binary systems where gas accreted from a companion star heats to millions of degrees and emits X-rays. The first widely accepted candidate, Cygnus X-1 (identified in 1971–72), contains a black hole of about 21 solar masses orbiting a blue supergiant. Other well-studied systems include V404 Cygni and the microquasar GRS 1915+105. The nearest known stellar black hole, in the Gaia BH1 system, lies about 1,560 light-years away and orbits a Sun-like star.

Since 2015, gravitational-wave observatories (LIGO and Virgo) have detected the mergers of stellar-mass black holes, beginning with GW150914 — the collision of black holes of roughly 36 and 29 solar masses — and have assembled a catalog of dozens of such binary black hole mergers, revealing black holes more massive than X-ray observations had suggested.
