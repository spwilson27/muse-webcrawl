Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil is a 1963 book by the philosopher Hannah Arendt recounting the trial in Jerusalem of Adolf Eichmann, the SS officer who organized much of the logistics of the Holocaust. Originally a five-part series for The New Yorker, the book became one of the most discussed works of the twentieth century for its subtitle concept — the "banality of evil" — and for its controversial treatment of the role of Jewish councils under Nazi rule.

Background

Eichmann had fled to Argentina after the war. In May 1960 Israeli agents abducted him in Buenos Aires and brought him to Israel for trial under a 1950 law on Nazi crimes. Arendt, a German-Jewish refugee and author of The Origins of Totalitarianism (1951), covered the 1961 trial for The New Yorker, expecting, she wrote, to confront a monster.

Argument

Instead, Arendt portrayed Eichmann as terrifyingly ordinary: a careerist, cliché-speaking bureaucrat whose evil flowed not from demonic hatred or sadism but from thoughtlessness — an inability to think from another's perspective, a surrender of judgment to procedure and orders. Eichmann insisted he had merely organized transports; the court found him a zealous perpetrator. Arendt nevertheless endorsed the verdict and the death sentence (Eichmann was hanged on 1 June 1962), arguing he had supported and carried out a policy of mass murder and that no member of the human race could be expected to share the earth with him.

The book also criticized the prosecution's theatricality, questioned aspects of the trial's legality (the kidnapping, retroactive law), and — most explosively — argued that Jewish community leadership, the Judenräte, by cooperating with Nazi administrative demands, had unwittingly facilitated the machinery of destruction. Arendt did not blame victims for the genocide itself, but critics read her irony as blaming the murdered for their fate.

Controversy and legacy

The book sparked a bitter public controversy, severing Arendt's friendships (notably with Hans Jonas and Gershom Scholem, who accused her of lacking love for the Jewish people). Later scholarship, drawing on the Argentine Sassen interviews, has shown Eichmann to have been a convinced, proud antisemite who lied at trial — complicating, though not wholly dissolving, Arendt's portrait of thoughtless bureaucracy; scholars such as Deborah Lipstadt and Bettina Stangneth argue Eichmann performed the role of the dull functionary. The phrase "banality of evil" has entered ordinary language, and the book remains a central text in discussions of bureaucracy, obedience, responsibility, and the moral psychology of perpetrators.
