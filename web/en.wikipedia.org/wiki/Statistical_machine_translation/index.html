Statistical machine translation

Statistical machine translation (SMT) is a machine translation paradigm where translations are generated on the basis of statistical models whose parameters are derived from the analysis of bilingual text corpora. It dominated machine translation from the 1990s until the rise of neural machine translation in the mid-2010s.

Statistical machine translation
Approaches: Word-based, phrase-based, syntax-based
Key models: IBM Models 1–5
Evaluation: BLEU

History

The approach was revived by IBM's Candide project (late 1980s–early 1990s) using ideas from speech recognition and Warren Weaver's 1949 memorandum. Phrase-based models, developed by Franz Josef Och and others, became standard, and open-source Moses toolkit spread SMT widely; Google Translate used SMT until switching to neural MT in 2016.

Method

SMT combines a translation model learned from aligned parallel corpora with a language model of the target language, searching for the most probable translation (the noisy channel model). Word alignment is commonly learned with the IBM models and GIZA++.

Advantages and limits

SMT needs no hand-written rules and adapts to domains with data, but produces disfluent output, struggles with distant language pairs, and requires large corpora.
