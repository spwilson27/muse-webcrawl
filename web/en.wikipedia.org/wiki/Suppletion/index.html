Suppletion

English go/went and good/better are classic examples of suppletion.

In linguistics and etymology, suppletion is the use of two (or more) phonetically unrelated roots for different forms of the same word — for example, when a verb's past tense comes from an entirely different root than its present tense. Frequent, everyday words are the most likely to be suppletive, because their forms are learned individually and resist the regularising pressure of analogy.

Examples

  • English go – went: the past tense derives from wend, originally the past of a different verb.
  • English be – am/is/are/was/were: a patchwork of forms from several Proto-Indo-European roots.
  • English good – better – best, and bad – worse – worst: comparative and superlative forms unrelated in sound to the positive.
  • French aller ("to go") combines forms from Latin ambulare, vadere, and a third root: je vais, nous allons, j'irai.
  • Irish and other Celtic languages show extensive suppletion in common verbs such as "to be" and "to go".

Terminology

The term was coined in the 19th century from Latin supplere, "to fill up" — a suppletive form "fills in" a gap in a paradigm left when a regular form was lost. Linguists distinguish strong suppletion, where the forms share no phonological material (go/went), from weak suppletion, where the forms are related but not derivable by regular rules (buy/bought). Suppletion is most common in paradigms of verbs meaning "be", "go", and "do", in pronouns, and in basic adjectives.
