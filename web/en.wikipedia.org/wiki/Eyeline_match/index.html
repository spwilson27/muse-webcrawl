Eyeline match

The eyeline match is a film editing technique associated with the continuity editing system. It is based on the premise that the audience will want to see what the character on-screen is looking at. An eyeline match begins with a character looking at something off-screen, followed by a cut to the object or person at which they are looking. For example, a man is looking off-screen to his left, and then the film cuts to a television that he is watching.

The shot following the cut should show the object from approximately the same angle and distance that the character sees it, which is why the succeeding shot is often close to a point-of-view shot. Eyeline matches are a fundamental tool for constructing spatial coherence in a scene and for guiding the viewer's attention and inferences about characters' thoughts and intentions.

Related techniques

Eyeline match is closely related to the shot/reverse shot pattern used in dialogue scenes and to the 180-degree rule, which keeps camera positions on one side of an imaginary axis so that characters' eyelines remain consistent across cuts. When an eyeline match is deliberately mismatched — as in some of the films of Yasujirō Ozu — it can create a subtle disorientation or a distinctive graphic rhythm. The Kuleshov effect demonstrates how viewers derive meaning from the juxtaposition of a neutral face with the object shown in the following shot.
