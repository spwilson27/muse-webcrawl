Eisenstein integer

In mathematics, the Eisenstein integers (named after Gotthold Eisenstein), occasionally also known as Eulerian integers, are the complex numbers of the form

z = a + bω,

where a and b are integers and ω = (−1 + √−3)/2 = e2πi/3 is a primitive (non-real) cube root of unity. The Eisenstein integers form a triangular lattice in the complex plane, in contrast with the Gaussian integers, which form a square lattice.

Properties

The Eisenstein integers form a commutative ring of algebraic integers in the algebraic number field ℚ(ω), the third cyclotomic field. They form a Euclidean domain with respect to the norm

N(a + bω) = a² − ab + b²,

and hence a unique factorisation domain. The units of the ring are the six sixth roots of unity: ±1, ±ω, ±ω². The prime elements of the ring are described on the companion page Eisenstein prime.

Applications

The ring of Eisenstein integers is used in classical proofs in algebraic number theory, for example in showing that a prime p ≡ 1 (mod 3) can be written as p = a² − ab + b², and in certain cases of Fermat's Last Theorem, such as the exponent 3 case, which can be handled by descent within the Eisenstein integers.
