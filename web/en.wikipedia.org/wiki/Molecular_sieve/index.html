A molecular sieve is a material with pores of uniform size that allow small molecules to enter while excluding larger ones, enabling separation of molecules by size and shape. The term is often applied to synthetic zeolites (crystalline aluminosilicates) whose pore diameters — commonly designated 3Å, 4Å, 5Å, and 13X — are precisely controlled.

Mechanism and materials

Zeolite frameworks contain cages and channels of molecular dimensions. Water and other polar molecules are adsorbed strongly inside the pores, so molecular sieves are powerful desiccants, capable of drying gases and solvents to very low water content and functioning at temperatures where silica gel and alumina are less effective.

Different cation forms of zeolite A and X give different effective pore sizes, allowing separation of molecules such as oxygen and nitrogen, or normal and branched hydrocarbons.

Applications

Molecular sieves are used to dry ethanol, refrigerants, natural gas, and air for oxygen concentrators; in pressure swing adsorption to produce oxygen and nitrogen; in petroleum refining for dewaxing and purification; and as supports and catalysts in petrochemical processing.

They are regenerated by heating or pressure reduction, releasing the adsorbed molecules for repeated cycles of use.
