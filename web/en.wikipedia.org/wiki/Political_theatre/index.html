Political theatre

Political theatre is theatre that comments on, protests against or seeks to change political and social conditions. Rather than entertaining alone, it aims to provoke thought and debate about power, injustice and public policy, often aligning itself with movements for social change.

Origins

Theatre has carried political content since the comedies of Aristophanes. In the modern sense the form crystallised in the early twentieth century with the agitprop troupes of the Russian Revolution and the work of Erwin Piscator in Weimar Germany, who pioneered documentary theatre using film, placards and statistics on stage.

Key practitioners

Bertolt Brecht developed epic theatre and the alienation effect (Verfremdungseffekt) to stop audiences identifying passively with characters and instead judge the social conditions shown. In Brazil, Augusto Boal created the Theatre of the Oppressed, turning spectators into "spect-actors". Other notable forms include the Living Newspaper of the US Federal Theatre Project, the plays of Dario Fo, British companies such as 7:84, and documentary or "verbatim" theatre drawn from interviews and transcripts.

Themes

Political theatre addresses war, class, race, gender, colonialism and state violence. Because it frequently challenges governments, it has often faced censorship or funding cuts, and some of its practitioners have been imprisoned or exiled. See also political satire.
