Moravia (Czech: Morava) is a historical region in the east of the Czech Republic, one of the three historical Czech lands together with Bohemia and Czech Silesia. It takes its name from the Morava River, which rises in the north-west of the region and flows south to join the Danube. The regional capital and largest city is Brno; other important cities include Ostrava (partly in Silesia), Olomouc, and Zlín.

History

In the 9th century Moravia was the core of Great Moravia, the first major West Slavic state, where the Byzantine missionaries Saints Cyril and Methodius introduced the Glagolitic script and Slavonic liturgy in 863. After Great Moravia fell to the Magyars around 907, Moravia was contested between Bohemia, Poland and Hungary before becoming a margraviate of the Bohemian Crown. From 1526 it passed with the Bohemian lands to the Habsburg monarchy. Within Austria-Hungary it was a separate crown land (Margraviate of Moravia) with its capital at Brno. In 1918 it became part of Czechoslovakia; since 1993 it has been part of the Czech Republic, though it no longer exists as an administrative unit.

Geography

Moravia is bounded by Bohemia to the west, Silesia to the north-east, Slovakia to the east, and Austria to the south. Its fertile lowlands along the Morava and Dyje rivers are among the Czech Republic's principal wine-growing areas, especially around Mikulov and Znojmo. The Moravian Karst north of Brno is noted for its caves and the Macocha Abyss.

Culture

Moravia has a distinct regional identity, with its own dialects, folk costumes, and wine traditions; many Moravians identify regionally in censuses. Gregor Mendel performed his foundational genetics experiments in Brno, and composer Leoš Janáček and writer Milan Kundera were Moravian. The Moravian Church (Unitas Fratrum), a Protestant denomination, traces its name to Moravian exiles who settled in Herrnhut, Saxony, in the 1720s.
