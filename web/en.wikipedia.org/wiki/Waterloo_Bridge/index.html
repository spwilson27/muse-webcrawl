Waterloo Bridge

Waterloo Bridge is a road and pedestrian traffic bridge crossing the River Thames in London, between Blackfriars Bridge and Hungerford Bridge. Its location at a bend in the river gives it celebrated views of Westminster and the City to the west and South Bank and the City to the east. The present bridge, designed by Sir Giles Gilbert Scott, was built during the Second World War and opened in 1945; it is Grade II* listed.

The first bridge

The original bridge, designed by John Rennie, was a nine-arch granite toll bridge opened in 1817 and named for the Allied victory at the Battle of Waterloo two years earlier. The artist John Constable painted it repeatedly. By the 1920s, scour around its piers — worsened by the demolition of old London Bridge changing the river's flow — caused serious subsidence, and it was closed in 1924, replaced temporarily and then demolished in 1934.

The Ladies' Bridge

Construction of the new five-span reinforced concrete bridge began as war broke out. Because much of the male workforce was conscripted, many of its builders were women, earning it the nickname "The Ladies' Bridge" — a fact only widely recognised decades later. It was the only Thames bridge in London damaged by German bombs during the war. Its Portland stone cladding is self-cleaning in London rain, a property Scott prized.

Assassination

On 7 September 1978 the Bulgarian dissident writer Georgi Markov was stabbed with a poisoned umbrella pellet — widely attributed to the Bulgarian secret service — while waiting for a bus on Waterloo Bridge; he died four days later. The bridge also stands beside Somerset House and the Royal National Theatre on the revived South Bank cultural quarter.
