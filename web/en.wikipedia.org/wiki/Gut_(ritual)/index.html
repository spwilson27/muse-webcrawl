TraditionKorean shamanism (Musok)

Gut (Korean: 굿) are the rites performed by Korean shamans (mudang), involving offerings and sacrifices to the gods, ancestors and spirits of Korean shamanism (Musok). Through song, dance, music and trance, the shaman seeks to mediate between the human and spirit worlds — to cure illness, expel misfortune, guide the souls of the dead, ensure a good catch or harvest, or bless a new home or business.

Structure

A gut unfolds as a sequence of segments called geori, each addressed to particular deities. It typically begins with purification and the invocation of the spirits, proceeds through offerings of food, money and cloth, entertainment of the gods with music and dance, divination or an oracle spoken by the possessed shaman, and ends with the sending-off of the spirits. At climactic moments the mudang may dance on the blades of straw cutters (jakdu-geori) to demonstrate spiritual power. Elaborate costumes are changed for each deity invoked, and the shaman is accompanied by musicians playing drums, gongs, flute and the hourglass drum (janggu).

Types

Gut vary by region and purpose. Village rites such as the byeolsin-gut of the east coast pray for communal prosperity and abundant fishing; jinogi-gut and related funerary rites guide the soul of the deceased to the afterlife; healing rites (byeong-gut) address illness attributed to spiritual disturbance. On Jeju Island the tradition has its own pantheon and mythology.

Status

Long suppressed or stigmatised as superstition, gut survived through the twentieth century and several regional forms are now designated Important Intangible Cultural Properties of South Korea, performed both as genuine rituals for clients and as cultural demonstrations.
