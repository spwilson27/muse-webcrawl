Sick building syndrome

Sick building syndrome (SBS) is a condition in which people in a building suffer from symptoms of illness or feel unwell for no apparent reason. The symptoms tend to increase in severity with the time people spend in the building, and improve over time or even disappear when people are away from it. The World Health Organization used the term in a 1983 report, and the syndrome has been associated principally with office work in sealed, air-conditioned buildings.

Symptoms

Reported symptoms include headaches, eye, nose, and throat irritation, dry cough, dry or itchy skin, dizziness and nausea, difficulty concentrating, and fatigue. In most cases no single illness or cause can be identified, distinguishing SBS from "building-related illness", in which symptoms — such as Legionnaires' disease or hypersensitivity pneumonitis — can be clinically diagnosed and traced to specific contaminants.

Causes

Investigations typically implicate inadequate ventilation as the main contributing factor, alongside indoor chemical contaminants from adhesives, carpeting, upholstery, and office equipment (volatile organic compounds, ozone from photocopiers), biological contaminants (mould, pollen, bacteria in stagnant water in humidifiers and drain pans), and outdoor pollutants drawn into intakes. The energy-conserving design of the 1970s, with sealed windows and reduced fresh-air supply, sharply increased reported cases. Psychological and organizational factors — job stress, low job satisfaction, lack of individual control over temperature and lighting — are also strongly associated with symptom reporting.

Prevention and remediation

Remedies include increasing ventilation rates and fresh-air distribution, removing or sealing pollutant sources, maintaining HVAC systems, keeping relative humidity between 40 and 60 per cent, and regular cleaning. Standards such as ASHRAE Standard 62.1 set minimum ventilation rates for acceptable indoor air quality. SBS has been linked to reduced productivity and increased absenteeism, and is of continuing concern in workplace health and building design.
