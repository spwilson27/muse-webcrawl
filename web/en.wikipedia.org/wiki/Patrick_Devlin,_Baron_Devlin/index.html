Patrick Devlin, Baron Devlin

Patrick Arthur Devlin, Baron Devlin, PC, FBA (25 November 1905 – 9 August 1992) was a British judge and legal philosopher. He served as a Lord of Appeal in Ordinary (law lord) and is best known outside the courtroom for his 1959 Maccabaean Lecture, The Enforcement of Morals, which set out the case for society's right to use the criminal law to uphold its shared morality and provoked H. L. A. Hart's famous rejoinder in the Hart–Devlin debate.

Devlin, one of the youngest High Court judges of his era, later a leading voice on law and morality.

Career

Devlin was called to the bar at Gray's Inn in 1929 and built a successful commercial practice. Appointed a High Court judge in 1948 at the age of 42, he presided over notable trials, including that of the alleged serial killer John Bodkin Adams in 1957, in which his handling of the case and his summing-up drew wide attention. He was promoted to the Court of Appeal in 1960 and to the House of Lords in 1961, retiring in 1964 to pursue public inquiries and writing; he chaired the 1959 commission of inquiry into the Nyasaland emergency, whose report criticised the colonial government's conduct.

The Hart–Devlin debate

Responding to the Wolfenden Report's recommendation that homosexual behaviour between consenting adults be decriminalized, Devlin argued that society may legitimately enforce its moral code because a shared morality is part of the cement of society, and that the suppression of vice is as much the law's business as the suppression of subversion. Hart, following John Stuart Mill, answered that using law to enforce morality as such is unjustified and that Devlin had confused the morality a society happens to hold with the conditions of its survival. The debate became a classic of jurisprudence and influenced decriminalization debates in Britain and the Commonwealth.
