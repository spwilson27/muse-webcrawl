Indigenous law

Indigenous law refers to the legal traditions, customs and systems of governance developed and maintained by Indigenous peoples, distinct from the state law that may govern Indigenous affairs. Indigenous legal orders — such as Anishinaabe, Māori (tikanga), Navajo (Diné) peacemaking and Inuit customary law — set out rights, responsibilities, dispute-resolution processes and relationships to land and community.

Recognition

Colonial legal systems historically suppressed or ignored Indigenous law, but many jurisdictions now give it some recognition: tribal courts exercise jurisdiction in the United States, Canada's courts recognise Aboriginal rights and title (as in Delgamuukw v British Columbia), New Zealand courts increasingly treat tikanga Māori as a source of law, and Bolivia and Ecuador have constitutionally recognised Indigenous jurisdictions. The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007) affirms Indigenous peoples' rights to maintain their juridical systems.

Study

Scholars distinguish "Indigenous law" (law generated within Indigenous societies) from "Aboriginal law" (the body of state law about Indigenous peoples). Revitalisation projects work with elders and knowledge-keepers to articulate and teach these traditions.
