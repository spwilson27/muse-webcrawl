Gable

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

TypeArchitectural element
LocationEnd wall of a building beneath a pitched roof
VariationsStepped/crow-stepped, Dutch, cross-gabled

A gable is the part of a wall that encloses the end of a pitched roof: the triangular portion of the wall at the end of a ridged roof, bounded by the two sloping edges of the roof above and the horizontal top of the wall below. The wall itself under the roof slopes is sometimes called the gable end, and a building with the gable facing the street is described as gable-fronted.

Design and construction

Gables may be formed by continuing the masonry of the end wall up to the roof line, or by lightweight timber framing and cladding. The roof may sit flush with the gable or overhang it; a substantial overhang is sometimes called a "gable ladder". The shape of the gable is determined by the roof type — simple pitched, hipped (which reduces the gable to a small triangle or eliminates it), gambrel or cross-gabled, where two gabled roofs intersect.

Decorative forms

Gables have often been treated decoratively. Northern European Renaissance architecture produced the Dutch gable, with curved or scrolled sides rising to a pediment, and the crow-stepped (or corbie-stepped) gable, whose edges ascend in steps — a signature of Scottish and Flemish building. In Gothic architecture the gable over a doorway or window, often steep and ornamented, became a prominent feature, while the term "gablet" denotes a small decorative gable used in tracery and on pinnacles.

Drawbacks

In hurricane and tornado regions, gable ends are a known weak point: wind pressure can push an inadequately braced gable wall inward or outward, leading to roof failure, and building codes in such areas commonly require gable-end bracing or hip roofs.
