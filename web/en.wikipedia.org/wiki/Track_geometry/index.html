Track geometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Track geometry is the three-dimensional geometry of railway tracks, including horizontal and vertical alignment, and the regular measurement and correction of deviations from the design. It is concerned with the position of the rails in space, which governs the safety, ride quality and speed of trains. Track geometry parameters are specified in design and maintained within tolerances that depend on the speed and importance of the line.

Parameters

  • Gauge — the distance between the inner faces of the two rail heads, measured below the rail head. On curves a small amount of gauge widening may be provided.
  • Cross level / cant (superelevation) — the difference in height between the two rails. On curves the outer rail is raised to counteract centrifugal acceleration; the difference between the applied cant and the equilibrium cant for a given speed is the cant deficiency (or excess).
  • Longitudinal level — the profile of each rail in the vertical plane, including "top" defects such as dips and humps.
  • Alignment — the position of the track in the horizontal plane, usually measured as the versine of a chord laid along the rail.
  • Twist — the rate of change of cross level over a short base (typically 3 m); excessive twist combined with wheel unloading can cause derailment.
  • Curvature — described by curve radius, with transition (spiral) curves easing the change from straight track, often combined with a cant gradient (ramp).

Measurement

Historically track was measured by hand with gauge templates, levels, string lines and theodolite surveys. Modern railways use dedicated track recording cars (also called track geometry cars), such as the British New Measurement Train or Amtrak's and Federal Railroad Administration's geometry cars in the United States, which measure geometry at line speed using lasers, inertial sensors and optical systems. In-service vehicles and axle-box accelerometers increasingly supplement dedicated measurement runs.

Maintenance

Geometry is restored by tamping, in which tamping machines lift the track, pack ballast under the sleepers (ties) to hold the corrected level and alignment, and by rail grinding, stoneblowing and ballast cleaning. Exceeding defined safety limits — immediate action limits on defects such as twist or wide gauge — triggers speed restrictions or line closure until repairs are made.

Relation to vehicle dynamics

Poor track geometry increases dynamic forces between wheel and rail, accelerating wear of both, worsening passenger comfort, and in extremes derailing trains. Vehicle–track interaction, including the hunting oscillation of wheelsets, is a central topic of railway engineering; track quality indices combine standard deviations of measured parameters into a single grade of track condition.
