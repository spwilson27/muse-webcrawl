The Murder Act 1752 (25 Geo. 2. c. 37), formally "An Act for better preventing the horrid Crime of Murder", was an Act of the Parliament of Great Britain intended to add "some further Terror and peculiar Mark of Infamy" to the punishment for murder. Although passed in the parliamentary session of 1751–52, it is conventionally dated 1752.

Provisions

The Act provided that convicted murderers were to be executed swiftly — normally two days after sentencing (three if the sentence fell on a Saturday) — rather than after the longer interval previously allowed. They were to be denied the benefit of a stay of execution for reprieve consideration, and in the days before execution were to be kept in solitary confinement and fed only bread and water.

Most famously, the Act required that the bodies of executed murderers should never be buried intact: the judge was to order the body either to be dissected by surgeons for anatomical study or to be hung in chains (gibbeted) at a public place. In practice most bodies were sent for public dissection, and the provision greatly increased the legal supply of cadavers available to anatomists, at a time when the only other lawful source was the few bodies granted under the earlier law of Henry VIII.

Repeal

The dissection provision was effectively superseded by the Anatomy Act 1832, which opened other sources of cadavers and ended the dissection of murderers; gibbeting was abolished in 1834. The last gibbeting in England had occurred in 1832.
