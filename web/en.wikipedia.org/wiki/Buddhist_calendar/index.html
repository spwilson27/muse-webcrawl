The Buddhist calendar is a set of lunisolar calendars primarily used in mainland Southeast Asia — Thailand, Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar and Sri Lanka — as well as in the Theravada Buddhist tradition more broadly. Its epoch is traditionally the death (parinirvana) of Gautama Buddha, dated to 543 BCE, so the Buddhist Era (BE) is 543 years ahead of the Common Era.

Structure

The calendar is lunisolar: months begin at the new moon and last 29 or 30 days, giving a year of 354 days. To keep pace with the solar year, an intercalary month or day is added periodically, following rules that vary between countries. In Thailand, the year is divided into 12 months, and the new year traditionally fell in April, at the festival of Songkran; Thailand now officially uses the Buddhist Era with the Gregorian calendar structure, so the Thai year begins on 1 January.

Regional variants

Myanmar uses the Burmese calendar, a lunisolar system with its own intercalation cycle; Cambodia and Laos follow similar systems. Sri Lanka uses the Buddhist calendar alongside the Gregorian calendar for religious observances. In each country the calendar fixes the dates of the Uposatha observance days, the full-moon festivals, and the three-month rains retreat (Vassa).

Festivals

The most important full-moon day is Vesak, in May, which commemorates the birth, enlightenment and death of the Buddha. Other major observances include Magha Puja (February), Asalha Puja (July, marking the first sermon) and the end of Vassa in October.
