Kawasaki disease is an acute febrile illness of childhood, first described by Japanese paediatrician Tomisaku Kawasaki in 1967, in which blood vessels throughout the body become inflamed. It primarily affects children under five and is the leading cause of acquired heart disease in children in developed countries. Its cause is unknown; genetic susceptibility — incidence is highest in children of Japanese and Korean ancestry — and an abnormal immune response to an infectious trigger are suspected.

Signs and symptoms

Classic features include fever lasting five days or more, bilateral non-exudative conjunctivitis, red cracked lips and "strawberry tongue", a polymorphous rash, swelling and redness of the hands and feet followed by peeling, and enlargement of cervical lymph nodes. Diagnosis is clinical; incomplete presentations are common in infants.

Complications and treatment

The most serious complication is coronary artery aneurysm, which develops in roughly a quarter of untreated children and can cause myocardial infarction, though it is usually preventable with timely treatment. Standard therapy is intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) given within ten days of fever onset, together with aspirin; corticosteroids or biologics may be added for high-risk or refractory cases. Long-term follow-up with echocardiography guides management.
