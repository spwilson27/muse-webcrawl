The Serapeum of Saqqara is an ancient Egyptian burial ground for the sacred Apis bulls, located at Saqqara, the necropolis of the ancient capital Memphis. The Apis bull was regarded as a living manifestation of the god Ptah, and at death was identified with Osiris, giving rise to the syncretic deity Serapis, from whom the Serapeum takes its name.

History

Bull burials at Saqqara began by the reign of Amenhotep III in the New Kingdom. Under Ramesses II, Prince Khaemweset developed the underground galleries known as the Lesser Vaults. The Greater Vaults, cut later, contain enormous sarcophagi of polished granite and basalt, some weighing up to 70 tonnes, in which the mummified bulls were interred with their grave goods. Burials continued into the Ptolemaic period.

Rediscovery

The site was rediscovered in 1850 by the French archaeologist Auguste Mariette, who followed the avenue of sphinxes described by Strabo and excavated the underground galleries. Mariette found one burial intact, that of a bull of the reign of Ramesses II, and recovered thousands of artefacts, stelae and votive objects, many now in the Louvre. The dated stelae from the Serapeum remain a key chronological source for ancient Egyptian history.
