Laager

A laager, also known as a wagon fort, is an encampment formed by a circle of wagons. The term comes from Afrikaans (from Dutch leger, "army" or "camp") and was used chiefly by the Boer trekkers of southern Africa, who formed their ox-wagons into a defensive ring — often with thorn bushes filling the gaps — to protect people and livestock from attack.

OriginSouth Africa; Afrikaans/Dutch leger
Used byVoortrekkers and Boer forces
PurposeDefensive encampment

History

The laager was the standard defensive tactic of the Voortrekkers during the Great Trek (1830s–1840s). Its most famous test came at the Battle of Blood River (1838), where about 470 trekkers under Andries Pretorius, laagered on a riverbank, defeated a much larger Zulu force. Laagers were used again by Boer commandos during the Anglo-Boer wars, though mobile warfare increasingly favored other tactics.

Figurative use

In South African English, "laager mentality" describes a defensive, inward-looking siege mentality — withdrawing into a protective circle against a perceived hostile world — a phrase often applied in political commentary.

Related formations

The laager is comparable to the Hussite Wagenburg, the wagon forts of American pioneers, and the Roman marching camp.
