The Kofun period (古墳時代, Kofun jidai) is a period in the history of Japan from about 300 to 538 AD (the date of the introduction of Buddhism), following the Yayoi period. The Kofun period and the succeeding Asuka period are sometimes collectively called the Yamato period. The period takes its name from the kofun, the large earthen burial mounds built for members of the ruling class — most distinctively the enormous keyhole-shaped tombs (zenpō-kōen-fun).

Kofun tombs

Kofun range from modest round or square mounds to monumental keyhole-shaped tombs hundreds of metres long. The largest, the Daisen Kofun in Sakai, Osaka — traditionally attributed to Emperor Nintoku — is about 486 metres long with its moats, and is among the largest tombs in the world by area. Burial chambers held wooden or stone coffins and rich grave goods: bronze mirrors, iron weapons and armour, horse trappings, jewellery of gold, gilt bronze and jasper, and Sue ware pottery. Mounds were often ringed with haniwa, unglazed clay figures shaped as houses, animals, warriors and dancers.

Tomb forms changed over the period: early kofun were built on hills with vertical pit-style stone chambers; later ones used corridor-style horizontal chambers that could be re-entered, allowing multiple burials and more elaborate goods such as decorated cave tombs (sōshoku kofun) in northern Kyushu.

Society and the Yamato state

The period saw the consolidation of a unified polity in the Yamato region (present-day Nara area) under a line of great kings (ōkimi). Society was organised into hereditary occupational groups (be) and clans (uji) headed by titled nobles (kabane). The Yamato court exchanged embassies with the kingdoms of the Korean Peninsula and with China; the "five kings of Wa" recorded in Chinese histories sent missions to the Liu Song dynasty seeking recognition. Close ties with the Korean kingdoms of Baekje and Gaya brought immigrants (toraijin) who carried continental technologies and, eventually, writing and Buddhism.

Culture and technology

Iron tools and weapons spread widely, agriculture expanded with improved irrigation, and mounted warfare with iron armour transformed society in the later ("terminal") Kofun period. Sue ware — a high-fired grey stoneware introduced from Korea — was produced in large kilns. By the fifth century, Chinese characters were in use for record-keeping, as seen in inscriptions on the Inariyama and Eta Funayama swords.

End of the period

The conventional end of the Kofun period is 538 AD, the traditional date for the official introduction of Buddhism from Baekje, after which the Asuka period begins. Kofun continued to be built into the seventh and even eighth centuries, but shrank in scale as Buddhist cremation spread and the great kings asserted authority over regional chiefs.
