Preload (engineering)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Preload is an engineering term describing the load applied to a component or assembly before it enters service, usually to improve its performance under working loads. The most familiar example is the tension induced in a bolt when its nut is tightened, clamping the joint members together.

Bolted joints

When a bolt is tightened it stretches elastically and the clamped parts compress; the resulting preload typically amounts to 70–75 percent of the bolt's proof load in structural practice. A correctly preloaded joint resists separation and shear slip, and the bolt experiences only a fraction of any external cyclic load, which greatly improves fatigue life. Preload is usually controlled by tightening torque, though turn-of-nut methods, tension-indicating washers, and direct bolt elongation measurement give better accuracy.

Bearings and other applications

Rolling-element bearings are preloaded — by springs or by axial adjustment — to remove internal clearance, increasing stiffness and positional accuracy and reducing noise and vibration. The term is also used for pre-stressing in concrete (prestressing), for spring assemblies, and in bicycle and machinery adjustment, e.g. headset and bottom-bracket bearings.
