Infrared

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A thermal camera image of a city street, people glowing orange against a blue background]
A thermal camera image of a city street, people glowing orange against a blue background

Infrared (IR) is Electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths longer than visible light but shorter than microwaves — from about 700 nanometres to 1 millimetre. It was discovered in 1800 by William Herschel, who found invisible heating rays beyond the red end of the spectrum. Most thermal radiation from objects at everyday temperatures is infrared, making IR the basis of night vision, thermal imaging, remote controls and infrared astronomy.

Bands and properties

Infrared is divided into near-infrared (closest to visible light, used in fibre optics and remote controls), mid-infrared (the 'thermal' band where room-temperature objects glow) and far-infrared. Water and CO₂ absorb strongly in parts of the spectrum, leaving atmospheric 'windows' exploited by astronomers and thermal cameras.

Applications

Thermal imaging serves medicine, building inspection, firefighting, military night vision and wildlife film-making; IR spectroscopy identifies molecules by their vibrational fingerprints; the James Webb Space Telescope observes the early universe mainly in infrared, since cosmic expansion redshifts ancient light into the band and IR penetrates the dust veiling star-forming regions.

Everyday presence

The warmth felt from the sun, a fire or a radiator is largely infrared radiation. Remote controls blink codes in near-IR, and animals from pit vipers to vampire bats have evolved IR sensors of their own.
