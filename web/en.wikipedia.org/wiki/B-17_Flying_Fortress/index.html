The Boeing B-17 Flying Fortress is an American four-engined heavy bomber developed in the 1930s for the United States Army Air Corps (USAAC). A fast and high-flying bomber of its era, the B-17 was used primarily in the European Theater of Operations and dropped more bombs than any other aircraft during World War II. It is the third-most produced bomber of all time, behind the four-engined Consolidated B-24 Liberator and the multirole twin-engined Junkers Ju 88.

The Air Corps regarded the plane as a strategic weapon: a relatively fast, high-flying, long-range bomber with heavy defensive armament. A reporter wrote of the prototype that it bristled with machine guns, and the name "Flying Fortress" stuck; Boeing trademarked it. Despite an accident during evaluation that cost the prototype, the Air Corps was impressed and ordered the type into production.

Development and design

The B-17 began as the Boeing Model 299, designed in response to a 1934 requirement for a multi-engined bomber capable of carrying a 2,000 lb bomb load 2,000 miles. The prototype first flew on 28 July 1935, powered by four Pratt & Whitney radial engines. After development through the Y1B-17 and B-17B/C/D models, the definitive B-17E introduced a redesigned tail with a prominent dorsal fin and a tail gun position. The B-17F and finally the B-17G — with a Bendix chin turret to counter head-on fighter attacks — followed; the G model, of which 8,680 were built, carried thirteen .50-calibre machine guns.

Operational history

The B-17 is best known for the daylight strategic bombing campaign over Europe conducted by the US Eighth Air Force from bases in England. Flying in tight "combat box" formations for mutual defence, B-17 groups attacked targets such as the ball-bearing plants at Schweinfurt and the aircraft factories at Regensburg, suffering heavy losses before long-range escort fighters arrived. Famous individual aircraft include the Memphis Belle, one of the first USAAF bombers to complete 25 missions. In the Pacific, B-17s fought at the Battle of Midway and in the Southwest Pacific, though in smaller numbers.

Specifications (B-17G)

The B-17G had a crew of ten, a wingspan of 103 ft 9 in (31.62 m), and was powered by four Wright R-1820-97 Cyclone turbo-supercharged radials of 1,200 hp each. Maximum speed was 287 mph (462 km/h), range about 2,000 mi (3,219 km) with a 6,000 lb bomb load, and service ceiling 35,600 ft (10,850 m).
