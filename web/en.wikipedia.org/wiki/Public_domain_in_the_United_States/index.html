Public domain in the United States

The public domain in the United States comprises creative works and knowledge not protected by intellectual property law, which anyone may use freely. Works enter the public domain when copyright expires, when they were never eligible for protection, or when rights are waived, for example through the CC0 dedication.

Works of the federal government

Under 17 U.S.C. § 105, works prepared by officers or employees of the United States federal government as part of their official duties receive no copyright protection; this covers most federal reports, NASA imagery and USGS maps. The rule does not generally apply to state and local government works, though the government edicts doctrine, affirmed in Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org (2020), places official law and annotated codes beyond copyright. Works by government contractors and by the US Postal Service may be protected.

Expiration of copyright

For works published before 1978, US copyright lasted a maximum of 95 years from publication after the Copyright Act of 1909's renewal system and later extensions; failure to renew, or to attach a copyright notice, placed many works in the public domain early. Works published in 1923 entered the public domain in 2019 after a twenty-year freeze caused by the Copyright Term Extension Act 1998; since then a new year's cohort has entered each 1 January, including Steamboat Willie (1928) in 2024. For works created after 1977 the general term is the author's life plus 70 years, or 95 years from publication for works made for hire. Sound recordings follow a separate schedule under the Music Modernization Act.

Other routes

Facts, ideas, procedures and short phrases are not copyrightable (see Feist Publications v. Rural Telephone Service, 1991), patents expire after 20 years, and trademark rights can lapse through abandonment or genericide. Public Domain Day, celebrated on 1 January, marks each year's new entrants.
