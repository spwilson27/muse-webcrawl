Elizabeth Loftus

Elizabeth F. Loftus (born Elizabeth Fishman; 16 October 1944) is an American cognitive psychologist and expert on human memory. She is best known for her research on the misinformation effect, on the reliability of eyewitness testimony, and on the creation and nature of false memories, including recovered memories of childhood sexual abuse. She is a Distinguished Professor at the University of California, Irvine.

Early life and education

Loftus was born in Los Angeles and grew up in Bel Air. Her mother died in a drowning accident when Loftus was fourteen — an event to which she later returned in her writing on the reconstruction of memory, describing how a relative's false recollection had briefly convinced her that she had found her mother's body. She earned a bachelor's degree in mathematics and psychology from UCLA in 1966 and her MA (1970) and PhD (1970) in psychology from Stanford University.

Research

Misinformation effect

In landmark studies with John Palmer in 1974, Loftus showed that the wording of a question can alter a witness's memory: participants who were asked how fast cars were going when they "smashed" into each other estimated higher speeds and later falsely remembered broken glass that had not been present. This "misinformation effect" — the impairment of episodic memory by misleading information presented after an event — became a foundation of her research programme.

False memories

In the "lost in the mall" studies of the 1990s, Loftus and colleagues suggested to adult subjects that they had been lost in a shopping mall as children; roughly a quarter came to recall partly or fully an event that had never happened. The work fuelled the "memory wars" over recovered-memory therapy, in which Loftus argued that some techniques used to recover supposedly repressed memories of abuse risked implanting false ones. She has served as an expert witness or consultant in hundreds of trials, including cases involving defendants accused on the basis of recovered memories.

Honours

Loftus has received numerous awards, including election to the National Academy of Sciences (2004), the Grawemeyer Award in Psychology (2005), and the John Maddox Prize (2016) for standing up for sound science. A 2002 review ranked her among the most eminent psychologists of the twentieth century, the highest-ranked woman on the list.
