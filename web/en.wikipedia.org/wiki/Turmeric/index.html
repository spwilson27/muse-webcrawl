Turmeric

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Turmeric rhizomes and a heap of bright yellow turmeric powder]

Turmeric is a spice and dye made from the boiled, dried and ground rhizome of Curcuma longa, a plant of the ginger family native to South Asia. Its active colouring compound, curcumin, gives a deep golden-yellow colour — turmeric is the principal colouring and a key flavour of most curry powders, essential to Indian, Thai and Persian cooking, and used to colour mustard, cheeses and fabrics. In India it also has deep religious and medicinal significance, used in Hindu ceremonies and Ayurvedic medicine for millennia.

Production

India produces the great majority of the world's turmeric (Erode in Tamil Nadu is a famous trading centre). Fresh rhizomes are boiled or steamed, dried until hard, then polished and ground. Fresh turmeric, increasingly available, is used like ginger — earthy, peppery and staining everything it touches bright yellow.

Uses

Beyond curry powders, turmeric colours and flavours lentil dals, rice dishes (including Indonesian nasi kuning for celebrations), Thai yellow curries, South African pickles, and drinks such as the Western café trend of the turmeric "golden latte". As a dye it gives the yellow of Buddhist and Hindu robes in some traditions, though it fades readily.

Health claims

Curcumin is widely sold as a supplement with anti-inflammatory claims. It is poorly absorbed by the body and, while intensively researched, has not been shown to treat specific diseases in convincing clinical trials; regulators caution against exaggerated claims. Culinary quantities are safe and traditional.
