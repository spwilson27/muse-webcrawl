Parsifal (WWV 111) is an opera in three acts by the German composer Richard Wagner. It is loosely adapted from Parzival, the 13th-century epic poem by the knight-poet Wolfram von Eschenbach, recounting the story of the Arthurian knight Parzival and his quest for the Holy Grail. Wagner conceived the work in April 1857 and completed it 25 years later; it was his last opera to be completed.

Premiere and the Bayreuth monopoly

Parsifal was premiered on 26 July 1882 at the second Bayreuth Festival, conducted by Hermann Levi. Wagner stipulated that the work — which he called a Bühnenweihfestspiel, a "festival play for the consecration of the stage" — should be performed only at Bayreuth, a restriction his widow Cosima upheld until copyright expiry at the end of 1913, though an unauthorised production was staged at the Metropolitan Opera in New York in 1903.

Synopsis

The knight Amfortas, king of the Grail brotherhood, suffers an unhealing wound inflicted by the sorcerer Klingsor using the Holy Spear. A prophecy foretells that only a "pure fool, enlightened by compassion" can heal him. The innocent youth Parsifal, raised in the forest, resists the seductions of Kundry and the Flower Maidens, seizes the Spear, and after long wandering returns to the Grail Temple, where his touch heals Amfortas and he is proclaimed king.

Music and influence

The score is noted for its slow harmonic rhythm, the recurring "Faith" motif and the transformation music of Acts I and III. Its dense chromaticism and orchestral atmosphere deeply influenced later composers, including Debussy, Mahler and Sibelius, while its blend of Christian, Buddhist and Schopenhauerian themes has generated an enormous interpretative literature.
