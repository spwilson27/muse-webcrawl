Food contamination

Food contamination refers to the presence in food of harmful chemicals and microorganisms, which can cause consumer illness. Contamination is generally classified as biological, chemical, or physical, and may occur at any stage of production, processing, distribution, preparation, or storage.

Biological contamination

Biological contaminants include pathogenic bacteria such as Salmonella, Escherichia coli O157:H7, Listeria monocytogenes, and Campylobacter; viruses such as norovirus and hepatitis A; and parasites. These are the leading cause of foodborne illness, which the World Health Organization estimates affects about 600 million people annually worldwide.

Chemical contamination

Chemical contamination may result from pesticide residues, heavy metals (such as lead or mercury in fish), environmental pollutants like dioxins and PCBs, food additives used beyond permitted levels, or deliberate adulteration, as in the 2008 melamine contamination of milk products in China.

Physical contamination

Physical contaminants are foreign objects — glass fragments, metal shavings, stones, hair, or plastic — introduced accidentally during processing. Metal detectors and X-ray inspection are widely used in food plants to detect them.

Prevention

Control systems include Good Manufacturing Practice, HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points), pasteurization, irradiation, and cold-chain management. See also Food safety and Foodborne illness.
