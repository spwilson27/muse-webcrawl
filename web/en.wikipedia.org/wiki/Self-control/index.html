Self-control

Self-control is an aspect of inhibitory control, one of the core executive functions. It is the ability to regulate one's emotions, thoughts, and behaviour in the face of temptations and impulses. Self-control is sometimes used synonymously with self-regulation, and is closely studied in psychology, economics, and neuroscience.

Definition

Self-control is the capacity to override an impulse in favour of a longer-term goal. A person with high self-control can resist immediate rewards, persist at difficult tasks, and regulate emotional responses. It is one of the personality traits most reliably associated with better health, wealth, academic achievement, and interpersonal relationships in longitudinal research.

Research

The most famous study of self-control is the Stanford marshmallow experiment, conducted by Walter Mischel in the late 1960s and early 1970s, in which children who could delay eating a marshmallow in order to receive two later tended, in follow-up studies, to have better life outcomes. Later replications have qualified these conclusions, showing that socioeconomic background accounts for much of the association.

Roy Baumeister proposed the "strength model" of self-control, likening it to a muscle that can be temporarily depleted ("ego depletion") by use and strengthened by exercise; the ego-depletion effect itself has been the subject of replication controversy. Self-control is also a central variable in the theory of crime proposed by Gottfredson and Hirschi, who argued that low self-control is a principal cause of criminal behaviour.

Measurement

Common measures include the Brief Self-Control Scale (Tangney, Baumeister and Boone, 2004) and behavioural tasks such as delay-discounting paradigms, in which participants choose between smaller sooner and larger later rewards.
