Epigenetics

Epigenetics
FieldGenetics, molecular biology
Key mechanismsDNA methylation, histone modification, non-coding RNA
TermFrom Greek epi-, 'over, above'

Epigenetics is the study of changes in organisms caused by modification of gene expression rather than alteration of the genetic code itself. Epigenetic changes are heritable through cell division and sometimes across generations, and include chemical modifications to DNA and to the histone proteins around which DNA is wrapped.

Mechanisms

DNA methylation adds a methyl group, typically at CpG sites, generally repressing transcription. Histone modifications — acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation — alter chromatin accessibility. Non-coding RNAs such as microRNAs can silence genes after transcription. Together these mechanisms explain how identical genomes produce neurons, liver cells, and skin cells: development is driven largely by which genes are switched on.

Examples

Classic cases include genomic imprinting, X-chromosome inactivation in females, the agouti mouse whose coat colour and obesity risk vary with methylation influenced by the mother's diet, and differences between genetically identical twins that accumulate over life. Studies of the Dutch Hunger Winter (1944–45) found persistent methylation differences in people exposed to famine before birth.

History

Conrad Waddington coined 'epigenetics' in the 1940s for the study of how genes produce phenotypes in development. The modern molecular meaning crystallised in the 1990s. Whether acquired epigenetic marks are transmitted across many generations in humans remains debated and is sometimes overstated in popular accounts.

Medical relevance

Abnormal epigenetic patterns are implicated in cancers and developmental disorders such as Prader–Willi and Angelman syndromes. Drugs that inhibit DNA methyltransferases or histone deacetylases are used in some cancer treatments, and epigenetic clocks based on methylation patterns estimate biological age.
