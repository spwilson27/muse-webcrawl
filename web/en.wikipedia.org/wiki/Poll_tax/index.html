Poll tax

Type: Fixed-sum tax per adult person

Also known as: Head tax, capitation tax

A poll tax, also known as a head tax or capitation, is a tax levied as a fixed sum on every liable individual, without reference to income or resources. Poll taxes are administratively simple to collect but are widely regarded as regressive, since the same amount represents a far greater burden on the poor than on the rich.

History

Poll taxes were common in antiquity and the Middle Ages. In England, poll taxes were levied intermittently from the 14th century; the tax of 1380–81, graduated by rank but collected harshly, was a principal trigger of the Peasants' Revolt of 1381. Poll taxes were later used in colonial America and in Britain's colonies.

Poll taxes and voting in the United States

After the American Civil War, several Southern states adopted poll taxes as a prerequisite for voting, a measure designed — alongside literacy tests and other devices — to disenfranchise African Americans and poor whites. The Twenty-fourth Amendment (ratified 1964) banned poll taxes in federal elections, and in Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections (1966) the U.S. Supreme Court struck them down for state elections as well.

United Kingdom "Community Charge"

In 1989–90 the government of Margaret Thatcher introduced a per-adult local tax officially called the Community Charge but universally known as the poll tax, replacing domestic rates based on property values. Widespread non-payment and the poll tax riots of 1990 contributed to Thatcher's fall; the charge was replaced by the Council Tax in 1993.

Economics

Because a lump-sum tax does not vary with behaviour, economists note that a poll tax is non-distortionary — it does not change incentives to work or save — which makes it theoretically efficient, though its distributional effects make it politically and socially contentious.
