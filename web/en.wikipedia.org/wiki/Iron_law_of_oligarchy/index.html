Iron law of oligarchy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The iron law of oligarchy is a political theory proposed by the German sociologist Robert Michels in Political Parties (1911), asserting that rule by an elite, or oligarchy, is inevitable in any democratic organisation as a result of the “tactical and technical necessities” of organisation itself. Studying the German Social Democratic Party — the most democratically committed mass party of its day — Michels argued that organisation requires full-time leaders, who gain control of communication, expertise and finance; that leaders develop interests in self-preservation distinct from the members'; and that members, grateful and apathetic, acquiesce: “who says organisation, says oligarchy”.

The law became a cornerstone of elite theory alongside Mosca and Pareto. Critics (Lipset, Trow and Coleman's Union Democracy, 1956, studying a printers' union with sustained internal democracy) show oligarchy is a tendency, not an inevitability, and identify counter-conditions: competing factions, an educated membership, external competition. The theory is routinely applied beyond parties — to unions, NGOs, revolutionary movements and digital platforms — as a standing caution about the gap between democratic forms and organisational reality.
