In number theory, Wilson's theorem states that a natural number p > 1 is a prime number if and only if the product of all positive integers less than p is congruent to −1 modulo p:

(p − 1)! ≡ −1 (mod p).

History

The result was known to the medieval mathematician Ibn al-Haytham and was stated by John Wilson, a student of Edward Waring, who published it in 1770 without proof. Joseph-Louis Lagrange gave the first proof in 1771. Leibniz had also known the result earlier but did not publish it.

Proof idea

For prime p, the non-zero residues modulo p form a multiplicative group; every element pairs with its distinct inverse except 1 and −1, so the product of all residues equals −1. Conversely, if n is composite, (n − 1)! shares factors with n and cannot be congruent to −1 modulo n.

Applications

Wilson's theorem gives a theoretical primality criterion, though computing factorials makes it impractical for large numbers. It is closely related to Fermat's little theorem and is a standard example in elementary number theory courses.
