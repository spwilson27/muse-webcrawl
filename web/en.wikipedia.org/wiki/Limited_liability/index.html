Limited liability

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Limited liability is a legal structure of an organisation where a loss that can be suffered by the parties involved does not exceed the amount invested. Thus, the personal assets of investors and owners are protected: if a company with limited liability is sued, the claimants are restricted to suing the company itself and cannot reach the shareholders' personal assets. Limited liability is the opposite of unlimited liability, under which a sole trader or partner is personally liable for all the debts of the business.

History

Although partnership-like arrangements with limited liability existed earlier, modern limited liability was established by statute in the nineteenth century: New York passed an early general act in 1811, and in England the Limited Liability Act 1855 and the Joint Stock Companies Act 1856 made incorporation with limited liability freely available. The landmark case Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] confirmed that a duly incorporated company is a legal person separate from its shareholders even when one person is effectively the whole company.

Justifications and criticism

Supporters argue that limited liability encourages investment and economic risk-taking, makes shares freely transferable and priced by markets, and allows passive investors to participate without monitoring managers. Critics contend that it externalises risk onto creditors and tort victims, who did not agree to bear it, and may encourage excessive risk-taking ("moral hazard"). Courts can in exceptional cases "pierce the corporate veil" and hold individuals liable, typically where the company is used to perpetrate fraud.

Forms

Limited liability is a feature of the corporation (Inc., Corp.), the private limited company (Ltd in the UK, GmbH in Germany), and the limited liability company (LLC) popular in the United States, as well as of limited partnerships for their limited partners.
