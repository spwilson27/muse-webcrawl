Indigenous peoples of Siberia

The indigenous peoples of Siberia are the diverse ethnic groups native to the vast region of Siberia in northern Asia, which stretches from the Ural Mountains in the west to the Pacific Ocean in the east. These peoples speak languages belonging to many unrelated families — including Turkic, Mongolic, Tungusic, Uralic, Yeniseian, Yukaghir, Chukotko-Kamchatkan, Eskaleut and the isolate Nivkh — and historically practised hunting, fishing, herding and reindeer pastoralism.

Major groups

Among the largest indigenous groups are the Sakha (Yakuts), Buryats and Tuvans, each numbering in the hundreds of thousands. Smaller peoples of the Russian Far North include the Evenks, Evens, Chukchi, Koryaks, Nenets, Khanty, Mansi, Selkups, Dolgans, Yukaghirs, Itelmens and Nivkhs. Many of these groups are officially recognised in Russia as "indigenous small-numbered peoples of the North, Siberia and the Far East".

History

Humans have inhabited Siberia for tens of thousands of years. From the late 16th century the region was incorporated into the Russian state, a process that brought colonisation, the fur trade (yasak tribute), epidemics and pressure on traditional ways of life. In the Soviet period, collectivisation, boarding schools and industrial development further transformed indigenous societies, while the post-Soviet era has seen cultural revitalisation movements and debates over land and resource rights.

Culture

Shamanism was historically the dominant spiritual tradition among many Siberian peoples and remains culturally significant. Reindeer herding, dog sledding, throat singing and distinctive epic traditions — such as the Sakha Olonkho, inscribed by UNESCO — are notable features of the region's cultures.
