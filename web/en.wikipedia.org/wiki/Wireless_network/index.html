Wireless network

A wireless router serving a home network

A wireless network is a computer network that uses wireless data connections between network nodes. Wireless networking is a method by which homes, telecommunications networks and business installations avoid the costly process of introducing cables into a building, or as a connection between various equipment locations. Wireless telecommunication networks are generally implemented and administered using radio communication, at the physical level (layer) of the OSI model network structure.

Examples of wireless networks include cell phone networks, wireless local area networks (WLANs), wireless sensor networks, satellite communication networks, and terrestrial microwave networks.

History

Wireless networks have their origins in radio experiments of the late 19th century; Guglielmo Marconi's transatlantic transmission in 1901 demonstrated long-distance wireless communication. A pioneering packet radio network, ALOHAnet, was developed at the University of Hawaii in 1970 to connect campuses across the islands, and influenced the development of Ethernet and later channel access methods. The modern era began with the allocation of unlicensed spectrum by the US Federal Communications Commission in 1985, the founding of the IEEE 802.11 committee, and the release of the first Wi-Fi standard in 1997. In parallel, cellular networks evolved from analogue 1G systems in the 1980s through 2G digital, 3G, 4G LTE, and 5G, carrying an ever larger share of the world's voice and data traffic.

Types

  • Wireless personal area networks (WPAN) — very short range; e.g., Bluetooth, Zigbee, infrared.
  • Wireless local area networks (WLAN) — short range; based on IEEE 802.11 (Wi-Fi) standards.
  • Wireless metropolitan area networks (WMAN) — city-scale; e.g., WiMAX.
  • Wireless wide area networks (WWAN) — the largest type, including cellular networks and satellite systems.

Uses

Wireless networks are used wherever mobility or the absence of cabling is valuable: in homes and offices, warehouses and hospitals, for public internet access in cafés and airports, and in industrial and military settings. They underpin mobile telephony, GPS, satellite television, cordless peripherals, and the Internet of things.

Security

Because radio signals pass through walls and can be received by anyone in range, wireless networks require encryption to protect traffic. Standards have progressed from the flawed Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP) through Wi-Fi Protected Access (WPA and WPA2) to WPA3. Cellular networks apply their own authentication and encryption schemes. Poor configuration — default passwords, outdated protocols, or rogue access points — remains a common source of breaches.
