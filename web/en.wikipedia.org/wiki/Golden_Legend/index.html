Golden Legend

The Golden Legend (Latin: Legenda aurea or Legenda sanctorum) is a collection of lives of the saints and accounts of church feasts compiled by the Dominican friar Jacobus de Voragine around 1260. One of the most popular books of the late Middle Ages, it survives in about a thousand manuscripts and was among the first books printed in several languages — William Caxton's English edition appeared in 1483. Its stories shaped medieval and Renaissance art, giving artists the standard episodes and attributes by which saints could be recognised.

Contents and structure

The work is organised by the liturgical year, from Advent to the end of November. Its chapters cover the major feasts of Christ and the Virgin and the lives of some 153 saints, blending scripture, earlier hagiography (such as the apocryphal gospels and the works of Gregory of Tours) and popular legend. Famous entries include Saint George and the dragon, Saint Nicholas, Saint Margaret, Saint Christopher carrying the Christ child, and the legends of the True Cross. Jacobus typically begins with a fanciful etymology of the saint's name before narrating the life and miracles.

Influence

Translated into every major European vernacular, the Golden Legend was a principal source for preachers, playwrights and painters: iconographic details in countless altarpieces — Saint Catherine's wheel, Saint Lucy's eyes, Saint Denis carrying his head — descend from its accounts. Humanist and Reformation critics later attacked its credulity; the name "golden" was sometimes read ironically as "legend of lead". Modern scholarship values it as a window onto medieval popular religion, and the standard English translation is William Granger Ryan's (1993).
