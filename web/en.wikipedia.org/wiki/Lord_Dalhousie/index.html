Lord Dalhousie

Lord Dalhousie is a Scottish peerage title held by successive members of the Ramsay family, the Earls and later Marquesses of Dalhousie. The title is most famous through James Andrew Broun-Ramsay, 10th Earl of Dalhousie (1812–1860), who served as Governor-General of India from 1848 to 1856 and is associated with the Doctrine of Lapse, the annexation of Punjab and Awadh, and sweeping administrative reforms.

History

The earldom of Dalhousie was created in the Peerage of Scotland in 1633 for William Ramsay, a soldier who had served in the Thirty Years' War. The family seat was Dalhousie Castle in Midlothian. The 10th Earl was elevated to Marquess of Dalhousie in 1849 in recognition of his services in India; the marquessate became extinct on his death in 1860, but the earldom continued in another branch of the family.

The 10th Earl in India

As Governor-General, Dalhousie pursued an aggressive policy of expansion and modernisation. His Doctrine of Lapse held that princely states without a natural male heir would lapse to the British East India Company, leading to the annexation of Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur and others. He also oversaw the introduction of railways, the telegraph, and a uniform postal system in India, and founded the Public Works Department. His annexation of Awadh in 1856 on grounds of misgovernment is often cited among the grievances behind the Indian Rebellion of 1857.

Later holders

The title passed through the Ramsay family of Brechin and later combined with the earldom of Panmure inheritance. Holders have served as courtiers and in the royal household; the 17th Earl, Simon Ramsay, served as Lord Steward of the Household. The present Earl retains Dalhousie Castle associations and the chiefship traditions of Clan Ramsay.
