Pater familias

The pater familias, also written as paterfamilias (plural patres familias), was the male head of a Roman family and household. Under Roman law, the pater familias held patria potestas — legal authority over his children, descendants, slaves, and property — making him the only fully independent person (sui iuris) in the family. The institution was one of the defining features of Roman society and law.

The pater familias stood at the apex of the Roman household, the basic unit of Roman society.

Patria potestas

The powers of the pater familias were extensive. He controlled all family property; his adult sons, even if married, senators, or magistrates, could legally own nothing in their own right, holding only a peculium (an allowance) at his discretion. In early Rome his authority was said to extend to a right of life and death (vitae necisque potestas) over his children, the power to sell them into slavery, and the right to expose newborn infants. Over the course of the Republic and Empire these harsher powers were progressively curtailed by legislation and custom, and by the time of Constantine the killing of a son was treated as murder.

Status and succession

A man became a pater familias when his own father (or paternal grandfather) died, or when he was emancipated or adopted into independence; a male could be a pater familias at any age, even in infancy, if he had no living male ascendant. Only Roman citizens could hold the status. Women could never be patres familias, though a woman who was sui iuris could head a household in practice. When a pater familias died, each of his sons became a pater familias of his own branch of the family, and his property was divided according to his will or the rules of intestacy.

Religious role

The pater familias was also the priest of the family's domestic cult, responsible for maintaining worship of the household gods — the Lares and Penates — and for honouring the family's ancestors. This religious duty reinforced his legal authority within the home.
