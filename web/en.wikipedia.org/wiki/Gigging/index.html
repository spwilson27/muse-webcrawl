Gigging

Gigging for flounder from a boat at night

Gigging is the practice of hunting fish or small game with a gig, a multi-pronged spear similar to a trident or leister. Commonly practised in the United States, gigging is done at night with lights, in shallow water, from boats or on foot. Popular targets include flounder, suckers, and other non-game fish, as well as frogs (frog gigging) and eels.

Methods

The gigger stands at the bow of a flat-bottomed boat, often fitted with bright lights that illuminate the water and reveal fish resting on the bottom. When a fish is spotted, the gig — a pole, typically of wood or fibreglass, tipped with barbed metal prongs — is thrust or thrown to pin the fish. In the Ozarks of Missouri and Arkansas, gigging for redhorse and other suckers in clear autumn streams has a long tradition, with purpose-built jon boats and community "gigging derbies".

Regulation

Because gigging is selective but effective, it is regulated by state fisheries agencies: game fish such as bass and trout must usually be released or may not be gigged at all, and seasons, gear restrictions, and protected species rules vary. Gigged fish are often injured if misidentified, so some jurisdictions restrict gigging to rough fish and frogs.
