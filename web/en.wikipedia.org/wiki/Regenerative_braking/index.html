Regenerative braking

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Regenerative braking is an energy recovery mechanism that slows a moving vehicle or object by converting its kinetic energy into a form that can be stored and reused, instead of wasting it as heat in friction brakes. In an electric or hybrid vehicle, the traction motor runs as a generator during deceleration, returning electricity to the battery or — in electric railways — to the power supply.

How it works

When the driver lifts off the accelerator or presses the brake pedal, the motor controller reverses the motor's torque; the wheels drive the motor, which generates current that charges the battery. Braking force is limited by motor and battery capacity (a full or cold battery accepts less charge), so friction brakes remain for hard stops, low speeds and emergencies, blended automatically in modern systems.

Benefits and history

Regeneration can recover a substantial share of the energy spent accelerating, markedly improving efficiency in stop-and-go city driving, and reducing brake wear. The principle is old — used on early 20th-century electric railways and trams — and became a hallmark of modern hybrids (Toyota Prius, 1997) and battery-electric cars, several of which allow "one-pedal driving". Formula 1's KERS (2009) and its successors applied the same idea, recovering energy under braking for a power boost.
