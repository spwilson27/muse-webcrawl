Alliteration

Alliteration is the repetition of the same initial consonant sound in successive or nearby words in a phrase or verse line. A familiar example is the tongue twister "Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers". The term derives from Latin littera, "letter (of the alphabet)". Alliteration operates on sounds rather than letters, so "phony people" alliterates while "hot coffee" does not despite appearances.

In poetry

Alliteration was the structural backbone of verse in many early Germanic languages. In Old English poetry, as in Beowulf, each line was divided by a caesura into two half-lines, with stressed syllables linked by alliteration across the break; Old Norse, Old Saxon and Old High German verse followed similar rules. The 14th-century "Alliterative Revival" in Middle English produced works such as Piers Plowman and Sir Gawain and the Green Knight.

Uses

Beyond poetry, alliteration is used for emphasis and memorability in rhetoric, proverbs ("safe and sound"), brand names, comic-book character names (Peter Parker, Lois Lane), and children's literature. Shakespeare used it extensively, as in "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes" in Romeo and Juliet.

Related figures

Assonance repeats vowel sounds and consonance repeats consonant sounds in other positions; alliteration is a specialised form of consonance affecting stressed initial syllables.
