Mixture model

In statistics, a mixture model is a probabilistic model for representing the presence of subpopulations within an overall population, without requiring that an observed data set identify the sub-population to which an individual observation belongs. Formally a mixture model corresponds to the mixture distribution that represents the probability distribution of observations in the overall population.

Definition

A finite mixture distribution is a weighted sum of component distributions: the density of an observation is the sum over components of the component weight (mixing proportion) times the component density. Each component is typically a standard parametric distribution such as a Gaussian, and the weights are non-negative and sum to one.

Gaussian mixture models

The most common case is the Gaussian mixture model (GMM), in which each component is a multivariate normal distribution with its own mean vector and covariance matrix. GMMs are widely used for clustering, density estimation, and as components in larger machine-learning systems. Parameters are usually estimated by the expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm, which alternates between computing soft assignments of points to components and re-estimating the parameters.

Applications

Mixture models are used in astronomy (separating stellar populations), finance, genetics, speech recognition (as the observation model in hidden Markov models), image segmentation, and topic modelling, where latent Dirichlet allocation can be viewed as a kind of mixture model. Because the assignment of observations to components is probabilistic, mixtures provide a "soft" alternative to hard partitioning methods such as k-means, to which GMMs are closely related.
