Square dance

Square dance
GenreFolk dance
OriginEngland and France, 17th century; developed in the United States
FormationFour couples in a square
Key figureThe caller

A square dance is a dance for four couples (eight dancers in total) arranged in a square, with one couple on each side, facing the middle of the square. Square dances are a form of folk dance that developed from the English country dances and French quadrilles brought to North America by European settlers, where they merged with traditions of the American frontier.

The defining feature of square dancing is the caller, a person who calls out a sequence of dance figures, often in time to music and frequently in a rhythmic, patter-like style ("patter calling"), interspersed with sung lyrics ("singing calls"). Dancers learn a standard vocabulary of calls; in modern western square dance, these are organized into programs of increasing difficulty, from Basic and Mainstream through Plus and the Advanced and Challenge levels.

History

Square dancing traces its roots to 17th-century English country dances and the French quadrille and cotillion. In colonial America, dancing masters taught these forms, but on the frontier the fiddler often doubled as caller. The dance became strongly associated with rural American social life, barn dances, and community gatherings.

In the 20th century, industrialist Henry Ford promoted old-time dancing in the 1920s as wholesome recreation, publishing Good Morning (1926) and employing dance instructor Benjamin Lovett. Educator Lloyd "Pappy" Shaw of Colorado documented and popularized western square dancing and helped spark a national revival. After the Second World War, modern western square dance took shape, with standardized calls taught in clubs; Callerlab, founded in 1974, maintains the standardized definitions and program lists.

Types

Traditional square dance uses a limited set of figures, often regional in character (New England, Southern Appalachian, and singing squares). Modern western square dance, danced in clubs worldwide, uses a much larger, standardized vocabulary with calls prompted in real time, so dancers do not memorize whole routines. Dress often includes western-style shirts, prairie skirts, and bolo ties.

Music

Traditional accompaniment is by a live string band — fiddle, banjo, guitar, and bass — playing reels and jigs. Modern clubs more often use recorded music ranging from country to pop, with the caller singing the calls.
