Arnold conjecture

Vladimir Arnold (1937–2010)

FieldSymplectic geometry
Proposed byVladimir Arnold
Proposed in1960s–1970s
StatusProved in many cases; general case in progress

The Arnold conjecture, named after mathematician Vladimir Arnold, is a famous problem in symplectic geometry concerning the number of fixed points of a Hamiltonian diffeomorphism of a compact symplectic manifold. It predicts that such a diffeomorphism has at least as many fixed points as a smooth function on the manifold must have critical points — in the weakest version, at least the sum of the Betti numbers of the manifold.

Statement

Let (M, ω) be a compact symplectic manifold and φ a Hamiltonian diffeomorphism. The strong Arnold conjecture states that the number of fixed points of φ, counted when they are all nondegenerate, is at least the sum of the Betti numbers of M. A weaker homological version replaces the Betti-number sum with a bound given by the minimal number of critical points of a Morse function.

Progress

The conjecture was proved first for tori by Conley and Zehnder (1983), and subsequently for many classes of manifolds — including complex projective spaces and, more generally, manifolds with restricted topology — through the development of Floer homology by Andreas Floer in the late 1980s. Work by Fukaya and Ono, and independently by Liu and Tian, addressed the general case via virtual fundamental cycle techniques, though details of the fully general proof remain an active area of scrutiny in symplectic topology.

Significance

The conjecture helped launch modern symplectic topology: attempts to prove it produced Floer homology, a powerful invariant relating the topology of a manifold to the dynamics of Hamiltonian systems, with deep connections to mirror symmetry and low-dimensional topology.
