Krypton (from Ancient Greek kryptós, "the hidden one") is a chemical element with the symbol Kr and atomic number 36. It is a colorless, odorless, tasteless noble gas that occurs in trace amounts in the atmosphere (about 1 part per million). Krypton is inert under most conditions but can form a small number of compounds, most notably krypton difluoride.

History

Krypton was discovered in Britain in 1898 by William Ramsay and Morris Travers, who isolated it from the residue left after boiling away liquid air components while studying liquefied air. Its name reflects how it had remained "hidden" in air. Between 1960 and 1983, the metre was officially defined in terms of the wavelength of light emitted by the krypton-86 isotope — 1,650,763.73 wavelengths of the orange-red emission line of Kr-86 in vacuum.

Properties and compounds

Like the other noble gases, krypton has a completely filled outer electron shell, making it very unreactive. Under extreme conditions it forms krypton difluoride (KrF2), a powerful oxidising and fluorinating agent first prepared in 1963, and compounds such as HKrCN have been characterised at low temperatures. Krypton has several stable isotopes; krypton-85 is a radioactive fission product used to detect leaks and monitor nuclear reprocessing.

Applications

Krypton is used in high-powered photographic flashes, airport runway lights, and some fluorescent and incandescent lamps, where it reduces filament evaporation. Krypton fluoride excimer lasers are used in scientific research. Krypton is also used in insulated glazing as a high-performance fill gas and in ion thrusters for spacecraft propulsion research.
