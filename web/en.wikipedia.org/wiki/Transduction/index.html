Transduction

Generalised transduction: a phage carries a fragment of donor bacterial DNA into a recipient cell.

Transduction is the process by which foreign DNA is introduced into a cell by a virus or viral vector. It is a horizontal gene transfer mechanism widely used by molecular biologists to introduce genes into cells, and it occurs naturally when bacteriophages accidentally package bacterial DNA and transfer it to a new host.

Discovery and types

Transduction was discovered in 1952 by Norton Zinder and Joshua Lederberg working with Salmonella and the phage P22. Two forms are distinguished:

  • Generalised transduction — during the lytic cycle, random fragments of the host chromosome are packaged into phage heads; nearly any bacterial gene can be transferred.
  • Specialised (restricted) transduction — a prophage excises imprecisely from the chromosome, carrying only genes adjacent to its integration site, as with phage λ in E. coli.

Significance

Transduction is an important route by which bacteria acquire new traits, including antibiotic resistance and virulence factors; for example, the toxins of diphtheria and botulism are encoded by phage-borne genes. In the laboratory, viral transduction (for instance with lentiviral vectors) is a standard method of delivering DNA into mammalian cells for research and gene therapy.

The term is also used more broadly in physiology for signal transduction and sensory transduction — the conversion of a stimulus into an electrical signal — but in genetics it refers specifically to virus-mediated gene transfer.

References: Zinder, N. D. & Lederberg, J., "Genetic exchange in Salmonella", Journal of Bacteriology 64 (1952).
