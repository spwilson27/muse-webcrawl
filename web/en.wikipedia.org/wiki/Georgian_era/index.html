The Georgian era is a period in British history from 1714 to c. 1830–37, named after the Hanoverian kings George I, George II, George III and George IV. The sub-period known as the Regency (1811–1820), when George, Prince of Wales, governed during his father's illness, is sometimes treated distinctly.

Politics and society

The era saw the consolidation of parliamentary government and the office of prime minister, the growth of Britain's overseas empire and its loss of the American colonies, the union with Ireland in 1801, and the long wars against Revolutionary and Napoleonic France (1793–1815). Socially it was marked by agricultural change, the beginnings of the Industrial Revolution, evangelical religious revival, and the abolition of the slave trade (1807) and then of slavery itself in most of the empire (1833).

Culture and architecture

Georgian culture is associated with neoclassical architecture — the terraces, crescents and squares of Bath, Edinburgh's New Town and London's Bloomsbury — the furniture and interior decoration of the Adam brothers, and the literature of Pope (early), Johnson, Austen and the Romantic poets. Georgian architecture emphasised symmetry, proportion and classical orders, and remains a lasting influence on the built environment of Britain and the former empire.
