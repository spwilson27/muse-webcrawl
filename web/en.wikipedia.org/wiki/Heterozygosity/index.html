Heterozygosity

Heterozygosity is the state of a diploid organism having two different alleles of a given gene on its homologous chromosomes. An individual with two different alleles at a locus is called a heterozygote for that gene, in contrast to a homozygote, which carries two identical alleles. In population genetics, heterozygosity is also used as a measure of genetic variation within a population: the expected heterozygosity is the probability that two alleles drawn at random from the population are different.

Measurement

Under the Hardy–Weinberg model, for a locus with two alleles at frequencies p and q, the expected frequency of heterozygotes is 2pq, which is maximised when the alleles are equally frequent. Across many loci, expected heterozygosity is calculated as H = 1 − Σ pi2, summed over alleles. Observed heterozygosity is simply the measured proportion of heterozygous individuals in a sample. The statistic is widely used in conservation genetics, where low heterozygosity often signals small population size, inbreeding, or a recent bottleneck.

Significance

Heterozygosity can mask deleterious recessive alleles, buffering individuals against genetic disease. In some cases the heterozygote is fitter than either homozygote — overdominance — as in sickle-cell trait, where carriers of one sickle allele and one normal allele gain resistance to malaria. Heterozygosity also declines predictably under inbreeding, which is why pedigree and genomic measures of heterozygosity are used to manage captive breeding programmes and to assess the viability of endangered populations.

Loss of heterozygosity

Loss of heterozygosity (LOH) refers to the loss of one parent's contribution to a cell, making a previously heterozygous region effectively homozygous or hemizygous. LOH is an important mechanism in cancer biology: if one copy of a tumour suppressor gene is already defective, loss of the remaining functional copy can remove growth control and promote tumour development, as described by Knudson's two-hit hypothesis.
