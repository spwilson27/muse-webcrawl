The Family Computer, commonly known by the portmanteau Famicom (ファミコン), is an 8-bit third-generation home video game console produced by Nintendo. It was released in Japan on 15 July 1983, and later redesigned and marketed internationally as the Nintendo Entertainment System (NES), launching in North America in 1985 and in Europe and Australia over the following years.

Designed by Masayuki Uemura, the Famicom was modelled on Nintendo's earlier Color TV-Game consoles but introduced interchangeable ROM cartridges. Its red-and-white plastic shell and twin hardwired controllers — the second controller equipped with a microphone — became iconic. The console is credited with revitalising the video game industry after the North American video game crash of 1983.

History

Nintendo president Hiroshi Yamauchi wanted an affordable cartridge-based machine that outperformed rivals such as the Epoch Cassette Vision. A recall of the first production run, caused by a faulty chip set, led Nintendo to replace motherboards at considerable cost, but the system's price of ¥14,800 and launch titles Donkey Kong, Donkey Kong Jr., and Popeye secured its success in Japan.

Hardware

The Famicom uses a Ricoh 2A03 processor based on the MOS Technology 6502, running at 1.79 MHz, with 2 KB of work RAM and a custom Picture Processing Unit generating a 256 × 240 resolution image. Cartridges could include extra hardware, such as battery-backed saves and memory mappers that expanded the system's capabilities over its lifetime.

Peripherals

Peripherals included the Famicom Disk System, which played rewritable floppy-like disks; the Family BASIC keyboard; the Famicom Modem; and even the Family Computer Robot, known in the West as R.O.B.

Legacy

Worldwide sales of the Famicom and NES exceeded 61 million units. The console established the modern business model of console licensing, popularised gamepads with directional pads, and launched franchises including Super Mario Bros., The Legend of Zelda, and Metroid. Manufacturing of the original Famicom continued in Japan until 2003.
