Pterodactylus is an extinct genus of pterosaurs whose members are commonly known as pterodactyls. It is thought to contain only a single species, Pterodactylus antiquus, the first pterosaur ever to be named and identified as a flying reptile. Fossil remains have been found primarily in the Solnhofen limestone of Bavaria, Germany, which dates to the Late Jurassic period.

The name derives from the Greek pteron (wing) and daktulos (finger), referring to the hugely elongated fourth finger that supported the flight membrane. Pterodactylus was a relatively small pterosaur, with an adult wingspan of roughly 1 metre (3.3 feet). Unlike the much later and much larger Pteranodon, it had a full set of small teeth set in sockets in its long, straight jaws, and a short tail.

Discovery and history

The first specimen was described in 1784 by Cosimo Collini, who interpreted it as an unknown marine animal. Georges Cuvier in 1809 correctly identified it as a flying reptile and coined the name "Ptéro-Dactyle"; the Latinised Pterodactylus followed. During the nineteenth century many pterosaur species were lumped into the genus, but most have since been reassigned, leaving P. antiquus as the only widely accepted species.

Paleobiology

Pterodactylus was a generalist carnivore that probably fed on small animals including fish and invertebrates around the lagoons of the Solnhofen archipelago. Growth studies of the many known specimens show that juveniles grew quickly and may have occupied different ecological niches at different ages, possibly feeding in flocks.

In popular culture

The word "pterodactyl" is widely used as a generic popular name for pterosaurs of all kinds, though scientifically it properly refers to this genus (and informally to members of the suborder Pterodactyloidea).
