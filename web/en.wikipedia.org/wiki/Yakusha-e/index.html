Yakusha-e

Yakusha-e
Japanese: 役者絵
Genre: Ukiyo-e woodblock prints
Subject: Kabuki actors
Flourished: c. 1700s–1800s

Yakusha-e (Japanese: 役者絵, "actor pictures") are Japanese woodblock prints of kabuki actors, one of the major genres of ukiyo-e. Produced from the early 18th century into the 20th, they functioned much like modern celebrity posters and publicity images, feeding the intense fan culture surrounding Edo's kabuki theatres.

History and styles

Early yakusha-e by the Torii school, official painters to the Edo theatres, doubled as theatre signboards and playbills. Artists such as Katsukawa Shunshō moved toward realistic individual likenesses (nigao-e), capturing actors' actual features rather than generic masks. The genre's most famous works are Tōshūsai Sharaku's dramatically expressive ōkubi-e (large-head portraits) of 1794–95, which exaggerated actors' expressions to startling effect. Utagawa Toyokuni and his school later dominated with glamorous, idealized portraits tied closely to fan devotion.

Characteristics

Prints typically depict actors in famous roles, striking the frozen mie pose, with the actor's crest, name, role, and theatre identified in cartouches. Memorial portraits (shini-e) of deceased actors also belong to the genre. Alongside bijin-ga (pictures of beautiful women) and landscape prints, yakusha-e were among the best-selling subjects of the Edo print market.
