Casting (metalworking)

Casting is a manufacturing process in which a liquid material — usually molten metal — is poured into a mould containing a hollow cavity of the desired shape and allowed to solidify. The solidified part, also called a casting, is then ejected or broken out of the mould. Casting is one of the oldest known shaping processes, dating back some 7,000 years.

Processes

Methods are divided into expendable-mould processes — sand casting, shell moulding, investment (lost-wax) casting, plaster and ceramic mould casting — and permanent-mould processes — gravity and pressure die casting, centrifugal casting, and continuous casting. Sand casting accounts for the largest share of production because of its versatility and low tooling cost.

Process steps

A pattern (with allowances for shrinkage) forms the mould cavity; cores create internal passages. Molten metal enters through a gating system designed to fill smoothly and avoid turbulence, then solidifies in risers-fed sections. After cooling, the casting is shaken out, cleaned (fettled), and finished by machining, grinding, or heat treatment.

Defects and applications

Common defects include porosity, shrinkage cavities, misruns, and inclusions, controlled by mould design and process monitoring. Castings range from gram-scale jewellery to engine blocks, machine beds, bells, and ship propellers weighing tonnes.
