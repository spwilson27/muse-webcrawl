Mandola

The mandola is a fretted, plucked string instrument of the mandolin family, pitched a fifth below the mandolin. It corresponds to the viola in the violin family: it is tuned C–G–D–A (from low to high), exactly like a viola, and reads the alto clef in orchestral scores. Like the mandolin, it normally has four courses of paired metal strings played with a plectrum.

Characteristics

The mandola has a larger body and longer scale length (typically about 42–45 cm) than the mandolin, giving it a warmer, mellower tone. Two body styles are common: the Neapolitan bowl-back, inherited from the Italian tradition, and flat-backed or carved-top designs developed in the United States and northern Europe.

Terminology

Naming in the mandolin family is confusing: in the United States the instrument tuned C–G–D–A is called the mandola, while in much of Europe the same term has historically been applied to the larger octave mandolin (tuned an octave below the mandolin). Players distinguish the "tenor mandola" and "octave mandola" accordingly. In mandolin orchestras the mandola supplies the inner harmony parts, and it is also used in folk music, including Celtic and bluegrass ensembles, where its range suits both melody and chordal accompaniment.

History

The mandola descends from the Renaissance and Baroque mandora and pandura family, by way of the 18th-century Neapolitan mandolin family, whose members were built in a range of sizes for consort playing. The modern mandolin orchestra movement of the late 19th and early 20th centuries standardised the mandola in its present alto role.
