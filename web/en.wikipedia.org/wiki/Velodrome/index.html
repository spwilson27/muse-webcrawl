Velodrome

A velodrome is an arena for track cycling. Modern velodromes feature steeply banked oval tracks consisting of two 180-degree circular bends connected by two straights. Riders use fixed-gear bicycles with no brakes, and the banking allows them to maintain high speeds through the turns.

The steep banking of a modern indoor velodrome, built for Olympic track cycling.

Track characteristics

Track length varies: modern Olympic and World Championship tracks are 250 metres, measured along a line 20 cm from the inner edge. Older and outdoor tracks may be 333.33 m, 400 m or longer (the historic Herne Hill track in London is about 450 m). Banking reaches 40–45 degrees on a 250 m track at the steepest point of the bends, easing to about 10–13 degrees on the straights. Surfaces are timber (often Siberian pine) indoors, and concrete or asphalt outdoors.

Markings and racing

Standard lines include the black measuring line, the red sprinters' line 90 cm above it, and the blue stayers' line near the top of the banking. Events held on velodromes include the sprint, keirin, team pursuit, individual pursuit, points race, scratch race, Madison and omnium, as well as hour-record attempts. Six-day racing, held on temporary indoor tracks, was a major spectator sport in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

History

Velodromes proliferated during the bicycle boom of the 1890s; famous early venues included the Vélodrome d'Hiver in Paris and Madison Square Garden in New York, whose name is preserved in the Madison event. Track cycling has been in the Olympic programme since 1896 (with interruptions), and indoor 250 m wooden tracks became the international standard in the late twentieth century. Notable modern velodromes include the Lee Valley VeloPark in London, built for the 2012 Olympics, and the Vélodrome de Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines near Paris.
