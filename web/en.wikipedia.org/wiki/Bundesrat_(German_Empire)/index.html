Bundesrat (German Empire)

The Bundesrat ("Federal Council") of the German Empire was, under the Constitution of 1871, the formal sovereign organ of the Reich: a council of delegates of the 25 federal states (Bundesstaaten), whose members were appointed by and voted on the instructions of the state governments. Together with the elected Reichstag it formed the legislature, and every imperial law required its consent.

Composition

The Bundesrat had 58 votes (61 after Alsace-Lorraine received voting representation in 1911). Prussia, with 17 votes, held a blocking minority: since constitutional amendments failed if 14 votes were cast against them, Prussia could veto any change. Bavaria had 6 votes, Saxony and Württemberg 4 each, Baden and Hesse 3, Mecklenburg-Schwerin and Brunswick 2, and the remaining states one each. The Imperial Chancellor, appointed by the Emperor, presided over the Bundesrat and conducted its business, linking the Prussian-dominated council to the imperial government.

Functions

Beyond legislation, the Bundesrat decided on the implementation of imperial laws, resolved disputes between states, and could approve the declaration of war and the conclusion of treaties in certain cases; its committee on foreign affairs, chaired by Bavaria, was a privilege of the reserved rights. In practice its work proceeded through specialised committees and was dominated by Prussian officials, while the Reichstag's budgetary power grew in importance. The institution was abolished in the revolution of 1918; its Weimar successor was the Reichsrat, and the modern Federal Republic's upper house, the Bundesrat, deliberately recalls the name.
