Composite armour is a type of vehicle armour consisting of layers of different materials such as metals, plastics, ceramics and sometimes air gaps. Most composite armours are lighter than equivalent all-metal armour but occupy a larger volume for the same resistance to penetration. Their multi-material construction is designed to defeat both kinetic-energy penetrators (long-rod APFSDS) and shaped-charge (HEAT) warheads, which defeat homogeneous steel by different mechanisms.

Mechanism

Hard ceramic tiles shatter and erode an incoming projectile, spreading its energy over a wider area, while ductile metallic and fibrous backing layers absorb the remaining energy and catch debris. Interfaces between materials of different acoustic impedance reflect and disrupt the jet of a shaped charge. Non-explosive reactive armour (NERA) sandwiches elastomer between plates which bulge under impact to feed material into the jet's path. Spacing and obliquity further increase effective protection; performance is quoted as RHA equivalence (RHAe) against specific threats.

History

The Soviet Union fielded the first composite arrays in the 1960s: the T-64's turret used cast steel with ceramic inserts and its glacis "Combination K" combined steel with glass-reinforced plastic. In Britain, researchers at the Fighting Vehicles Research and Development Establishment at Chobham, led by Gilbert Harvey, developed "Burlington" armour in the 1960s–70s, publicly known as Chobham armour. It debuted on the British Chieftain's successors and the American M1 Abrams (as "RLP" special armour) and remains classified in composition. Later variants — Dorchester on Challenger 2, and the Abrams' heavy armour packages incorporating depleted uranium mesh — progressively increased protection. Israel, Germany (Leopard 2), France (Leclerc), China and others fielded their own composite systems.

Applications

Composite arrays protect the turret cheeks and hull fronts of virtually all modern main battle tanks, where the threat is greatest; add-on composite and ceramic kits also armour infantry fighting vehicles, armoured personnel carriers and even helicopters. Body armour plates of boron or silicon carbide are a small-scale analogue of the same principle.
