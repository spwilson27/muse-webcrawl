Falun Mine

Falun Mine (Swedish: Falu Gruva) was a copper mine in Falun, Sweden, that operated for a millennium, from the 10th century to 1992. It produced as much as two-thirds of Europe's copper needs in the 17th century and helped fund Sweden's emergence as a great power. The mine is now a museum and, together with the surrounding Mining Area of the Great Copper Mountain in Falun, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2001.

History

Mining at Falun likely began in the late 8th or 10th century, and the first documented reference dates to 1288, when King Magnus III acquired shares in the mine. At its peak in the mid-17th century, the mine employed over a thousand workers and produced around 3,000 tonnes of crude copper per year. In 1687 a massive collapse created the Great Pit (Stora stöten), a chasm nearly 100 metres deep — miraculously, no one was killed, as the collapse occurred on Midsummer's Day when the miners were on holiday.

Falu red and byproducts

Waste from the mine's roasting heaps gave rise to Falu red, the iconic pigment used to paint Swedish wooden houses and barns. The mine also produced iron, silver, gold, sulfur, and vitriol. Another famous episode is the story of Fet-Mats, a miner whose remarkably preserved body was found in the mine in 1719, some 42 years after his death.

Closure and heritage

Production declined through the 18th and 19th centuries as richer ores were found elsewhere. The mine closed on 8 December 1992. Today visitors can tour underground galleries and the museum, and the site anchors the Ecomuseum Bergslagen.
