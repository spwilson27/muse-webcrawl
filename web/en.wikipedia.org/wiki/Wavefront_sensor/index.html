Wavefront sensor

Wavefront sensor
TypesShack–Hartmann, curvature sensor, pyramid sensor, interferometer
UsesAdaptive optics, optical testing, ophthalmology

A wavefront sensor is a device for measuring the aberrations of an optical wavefront. Such sensors are a central component of adaptive optics systems, where they measure distortions in incoming light — for example those caused by atmospheric turbulence — so that a deformable mirror can correct them in real time. They are also used in optical fabrication and testing, laser beam characterisation, and in ophthalmology for measuring aberrations of the human eye.

Shack–Hartmann sensor

The most common design is the Shack–Hartmann wavefront sensor, developed from Johannes Hartmann's 1900 test plate and refined by Roland Shack in the 1970s. An array of small lenses (lenslets) divides the wavefront into subapertures, each focusing its portion of the beam onto a detector. Where the local wavefront slope differs from flat, the focal spot is displaced; measuring the displacements of all spots yields the wavefront gradient, from which the wavefront shape can be reconstructed, often in terms of Zernike polynomials.

Other types

Curvature sensors infer wavefront curvature from intensity patterns measured on either side of the focal plane and are widely used in astronomy. The pyramid wavefront sensor, introduced by Roberto Ragazzoni in 1996, uses a glass pyramid at the focal plane and offers higher sensitivity, and is used on large telescopes. Interferometers measure wavefront phase directly and are standard in precision optical metrology, though they are generally less suited to real-time atmospheric correction.

Applications

Wavefront sensors enable ground-based telescopes to approach the sharpness of space telescopes, guide laser eye surgery (aberrometry), characterise microscope and camera optics, and monitor high-power laser beams.
