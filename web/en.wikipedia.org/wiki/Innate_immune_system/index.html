The innate immune system, also known as the nonspecific immune system, is one of the two main immunity strategies found in vertebrates (the other being the adaptive immune system). It is an evolutionarily older defense strategy and is the dominant immune system response found in plants, fungi, insects, and primitive multicellular organisms. Unlike the adaptive immune system, the innate system responds to pathogens in a generic way and does not confer long-lasting immunity in the classical sense.

The major functions of the vertebrate innate immune system include recruiting immune cells to sites of infection through the production of chemical factors called cytokines; activation of the complement cascade to identify bacteria and promote clearance of dead cells; identification and removal of foreign substances by specialized white blood cells; and acting as a physical and chemical barrier to infectious agents.

Anatomical barriers

The first line of defense against infectious agents is the anatomical barriers: the skin and mucous membranes. The skin forms a nearly impenetrable physical barrier, while mucosal surfaces trap microbes in mucus and move them away by ciliary action. Chemical defenses at these surfaces include lysozyme in tears and saliva, acidic gastric juice, and antimicrobial peptides such as defensins.

Inflammation

Inflammation is one of the first responses of the immune system to infection. It is stimulated by chemical factors released by injured cells, including histamine, bradykinin, serotonin, and prostaglandins. These chemicals cause blood vessels to dilate and become more permeable, producing the classic signs of redness, heat, swelling, and pain, and they recruit phagocytes to the site of injury.

Complement system

The complement system is a biochemical cascade of plasma proteins that helps clear microbes and damaged cells, promotes inflammation, and attacks the pathogen's plasma membrane through the membrane attack complex. It can be activated by the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways.

Cellular barriers

White blood cells (leukocytes) are the cellular arm of innate immunity. They include:

  • Phagocytes — macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells, which engulf and digest pathogens and debris; neutrophils are the most abundant phagocyte in the blood.
  • Mast cells and basophils — resident sentinels that release histamine and coordinate inflammatory and allergic responses.
  • Natural killer (NK) cells — cytotoxic lymphocytes that destroy virus-infected and tumor cells that have downregulated MHC class I molecules.
  • Eosinophils — cells specialized for defense against multicellular parasites.

Pattern recognition receptors

Innate immune cells identify microbes using pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), germline-encoded sensors that bind conserved microbial structures called pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). The best-characterized family is the Toll-like receptors (TLRs), first identified in Drosophila and central to sensing bacterial lipopolysaccharide, viral double-stranded RNA, and unmethylated CpG DNA. Intracellular sensors, such as RIG-I-like receptors and the inflammasome-forming NOD-like receptors, detect pathogens that breach the cell membrane.

Innate immunity in invertebrates and plants

Invertebrates rely entirely on innate mechanisms, including phagocytosis, encapsulation, antimicrobial peptides, and the phenoloxidase cascade. Plants lack mobile immune cells and instead use pattern-triggered immunity at each cell, reinforced by effector-triggered immunity and systemic acquired resistance mediated by salicylic acid.
