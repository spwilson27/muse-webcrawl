Salinity

Salinity is the saltiness or amount of salt dissolved in a body of water, called saline water. It is an important factor in determining many aspects of the chemistry of natural waters and of biological processes within them, and is a thermodynamic state variable that, along with temperature and pressure, governs physical characteristics like the density and heat capacity of the water.

Measurement

Historically salinity was measured by evaporating a water sample and weighing the residue, or by titration of chloride. Modern oceanography defines practical salinity (from the Practical Salinity Scale 1978, PSS-78) by the electrical conductivity ratio of a seawater sample to a standard potassium chloride solution; it is reported in practical salinity units (psu), which are effectively dimensionless. Average ocean salinity is about 35 psu—roughly 35 grams of dissolved salts per kilogram of seawater, dominated by sodium and chloride ions. Since 2010, TEOS-10 has defined absolute salinity in grams per kilogram.

Variation and classification

Surface salinity varies with evaporation, precipitation, river runoff and ice formation: the subtropical Atlantic is relatively salty, while high-latitude oceans and coastal seas such as the Baltic (under 10 psu) are fresher. The Red Sea exceeds 40 psu, and hypersaline lakes like the Dead Sea exceed 200 g/kg. Waters are classified as fresh (below 0.5 psu), brackish (0.5–30), saline (30–50) and brine (above 50).

Importance

Together with temperature, salinity controls seawater density and thus drives thermohaline circulation. Salinity also determines what organisms can live in a body of water, affects soil quality in irrigated agriculture (soil salinization being a major cause of land degradation), and is monitored from satellites such as SMOS and Aquarius to study the global water cycle.
