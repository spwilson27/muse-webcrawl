Alfred Thayer Mahan (27 September 1840 – 1 December 1914) was a United States naval officer and historian, widely regarded as the most influential naval strategist of his era. His book The Influence of Sea Power upon History, 1660–1783 (1890) argued that command of the sea, won by concentrated battle fleets, had been the decisive factor in great-power success, and it profoundly shaped naval building and strategy worldwide before the First World War.

Naval career

The son of Dennis Hart Mahan, the celebrated West Point professor of engineering, Mahan graduated from the U.S. Naval Academy in 1859 and served on blockade duty during the Civil War and on stations in South America and Asia. Disliking sea duty, he found his vocation ashore: appointed lecturer at the new Naval War College in Newport in 1885 and twice its president, he developed his lecture notes into the books that made his reputation.

Sea power theory

Mahan held that national greatness and prosperity depend on maritime commerce, and commerce on a navy able to secure command of the sea by defeating the enemy's fleet in decisive battle. He listed geographical position, physical conformation, extent of territory, population, national character and government character as the conditions of sea power, and he urged the United States to build battleships, acquire coaling stations and colonies, fortify the Caribbean and construct an isthmian canal. Follow-up volumes extended the analysis to the French Revolution and Empire and to American policy.

Impact and criticism

Mahan's works were devoured by Theodore Roosevelt, the Kaiser Wilhelm II — who kept the book on his ships — and the Imperial Japanese Navy, and they helped fuel the pre-1914 battleship race. Critics, notably Julian Corbett, countered that command of the sea is normally in dispute rather than absolute, and that Mahan underrated commerce protection, combined operations and the submarine — a critique sharpened by the First World War. His concepts of sea power, chokepoints and fleet concentration nonetheless remain foundational in strategic studies. Mahan retired as a captain in 1896, was promoted rear admiral on the retired list in 1906, served as a delegate at the Hague Peace Conference of 1899, and died in 1914.
