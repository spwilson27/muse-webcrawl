The intersection of 18th Street and Vine Street, Kansas City

18th and Vine is a street intersection and historic district in Kansas City, Missouri, that was the centre of the city's African American community and of Kansas City jazz during the early and mid-20th century. Segregation concentrated Black businesses, theatres, clubs, and professional offices around the intersection, making it a self-contained cultural and economic hub.

History

From the 1920s through the 1940s, the district's clubs — often operating around the clock under the permissive Pendergast political machine — nurtured Kansas City jazz, a blues-based, riff-driven style associated with Count Basie, Jay McShann, Mary Lou Williams, and the young Charlie Parker. The Mutual Musicians Foundation building on Highland Avenue served as the local of the Black musicians' union and as an after-hours jam-session venue. The district also housed the Kansas City Monarchs' offices and the offices of the Kansas City Call newspaper.

Decline and revival

The district declined after the Second World War as desegregation dispersed residents and businesses and jazz's popularity waned. From the 1980s onward, civic investment revived the area: the American Jazz Museum and the Negro Leagues Baseball Museum opened in the Museums at 18th & Vine complex in 1997, the Gem Theater was restored as a performing arts venue, and the district was listed on the National Register of Historic Places, anchoring heritage tourism in Kansas City.
