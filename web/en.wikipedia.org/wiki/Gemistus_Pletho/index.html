Georgios Gemistos Plethon (Greek: Γεώργιος Γεμιστὸς Πλήθων; c. 1355/1360 – 1452/1454), commonly known as Gemistus Pletho, was a Byzantine scholar of Neoplatonic philosophy. He was a leading figure in the revival of Platonic studies in the late Byzantine world and, through his visit to Italy in 1438–1439, one of the catalysts of the Renaissance revival of Platonism in Western Europe.

Life

Pletho was born in Constantinople and spent most of his career at Mystras in the Peloponnese, where he served as a teacher and adviser at the court of the Despotate of the Morea. He taught a circle of students including Bessarion and Mark Eugenikos, and wrote commentaries on Plato, Aristotle, and Zoroastrian and Hermetic texts. As a delegate attached to the Byzantine delegation at the Council of Ferrara–Florence (1438–1439), which sought union of the Greek and Latin churches, his lectures on Plato in Florence captivated Italian humanists.

Philosophy

In his short treatise On the Differences of Aristotle from Plato (1439), Pletho attacked the medieval preference for Aristotle and argued for Plato's superiority, igniting a debate that continued for decades. His major work, the Laws (Nomoi), composed late in life and only partly surviving, set out a utopian political and religious order modeled on Plato, blending Neoplatonic theology with reverence for Zeus and the ancient Greek gods — effectively a program for a revived pagan Hellenism. After his death, the Orthodox patriarch Gennadios II ordered the book burned; only fragments and summaries survive. Pletho's political memoranda to the Byzantine emperors proposed sweeping economic and military reforms of the Morea.

Legacy

Pletho's Florentine lectures are traditionally credited with inspiring Cosimo de' Medici to found a Platonic Academy in Florence, later associated with Marsilio Ficino, whose Latin translations of Plato transformed Renaissance thought. Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta later brought Pletho's remains to Rimini, where they were interred in the Tempio Malatestiano.
