Lincrusta is a deeply embossed wallcovering invented in 1877 by the English entrepreneur Frederick Walton, who had earlier patented linoleum. The name derives from the Latin linum (flax) and crusta (relief). Like linoleum, it is made from oxidised linseed oil, in this case formed into a thick patterned layer on a paper backing.

Characteristics and use

Lincrusta is supplied in rolls like wallpaper but is far more durable: once hung with a strong adhesive, the embossed relief hardens and can be painted, glazed, gilded or otherwise finished, and it resists scuffs and moisture better than paper. It became fashionable in Victorian and Edwardian interiors for hallways, dining rooms and staircases, and was used aboard ocean liners, in railway carriages and in grand public buildings; it can be found in the White House and in numerous National Trust properties.

History

Walton launched the product as "Lincrusta-Walton". Its cheaper embossed-paper rival, Anaglypta, was developed by one of Walton's former employees in 1887. Lincrusta production continued through the twentieth century despite changing fashions, and the material is still manufactured in Britain for the restoration and heritage markets, using patterns that in some cases descend from the original nineteenth-century rollers.
