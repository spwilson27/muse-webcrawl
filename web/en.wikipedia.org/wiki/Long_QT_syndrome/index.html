Long QT syndrome

Long QT syndrome
SpecialtyCardiology
SymptomsFainting, seizures, palpitations, sudden cardiac death

Long QT syndrome (LQTS) is a condition affecting the electrical activity of the heart, characterised by a prolonged QT interval on the electrocardiogram (ECG). It predisposes affected individuals to fast, chaotic heart rhythms — in particular the ventricular arrhythmia torsades de pointes — which can cause fainting, seizures, and sudden cardiac death.

Signs and symptoms

Many people with long QT syndrome have no symptoms and are diagnosed only through family screening or an incidental ECG. When symptoms occur they most often take the form of syncope (fainting) triggered by exercise, emotional stress, sudden auditory stimuli such as an alarm clock, or swimming, depending on the genetic subtype. Episodes may be mistaken for epilepsy because the loss of blood flow to the brain can cause seizure-like activity.

Causes

LQTS may be inherited or acquired. Congenital forms result from mutations in genes encoding cardiac ion channels: the most common are KCNQ1 (LQT1), KCNH2 (LQT2), and SCN5A (LQT3). The autosomal recessive Jervell and Lange-Nielsen syndrome is associated with congenital deafness, while the autosomal dominant Romano–Ward syndrome is not. Acquired long QT is most often caused by medications that block the hERG potassium channel, or by low blood potassium, magnesium, or calcium.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on measuring the corrected QT interval (QTc) on the ECG, together with the clinical history and, increasingly, genetic testing. A QTc longer than about 480 ms in the absence of other causes is generally considered diagnostic. The Schwartz score combines ECG features, symptoms, and family history to estimate the probability of the condition.

Management

Management includes avoiding QT-prolonging drugs and known triggers, beta-blocker therapy (such as nadolol or propranolol), and in high-risk patients an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD). Left cardiac sympathetic denervation surgery is an option for patients who continue to have events despite medication. Correcting electrolyte abnormalities is essential in acquired cases.
