Jersey Legal French

Region: Jersey, Channel Islands
Language family: Romance (langues d'oïl)
Use: Ceremonial and legal formulae
Related: Norman French, Law French, Jèrriais

Jersey Legal French is the variety of French historically used for legal, administrative, and ceremonial purposes on the island of Jersey. Descended from the Norman French brought to the Channel Islands in the medieval period — closely related to Law French as used in England — it long served as the official language of the island's legislature, the States of Jersey, and of its courts and parish assemblies.

History and use

For centuries, Jersey's laws were enacted in French, court proceedings were recorded in French, and contracts — particularly conveyances of land before the Royal Court — were drawn up in French. Although English progressively displaced French in ordinary administration during the 20th century (the States adopted English for debates in 1900, and laws began to be drafted in English), legal French persisted: land contracts in French remained standard until reforms near the end of the century.

Certain formulae survive in ceremonial use. The traditional loyal toast at official functions is given in French: "La Reine, notre Duc" ("The Queen, our Duke"), reflecting the sovereign's title in the islands as Duke of Normandy. The cry "Haro!" of the ancient Norman clameur de haro — a legal injunction still occasionally invoked in the Channel Islands — is likewise rooted in this Norman legal tradition. Some acts and official documents continue to be published in both English and French.

Characteristics

Jersey Legal French preserves archaic spellings, Norman vocabulary, and set phrases fossilized from earlier centuries, distinct from both modern standard French and the island's vernacular Norman dialect, Jèrriais.
