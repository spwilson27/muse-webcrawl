Social progress

Social progress is the idea that societies can improve in terms of their social, political, and economic structures, and in the well-being, rights, and capabilities of their members. The concept became influential during the Age of Enlightenment, when thinkers argued that society could be bettered through knowledge, reason, and reform — in contrast to older cyclical views of history. Social progress may be measured by indicators such as life expectancy, literacy, poverty rates, gender equality, environmental quality, and access to education and healthcare.

History of the idea

Enlightenment philosophers including Condorcet (Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind, 1795) articulated staged theories of human improvement. In the 19th century, thinkers such as Herbert Spencer and Karl Marx developed their own (very different) theories of societal evolution. The concept underpinned movements for the abolition of slavery, universal suffrage, labour rights, and public education. In the 20th century, faith in progress was shaken by world wars, totalitarianism, and environmental harm, prompting critiques by figures such as Theodor Adorno and by postmodern and postcolonial writers, who questioned whether "progress" is a universal or culturally specific narrative; yet measurable improvements in health, longevity, and material welfare continued in most regions.

Measurement

Because gross domestic product captures only economic output, alternative indices attempt to measure social progress directly. The Social Progress Index, published by the non-profit Social Progress Imperative since 2013–2014, ranks countries on 50+ indicators grouped into basic human needs, foundations of well-being, and opportunity. Other measures include the Human Development Index (HDI) of the United Nations Development Programme, the OECD Better Life Index, and the Genuine Progress Indicator. Long-run data projects such as Our World in Data document global trends in health, education, and poverty that bear on debates about progress.

Criticism

Critics note trade-offs between economic growth and the environment, the persistence of inequality alongside aggregate improvement, and the risk that metrics encode the values of those who design them. Others, such as Steven Pinker in Enlightenment Now (2018), argue that data show broad, sustained progress on health, prosperity, knowledge, and safety, and defend continued meliorism — the belief that the world can be made better by human effort.
