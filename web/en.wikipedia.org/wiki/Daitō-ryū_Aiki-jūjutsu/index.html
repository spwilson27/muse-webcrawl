Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu

Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu is a Japanese martial art focusing on joint locks, throws, pins and atemi strikes, best known as the principal technical influence on aikido. It was made public in the late 19th and early 20th centuries by Takeda Sōkaku, who taught it widely across Japan.

History

According to the school's tradition, its techniques descend from the Minamoto clan and were preserved within the Takeda family of Aizu, passed down through generations as a close-combat art of the samurai. Sōkaku, who studied several martial traditions including sumo and swordsmanship, began teaching Daitō-ryū around the turn of the 20th century. His students included Morihei Ueshiba, founder of aikido, and Choi Yong-sool, founder of hapkido, both of whom adapted its methods into their own arts.

Practice

Training is centred on paired kata. The curriculum is organised into scrolls (hiden mokuroku, aiki no jutsu, hiden ōgi, and more advanced transmissions). Central principles include aiki — blending with and unbalancing an attacker at the moment of contact — and precise application of joint manipulation rather than muscular strength.
