An AMBER Alert (also written as Amber Alert) is a child abduction emergency alert message system. It originated in the United States in 1996 after the abduction and murder of nine-year-old Amber Hagerman in Arlington, Texas. AMBER is a backronym for America's Missing: Broadcast Emergency Response. The alerts are distributed through radio stations, television, highway signs, mobile phones via Wireless Emergency Alerts, and the internet, asking the public to help locate an abducted child and the suspect.

Criteria

For an alert to be issued, guidelines recommended by the U.S. Department of Justice generally require that law enforcement confirm a child aged 17 or younger has been abducted, that the child is believed to be in imminent danger of serious bodily injury or death, and that there is enough descriptive information about the victim, the abductor, or a vehicle to make the alert useful.

History

Amber Hagerman was kidnapped while riding her bicycle in January 1996; her body was found four days later. In response, Dallas–Fort Worth broadcasters worked with local police to develop an early warning system for abducted children. The system spread across the United States and was later adopted in Canada, Mexico, and several other countries. In 2003, the federal PROTECT Act established a national AMBER Alert coordinator.

Effectiveness and criticism

Supporters credit the system with the recovery of many children. Critics have raised concerns about "alert fatigue," noting that many alerts involve family custody disputes rather than stranger abductions, and that overuse may reduce public responsiveness.
