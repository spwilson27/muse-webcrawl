Sicilian wine

Sicilian wine is wine made on the Mediterranean island of Sicily in Italy. The region has one of the oldest wine traditions in Europe and today has more vineyard area than any other Italian region, though much of its production historically went to bulk wine and blending. Since the late 20th century, Sicily has gained an international reputation for quality wines, both from indigenous grape varieties and from wines grown on the volcanic slopes of Mount Etna.

History

Viticulture in Sicily dates to the Mycenaean and Phoenician eras and flourished under Greek colonisation from the 8th century BC. The fortified wine of Marsala, developed by English merchants in the 18th century, became the island's most famous export. Phylloxera devastated Sicilian vineyards in the late 19th century, and for much of the 20th century the island's enormous output was shipped in bulk to strengthen wines elsewhere in Italy and France. A quality revolution from the 1990s onward refocused production on bottled estate wines.

Grapes and wines

The most important native red grape is Nero d'Avola, producing full, fruity reds; other reds include Frappato, Nerello Mascalese and Nerello Cappuccio (the basis of Etna Rosso), Perricone and the rare Nocera. Leading white varieties include Catarratto (the most planted), Grillo (a base wine of Marsala, now also made as a dry white), Inzolia, Carricante (the principal grape of Etna Bianco), Zibibbo (Muscat of Alexandria, used for the sweet wines of Pantelleria), and Malvasia.

Appellations

Sicily's DOCG is Cerasuolo di Vittoria, a blend of Nero d'Avola and Frappato. Notable DOCs include Etna, Marsala, Faro, Mamertino, Alcamo, and the island-wide Sicilia DOC, established in 2011 to promote the region's wines under a single banner. Sweet passito wines from the island of Pantelleria, made from sun-dried Zibibbo grapes, are among Italy's most prized dessert wines.
