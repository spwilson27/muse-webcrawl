Cahuachi, overlooking the Nazca River in southern Peru, was the principal ceremonial centre of the Nazca culture, flourishing roughly from 1 AD to 500 AD. Situated about 28 kilometres from the modern city of Nazca, the site consists of more than forty adobe mounds and pyramids spread across the desert pampa, of which the largest, the Great Pyramid, rises some 28 metres above the plain.

Function

Excavations, most notably by the Italian archaeologist Giuseppe Orefici since the 1980s, have shown that Cahuachi had only a small permanent population. Instead, it appears to have been a pilgrimage centre, visited seasonally by large numbers of people for ceremonies connected with agriculture and water in the arid Nazca environment. Offerings found at the site include fine polychrome pottery, textiles, musical instruments such as panpipes, and trophy heads.

Cahuachi's period of greatest construction coincided with the florescence of the Nazca lines, and the ceremonial centre is thought to have been closely connected with the geoglyphs etched into the surrounding desert. The site was gradually abandoned around 450–500 AD, following environmental stresses including flooding and earthquakes; its structures were ritually sealed.
