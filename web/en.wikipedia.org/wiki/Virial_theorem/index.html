Virial theorem

In statistical mechanics, the virial theorem provides a general equation that relates the average over time of the total kinetic energy of a stable system of discrete particles, bound by a conservative force, to that of the total potential energy of the system. Mathematically, the theorem states that the average kinetic energy ⟨T⟩ equals minus one half the average of the virial ⟨Σ Fk · rk⟩:

⟨T⟩ = −(1/2) ⟨ Σk Fk · rk ⟩

For a system bound by an inverse-square force such as gravity or the Coulomb force, this reduces to the celebrated relation 2⟨T⟩ + ⟨U⟩ = 0, so the total energy is E = ⟨T⟩ + ⟨U⟩ = −⟨T⟩ = ⟨U⟩/2. The theorem was formulated by Rudolf Clausius in 1870; the word "virial" derives from the Latin vis, meaning force or energy.

Derivation outline

The theorem follows from considering the time derivative of the moment of inertia of the system, I = Σ mk|rk|². Twice differentiating and time-averaging over a long interval, the average of the second derivative vanishes for a bounded, stable system, leaving the balance between kinetic energy and the force–position correlations known as the virial of Clausius.

Applications

The virial theorem is a workhorse of astrophysics: it allows the total mass of a galaxy or galaxy cluster to be estimated from the observed velocity dispersion of its members. Fritz Zwicky's application of the theorem to the Coma Cluster in 1933 produced the first evidence for dark matter, since the inferred mass vastly exceeded the luminous mass. In stellar structure, the theorem explains the counterintuitive fact that a contracting, radiating star heats up (gravothermal instability), and in chemistry it relates kinetic and potential energies of molecules in equilibrium geometries. A quantum-mechanical version holds for stationary states of the Schrödinger equation.
