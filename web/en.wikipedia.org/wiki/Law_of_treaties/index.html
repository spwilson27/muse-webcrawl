Law of treaties

Law of treaties
Key instrument: Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969)
Entered into force: 27 January 1980

The law of treaties is the body of international law governing written agreements between states and other subjects of international law. It is largely codified in the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT), adopted in 1969.

Definition and scope

Article 2 of the VCLT defines a treaty as an international agreement concluded between states in written form and governed by international law, whatever its designation. The Convention applies only to treaties between states concluded after its entry into force, though many provisions reflect customary international law.

Formation

Treaties are negotiated by representatives with full powers, adopted by vote or consensus, authenticated, and enter into force after consent to be bound — by signature, ratification, acceptance or accession. Reservations may be made unless prohibited, under Articles 19–23.

Interpretation and validity

Articles 31–33 require interpretation in good faith according to ordinary meaning, context, and object and purpose, supplemented by subsequent practice and travaux préparatoires. A treaty is void if procured by coercion, or if it conflicts with a peremptory norm (jus cogens). The principle pacta sunt servanda (Article 26) obliges parties to perform treaties in good faith.

Termination

Treaties may terminate by expiry, withdrawal under their terms, material breach, impossibility, or fundamental change of circumstances (rebus sic stantibus, Article 62, narrowly construed).
