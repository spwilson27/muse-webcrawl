Vector calculus


A vector field in the plane

Vector calculus is a branch of mathematics concerned with differentiation and integration of vector fields, primarily in two and three dimensions. It extends the methods of calculus to scalar fields and vector fields and provides the language of classical electromagnetism, fluid dynamics, and much of physics and engineering.

Basic objects

A scalar field assigns a number (such as temperature) to each point of space; a vector field assigns a vector (such as velocity or force) to each point. The fundamental differential operator is the del operator ∇ ("nabla").

Differential operations

Gradient

The gradient of a scalar field f is the vector field pointing in the direction of steepest increase of f:

grad f = ∇f = (∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y, ∂f/∂z)

Divergence

The divergence of a vector field F measures the net outward flow per unit volume at a point — the strength of sources or sinks:

div F = ∇·F

Curl

The curl of a vector field measures its local rotation or circulation density:

curl F = ∇×F

Laplacian

The Laplacian Δf = ∇·∇f combines second derivatives and appears in the heat, wave, and Poisson equations.

Integral theorems

Vector calculus is unified by integral theorems relating integrals over a region to integrals over its boundary:

  • Gradient theorem — line integrals of a gradient depend only on endpoints.
  • Divergence theorem (Gauss's theorem) — the flux of a field out of a closed surface equals the integral of its divergence over the enclosed volume.
  • Stokes' theorem — the circulation of a field around a closed curve equals the flux of its curl through any surface bounded by the curve.
  • Green's theorem — the two-dimensional case of Stokes' theorem.

All are special cases of the generalised Stokes theorem for differential forms.

Applications

Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism, the Navier–Stokes equations of fluid flow, and the equations of elasticity and gravitation are all formulated in the language of vector calculus. It also underlies computer graphics, robotics, and optimisation.

History

The subject developed in the nineteenth century from the work of Hamilton (quaternions), Gibbs, and Heaviside, whose competing notations were resolved in favour of the modern Gibbs–Heaviside system popularised by E. B. Wilson's 1901 textbook Vector Analysis.
