Japanology

Image placeholder: Japanology

Japanology, also known as Japanese studies, is the study of Japan, incorporating the social sciences, humanities, and the study of the Japanese language. Japanese studies in Europe may be traced to the travels of early missionaries and merchants in the 16th and 17th centuries, and was institutionalized in the 19th century.

History

The field developed alongside Western contact with Japan. Early works include Engelbert Kaempfer's History of Japan (1727). Following the opening of Japan in the 1850s, scholars such as Ernest Mason Satow, Basil Hall Chamberlain, and William George Aston made foundational contributions in Britain, while Philipp Franz von Siebold worked in the Netherlands and Germany.

In the United States, Japanese studies expanded enormously during and after World War II, supported by government funding for area studies. Figures such as Edwin O. Reischauer, Donald Keene, and Edward Seidensticker became influential interpreters of Japanese literature and history.

Scope

Japanology covers history, literature, religion, art, economics, politics, sociology, and anthropology of Japan. Professional associations include the Association for Asian Studies, the European Association for Japanese Studies, and the British Association for Japanese Studies. The Japanese term Nihonjinron refers to theories of Japanese cultural uniqueness, a topic critically examined within the field.
