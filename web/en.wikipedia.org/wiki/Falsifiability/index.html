Falsifiability

Falsifiability
FieldPhilosophy of science
Proposed byKarl Popper, The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934)
CriterionA theory is scientific if it forbids some observable states of affairs — it can be tested and potentially refuted
[Image: A swan silhouette split half white and half black, Popper's emblem of refutation]

Falsifiability (refutability) is the property of a statement or theory that it can be contradicted by observation. Karl Popper made it his criterion of demarcation between science and non-science: a scientific theory must take risks — forbid outcomes — whereas, he argued, Marxist history and psychoanalysis could explain everything and so explained nothing. The sighting of one black swan falsifies 'all swans are white', which no number of white swans can verify (the Problem of induction).

Criticisms

Historians and philosophers (Kuhn, Lakatos, Feyerabend) showed scientists rarely abandon theories at a single counter-instance (the Duhem–Quine problem: tests implicate auxiliary assumptions too), so falsificationism survives more as a norm of testability than a description of practice. It remains legally and culturally influential (cited in US court rulings on creation science).
