Lamian War

The Lamian War, or the Hellenic War, was fought by a coalition of Greek cities, led by Athens, against Macedon and its ally Boeotia in 323–322 BC, immediately after the death of Alexander the Great. It takes its name from the siege of the Macedonian regent Antipater at Lamia in Thessaly. Although the Greeks won early successes, Macedonian reinforcements from Asia reversed the war; the decisive defeat at the Battle of Crannon in 322 BC ended the revolt and confirmed Macedonian domination of Greece.

Background

The revolt was triggered by Alexander's Exiles Decree of 324 BC, ordering Greek cities to take back their exiles — a measure that threatened Athens' hold on Samos and outraged many states — and by news of Alexander's death in Babylon in June 323 BC. The Athenian orator Hyperides and the general Leosthenes, who had assembled a force of discharged mercenaries at Taenarum, led the war party. Athens voted for war, recalled the exiled Demosthenes, and built a coalition including Aetolia, Thessaly, and much of central Greece.

Course of the war

Leosthenes occupied Thermopylae and defeated a Boeotian force; Antipater, caught with only a small army, was beaten and shut himself in the town of Lamia, where the Greeks besieged him through the winter. Leosthenes was killed by a stone or spear while inspecting the siege works, a severe blow. At sea, the Macedonian admiral Cleitus defeated the Athenian fleet, culminating in the Battle of Amorgos (322 BC), which ended Athenian sea power forever. On land, Leonnatus brought reinforcements from Asia but was killed in a cavalry battle in Thessaly; the arrival of Craterus with veteran troops nonetheless gave Antipater superiority. At the Battle of Crannon (September 322 BC) the Macedonians narrowly defeated the allied army, and the coalition dissolved as each city made separate peace.

Aftermath

Antipater imposed harsh terms on Athens: a Macedonian garrison in Munychia, a property-based oligarchy that disenfranchised thousands of citizens, and the surrender of the anti-Macedonian orators. Demosthenes took poison on Calauria to escape arrest; Hyperides was executed. The war marked the effective end of Athens as an independent power and of classical Greek resistance to Macedon.
