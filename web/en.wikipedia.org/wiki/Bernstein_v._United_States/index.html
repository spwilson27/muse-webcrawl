Bernstein v. United States

Bernstein v. United States is a series of cases brought by Daniel J. Bernstein challenging United States export regulations on cryptography in the 1990s. The litigation established, in the Ninth Circuit, that computer source code is speech protected by the First Amendment, and it contributed to the Clinton administration's relaxation of Cold War-era controls on encryption software.

Background

Under the International Traffic in Arms Regulations, cryptographic software was classified as a munition, and publishing it on the internet counted as an export requiring a licence. Bernstein, then a graduate student at UC Berkeley, wished to publish the source code and a paper describing his "Snuffle" encryption system. In 1995, represented by the Electronic Frontier Foundation, he sued the State and Commerce Departments.

Litigation

Judge Marilyn Hall Patel ruled in 1996 that source code is speech, and in 1997 that the export rules operated as an unconstitutional prior restraint. After jurisdiction over encryption exports moved to the Commerce Department's Export Administration Regulations, the case continued as Bernstein v. U.S. Department of Commerce. In May 1999 a Ninth Circuit panel affirmed that the licensing regime burdened protected expression, though the opinion was withdrawn when the court voted to rehear the case en banc.

Aftermath

In January 2000 the administration substantially liberalised the export rules, allowing most publicly available encryption source code to be published after a notification. The Ninth Circuit rehearing never proceeded and the case was eventually dismissed as moot. Together with the Junger case in the Sixth Circuit, Bernstein is a landmark in the legal treatment of code as speech and in the 1990s "crypto wars".
