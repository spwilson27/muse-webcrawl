Acid-fastness

Acid-fastness is a physical property of certain bacteria and eukaryotic cells, as well as some sub-cellular structures, specifically their resistance to decolorization by acids during laboratory staining procedures. Once stained as part of a sample, these organisms can resist the acid and/or ethanol-based decolorization procedures common in many staining protocols, hence the name acid-fast.

Mechanism

The high mycolic acid content of certain bacterial cell walls, most notably those of the genus Mycobacterium, accounts for the staining pattern of poor absorption followed by high retention. Mycolic acids are long-chain fatty acids that form a waxy, hydrophobic barrier. The primary stain, carbol fuchsin, is driven into the cell (often with heat, in the Ziehl–Neelsen method) and is then retained even when the smear is treated with acid alcohol, while other bacteria are decolorised and take up the counterstain, such as methylene blue.

Acid-fast organisms

  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis and M. leprae, the causes of tuberculosis and leprosy — strongly acid-fast.
  • Other mycobacteria, including the M. avium complex.
  • Nocardia species — weakly or partially acid-fast.
  • The oocysts of some parasitic protozoa, such as Cryptosporidium and Cyclospora.

Staining methods

The classic method is the Ziehl–Neelsen stain, developed in the 1880s from the work of Paul Ehrlich, Franz Ziehl, and Friedrich Neelsen. The cold Kinyoun method uses a more concentrated stain without heat, and fluorescent auramine–rhodamine staining is widely used for screening because acid-fast bacilli can be detected at lower magnification.

Clinical significance

Detection of acid-fast bacilli (AFB) in sputum remains a rapid front-line test for pulmonary tuberculosis worldwide, although culture and nucleic-acid tests are more sensitive and specific. Acid-fast staining is also used on tissue sections and in veterinary and parasitology laboratories.
