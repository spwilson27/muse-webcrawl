Sitar

ClassificationString instrument (plucked lute)
OriginIndian subcontinent (developed by the 18th century)
RelatedSetar; tanbur; veena; surbahar

The sitar (Urdu: ستار; Hindi: सितार, sitār) is a plucked stringed instrument, originating from the Indian subcontinent, used in Hindustani classical music. It has a long hollow neck, a gourd-shaped body, up to 20 movable metal frets, and typically six or seven main playing strings above 11–13 sympathetic strings (taraf) that resonate to produce its distinctive shimmering sound. The name derives from the Persian setar, "three strings".

History

The modern sitar developed in Mughal India from the fusion of Persian long-necked lutes with Indian instruments such as the veena; popular tradition credits the poet-musician Amir Khusrau (13th–14th century), though the instrument in its present form is documented from the 18th century. Ravi Shankar (1920–2020), its most famous exponent, brought the sitar to worldwide audiences, teaching George Harrison of the Beatles, whose use of the instrument on "Norwegian Wood" (1965) sparked a Western vogue for raga-influenced pop. Other leading players include Vilayat Khan, Nikhil Banerjee, and Anoushka Shankar.

Construction and playing

The body is made from a seasoned pumpkin gourd or carved wood; the frets are curved so that strings can be pulled sideways to bend notes (meend), a technique essential to rendering ragas. The player plucks with a wire plectrum (mizrab) worn on the index finger. Sympathetic strings are tuned to the notes of the raga being performed, and drone strings (chikari) provide rhythmic punctuation. The larger, deeper-toned surbahar is a related bass instrument.
