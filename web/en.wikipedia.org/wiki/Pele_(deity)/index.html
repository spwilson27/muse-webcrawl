Pele (deity)

Pele
AbodeHalemaʻumaʻu crater, Kīlauea, Hawaiʻi
DomainVolcanoes, fire, lightning, dance, violence
ParentsHaumea (mother); Kāne Milohai (father, in some traditions)
SiblingsHiʻiaka, Kāne Milohai, Kamohoaliʻi, Nāmaka

Pele (pronounced [ˈpɛlɛ]) is the Hawaiian goddess of volcanoes and fire and the creator of the Hawaiian Islands. Often called Pelehonuamea ("Pele of the sacred land") or Tutu Pele with respect, she is a prominent figure in Native Hawaiian religion and mythology, and remains an active presence in Hawaiian culture — lava flows are understood by many practitioners as her physical manifestation.

Mythology

According to legend, Pele travelled to Hawaii from Kahiki (Tahiti) in a great canoe, driven from her homeland after a conflict with her elder sister Nāmaka, the sea goddess. She carried with her her little sister Hiʻiaka in egg form. Digging with her digging stick (pāoa), she created fire pits island by island — on Kauaʻi, Oʻahu, and Maui — pursued each time by Nāmaka, until she made her home at Halemaʻumaʻu crater at the summit of Kīlauea on the island of Hawaiʻi, where her spirit resides.

Famous legends tell of her doomed love for the chief Lohiʻau, whom Hiʻiaka was sent to fetch, and of her rivalry with the rain goddess Poliʻahu on Mauna Kea. She is also said to appear to people as a beautiful young woman or an elderly woman with a white dog, and those who encounter her should greet her respectfully.

Cultural significance

Offerings are still left for Pele at Kīlauea, and hula dances are performed in her honour. A popular belief, sometimes called "Pele's curse", holds that removing lava rocks from Hawaiʻi brings misfortune; the Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park receives packages of returned rocks each year, though park officials note the belief is a modern invention rather than an ancient tradition.
