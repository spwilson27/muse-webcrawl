The Asch conformity experiments were a series of studies in social psychology conducted by Solomon Asch at Swarthmore College in the 1950s. They demonstrated the power of group pressure to make individuals conform to an obviously incorrect majority judgement, and became among the most famous experiments in psychology.

Procedure

A participant was seated with a group of confederates of the experimenter, believing them to be fellow volunteers in a study of visual perception. The group was shown a card with a single "standard" line alongside three comparison lines, and asked in turn to say aloud which comparison line matched the standard. The correct answer was always obvious. The genuine participant answered last or near-last. On most trials the confederates answered correctly, but on twelve "critical" trials they had been instructed to unanimously give the same wrong answer.

Results

Participants conformed to the wrong majority answer on about one-third of the critical trials (roughly 37 per cent); about 75 per cent of participants conformed at least once, while about 25 per cent never conformed. In a control condition without group pressure, errors were under 1 per cent. Interviews afterwards showed most conforming participants knew the majority was wrong but did not want to stand out — normative influence — while a minority reported actually doubting their own perception, an informational influence.

Variations and findings

Asch and later researchers found that conformity increased with group size up to about three or four confederates, dropped sharply when even one confederate "ally" gave the correct answer, and was lower when responses could be given privately. Later meta-analyses found conformity has declined somewhat in the United States since the 1950s and varies across cultures, being higher in collectivist societies.

Criticism and legacy

The experiments have been criticised for their all-male student samples, their artificiality, and (from a historical perspective) for reflecting the conformist climate of 1950s America; some commentators have also questioned the ethics of the deception involved, though participants were debriefed. Nevertheless, the studies remain a foundational demonstration of social influence and a staple of psychology teaching.
