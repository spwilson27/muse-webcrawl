Canadian nationalism

Canadian nationalism is a political ideology and cultural framework that seeks to promote the identity, interests, values and sovereignty of Canada and the Canadian people. It encompasses a broad spectrum of viewpoints, from civic and cultural nationalism emphasising a shared Canadian identity across the country's linguistic and regional divisions, to economic nationalism aimed at limiting foreign — particularly American — ownership and cultural influence.

History

Canadian nationalist sentiment emerged in the 19th century, shaped by loyalty to the British Crown among English-speaking Canadians and by the survival of French-Canadian culture after the Conquest of 1760. Confederation in 1867 created a federal Dominion whose leaders, notably John A. Macdonald, pursued nation-building policies such as the National Policy of protective tariffs (1879), the Canadian Pacific Railway, and westward settlement.

In the 20th century, Canadian nationalism was increasingly defined in opposition to continental integration with the United States. The "Quiet Revolution" in Quebec transformed French-Canadian nationalism into a distinctly Québécois nationalism, culminating in sovereignty referendums in 1980 and 1995, while in English Canada a "new nationalism" of the 1960s and 1970s — associated with figures such as Walter Gordon and Mel Hurtig's Committee for an Independent Canada — promoted Canadian ownership of industry, a distinctive cultural policy, and the adoption of the Maple Leaf flag in 1965.

Variations

Historians distinguish several strands: civic nationalism, based on shared citizenship and institutions; cultural nationalism, expressed through support for Canadian literature, broadcasting and the arts; economic nationalism, favouring limits on foreign investment; and provincial nationalisms, including Quebec nationalism and Western alienation. Indigenous nationalisms, asserting the sovereignty of First Nations, Métis and Inuit peoples, form a further distinct current.
