The national flag of the United Kingdom is the Union Flag, commonly known as the Union Jack. It combines the flags of three of the constituent countries of the United Kingdom: the red cross of Saint George (England), the white saltire of Saint Andrew (Scotland), and the red saltire of Saint Patrick (representing Ireland).

History

The first Union Flag was created in 1606, after James VI of Scotland became James I of England, by combining the crosses of St George and St Andrew. The current design dates from the union of Great Britain and Ireland in 1801, when the red saltire of St Patrick was added. Wales is not represented in the flag, having been legally incorporated into England before the first union flag was created.

Construction and orientation

The flag is not symmetrical: the white diagonal stripes of St Andrew should appear above the red diagonals of St Patrick in the upper hoist-side corner, a rule derived from the order of precedence of the patron saints. Flying the flag upside down is traditionally regarded as a sign of distress. The red saltire is counterchanged with the white so that it is offset rather than superimposed simply upon it.

Use

The Union Flag is flown from government buildings on designated flag days and appears in the canton of the flags of many Commonwealth realms and territories, including Australia, New Zealand, Fiji and Tuvalu, as well as in ensigns of the British armed forces. The term "Union Jack" is popular usage; it was historically argued that "Jack" properly applies only when the flag is flown from a ship's jack staff, though Parliament has recognised both names as acceptable.
