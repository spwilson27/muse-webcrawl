Guyot

A guyot, also known as a tablemount, is an isolated underwater volcanic mountain (seamount) with a flat top more than 200 metres below the sea surface.

Formation

Guyots begin as volcanic islands built up from the ocean floor. Wave erosion planes off the summit at sea level; as the oceanic crust cools and subsides — and as plate tectonics carries the volcano away from its magma source — the flat top sinks below the surface, often accumulating a cap of coral reef and limestone sediment as it descends through tropical waters.

Discovery

The features were recognised during the Second World War from echo-sounding surveys by Harry Hammond Hess, who named them after the 19th-century geographer Arnold Henry Guyot. Hess's guyots — flat-topped mountains far below the sea — became key evidence for seafloor spreading and plate tectonics, since their drowned reefs and increasing age with distance from mid-ocean ridges recorded the motion and subsidence of the seafloor.
