Amyloidosis is a group of diseases in which abnormal proteins, called amyloid fibrils, misfold and deposit in tissues and organs, progressively impairing their function. The deposits are insoluble beta-pleated sheet aggregates that stain with Congo red and show characteristic apple-green birefringence under polarised light. More than three dozen proteins are known to form amyloid in humans.

Classification

Amyloidoses are classified by the precursor protein. The commonest types are:

  • AL amyloidosis (primary): caused by misfolded immunoglobulin light chains produced by an abnormal plasma-cell clone; the most common form in developed countries and often associated with multiple myeloma.
  • AA amyloidosis (secondary): derived from serum amyloid A protein in chronic inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease.
  • ATTR amyloidosis: derived from transthyretin, either hereditary (mutant) or wild-type, the latter increasingly recognised in older adults with heart failure.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms depend on the organs affected. Kidney involvement causes proteinuria and nephrotic syndrome; cardiac involvement causes restrictive cardiomyopathy, heart failure and conduction disease; nerve involvement causes peripheral neuropathy and autonomic dysfunction (orthostatic hypotension, gastroparesis). Other features include hepatomegaly, macroglossia (especially in AL), carpal tunnel syndrome (often preceding ATTR cardiac disease by years), easy bruising and fatigue.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires demonstrating amyloid in tissue — classically an abdominal fat-pad aspirate or biopsy of an affected organ — stained with Congo red. The amyloid type is then identified, most reliably by laser-capture mass spectrometry, because treatment differs radically by type. Imaging such as cardiac MRI and bone scintigraphy (for ATTR) and biomarkers such as NT-proBNP and free light chains support assessment and staging.

Treatment

Treatment aims to halt production of the precursor protein and support failing organs. AL amyloidosis is treated with chemotherapy directed at the plasma-cell clone (for example daratumumab-based regimens and autologous stem cell transplantation in eligible patients). ATTR amyloidosis is treated with transthyretin stabilisers such as tafamidis and with gene-silencing therapies (patisiran, inotersen) for hereditary neuropathy. AA amyloidosis is treated by controlling the underlying inflammation. Supportive care and, in selected cases, organ transplantation are also used.
