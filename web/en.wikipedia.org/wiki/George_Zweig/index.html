George Zweig (born 30 May 1937) is an American physicist of Russian-Jewish origin. He was trained as a particle physicist at Caltech under Richard Feynman, but after proposing one of the foundational ideas of the Standard Model he turned to neurobiology, studying the transduction of sound into nerve impulses in the cochlea of the human ear.

The quark model

In 1964, while a graduate student and then postdoctoral researcher associated with CERN, Zweig independently proposed the existence of fractionally charged fundamental constituents of hadrons — the particles now called quarks. He called them "aces", imagining four constituents (analogous to the four aces in a deck of cards) from which protons, neutrons, and other hadrons were built. The proposal appeared in two CERN preprints in 1964. Murray Gell-Mann, Zweig's doctoral advisor at Caltech, published a similar proposal the same year and coined the name "quark", which prevailed.

Zweig's model explained the patterns of hadron masses and decays, including what became known as the Zweig rule (or OZI rule) suppressing certain decay processes. The quark model was initially received with scepticism, but became the foundation of quantum chromodynamics. Many physicists consider that Zweig's contribution deserved a share of the recognition later accorded to the quark model.

Neurobiology

From the 1970s, at Los Alamos National Laboratory, Zweig worked on the physics and neurobiology of hearing, developing models of how the cochlea converts sound waves into neural signals, and on signal processing. He later also co-founded a quantitative trading firm, Signition, applying pattern-recognition methods to financial markets.

Honours

Zweig received a MacArthur Fellowship in 1985 and was elected to the National Academy of Sciences. In 1996 he received the Sakurai Prize of the American Physical Society for his work on the quark model, shared with others working on hadron spectroscopy.
