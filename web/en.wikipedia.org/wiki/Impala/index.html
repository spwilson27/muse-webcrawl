The impala (Aepyceros melampus, from Greek "high-horned black-foot") is a medium-sized antelope found in eastern and southern Africa, the sole member of the genus Aepyceros. Known for its spectacular leaping — it can jump up to 3 metres high and 10 metres long — the impala is among the most abundant antelopes, with an estimated population of around two million.

Description

Impalas have glossy reddish-brown coats, white underparts and distinctive black stripes on the rump and ears; males carry lyre-shaped horns up to about 90 cm long, while females are hornless. They have scent glands on their feet found in no other antelope. Two subspecies are recognised: the common impala and the rarer black-faced impala of Namibia and Angola.

Behaviour and ecology

Impalas inhabit savanna and light woodland near water, feeding as both grazers and browsers. Social organisation is flexible: females and young form herds, bachelor males group separately, and during the rut dominant males hold territories, bellowing and fighting for harems. Lambs are born mainly in the wet season, and mothers hide them in vegetation for the first days. Impalas are key prey for leopards, cheetahs, lions and wild dogs, and frequently associate with baboons and other antelope for vigilance.
