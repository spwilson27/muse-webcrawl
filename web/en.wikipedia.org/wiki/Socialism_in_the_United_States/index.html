Socialism in the United States

Socialism in the United States has a history stretching from early nineteenth-century utopian communities through the mass Socialist Party of Eugene V. Debs to contemporary democratic socialist movements. Although no socialist party has won national power, socialist ideas influenced labour law, the New Deal and later reform movements.

Early history

Utopian experiments such as Robert Owen's New Harmony (1825) and Fourierist communes preceded an organised movement. German immigrants after 1848 brought Marxist ideas, and the Socialist Labor Party was founded in 1876. The Socialist Party of America (1901) grew rapidly: Debs won 6 percent of the presidential vote in 1912, and the party elected more than a thousand officials, including congressmen Victor Berger and Meyer London and the long-serving Milwaukee mayor Daniel Hoan.

Repression and decline

The party split over the First World War and the Russian Revolution; Debs was imprisoned for opposing the draft, and the Palmer Raids of 1919–20 targeted radicals. Norman Thomas led a diminished party through the 1930s as the New Deal absorbed parts of its programme. Cold War anti-communism further marginalised the left, though Michael Harrington's The Other America (1962) influenced the War on Poverty, and his Democratic Socialist Organizing Committee fed into today's Democratic Socialists of America (DSA).

Contemporary movement

Interest revived after the 2008 financial crisis and the presidential campaigns of self-described democratic socialist Bernie Sanders in 2016 and 2020. DSA membership grew dramatically, and democratic socialists such as Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez have been elected to Congress, campaigning for Medicare for All, a Green New Deal and stronger unions. Historians still debate "American exceptionalism" — Werner Sombart's 1906 question of why no mass socialist party took root in the United States.
