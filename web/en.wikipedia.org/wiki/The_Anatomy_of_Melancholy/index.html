The Anatomy of Melancholy

The Anatomy of Melancholy (full title: The Anatomy of Melancholy, What it is: With all the Kinds, Causes, Symptomes, Prognostickes, and Several Cures of it) is a book by Robert Burton, first published in 1621. Written under the pseudonym "Democritus Junior", it is at once a medical treatise, a scholarly compendium and a sprawling work of literature: a vast, digressive, endlessly quotable survey of melancholy — the early modern term encompassing much of what is now called depression and anxiety — and of human folly in general.

Contents

Burton, a scholar and librarian at Christ Church, Oxford, claimed: "I write of melancholy, by being busy to avoid melancholy." The book is organised into three partitions. The first examines the causes, symptoms and nature of melancholy in general; the second its cures, from diet and exercise to music and mirth; the third is devoted to love melancholy and religious melancholy. Woven through the treatise are quotations from hundreds of classical, medieval and contemporary authors, in Latin and English, which Burton stitches into a mosaic that is often described as a forerunner of the modern essay and even of stream-of-consciousness writing.

Publication and influence

Burton revised and expanded the book for five editions during his lifetime (1621, 1624, 1628, 1632, 1638), until it grew to well over half a million words. It was one of the most popular books of the 17th century. Samuel Johnson called it the only book that ever got him out of bed two hours earlier than he wished, Laurence Sterne plundered it for Tristram Shandy, and it has since been admired by writers from John Keats to Jorge Luis Borges and Samuel Beckett. Modern readers value it as an inexhaustible commonplace book and a wry, humane monument of Renaissance learning.
