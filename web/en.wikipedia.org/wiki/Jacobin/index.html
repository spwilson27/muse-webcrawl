Jacobins

Jacobin Club (Société des Jacobins)

Founded: 1789, Versailles/Paris

Dissolved: November 1794

Full name: Society of the Friends of the Constitution

Key figures: Maximilien Robespierre, Louis Antoine de Saint-Just, Georges Danton (early)

Ideology: Revolutionary republicanism, popular sovereignty

The Jacobins were members of the Jacobin Club, the most influential political club of the French Revolution. Officially the Society of the Friends of the Constitution, the club took its nickname from its meeting place in a former Dominican convent in Paris — the Dominicans being known in France as "Jacobins" after their first house on the Rue Saint-Jacques. At its height, a network of thousands of affiliated provincial clubs made the Jacobins a powerful force in revolutionary politics.

History

The club began in 1789 as the Breton Club of deputies to the Estates-General and moved with the Assembly to Paris, opening its membership to citizens. Initially moderate and monarchist-constitutional, it radicalized in stages: the Feuillants split off in 1791, and the Girondins were expelled in 1793, leaving the club dominated by the Montagnards around Maximilien Robespierre. Allied with the Paris Commune and the sans-culottes, the Jacobins supplied the ideological core of the revolutionary government of 1793–94, defending the Republic against foreign invasion and internal revolt but also sustaining the Committee of Public Safety and the Reign of Terror.

Fall and legacy

After Robespierre's overthrow in the Thermidorian Reaction of July 1794, the club was closed by the Convention in November 1794. "Jacobin" nevertheless became a lasting political label: in French usage it came to denote a staunch defender of a centralized, indivisible republic, and in English a radical revolutionary. Jacobin currents influenced nineteenth-century republicanism, later revolutionary movements, and debates about the relationship between democracy and centralized state power.
