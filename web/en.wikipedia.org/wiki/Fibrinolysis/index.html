Fibrinolysis is a process that prevents blood clots from growing and becoming problematic. It involves the enzymatic breakdown of the fibrin mesh of a clot by the serine protease plasmin, which is activated from its inactive precursor, plasminogen. The process forms part of normal haemostasis: as a vessel heals, the clot that sealed it is gradually dissolved so that blood flow is restored.

Mechanism

Plasminogen circulates in plasma and binds to fibrin as a clot forms. Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA), released from damaged endothelium, and urokinase convert plasminogen to plasmin in the presence of fibrin. Plasmin then cleaves the cross-linked fibrin polymer into soluble fibrin degradation products, the smallest of which is the D-dimer — a clinically important marker of clot breakdown.

Regulation

Fibrinolysis is tightly controlled. Plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) neutralises tPA and urokinase, while α2-antiplasmin directly inactivates plasmin. Thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI), activated by thrombin, makes fibrin more resistant to plasmin by removing the lysine residues to which plasminogen binds.

Clinical significance

Excessive fibrinolysis contributes to bleeding in trauma, surgery, and certain coagulopathies; inherited or acquired hyperfibrinolysis can be treated with antifibrinolytic drugs such as tranexamic acid. Conversely, inadequate fibrinolysis favours thrombosis. The D-dimer blood test is used to help exclude venous thromboembolism when clinical suspicion is low.

Fibrinolytic drugs

Pharmacological fibrinolysis ("thrombolysis") uses recombinant tPA (alteplase, tenecteplase) or bacterial streptokinase to dissolve clots in acute ischaemic stroke, myocardial infarction, and massive pulmonary embolism, at the cost of an increased risk of major haemorrhage.
