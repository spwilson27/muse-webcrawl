Atlantic multidecadal oscillation

The Atlantic multidecadal oscillation (AMO), also known as the Atlantic multidecadal variability (AMV), is a climate cycle that affects the sea surface temperature (SST) of the North Atlantic Ocean based on different modes on multidecadal timescales. While there is some support for this mode in models and in historical observations, there is controversy over its amplitude and whether it has a typical timescale and can be classified as an oscillation.

The AMO is identified as a pattern of coherent SST variability in the North Atlantic, with alternating warm and cool phases lasting roughly 20 to 40 years each. An AMO index is commonly computed from the detrended average of North Atlantic sea surface temperatures, typically between 0° and 70°N.

Mechanism

Evidence from ocean models suggests the oscillation is connected to variations in the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation (AMOC), which transports warm water northward at the surface and returns colder water at depth. Other proposed mechanisms involve atmospheric circulation patterns such as the North Atlantic Oscillation, ocean–atmosphere feedbacks, and external influences including aerosols and volcanic eruptions.

Effects

The AMO is correlated with air temperatures and rainfall over much of the Northern Hemisphere, in particular North America and Europe. Warm phases have been associated with increased Atlantic hurricane activity, greater rainfall in the Sahel and India, drought in the North American Midwest, and shifts in European summer climate. Cool phases tend to reverse several of these tendencies.

Phases

Instrumental records suggest warm phases around the 1930s–1950s and from the mid-1990s onward, and cool phases around 1900–1920s and 1965–1994. Proxy records extending back several centuries indicate that similar variability has occurred for at least the past millennium.

Debate

Some researchers argue that the apparent oscillation largely reflects forced changes—from greenhouse gases, sulfate aerosols, and volcanic eruptions—rather than a purely internal oscillation of the climate system, and prefer the term Atlantic multidecadal variability.
