In complex analysis, the Riemann mapping theorem states that if U is a non-empty simply connected open subset of the complex number plane C which is not all of C, then there exists a biholomorphic mapping f (that is, a bijective holomorphic mapping whose inverse is also holomorphic) from U onto the open unit disk. Intuitively, the theorem says that any simply connected planar domain, other than the whole plane, can be "reshaped" by a conformal map into the unit disk without tearing or stretching angles.

Theorem. Let U ⊂ C be a simply connected open set, U ≠ ∅, U ≠ C. Then there exists a biholomorphic map f : U → D = { z : |z| < 1 }. Moreover, the map is unique once one prescribes the image of one point of U and the argument of the derivative at that point.

History

The theorem was stated by Bernhard Riemann in his 1851 dissertation, where his proof relied on the Dirichlet principle — a minimisation argument whose justification was later challenged by Karl Weierstrass. David Hilbert rehabilitated the Dirichlet principle around 1900, and William Fogg Osgood gave the first rigorous proof for restricted classes of domains the same year. Fully general proofs followed from the work of Lipót Fejér, Paul Koebe, and Henri Poincaré in the following decade. Koebe also proved the general uniformization theorem, of which the mapping theorem is a special case.

Remarks

The theorem fails in several natural directions. There is no conformal bijection between the whole complex plane and the unit disk (by Liouville's theorem), and no conformal bijection between a disk and an annulus (an elementary consequence of the extremal length or of considering winding numbers). The higher-dimensional analogue is also false: by Liouville's theorem on conformal maps, conformal mappings in dimensions three and above are extremely rigid. The mapping, although guaranteed to exist, cannot generally be written in closed form; explicit formulas are known only for special domains such as half-planes, strips, and polygons (via the Schwarz–Christoffel mapping). By the Carathéodory extension theorem, when the boundary of U is a Jordan curve the map extends to a homeomorphism of the closures.

Proof sketches

Standard modern proofs proceed by normal families: one considers the family of injective holomorphic maps from U into the disk fixing a chosen point, and uses Montel's theorem to extract a convergent subsequence whose limit maximises the derivative at that point; extremality is then shown to force surjectivity. Alternative approaches use the Perron method for the Dirichlet problem or extremal length.
