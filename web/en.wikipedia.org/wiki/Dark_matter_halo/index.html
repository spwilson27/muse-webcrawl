Dark matter halo

A dark matter halo is the hypothetical, roughly spherical distribution of dark matter that surrounds a galaxy and extends far beyond its visible component. In the standard ΛCDM model of cosmology, galaxies form within dark matter haloes grown by gravitational instability, and the halo's gravity dominates the total mass budget of the system.

Properties

For the Milky Way, the dark matter halo is estimated to extend to a virial radius of roughly 200–300 kiloparsecs and to contain on the order of a trillion solar masses, several times the mass of all its stars and gas. The density profile of haloes in simulations is commonly described by the Navarro–Frenk–White (NFW) profile, which rises steeply toward the centre, although observations of some galaxies favour shallower central "cores" — a discrepancy known as the core–cusp problem.

Evidence

Evidence for dark matter haloes comes chiefly from galaxy rotation curves, which remain flat at large radii rather than falling off as visible matter alone predicts; from gravitational lensing; and from the dynamics of satellite galaxies and hot gas in galaxy clusters. According to theory, haloes grow hierarchically by mergers, and galaxies themselves are expected to contain thousands of smaller dark matter subhaloes.
