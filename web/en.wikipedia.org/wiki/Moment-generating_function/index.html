Moment-generating function

Moment-generating function
FieldProbability theory, statistics
NotationMX(t)
RelatedCharacteristic function, cumulant-generating function

In probability theory and statistics, the moment-generating function (MGF) of a real-valued random variable is an alternative specification of its probability distribution, often more convenient than working directly with probability density or mass functions. As its name implies, the derivatives of the MGF at zero give the moments of the distribution.

Definition

The moment-generating function of a random variable X is

MX(t) = E[etX] = ∫ etx fX(x) dx

wherever the expectation exists — that is, for t in some open interval around 0. If the expectation does not exist in a neighbourhood of 0 (as for some heavy-tailed distributions, such as the Cauchy distribution), the MGF is undefined, and the characteristic function, which always exists, is used instead. For a discrete random variable, the integral is replaced by a sum over the probability mass function. The MGF is the two-sided Laplace transform of the density evaluated with a sign change, and can be viewed as encoding all moments in a single analytic object.

Moments

Provided the MGF exists near 0, it can be expanded as a power series whose coefficients are the moments:

MX(t) = 1 + t·E[X] + t²/2!·E[X²] + t³/3!·E[X³] + ⋯

Consequently, the n-th moment is the n-th derivative at zero: E[Xn] = MX(n)(0). The first two derivatives give the mean and, with the mean, the variance: Var(X) = M″(0) − (M′(0))². The logarithm of the MGF is the cumulant-generating function, whose derivatives give the cumulants.

Properties and examples

  • If the MGF exists in an open interval around 0, it uniquely determines the distribution: two random variables with the same MGF have the same distribution.
  • For independent random variables, the MGF of the sum is the product of the MGFs, mirroring the convolution of distributions; this makes MGFs a standard tool for deriving the distributions of sums (for example, that independent normal and Poisson variables are additive in their parameters).
  • For a normal distribution N(μ, σ²), M(t) = exp(μt + σ²t²/2). For a Poisson distribution with rate λ, M(t) = exp(λ(et − 1)).
  • An affine transformation gives MaX+b(t) = ebt MX(at).

MGFs are also used in the proof of limit theorems: pointwise convergence of MGFs to the MGF of a limiting distribution implies convergence in distribution, a route to the central limit theorem. In actuarial science and economics, the MGF of the exponential distribution underlies risk and utility calculations.
