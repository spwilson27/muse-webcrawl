Vitamin A deficiency (VAD) is the lack of vitamin A in blood and tissues. It is common in poorer countries, especially among children and women of reproductive age, but is rarely seen in more developed countries. The World Health Organization estimates that hundreds of millions of children worldwide are affected; it is the leading preventable cause of blindness in children and also increases the risk of death from infections such as measles and diarrhoea.

Signs and symptoms

The earliest symptom is impaired dark adaptation and night blindness (nyctalopia). As deficiency progresses, the conjunctiva and cornea become dry (xerophthalmia); foamy white accumulations of keratin called Bitot's spots appear on the conjunctiva; and in severe cases the cornea softens and ulcerates (keratomalacia), leading to irreversible blindness. Deficiency also impairs immune function, growth, and reproduction, and worsens anaemia.

Causes

Primary deficiency results from prolonged dietary deprivation of vitamin A and provitamin A carotenoids, common where diets are dominated by milled rice or other staples and poor in animal foods and coloured vegetables. Secondary deficiency arises from conditions that impair fat absorption or vitamin storage, including cystic fibrosis, chronic diarrhoea, liver disease, and measles infection itself, which sharply increases vitamin A losses.

Prevention and treatment

Established strategies include promoting dietary diversity, fortifying foods such as sugar, oil and flour, biofortification (for example orange-fleshed sweet potato and Golden Rice), and periodic high-dose vitamin A supplementation of young children, which trials link to reduced child mortality. Ocular disease from deficiency is treated as an emergency with high-dose oral vitamin A on a defined schedule.
