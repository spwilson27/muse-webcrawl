Poker strategy

Poker strategy is the set of concepts and techniques by which poker players seek to make profitable decisions over the long run. Although poker includes an element of chance in the deal, the outcome over many hands is dominated by the players' betting decisions. The fundamental theorem of poker, stated by David Sklansky, holds that a player gains whenever opponents play differently from how they would if they could see the player's cards, and loses whenever the reverse occurs.

Core concepts

Pot odds and expected value: Decisions are evaluated by comparing the cost of a call with the size of the pot and the probability of winning — a call is profitable when the odds against winning are better than the odds offered by the pot. Drawing hands are judged against outs, the unseen cards that complete the hand.

Position: Acting later in a betting round confers an informational advantage, so strong players play more hands in late position and fewer in early position.

Starting hand selection and aggression: Playing fewer, stronger hands ("tight") and betting or raising rather than calling ("aggressive") is the classic winning profile, summarised as "tight-aggressive" (TAG).

Bluffing and deception: Betting with a weak hand to induce folds, and its complement, slow playing a strong hand, keep opponents unable to read one's range. Game theory dictates optimal bluffing frequencies so that an opponent is indifferent between calling and folding.

Reading opponents: Players categorise opponents by their tendencies (loose/tight, passive/aggressive) and adjust, while managing their own table image.

Game theory optimal play

Modern strategy, especially for heads-up and high-stakes no-limit hold'em, is heavily influenced by computational solvers that compute approximate Nash equilibrium strategies, known as game theory optimal (GTO) play. Solvers prescribe mixed strategies, range-based thinking, and standardised bet sizes. Human play often deviates profitably from GTO by exploiting opponents' specific errors, a tension between "exploitative" and equilibrium approaches that defines contemporary poker study.

Bankroll management

Because of variance, successful players keep a bankroll large enough to withstand losing streaks typical of their stakes, moving down in limits when necessary, and avoid playing in games where the rake or the skill edge of opponents makes the game unprofitable.
