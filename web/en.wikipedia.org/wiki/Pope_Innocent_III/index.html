Pope Innocent III (Latin: Innocentius III; 1160 or 1161 – 16 July 1216), born Lotario dei Conti di Segni, was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 8 January 1198 until his death on 16 July 1216. He is widely regarded as one of the most powerful and influential of the medieval popes, and his reign is often considered the high point of papal authority in the Middle Ages.

Innocent asserted the plenitude of papal power (plenitudo potestatis), claiming that the pope stood between God and man, "lower than God but higher than man," and that he held authority over kings and emperors as well as over the Church. He intervened repeatedly in the politics of the Holy Roman Empire, supporting first Otto of Brunswick and then Frederick II in the disputed imperial succession, and he placed England and other kingdoms under interdict when their rulers defied him.

Early life and election

Lotario was born at Gavignano near Anagni into the noble Conti di Segni family, which had already produced several popes. He was educated in Rome, Paris, and Bologna, where he studied theology and canon law. His treatise De miseria condicionis humane ("On the Misery of the Human Condition"), written before his election, became one of the most widely read works of the Middle Ages. He was made a cardinal-deacon by his uncle, Pope Clement III, in 1190. On the death of Celestine III in January 1198, the cardinals elected Lotario pope on the very day of his predecessor's death; at about 37, he was one of the youngest popes of the era.

Papacy

Innocent moved quickly to restore papal authority in Rome and the Patrimony of St Peter, and he asserted the pope's right to judge and depose secular rulers. He famously described the relationship between priestly and royal power through the image of the sun and the moon: as the moon receives its light from the sun, so royal power derives its dignity from the priestly authority. His decretal Venerabilem (1202) claimed for the papacy the right to examine the fitness of an emperor-elect.

In England, Innocent refused to accept King John's nominee for the see of Canterbury and consecrated Stephen Langton instead. When John resisted, Innocent placed England under an interdict (1208) and excommunicated the king (1209); in 1213 John submitted, surrendering his kingdoms of England and Ireland to the pope and receiving them back as papal fiefs. In France, Innocent used an interdict to pressure Philip II Augustus over his marriage to Ingeborg of Denmark.

Crusades

Innocent was an energetic promoter of crusading. He proclaimed the Fourth Crusade in 1198, which, contrary to his intentions, was diverted first to Zara and then to Constantinople, which the crusaders sacked in 1204 — an outcome Innocent condemned but ultimately exploited in hopes of reuniting the Latin and Greek churches. He also sponsored the Albigensian Crusade against the Cathar heretics of southern France, and in 1215 he proclaimed the Fifth Crusade at the Fourth Lateran Council. The ill-fated Children's Crusade of 1212 also belongs to his reign.

Fourth Lateran Council

In November 1215 Innocent convened the Fourth Council of the Lateran, the largest and most important church council of the Middle Ages, attended by more than 400 bishops and hundreds of other prelates and envoys. Its seventy decrees reformed clerical discipline, defined the doctrine of transubstantiation in the Eucharist, required annual confession and communion of all the faithful (the "Easter duty"), regulated preaching and episcopal elections, and ordered measures concerning Jews and heretics.

Death and legacy

Innocent died suddenly of a fever at Perugia on 16 July 1216 while travelling in Italy to reconcile Pisa and Genoa so that they might support the coming crusade. He was buried in Perugia cathedral; in 1891 his remains were transferred to the Lateran Basilica in Rome. His voluminous registers of letters remain a principal source for the history of the medieval Church, and his legislation shaped canon law for centuries. Dante placed him in Paradise in the Divine Comedy.
