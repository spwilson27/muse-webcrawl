Old Javanese, also known as Kawi, is the oldest attested stage of the Javanese language, spoken and written in Java from roughly the 8th century until the 16th century, during the Hindu-Buddhist period of Javanese history. It is the language of a vast corpus of inscriptions and of kakawin — courtly epic poems composed in Indian metres — and it is heavily influenced by Sanskrit in vocabulary and literary convention.

History

The earliest Old Javanese inscriptions date from the Central Javanese Mataram kingdom; the language's classical period coincides with the East Javanese kingdoms of Kediri, Singhasari, and Majapahit. With the Islamization of Java in the 15th and 16th centuries, Old Javanese gave way in Java itself to Middle and then Modern Javanese, but it survived in Bali, where it remains in use as a sacred and literary language alongside Balinese.

Literature

The most celebrated Old Javanese work is the Kakawin Ramayana (c. 9th–10th century), a Javanese reworking of the Sanskrit epic. Under the Majapahit court, Mpu Tantular composed the Kakawin Sutasoma, from which Indonesia's national motto, Bhinneka Tunggal Ika ("Unity in Diversity"), is drawn, and Mpu Prapanca wrote the Nagarakretagama (1365), a eulogy of King Hayam Wuruk that is a major historical source. Old Javanese legal texts such as the Agama and the Shaivite tutur literature also survive.

Language

Old Javanese borrowed thousands of Sanskrit loanwords for religious, political, and literary concepts, while retaining an Austronesian grammatical core, including a voice system contrasting actor and undergoer focus. Unlike Modern Javanese, it lacked the elaborate speech-level system (such as krama and ngoko) that later became characteristic of the language. The great scholarly authority on Old Javanese was P. J. Zoetmulder, whose dictionary and grammar remain standard references.
