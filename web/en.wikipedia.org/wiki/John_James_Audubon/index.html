John James Audubon (born Jean-Jacques Rabin; April 26, 1785 – January 27, 1851) was a French-American ornithologist, naturalist and artist. His major work, The Birds of America (1827–1839), a collection of 435 life-size prints of North American birds, is considered one of the finest ornithological and book-art achievements ever produced.

Early life

Born in the French colony of Saint-Domingue (now Haiti), the illegitimate son of a French naval officer, Audubon was raised in France and emigrated to the United States in 1803 to avoid conscription into Napoleon's armies. He ran businesses in Kentucky and Missouri with mixed success while pursuing his passion for studying and drawing birds; at his property near Philadelphia he conducted the first known bird-banding experiments in North America.

The Birds of America

Unable to find American backers or engravers equal to his vision, Audubon travelled to Britain in 1826, where his dramatic watercolours — made from wired specimens he had shot and posed — caused a sensation. The double-elephant-folio edition was engraved in Edinburgh and London and sold by subscription. He followed it with the Ornithological Biography and later a work on North American mammals.

Legacy

The National Audubon Society, founded in 1905, bears his name, as do numerous places and species. Modern scholarship has also examined his slaveholding and disputed episodes of scientific dishonesty, complicating his reputation.
