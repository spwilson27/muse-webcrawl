Charles Émile Picard

Charles Émile Picard (24 July 1856 – 11 December 1941) was a French mathematician. He was elected the fifteenth member to occupy seat 1 of the Académie française in 1924. His mathematical contributions include the Picard theorems of complex analysis and foundational work on differential equations, including the Picard–Lindelöf theorem on the existence and uniqueness of solutions.

Life

Picard was born in Paris and educated at the Lycée Henri-IV and the École Normale Supérieure, where he obtained his doctorate in 1877. He taught at the University of Toulouse and then at the Sorbonne in Paris, where he held the chair of analysis. He became a member of the Academy of Sciences in 1889 and its perpetual secretary in 1917.

Work

In 1879 Picard proved his famous "little theorem": a non-constant entire function takes every complex value with the possible exception of one. A year later he proved the "great theorem": such a function takes every value infinitely often, with the possible exception of one, in any neighbourhood of an essential singularity. These results substantially extended the work of Casorati and Weierstrass on essential singularities.

His method of successive approximations underlies the Picard–Lindelöf theorem, guaranteeing local existence and uniqueness of solutions to ordinary differential equations. Picard also worked on algebraic surfaces (the Picard group), Painlevé transcendents, and the theory of functions of two complex variables.

Legacy

Picard trained a generation of French mathematicians and wrote an influential Traité d'analyse. The Émile Picard Medal of the French Academy of Sciences is awarded in his honour.
