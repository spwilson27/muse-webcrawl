Power factor


Labelled image placeholder: the power triangle relating real, reactive, and apparent power

In electrical engineering, the power factor of an AC power system is defined as the ratio of the real power absorbed by the load to the apparent power flowing in the circuit. It is a dimensionless number in the closed interval of −1 to 1.

Definition

Real power (P, in watts) is the power that does actual work; apparent power (S, in volt-amperes) is the product of the circuit's root-mean-square voltage and current; and reactive power (Q, in vars) is exchanged with energy-storage elements such as inductors and capacitors without doing net work. For sinusoidal waveforms, the power factor equals cos φ, where φ is the phase angle between voltage and current. A load is described as having a lagging power factor if it draws current that lags the voltage (typical of induction motors) and a leading power factor if the current leads.

Significance

A low power factor means the supply must carry more current than the useful power alone requires, increasing losses in conductors and transformers and reducing system capacity. Utilities therefore often impose penalties on industrial customers whose power factor falls below a set threshold, commonly 0.9 or 0.95.

Correction

Power-factor correction is usually achieved by connecting capacitor banks (or, for leading loads, inductors) near the load to cancel part of the reactive power, or by using synchronous condensers and active power-factor-correction circuits in electronic power supplies.
