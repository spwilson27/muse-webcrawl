Hermaphrodite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hermaphrodite is a sexually reproducing organism that produces both male and female gametes. The term derives from Hermaphroditus, the son of Hermes and Aphrodite in Greek mythology, who was fused with the nymph Salmacis. Hermaphroditism is widespread across the animal kingdom and universal in most flowering plants, though it does not occur naturally in mammals as a functional reproductive system.

Types

Simultaneous hermaphroditism

The organism possesses functional male and female reproductive organs at the same time, as in earthworms, most land snails and slugs, many fish (such as hamlets), and most flowering plants — in botanical terms, perfect flowers contain both stamens and carpels. Self-fertilisation is possible in some species but cross-fertilisation is common.

Sequential hermaphroditism

The organism changes sex during its lifetime. In protandry, it begins as male and becomes female (e.g., clownfish); in protogyny, it begins as female and becomes male (e.g., many wrasses and groupers). Sex change is usually triggered by size, age, or social status.

Plants

The great majority of flowering plants are hermaphroditic, bearing bisexual (perfect) flowers. Monoecious species bear separate male and female flowers on the same plant, while dioecious species have the sexes on separate plants — the latter being a minority strategy.

Use regarding humans

The term hermaphrodite was historically applied in medicine to people with intersex variations (differences of sex development), but this usage is now considered outdated and misleading, since humans do not possess functional reproductive systems of both sexes; the preferred terms are intersex and specific diagnoses under "differences (or disorders) of sex development".
