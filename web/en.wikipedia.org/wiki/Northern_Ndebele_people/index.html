The Northern Ndebele people (isiNdebele: amaNdebele) are an Nguni ethnic group native to southern Africa, principally in the Matabeleland region of western Zimbabwe. They are closely related to the Zulu of South Africa, from whom they separated during the upheavals of the early 19th century known as the Mfecane. They should not be confused with the Southern Ndebele of South Africa's Mpumalanga and Gauteng provinces, a distinct Nguni group.

History

The Northern Ndebele trace their origin to Mzilikazi (c. 1790–1868), a former general of the Zulu king Shaka, who broke away in the early 1820s and led his followers — the Khumalo and allied clans — northwards across the Limpopo. After periods in the Marico valley and further displacement during conflict with Boer trekkers and the Tswana, Mzilikazi established a new kingdom in south-western Zimbabwe around 1840, centred on Bulawayo. The Ndebele kingdom incorporated many Shona-speaking people and adopted a regimental system modelled on the Zulu state.

Mzilikazi's successor, Lobengula, signed agreements with British interests, including the 1888 Rudd Concession, which paved the way for colonisation by Cecil Rhodes's British South Africa Company. The kingdom was destroyed in the First Matabele War of 1893–94, and the Ndebele joined the Shona in the First Chimurenga uprising of 1896–97.

Modern period

In independent Zimbabwe, Ndebele people form a large minority concentrated in Matabeleland and Bulawayo, the country's second city. The 1980s Gukurahundi campaign, in which the Zimbabwean army's Fifth Brigade killed thousands of civilians in Matabeleland, remains a deeply traumatic episode and a continuing point of political sensitivity. The Northern Ndebele language is one of Zimbabwe's 16 officially recognised languages and is taught in schools and broadcast nationally.

Culture

Traditional Ndebele religion honours a creator, uNkulunkulu, and ancestral spirits; the Mlimo cult historically linked the scattered chiefdoms. Social organisation was built around the town (umuzi), cattle wealth, and age regiments (amabutho). Today most Northern Ndebele are Christian, and cultural life in Bulawayo — often called the cultural capital of the Ndebele — sustains isiNdebele literature, music and theatre.
