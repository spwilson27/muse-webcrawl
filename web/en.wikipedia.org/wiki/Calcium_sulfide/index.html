Calcium Sulfide

Formula: CaS
Molar mass: 72.14 g/mol
Appearance: white to yellowish hygroscopic solid
Density: 2.59 g/cm³
Melting point: 2,525 °C

Calcium sulfide is the inorganic compound with the formula CaS. It crystallizes in the rock-salt structure like sodium chloride, reflecting its ionic character. The pure material is white, but commercial and naturally weathered samples are usually yellowish because of impurities and partial hydrolysis.

Production

Calcium sulfide is produced by carbothermic reduction of calcium sulfate (gypsum): CaSO4 + 2 C → CaS + 2 CO2. It arises as a byproduct in the Leblanc process for soda ash, where the waste heaps of "galligu" containing CaS caused notorious pollution in 19th-century industrial Britain, weeping hydrogen sulfide and prompting some of the earliest environmental legislation (the Alkali Act 1863).

Reactions and uses

CaS hydrolyzes in moist air, releasing hydrogen sulfide and forming calcium hydroxide and calcium hydrosulfide; this chemistry underlies its historical use in depilatories and in lime-sulfur sprays. Calcium sulfide is phosphorescent when doped with trace metals — "Cantor's phosphorus", prepared in the 17th century from oyster shells and sulfur, was an early artificial phosphor — and CaS doped with europium and other activators has been studied for phosphors and photostimulable storage materials.
