Game controller

A game controller, or simply controller, is an input device used to control video games or entertainment systems. Controllers connect to a console, computer or other device, wired or wirelessly, and translate a player's physical actions — button presses, stick movements, gestures — into game input. The controller primarily used with a console is normally called a gamepad in North America and a joypad in Europe.

History

Early controllers were simple: the dial controllers (paddles) of Pong-era systems and the single-button joystick of the Atari 2600 (1977). Nintendo's Famicom/NES controller (1983–85) established the gamepad form factor with a cross-shaped directional pad (D-pad, patented from the Game & Watch Donkey Kong), a design widely imitated. The 1990s added shoulder buttons (SNES), analogue sticks and rumble feedback (Nintendo 64, 1996), and dual analogue sticks (Sony's DualShock, 1997) — the layout that remains standard. Motion control reached mass popularity with the Wii Remote (2006), and modern pads add touchpads, adaptive triggers, haptic feedback and motion sensors.

Types

Controller types include gamepads, joysticks and flight sticks, steering wheels for racing games, light guns, dance mats, musical-instrument controllers, paddles and trackballs, and touchscreen and motion-sensing devices. Keyboards and mice dominate precision input on PCs, especially for strategy and first-person games. Specialist accessible controllers, such as Microsoft's Xbox Adaptive Controller, allow modular switches and inputs for players with limited mobility.

Design considerations

Controller design balances ergonomics, latency, battery life and the mapping of buttons to game actions. Standards such as XInput and the USB HID protocol let one controller work across platforms. Haptic technologies — from simple eccentric-mass rumble to the voice-coil actuators in Sony's DualSense — feed physical sensations back to the player, completing the input loop.
