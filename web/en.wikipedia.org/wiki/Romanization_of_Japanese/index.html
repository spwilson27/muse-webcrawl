Romanization of Japanese

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The romanization of Japanese is the use of Latin script to write the Japanese language. This method of writing is sometimes referred to in Japanese as rōmaji (ローマ字, literally "Roman letters"). Japanese is normally written in a combination of logographic characters borrowed from Chinese (kanji) and syllabic scripts (kana) derived from Chinese characters.

There are several different romanization systems. The three main ones are Hepburn romanization, designed for English speakers; Kunrei-shiki ("Cabinet-order style"), the system promulgated by the Japanese government; and Nihon-shiki, a variant of Kunrei-shiki that preserves the one-to-one correspondence with kana spelling. Hepburn is the most widely used internationally, appearing on road signs, passports, and most English-language publications.

History

The earliest romanization of Japanese was based on Portuguese orthography, developed by Jesuit missionaries in the late sixteenth century, who printed works such as the Vocabulario da Lingoa de Iapam (1603). In the nineteenth century, the American missionary James Curtis Hepburn devised his system for the first Japanese–English dictionary (1867). Japanese intellectual societies debated romanization versus kana and kanji reform during the Meiji period, and the government adopted Kunrei-shiki by cabinet order in 1937, revised in 1954.

Treatment of long vowels and particles

Systems differ in how they mark long vowels: Hepburn uses macrons (ō, ū), while informal writing often omits them or doubles the vowel. The particles は, へ, and を, pronounced wa, e, and o, are romanized in Hepburn as pronounced, whereas Kunrei-shiki writes them as pronounced as well for は and へ but retains wo for を. Syllabic n (ん) is written m before certain consonants in traditional Hepburn, a practice abandoned in Modified Hepburn.

Usage

Romanization is used for the benefit of non-Japanese speakers: on railway and road signage, in passports, in academic linguistics, and for inputting Japanese text on keyboards (rōmaji input). It is rarely used for extended texts intended for native readers.
