Low fantasy

Low fantasy, or intrusion fantasy, is a subgenre of fantasy fiction in which magical events intrude on an otherwise normal, realistic world. The term contrasts with high fantasy, which takes place in a fictional secondary world with its own internal rules and geography. Low fantasy stories are typically set in a recognisable, often contemporary, Earth-like setting, and the intrusion of the magical into the mundane generates much of their tension and atmosphere.

Characteristics

Low fantasy narratives tend to place relatively more emphasis on realism and plausibility, with the fantastic elements forming a puncture into everyday life rather than a complete alternative reality. The genre overlaps with urban fantasy, which sets the intrusion in a city, and with works in which magical realism blends the marvellous into ordinary settings. Children's literature has a strong low-fantasy tradition, in which child protagonists encounter magical beings or objects in the everyday world — examples include E. Nesbit's Five Children and It, Mary Poppins, and much of Roald Dahl's work.

Terminology debate

A secondary usage defines low fantasy by tone and content rather than setting — gritty, earthy stories with limited magic, high stakes for ordinary characters and morally ambiguous worlds, as in many sword-and-sorcery tales. Critics note the ambiguity: a story can be "low" in setting but epic in scale, or set in a secondary world but low in its restrained use of magic. The pairing of high and low fantasy remains a widely used, if contested, shorthand in publishing and criticism.
