Earth's core

Earth's core
Composition: Iron–nickel alloy
Outer core depth: 2,890–5,150 km
Inner core radius: ~1,220 km

Earth's core is the very hot, dense metallic centre of the planet, composed mainly of an iron–nickel alloy. It consists of a liquid outer core about 2,260 km thick and a solid inner core with a radius of about 1,220 km.

Properties

Temperatures at the inner core boundary reach roughly 5,400 °C, comparable to the surface of the Sun, but extreme pressure — over 3 million atmospheres at the centre — keeps the inner core solid. The core's existence and structure are inferred from seismology: S-waves do not pass through the liquid outer core, while P-waves refract at its boundaries.

Magnetic field

Convection in the electrically conducting liquid outer core, driven by cooling and the release of light elements as the inner core grows, sustains the geodynamo that generates Earth's magnetic field. That field shields the surface from much of the solar wind and cosmic radiation.

Formation

The core formed early in Earth's history, about 4.5 billion years ago, when dense iron sank to the centre during planetary differentiation, an event sometimes called the iron catastrophe.
