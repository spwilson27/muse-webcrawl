In abstract algebra, a principal ideal domain (PID) is an integral domain in which every ideal is a principal ideal — that is, generated by a single element. Principal ideal domains are a central class of rings in commutative algebra and algebraic number theory, generalising the arithmetic of the integers.

Definition and examples

A ring R is a principal ideal domain if it is an integral domain and every ideal I ⊆ R has the form I = (a) for some element a ∈ R. Important examples include:

  • The ring of integers ℤ, where every ideal consists of the multiples of a single integer.
  • Any field, trivially.
  • The polynomial ring F[x] over a field F, where the generator can be chosen as the monic polynomial of least degree in the ideal.
  • The Gaussian integers ℤ[i].
  • Every Euclidean domain and every discrete valuation ring.

By contrast, the polynomial ring ℤ[x] and the ring F[x, y] in two variables are not PIDs: the ideal of polynomials vanishing at the origin cannot be generated by one element.

Properties

Every principal ideal domain is a unique factorisation domain (UFD): every element factors into irreducibles, and the factorisation is unique up to order and units. The converse is false — ℤ[x] is a UFD but not a PID. Every PID is Noetherian, integrally closed, and of Krull dimension at most one (apart from fields). In a PID, an element is irreducible if and only if it generates a maximal ideal, i.e. prime elements and irreducible elements coincide.

Structure theorem

The fundamental theorem of finitely generated modules over a PID states that every finitely generated module decomposes into a free part and a torsion part that is a direct sum of cyclic modules of prime-power order. Applied to ℤ this yields the classification of finitely generated abelian groups; applied to F[x] it yields the rational and Jordan canonical forms of linear operators.
