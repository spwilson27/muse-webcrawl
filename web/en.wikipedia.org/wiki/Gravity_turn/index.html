Gravity turn

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A gravity turn or zero-lift turn is a trajectory optimisation technique used in launching a spacecraft into, or descending from, an orbit around a planetary body. Rather than being steered by active guidance alone, the vehicle allows gravity to bend its flight path progressively from vertical toward horizontal, while its attitude keeps the thrust vector aligned with the velocity vector so that aerodynamic angle of attack remains near zero.

Procedure

Shortly after liftoff, the launch vehicle performs a small pitch manoeuvre (the "pitch kick") that tilts its velocity vector slightly away from vertical. Thereafter, the component of gravity perpendicular to the velocity vector continually rotates the trajectory toward the horizon. If the vehicle simply holds its nose along the velocity vector — flying at zero angle of attack — the path arcs over smoothly until the vehicle is flying horizontally at orbital altitude and can accelerate to orbital velocity. The Saturn V and most modern launchers fly gravity-turn-like profiles through the lower atmosphere.

Advantages

  • Low structural loads — zero angle of attack means minimal lateral aerodynamic forces on the airframe, allowing a lighter structure.
  • Low steering losses — thrust is never directed against the velocity, so little propellant is wasted on vectoring.
  • Simplicity — much of the trajectory is flown passively by holding attitude relative to the velocity vector.

Limitations

The pure gravity turn is sensitive to winds and to variations in thrust and mass, and it constrains the ascent profile; practical launchers therefore use closed-loop guidance for much of the flight, particularly in the upper stages above the sensible atmosphere where aerodynamic considerations vanish. The technique is also used in reverse for powered descent and landing, notably in lunar landing profiles.
