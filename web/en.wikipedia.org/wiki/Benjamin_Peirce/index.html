Benjamin Peirce (April 4, 1809 – October 6, 1880) was an American mathematician, astronomer and polymath who worked for over forty years at Harvard University. Regarded in his lifetime as the leading American mathematician, he contributed to celestial mechanics, statistics, number theory and algebra, served as director of the United States Coast Survey, and was a founding figure of the National Academy of Sciences. He was the father of the philosopher Charles Sanders Peirce.

Career

Peirce graduated from Harvard in 1829 and, after teaching at the Round Hill School in Northampton, joined the Harvard faculty in 1831, holding the Perkins chair of mathematics and astronomy from 1842 until his death. As consulting astronomer he helped establish the Harvard College Observatory's reputation, computed the orbit perturbations of the newly discovered Neptune, and from 1852 worked for the U.S. Coast Survey, serving as its superintendent from 1867 to 1874, where he directed longitude determinations and pendulum gravity measurements.

Contributions

In statistics, Peirce published in 1852 his "criterion" for rejecting doubtful observations — an early rigorous method for handling outliers, prompted by work on the discovery observations of Neptune. His most original mathematical work, Linear Associative Algebras (written 1870, published posthumously), classified algebras of dimension up to six and introduced concepts (idempotent and nilpotent elements) central to modern algebra; he famously opened a Harvard lecture by defining mathematics as "the science which draws necessary conclusions". Peirce also famously testified in the Howland will forgery trial (1868), applying probability to handwriting — an early instance of forensic statistics — showing the near impossibility that certain signature resemblances were coincidental.

Legacy

Peirce was a member of the informal "Scientific Lazzaroni" who pushed for professionalised American science, and a charter member of the National Academy of Sciences in 1863. The lunar crater Peirce and the Peirce quincuncial map projection of his son Charles are named for the family; his collected writings were edited by his son James Mills Peirce.
