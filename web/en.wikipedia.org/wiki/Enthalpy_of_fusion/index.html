Enthalpy of fusion

The enthalpy of fusion of a substance, also known as the (latent) heat of fusion, is the change in enthalpy that results from providing energy, typically heat, to a specific quantity of the substance in order to melt it — changing its state from a solid to a liquid — at constant pressure. It is measured in kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol) in chemistry, or joules per gram (J/g) in engineering. Melting absorbs energy, so the enthalpy of fusion is positive; the reverse process, freezing, releases the same quantity of energy.

Values

For water at 0 °C, the enthalpy of fusion is about 6.01 kJ/mol, or 334 J/g — a large value reflecting the strong hydrogen bonding in ice. This is why ice is an effective coolant and why melting snow and ice moderate temperature changes in nature. Some other examples (approximate):

SubstanceEnthalpy of fusion (kJ/mol)
Water6.01
Methane0.94
Sodium chloride28.2
Aluminium10.7
Iron13.8
Lead4.77

Applications and relations

The enthalpy of fusion determines how much heat must be removed to freeze materials or supplied to melt them, and it governs the temperature plateau observed while a pure substance melts: the supplied heat rearranges particles rather than raising the temperature. Together with the freezing point, it determines how freezing points are depressed by dissolved solutes (cryoscopy), and it enters solubility predictions through the ideal solubility equation. Materials with high enthalpy of fusion per unit volume are used as phase-change materials for thermal energy storage.

The enthalpy of fusion is generally much smaller than the enthalpy of vaporization of the same substance, because melting only partially disrupts the ordered structure, whereas vaporization separates the particles completely.
