Tattoo

A tattoo is a form of body modification made by inserting ink, dyes, or pigments into the dermis layer of the skin to form a permanent design. The word derives from the Tahitian tatau, introduced into English after Captain James Cook's voyages in the 18th century. Tattooing has been practised across cultures for millennia — for ritual, status, identification, cosmetics, and personal expression — and in the 21st century has become a widespread mainstream art form.

History

Tattooing is ancient: the iceman Ötzi (c. 3300 BC) bore dozens of simple tattoos, and tattooed mummies are known from ancient Egypt, Siberia (the Pazyryk burials), and pre-Columbian America. Elaborate traditions developed independently among Polynesian peoples — for whom tattoo signified rank, genealogy, and protection — the Ainu and later Japanese (irezumi), Māori (tā moko), and many other cultures. In Europe and America, tattoos were long associated with sailors, soldiers, and marginal groups; Samuel O'Reilly's 1891 patent of the electric tattoo machine, adapted from Edison's printing pen, made the process faster and standardised the modern studio. A "Tattoo Renaissance" from the 1970s onward, with trained fine-art practitioners and custom designs, moved tattooing into the cultural mainstream.

Process

A modern tattooist uses a machine that drives needles up and down (typically thousands of punctures per minute) depositing pigment into the dermis, whose cells are stable enough that the design persists for life, though tattoos fade and blur slowly over decades. Styles include traditional ("old school"), realism, tribal, Japanese, blackwork, watercolour, and fine-line work. Professional practice requires sterile single-use needles and regulated inks to prevent infection and blood-borne disease transmission.

Purposes and meanings

Historically tattoos have marked identity, status, rites of passage, religious devotion, and group membership; they have also been imposed punitively or as marks of enslavement and imprisonment. Today most tattooing is voluntary self-expression, commemoration, or aesthetic decoration, though cultural and religious attitudes vary — tattooing is prohibited in conservative interpretations of some faiths and remains stigmatised in a few professional contexts.

Health risks and removal

Risks include allergic reactions, granulomas, keloid scarring, and infection when hygiene is poor. Laser removal — usually Q-switched or picosecond lasers that fragment pigment for immune clearance — can lighten or remove tattoos over multiple sessions, though complete removal is not guaranteed.
