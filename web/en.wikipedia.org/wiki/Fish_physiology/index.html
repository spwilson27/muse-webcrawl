Fish physiology is the scientific study of how the component parts of fishes function together in living organisms. It addresses the adaptations that allow fish to breathe, swim, feed, and maintain internal balance in water — a medium far denser and poorer in oxygen than air. Fish physiologists study everything from the molecular operation of gill ion transporters to the whole-animal energetics of migration.

Respiration

Most fish extract oxygen using gills: feathery filaments with lamellae across which water and blood flow in opposite directions. This countercurrent exchange can extract up to 80–90% of the oxygen from water. Many fish pump water by buccal and opercular movements; fast swimmers such as tunas rely on ram ventilation. Some species supplement gills with air breathing via labyrinth organs or a vascularised gut.

Circulation

Fish have a single circulation: a two-chambered heart pumps blood through the gills, then around the body, and back. Because pressure drops across the gill capillaries, systemic pressure is low compared with land vertebrates. Tunas and some sharks maintain elevated temperatures in swimming muscles and viscera through countercurrent heat exchangers (retia mirabilia) — regional endothermy.

Osmoregulation

Freshwater fish are hyperosmotic to their environment: they gain water and lose salts, so they excrete copious dilute urine and actively absorb ions at the gills. Marine fish face the opposite problem: they drink seawater, excrete salts via gill chloride cells, and produce scant urine. Sharks instead retain urea so their tissues are nearly iso-osmotic with the sea. Migratory salmon switch strategies entirely when moving between river and ocean.

Buoyancy

Most bony fish regulate buoyancy with a swim bladder, a gas-filled sac whose volume is adjusted by secreting or absorbing gas (involving the gas gland, rete mirabile, and the Root effect of fish haemoglobin). Sharks lack a swim bladder and rely on oily livers rich in squalene and constant swimming with lift from heterocercal tails and pectoral fins.

Senses

The lateral line detects water movement and vibration via neuromast hair cells; many fish also possess acute smell and taste, excellent colour vision in shallow-water species, and in sharks and catfish, electroreception through the ampullae of Lorenzini. Some groups communicate with sound and generate weak electric fields for navigation.
