Safiye Sultan, Haseki of Murad III and Valide Sultan to Mehmed III, one of the most powerful women of the Ottoman imperial harem.

Safiye Sultan (c. 1550 – c. 1618/19) was the Haseki Sultan of the Ottoman sultan Murad III and the Valide Sultan to her son Mehmed III. She was one of the most prominent and powerful figures of the period known as the Sultanate of Women, exercising great political influence at the Ottoman court for several decades, maintaining an extensive correspondence with foreign rulers, including Queen Elizabeth I of England.

Early life

Safiye was of Albanian origin, born around 1550 in the Dukagjin highlands in Venetian Albania. Captured as a girl — reportedly by corsairs — she was brought to Constantinople and entered the imperial harem. Around 1563 she was presented to the then-prince Murad, the future Murad III, and became his favourite concubine. In 1566 she bore him a son, the future Mehmed III. After Murad's accession in 1574 she retained her position as his chief consort (Haseki) for many years, a position of influence contested by Murad's formidable mother, Nurbanu Sultan, until Nurbanu's death in 1583.

Valide Sultan

When Murad III died in 1595, Safiye's son ascended the throne as Mehmed III, and Safiye became Valide Sultan — the most powerful woman in the empire, with a vast stipend reported to be the largest of any valide. She wielded substantial political influence, intervened in appointments, and accumulated enormous wealth. She conducted a famous diplomatic correspondence with Elizabeth I of England, exchanging gifts that included a jewel-encrusted portrait and, from England, a coach — a celebrated vehicle in which Safiye rode through Constantinople. The English ambassador and merchants sought her favour as a channel to the sultan.

Safiye was also a notable patron of architecture. In 1597 she began construction of the New Mosque (Yeni Cami) in the Eminönü district of Constantinople, on a site cleared of a Karaite Jewish neighbourhood; work was halted after her fall from power and the mosque was completed only in the 1660s by Turhan Sultan, mother of Mehmed IV.

Fall and later life

Safiye's influence generated resentment, including among the military; a sipahi revolt in 1600 was directed in part against the power of the valide and her intermediaries. When her grandson Ahmed I ascended the throne in 1603, he exiled Safiye to the Old Palace in January 1604, ending her political career, although she continued to receive a large stipend. She lived in retirement for many years, dying around 1618 or 1619, and was buried in the mausoleum of Murad III at the Hagia Sophia.
