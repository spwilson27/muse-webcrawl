Constitutional law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Constitutional law is a body of law which defines the role, powers and structure of the different entities within a state, namely the executive, the parliament or legislature and the judiciary, as well as the basic rights of citizens. It is the field concerned with constitutions themselves: their drafting, interpretation, amendment and enforcement.

Scope

Topics include the distribution of powers in federations, the separation of powers, the procedures of legislatures and elections, emergency powers, and the protection of fundamental rights against the state — freedom of expression, religion, due process and equality. Constitutional lawyers litigate before supreme and constitutional courts and advise governments; public international and supranational law (such as European Union law) interact with national constitutional law in doctrines of supremacy and constitutional identity.

Approaches

Interpretive methods are famously contested: originalism and living-constitutionalism in the United States, purposive and proportionality analysis in Canada, Germany, South Africa and many newer constitutions. Comparative constitutional law studies how courts borrow doctrines across borders — proportionality, dignity and socio-economic rights have travelled widely — and how constitution-making after conflict or transition shapes new democracies.
