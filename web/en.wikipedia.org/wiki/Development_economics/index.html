Development economics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Development economics is a branch of economics that deals with the economic aspects of the development process in low- and middle-income countries: poverty, inequality, health, education, demography, agriculture, industrialisation and the institutions that help or hinder growth. It emerged as a distinct field after 1945 with thinkers such as Rosenstein-Rodan, Arthur Lewis (dual-economy model, 1954) and Walt Rostow (stages of growth, 1960), and with the founding of institutions like the World Bank and UN development agencies.

The field's history is one of successive frameworks: state-led modernisation and import substitution; the market-oriented turn of the 1980s (Structural adjustment, the Washington Consensus); Amartya Sen's capability approach and the Human Development Index (1990); institutional economics (Acemoglu, North) stressing property rights and political institutions; and the experimental turn of the 2000s — randomised controlled trials of specific interventions (Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer; Nobel Prize 2019). Critics of each wave accuse it of technocratic overconfidence; the field's underlying question — why some societies became rich while others did not — remains open.
