Univocity of being

The univocity of being is the scholastic thesis, most famously defended by the Franciscan philosopher and theologian John Duns Scotus (c. 1266–1308), that the concept of being is univocal — that is, it has a single, unified meaning when predicated of God and of creatures, and of substance and accident alike. The doctrine stands in contrast to the analogy of being associated with Thomas Aquinas, according to which "being" is said of God and creatures neither univocally nor equivocally but analogically.

Scotus's argument

Scotus argued that unless the concept of being were univocal, natural theology would be impossible: the intellect could not move from concepts formed from creatures to conclusions about God, since any inference would equivocate. He held that the intellect can be certain that something is a being while remaining doubtful whether it is finite or infinite, created or uncreated — proof, he claimed, that being is a concept distinct from, and common to, both. Univocity for Scotus is a logical and semantic claim about concepts, not a claim that God and creatures share a common reality or genus; being is not a genus, and God remains infinitely different from creatures in mode.

Reception and influence

The doctrine was debated throughout late medieval and early modern scholasticism. In the twentieth century the French philosopher Gilles Deleuze took up univocity — drawing on Scotus, Spinoza, and Nietzsche — as a cornerstone of his ontology, arguing for a philosophy of immanence in which being is said in a single sense of everything that is. The univocity thesis also features in contemporary analytic metaphysics and philosophical theology, where it is both defended and criticised in debates over the metaphysics of God and the semantics of religious language.
