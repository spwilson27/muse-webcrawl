Five-year plans of the Soviet Union

The five-year plans for the development of the national economy of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (Russian: pyatiletniye plany) consisted of a series of nationwide centralized economic plans in the Soviet Union, beginning in 1928. Initiated by Joseph Stalin and administered by Gosplan, the plans set ambitious production targets for industry, agriculture, and transport, and formed the backbone of the Soviet command economy. Thirteen five-year plans were enacted between 1928 and 1991.

The First Five-Year Plan (1928–1932)

The First Five-Year Plan aimed to transform the USSR from an agrarian economy into an industrial power. It prioritized heavy industry — steel, coal, electricity, and machine building — and launched giant projects such as the Magnitogorsk steel works and the Dnieper hydroelectric station. The plan coincided with the forced collectivization of agriculture, which disrupted food production and contributed to famine, including the Holodomor in Ukraine. Although many targets were not fully met, industrial output grew rapidly.

Subsequent plans

The Second (1933–1937) and Third (1938–1941) plans continued industrialization with increasing emphasis on armaments as war approached. The Fourth Plan (1946–1950) addressed postwar reconstruction; later plans under Khrushchev and Brezhnev addressed housing, consumer goods, and agriculture, with growth slowing from the 1970s. The Seventh Plan (1959–1965) was in fact a seven-year plan. The final, Thirteenth Plan began in 1991 but was abandoned with the dissolution of the Soviet Union.

Legacy

The five-year plans industrialized the USSR and helped it become a superpower, but at immense human cost and with chronic inefficiency, shortages of consumer goods, and falsified reporting. The model was widely copied by other communist states, including China's five-year plans.
