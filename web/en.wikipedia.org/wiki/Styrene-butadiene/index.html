Styrene-butadiene

Styrene-butadiene or styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR) describe families of synthetic rubbers derived from styrene and butadiene. These materials have good abrasion resistance and good aging stability when protected by additives. SBR is the highest-volume synthetic rubber: roughly half of all car tires are made of various types of SBR. The styrene/butadiene ratio influences the properties of the polymer: with a high styrene content, the rubbers are harder and less rubbery.

Production

SBR is produced by emulsion polymerization (E-SBR) or solution polymerization (S-SBR). Emulsion SBR, developed during the Second World War — in the United States under the government rubber program as GR-S ("government rubber-styrene") and in Germany as Buna S — is polymerized either hot (about 50 °C) or cold (about 5 °C), cold rubber having better wear properties. Solution SBR, produced with organolithium initiators, offers narrower molecular weight distribution and is increasingly favoured for high-performance "green tire" treads with lower rolling resistance.

Properties and applications

Typical SBR contains about 23% styrene; it has good abrasion resistance, crack endurance, and ageing stability, though lower resilience and higher heat build-up than natural rubber, and poor resistance to oils and ozone without protection. It is compounded with carbon black or silica fillers. Uses include tire treads and carcasses, shoe heels and soles, floor mats, gaskets, adhesives, and as the latex in paper coating and carpet backing (styrene-butadiene latex).

History

SBR was first prepared in Germany by Walter Bock and Eduard Tschunkur, patented in 1929 (Buna S). Wartime loss of natural-rubber supplies after 1941 drove a crash program in the United States, which built a large government-owned SBR industry whose plants were sold to private companies in 1955. SBR remains the world's most produced synthetic elastomer.
