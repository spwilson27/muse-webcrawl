United States labor law

Labor law
Key statutes: NLRA (1935), FLSA (1938), Title VII (1964), OSHA (1970)

United States labor law sets the rights and duties of employees, labor unions, and employers in the United States. Modern American labor law derives substantially from statutes enacted during the New Deal of the 1930s, supplemented by later civil rights and workplace safety legislation and by state law.

Collective labor law

The National Labor Relations Act of 1935 (Wagner Act) guarantees private-sector employees the right to organize unions, bargain collectively, and strike, and created the National Labor Relations Board to police unfair labor practices. The Taft–Hartley Act of 1947 restricted some union practices and allowed states to pass "right-to-work" laws. The Railway Labor Act covers railroad and airline workers, and public employees are governed by separate federal and state regimes.

Individual employment law

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 establishes a federal minimum wage, overtime pay, and child labor rules. The United States follows the doctrine of employment at will — employment may be terminated by either party for any lawful reason — subject to exceptions and anti-discrimination statutes such as Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Age Discrimination in Employment Act, and the Americans with Disabilities Act.

Safety and benefits

The Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 created OSHA to set workplace safety standards. ERISA (1974) regulates pension and health benefit plans, and the Family and Medical Leave Act of 1993 guarantees unpaid job-protected leave.
