Gracie challenge

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Gracie family promoted their style through open challenge matches.

The Gracie challenge was an open invitation issued by members of the Gracie family, founders of Brazilian jiu-jitsu (Gracie jiu-jitsu), offering to fight practitioners of any other martial art under minimal rules to demonstrate the effectiveness of their grappling system. Cash prizes were at various times offered to anyone who could defeat a Gracie representative.

History

The practice began in Brazil in the 1920s and 1930s, when Carlos and Hélio Gracie promoted challenge matches and vale tudo bouts against boxers, judoka, wrestlers, and capoeiristas to publicise their academy. The most famous of these contests was Hélio Gracie's 1951 bout with Japanese judoka Masahiko Kimura, in which Kimura won by breaking Hélio's arm with the shoulder lock that thereafter bore his name in jiu-jitsu.

In the United States, Rorion Gracie revived the challenge in the 1980s, advertising it in martial arts magazines and filming "Gracie in Action" tapes of academy challenge matches. The campaign culminated in the creation of the Ultimate Fighting Championship in 1993, where Royce Gracie's victories over larger opponents from other disciplines brought Brazilian jiu-jitsu worldwide prominence.

Legacy

The Gracie challenge is credited with sparking the "style versus style" era that gave rise to modern mixed martial arts. Similar open challenges later appeared in other martial arts communities, though the Gracie version remains the best known.
