Gustave Le Bon

Born7 May 1841, Nogent-le-Rotrou, France
Died13 December 1931 (aged 90), Marnes-la-Coquette, France
Known forThe Crowd: A Study of the Popular Mind (1895)

Charles-Marie Gustave Le Bon (7 May 1841 – 13 December 1931) was a French polymath whose interests included anthropology, psychology, sociology, medicine and physics. He is best known for his 1895 work The Crowd: A Study of the Popular Mind, one of the founding texts of crowd psychology.

Le Bon in 1888

Ideas

Le Bon argued that individuals in a crowd undergo a transformation: anonymity, contagion and suggestibility produce a collective mind in which personal responsibility dissolves and primitive, emotional impulses dominate. Crowds, he held, are swayed not by reason but by images, affirmations and repetition, and they are led by leaders of prestige. The book appeared in an era of mass politics and labour unrest, and Le Bon — a conservative pessimist about democracy — advised elites on how mass movements could be managed.

The Crowd influenced later theorists including Sigmund Freud, who critically engaged it in Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego (1921), and its analyses of propaganda and mass persuasion were read by twentieth-century political leaders, for good and ill. Le Bon also wrote extensively on race, civilisation and education, and late in life published speculative works on physics, including The Evolution of Matter (1905). Many of his racial and psychological theories are now regarded as scientifically unsound, but his role in founding social psychology is widely acknowledged.
