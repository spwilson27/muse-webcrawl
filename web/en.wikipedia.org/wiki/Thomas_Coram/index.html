Thomas Coram (c. 1668 – 29 March 1751) was an English sea captain and philanthropist, best known as the founder of the Foundling Hospital in London, the city's first home for abandoned children.

Early life and career

Coram was born in Lyme Regis, Dorset, and went to sea at a young age. He worked in shipping and shipbuilding in the American colonies, spending years in Massachusetts, and later traded from London. His colonial ventures included promoting settlement schemes, some of which entangled him in disputes that left him short of money but with a reputation for plain-spoken honesty.

The Foundling Hospital

On returning to London, Coram was appalled by the sight of infants abandoned in the streets. At the time, no institution in the capital accepted such children. For seventeen years he campaigned for a royal charter to establish a hospital for the "education and maintenance of exposed and deserted young children," enlisting the support of prominent ladies of the aristocracy and, eventually, King George II, who granted the charter in 1739. The Foundling Hospital opened in 1741 in temporary premises in Hatton Garden before moving to a purpose-built home in Bloomsbury. Mothers left tokens with their infants — coins, scraps of fabric, or trinkets — by which a child might one day be reclaimed.

Death and legacy

Coram's relations with the hospital's governors deteriorated and he was effectively removed from its management, though he remained present in its life. He died in 1751 and was buried in the hospital's chapel; his remains were later moved to St Andrew's, Holborn. The Foundling Hospital continued its work into the twentieth century, and its legacy endures in the children's charity Coram and the Foundling Museum in London, which holds art donated by supporters such as William Hogarth and music associated with George Frideric Handel.
