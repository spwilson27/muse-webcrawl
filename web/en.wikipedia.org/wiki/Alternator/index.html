An alternator is an electrical generator that converts mechanical energy into electrical energy in the form of alternating current (AC). It works on the principle of electromagnetic induction: a rotating magnetic field (the rotor) induces an alternating voltage in stationary windings (the stator). Alternators range from the enormous machines in power stations, driven by steam, water, or gas turbines, to the compact belt-driven units in road vehicles that charge the battery and power the electrical system.

Construction and operation

In most alternators the field winding is on the rotor and the armature windings are on the stator — the reverse of a DC generator — because the generated power can then be taken from stationary conductors, needing only small slip rings to feed excitation current to the rotor. The frequency of the output depends on the number of poles and the rotation speed. Where a DC output is required, as in cars, the AC is rectified by diodes, and a voltage regulator controls the field current to hold the output near 14 volts regardless of engine speed. Vehicle alternators replaced DC generators in the 1960s because they are lighter, simpler, more reliable, and can charge at idle.

History

AC generators were developed in the 1880s during the "war of the currents"; designs by engineers including Nikola Tesla, Galileo Ferraris, and the Ganz works in Budapest underpinned the spread of alternating-current power systems. Modern grid alternators are usually synchronous machines kept in exact step with the network frequency.
