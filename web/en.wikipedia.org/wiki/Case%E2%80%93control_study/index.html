Case–control study

A case–control study is a type of observational study in epidemiology in which two groups differing in outcome are identified and compared on the basis of some supposed causal attribute. Researchers start with people who have a disease or outcome of interest (cases) and a comparison group without it (controls), then look backwards in time to compare how frequently a risk factor was present in each group.

Advantages and limitations

Case–control studies are relatively quick and inexpensive and are especially useful for rare diseases or diseases with long latent periods; a single study can examine many risk factors. However, they cannot directly establish incidence, are prone to recall and selection bias, and the strength of association is usually expressed as an odds ratio rather than a relative risk.
