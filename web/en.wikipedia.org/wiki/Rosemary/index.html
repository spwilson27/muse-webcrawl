Rosemary

Rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus, formerly Rosmarinus officinalis) is a fragrant evergreen herb with needle-like leaves and white, pink, purple or blue flowers. A member of the mint family Lamiaceae, it is native to the Mediterranean region but is cultivated worldwide in warm, dry climates. It is used widely as a culinary herb, in perfumes, and in traditional medicine.

Description

Rosemary is an aromatic evergreen shrub with fibrous roots, growing 1–2 metres (3–6.5 ft) tall in favourable conditions. Its stems are woody and bear numerous slender, needle-like leaves 2–4 cm long, dark green above and pale and downy beneath. The leaves are intensely fragrant, with a pungent, slightly piney and camphoraceous scent arising from essential oils including camphor, 1,8-cineole, α-pinene and carnosic acid. Flowers appear in spring and summer (and intermittently in mild climates), borne in clusters; they are usually pale blue to violet, though white and pink forms occur.

The plant was long placed in the small genus Rosmarinus, but molecular phylogenetic studies showed it nestled within the sage genus Salvia, and in 2017 it was formally transferred as Salvia rosmarinus; the old name Rosmarinus officinalis remains a widely used synonym. The genus name derives from the Latin ros marinus, "dew of the sea", reflecting its coastal Mediterranean habitat.

Cultivation

Rosemary thrives in well-drained, somewhat sandy soil in full sun and is drought-tolerant once established, but it is frost-sensitive below about −10 °C. It is propagated mainly from cuttings, as seed germination is slow and unreliable. Numerous cultivars are grown, ranging from upright forms such as 'Tuscan Blue' to prostrate, ground-cover varieties. It is a popular hedging and ornamental plant in Mediterranean-style gardens and attracts bees.

Culinary use

The leaves, used fresh or dried, are a staple of Mediterranean cooking, especially Italian, French and Spanish cuisines. Rosemary pairs with roasted meats such as lamb, pork and chicken, potatoes, breads such as focaccia, and bean dishes; whole sprigs are used to flavour oils and as skewers for grilling. It is an ingredient of the French blend herbes de Provence. Because its flavour is strong, it is used sparingly.

History and folklore

Rosemary has been associated with remembrance since antiquity: Greek students were said to wear garlands of it while studying, and it was used at both weddings and funerals in Europe. In Shakespeare's Hamlet, Ophelia says, "There's rosemary, that's for remembrance." The plant also features in the legend of Hungary water, a perfume attributed to a Queen of Hungary. In modern times sprigs of rosemary are worn in Australia and New Zealand on Anzac Day, since the herb grows wild on the Gallipoli Peninsula.

Uses in medicine and fragrance

Rosemary oil is used in perfumery, soaps and cosmetics. In folk medicine the herb was used for digestive complaints, headaches and poor circulation, and extracts are used as food preservatives for their antioxidant compounds, notably carnosic acid and rosmarinic acid.
