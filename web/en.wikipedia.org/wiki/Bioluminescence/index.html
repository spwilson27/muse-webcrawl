Bioluminescence

Bioluminescence is the production and emission of light by a living organism. It occurs widely in marine animals, especially in the deep sea, and also in some fungi, bacteria, and terrestrial invertebrates such as fireflies and glow-worms. The light is generated by a chemical reaction in which a light-emitting molecule, generically called a luciferin, is oxidised by an enzyme called a luciferase.

Chemistry

Luciferins vary between groups: firefly luciferin, bacterial luciferin, and coelenterazine — widespread among marine animals — are chemically distinct, evidence that bioluminescence evolved independently many times, by some estimates more than 40 times. Most marine bioluminescence is blue or green, the wavelengths that travel furthest through seawater; some dragonfish produce rare red light, which most other deep-sea animals cannot see.

Functions

In the ocean, bioluminescence is used for camouflage by counter-illumination (matching downwelling light), for startling or distracting predators, for luring prey — as in the anglerfish's glowing lure — and for communication. On land, fireflies use species-specific flash patterns to attract mates. Some organisms host symbiotic luminous bacteria in specialised light organs.

Applications

Luciferase genes are widely used as reporter genes in molecular biology, and the green fluorescent protein derived from the jellyfish Aequorea victoria, though fluorescent rather than bioluminescent, is a related research tool. ATP bioluminescence assays are used to test hygiene in food production and hospitals.
