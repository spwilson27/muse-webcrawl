Rotaxane

A rotaxane is a mechanically interlocked molecular architecture consisting of a dumbbell-shaped molecule that is threaded through a macrocycle (a molecular ring). The two components are linked mechanically rather than covalently: the bulky stopper groups at the ends of the dumbbell are large enough to prevent the ring from slipping off, yet the ring remains free to move along and rotate around the axle. The name derives from the Latin rota (wheel) and axis (axle).

Notation and synthesis

Rotaxanes are classified by the number of components: a [2]rotaxane comprises one axle and one ring. Common syntheses use template-directed threading, in which the ring assembles around a recognition site on the axle (for example a cyclodextrin, cucurbituril or crown ether ring around a matching thread), followed by "stoppering" — attaching the bulky end groups. Alternative strategies include clipping a ring shut around a pre-formed dumbbell and active-template synthesis, in which a metal centre inside the macrocycle catalyses formation of the axle bond.

Applications

Because the ring can shuttle between stations along the axle in response to stimuli such as light, pH or redox changes, rotaxanes are prototypes of molecular machines — switches, motors and elevators at the nanoscale. J. Fraser Stoddart shared the 2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for work on such mechanically interlocked molecules. Proposed applications include molecular electronics, drug delivery and smart materials; ring-like protein complexes that clamp onto DNA are natural analogues.
