Psychomotor learning

Psychomotor learning is the relationship between cognitive functions and physical movement: the development of skills in which mental processes direct coordinated bodily actions. Psychomotor learning is demonstrated by physical skills such as movement, coordination, manipulation, dexterity, grace, strength and speed — actions performed while applying knowledge, as in driving a car, playing an instrument, typing, surgery or athletic performance.

Domains and taxonomy

In educational theory, psychomotor learning is one of three domains of learning alongside cognitive and affective domains (Bloom's taxonomy). Psychomotor taxonomies, such as those of Elizabeth Simpson and R. H. Dave, order skills from perception and imitation through guided response, mechanism and complex overt response to adaptation and origination of new movement patterns.

Stages of acquisition

Fitts and Posner's influential model describes three stages: a cognitive stage, in which the learner consciously works out the task and performance is error-prone; an associative stage, in which movements are refined and errors decrease; and an autonomous stage, in which the skill runs largely automatically with little attention. Practice schedules, feedback, and observation of models influence the rate and durability of learning; with extensive practice, motor programs become encoded in procedural memory involving the cerebellum and basal ganglia.

Assessment and applications

Psychomotor outcomes are assessed through performance tests measuring accuracy, speed and quality against criteria. The concept underpins training in medicine, dentistry, aviation, sport, music and the manual trades, where simulators and deliberate practice are used to build skill safely.
