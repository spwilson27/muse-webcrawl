Willis H. O'Brien

Born: 2 March 1886, Oakland, California
Died: 8 November 1962, Los Angeles, California
Occupation: Special effects artist, stop-motion animator
Known for: The Lost World (1925); King Kong (1933)

Willis Harold O'Brien (March 2, 1886 – November 8, 1962) was an American motion picture special effects and stop-motion animation pioneer, whose work on The Lost World (1925) and King Kong (1933) established the art of stop-motion creature effects in cinema.

Early work

O'Brien worked as a cartoonist, sculptor, and boxer before making short films such as The Dinosaur and the Missing Link (1915) for Edison. For the Edison company and later independent producers he refined the technique of animating articulated models frame by frame, and created effects for The Ghost of Slumber Mountain (1918).

The Lost World and King Kong

The Lost World (1925), adapted from Arthur Conan Doyle's novel, brought dinosaurs to life with unprecedented realism. At RKO, O'Brien led the effects team on King Kong (1933), animating Kong and the creatures of Skull Island and blending them with live actors using miniature rear projection and other innovations. He mentored Ray Harryhausen, who assisted him on Mighty Joe Young (1949), for which O'Brien won the Academy Award for Best Visual Effects.

Later years

Many of O'Brien's later projects, including The War Eagles and the story that became Gwangi, went unproduced in his lifetime. He died in Los Angeles in 1962, recognised as the founding figure of stop-motion effects work.
