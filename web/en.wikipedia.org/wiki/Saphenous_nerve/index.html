Saphenous nerve

The saphenous nerve (also the long or internal saphenous nerve) is the largest cutaneous branch of the femoral nerve. It is a purely sensory nerve, derived from the L3 and L4 spinal roots, supplying the skin of the medial side of the leg and foot.

Course

The saphenous nerve arises from the femoral nerve in the femoral triangle, descends lateral to the femoral artery, then enters the adductor canal (subsartorial canal), where it crosses the artery anteriorly from lateral to medial. It emerges between the sartorius and gracilis tendons, becomes subcutaneous behind the medial condyle of the femur, and accompanies the great saphenous vein down the medial leg. It gives off an infrapatellar branch to the skin below the kneecap and medial crural cutaneous branches, terminating in the skin of the medial side of the foot, often reaching the ball of the great toe.

Clinical significance

The saphenous nerve is commonly used in nerve blocks for surgery of the foot and ankle, often combined with sciatic nerve block, and can be anaesthetized in the adductor canal (subsartorial block) for knee surgery analgesia. It may be injured during great saphenous vein stripping or harvesting for coronary artery bypass grafting, producing numbness or neuropathic pain along the medial leg.
