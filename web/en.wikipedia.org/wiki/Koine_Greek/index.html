Koine Greek (ἡ κοινὴ διάλεκτος, hē koinḕ diálektos, "the common dialect"), also known as Alexandrian dialect, common Attic, Hellenistic Greek or Biblical Greek, was the common supra-regional form of Greek spoken and written during the Hellenistic period, the Roman Empire and the early Byzantine Empire. It evolved from the spread of Greek following the conquests of Alexander the Great in the fourth century BC, and served as the lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean and parts of the Middle East for many centuries.

Origins

Koine emerged as a common dialect in the armies of Alexander, composed of soldiers drawn from many Greek states. It was based principally on Attic Greek with admixtures from Ionic and other dialects, and it levelled out many local dialectal differences. As Greek became the language of administration and commerce in the Hellenistic kingdoms — in Egypt, Syria, Asia Minor and beyond — the Koine spread among non-native speakers, simplifying in the process. Greek continued to be the dominant language of the eastern Roman Empire, and Koine is the direct ancestor of Medieval and Modern Greek.

Sources

The most widely read texts in Koine are the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible produced in Alexandria from the third century BC, and the New Testament, written in the first century AD. Other sources range from the histories of Polybius and the philosophical discourses of Epictetus to vast numbers of papyri from Egypt — letters, contracts, petitions and receipts — which preserve the everyday language of ordinary people, and inscriptions throughout the Greek world.

Features

Compared with Classical Attic, Koine shows simplification and regularisation: the dative case fell out of spoken use, the optative mood largely disappeared, the dual number was lost, many irregular verb forms were regularised by analogy, and vocabulary shifted through borrowing (from Latin, Hebrew and Aramaic) and semantic change. Pronunciation also changed markedly: the pitch accent gave way to a stress accent, diphthongs monophthongised, and several classical distinctions of vowel length and quality collapsed (iotacism), moving Greek toward its modern sound system.

Legacy

Because the New Testament and Septuagint are written in Koine, the language remains central to biblical scholarship and to the liturgy of the Greek Orthodox Church. Its study also transformed linguistics: the papyri showed that New Testament Greek was not a special "Holy Ghost language" but the vernacular of its day. Koine forms the bridge between ancient and modern Greek, and most features of the modern language can be traced to developments in the Koine period.
