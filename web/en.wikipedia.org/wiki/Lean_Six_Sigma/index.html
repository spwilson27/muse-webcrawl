Lean Six Sigma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lean Six Sigma is a process improvement methodology that combines the waste-reduction principles of lean manufacturing with the variation-reduction and defect-control statistics of Six Sigma. The goal of Lean Six Sigma is to improve process performance by systematically removing waste and reducing variation, thereby improving quality, speed, and customer satisfaction.

Approach

Six Sigma, developed at Motorola in the 1980s and popularised by General Electric in the 1990s, uses the DMAIC cycle (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control) and statistical tools to reduce defects toward a target of 3.4 defects per million opportunities. Lean, derived from the Toyota Production System, focuses on identifying and eliminating the seven wastes (muda): transport, inventory, motion, waiting, overproduction, overprocessing, and defects.

Lean Six Sigma merges the two: lean tools such as value stream mapping, 5S, kaizen, and kanban increase flow and speed, while Six Sigma tools such as control charts, design of experiments, and capability analysis reduce variation. Practitioners are certified through a belt hierarchy—White, Yellow, Green, Black, and Master Black Belt—borrowed from martial arts.

Criticism

Critics argue that Lean Six Sigma can become overly bureaucratic, that belt certifications vary widely in rigour, and that an emphasis on incremental efficiency may stifle innovation. Some quality experts have also questioned the statistical assumptions behind the 3.4 defects-per-million figure and its "1.5 sigma shift".
