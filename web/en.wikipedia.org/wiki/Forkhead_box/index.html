Forkhead box

Forkhead box (FOX) proteins are a family of transcription factors that play important roles in regulating the expression of genes involved in cell growth, proliferation, differentiation, and longevity. They share a characteristic DNA-binding domain known as the forkhead box or "winged helix" domain, a sequence of about 80–100 amino acids that forms a structure resembling a butterfly's wings.

Discovery and naming

The founding member of the family was identified in the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, where a mutation in the gene caused defects in head development, producing a forked head structure — hence "forkhead". The corresponding DNA-binding region was termed the forkhead box. Homologous genes were subsequently found throughout animals, fungi, and other eukaryotes.

Classification

FOX genes are divided into subfamilies designated by letters (FOXA, FOXB, FOXC, and so on through FOXS). Humans have around 50 FOX genes. Notable members include:

  • FOXA1/FOXA2 — pioneer factors important in liver, lung, and endoderm development.
  • FOXP2 — implicated in speech and language development; mutations cause a severe speech and language disorder.
  • FOXP3 — the master regulator of regulatory T cells; mutations cause the autoimmune syndrome IPEX.
  • FOXO proteins (FOXO1, FOXO3, FOXO4) — key effectors of insulin/IGF-1 signalling, stress resistance, and ageing; FOXO3 variants are associated with human longevity.
  • FOXM1 — a proliferation-associated factor frequently overexpressed in cancers.

Function

FOX proteins bind DNA through their forkhead domain and act as transcriptional activators or repressors, often in combination with other factors. Several subfamilies act as "pioneer factors" able to bind compacted chromatin and open it for other regulators. Their activity is controlled by signalling pathways: FOXO proteins, for example, are phosphorylated by the kinase AKT downstream of insulin signalling, which excludes them from the nucleus and switches off their target genes.

Clinical significance

Mutations in FOX genes cause a variety of developmental disorders, and dysregulation of FOX factors is implicated in cancer, diabetes, immune disorders, and neurodegeneration.
