Effects of climate change on mental health

The effects of climate change on mental health are the direct and indirect psychological consequences of climate change, including extreme weather events, gradual environmental degradation, and awareness of the climate crisis itself. Research in this area has grown rapidly since the 2010s, and the IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report acknowledged mental health impacts as a significant health risk of climate change.

Pathways

  • Direct effects: trauma, PTSD, depression and anxiety following floods, wildfires, hurricanes and heatwaves; heat itself is associated with increased aggression, hospital admissions for mental disorders and suicide rates.
  • Indirect effects: displacement, loss of livelihood and homes, food and water insecurity, conflict, and the erosion of place attachment — grief for changed home environments, termed solastalgia.
  • Psychosocial awareness effects: eco-anxiety (chronic fear of environmental doom) and ecological grief, especially among children and young people; a 2021 survey of 10,000 young people in 10 countries found 59% very or extremely worried about climate change.

Vulnerable groups

Children, the elderly, people with pre-existing mental illness, farmers and Indigenous communities with deep cultural ties to land are considered particularly vulnerable. Clinicians have proposed new diagnostic framings and interventions, while critics debate the risk of pathologising rational concern about a genuine threat.

Responses

Proposed responses include integrating mental health into climate adaptation plans, psychological first aid after disasters, community resilience building, and climate action itself as a source of agency that reduces distress.
