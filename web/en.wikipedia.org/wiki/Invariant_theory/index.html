Invariant theory


The classical invariant theorists Cayley, Sylvester and Hilbert.

Invariant theory is a branch of abstract algebra dealing with actions of groups on algebraic varieties, such as the action of the general linear group on the space of homogeneous forms in many variables. It studies polynomial functions — invariants — that remain unchanged under the group action.

Classical theory

The subject flourished in the nineteenth century through the work of Arthur Cayley, James Joseph Sylvester, Paul Gordan and others, who computed invariants and covariants of binary and ternary forms by elaborate symbolic methods. In 1890 David Hilbert transformed the field with his basis theorem, proving nonconstructively that the ring of invariants of a reductive group acting on a polynomial ring is finitely generated — prompting Gordan's famous remark that "this is not mathematics, this is theology."

Modern developments

Hilbert's fourteenth problem asked whether finite generation holds in greater generality; Masayoshi Nagata found a counterexample in 1959. David Mumford's geometric invariant theory (1965) recast the theory to construct quotient spaces and moduli spaces in algebraic geometry, and invariant theory remains closely connected with representation theory, algebraic geometry and classical mechanics through the theory of moment maps.
