Cyanosis

Placeholder image: Cyanosis

Cyanosis is the bluish or purplish discoloration of the skin or mucous membranes due to the tissues near the skin surface having low oxygen saturation. Based on earlier work by Lundsgaard and Van Slyke, it is typically described as occurring when the absolute concentration of deoxygenated hemoglobin exceeds about 3–5 g/dL. Cyanosis is divided into two main types: central cyanosis, due to poor oxygenation of arterial blood and visible in the lips and tongue, and peripheral cyanosis, due to reduced local blood flow and visible in the extremities.

Central cyanosis

Central cyanosis results from arterial desaturation. Causes include respiratory disorders impairing gas exchange, congenital heart defects with right-to-left shunting (such as tetralogy of Fallot), hypoventilation, and high altitude. Infants with certain congenital heart conditions may appear cyanotic ("blue babies").

Peripheral cyanosis

Peripheral cyanosis, or acrocyanosis when it affects the hands and feet, arises from vasoconstriction or sluggish circulation, as in cold exposure, shock, heart failure, or Raynaud phenomenon, while arterial oxygen saturation remains normal.

Other forms

Differential or regional cyanosis can occur in some congenital heart lesions. Pseudocyanosis is a bluish skin tint not caused by deoxyhemoglobin, for example from certain drugs or metal deposits. Abnormal hemoglobins, such as in methemoglobinemia and sulfhemoglobinemia, can also cause a cyanotic appearance disproportionate to symptoms.

Diagnosis and management

Assessment includes pulse oximetry and arterial blood gas analysis; cyanosis is a clinical sign rather than a diagnosis, and management targets the underlying cause, often with supplemental oxygen and urgent evaluation in acute cases. Detection can be more difficult in patients with darker skin or anemia, since visible cyanosis depends on absolute deoxyhemoglobin levels.
