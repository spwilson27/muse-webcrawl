Variance inflation factor

In statistics, the variance inflation factor (VIF) is the ratio of the variance of a parameter estimate when fitting a full model containing other parameters to the variance of the parameter estimate if the model were estimated with only that parameter. It quantifies how much the variance of a regression coefficient is increased because of multicollinearity — correlation among the predictor variables — in an ordinary least squares regression analysis.

Collinear predictors inflate the variance of estimated coefficients.

Definition

For predictor j, the VIF is defined as VIFj = 1 / (1 − Rj2), where Rj2 is the coefficient of determination from regressing predictor j on all the other predictors. The reciprocal, 1 − Rj2, is called tolerance. A VIF of 1 indicates no correlation with the other predictors; values above 5 or 10 are commonly taken as evidence of problematic multicollinearity, though the thresholds are rules of thumb.

Interpretation and remedies

Large VIFs signal that a coefficient's standard error is inflated, so its estimate is imprecise even though overall prediction may remain good. Remedies include removing redundant predictors, combining them (for example into an index or principal components), centring variables, collecting more data, or using penalised methods such as ridge regression. VIFs can be generalised to sets of predictors (generalised variance inflation factors) for categorical variables represented by several dummy terms.
