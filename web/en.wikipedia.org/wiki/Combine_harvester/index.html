Combine harvester

TypeAgricultural machinery
FunctionReaping, threshing and winnowing grain crops

The modern combine harvester, or simply combine, is a machine designed to harvest a variety of grain crops efficiently. The name derives from its combining three separate harvesting operations — reaping, threshing, and winnowing — into a single process. The waste straw left behind is either chopped and spread on the field or baled; the grain is collected in an onboard tank and unloaded periodically into trailers.

Operation

A wide header at the front cuts the crop and feeds it by auger to the threshing mechanism, where a rotating cylinder or rotor beats or rubs the grain from the heads. Sieves and a fan separate the grain from chaff and straw (winnowing). Specialized headers exist for corn (maize), soybeans, rice, sunflowers and other crops, and hillside ("levelling") combines adjust their body to work steep slopes such as those of the Pacific Northwest.

History

The combine was patented in the United States by Hiram Moore in 1834 and developed in Michigan and California; horse-drawn combines with enormous ground-driven cutter bars worked the wheat fields of the western United States in the late 19th century. Steam and then petrol tractors supplied power before self-propelled combines became standard after the Second World War. Modern machines add GPS guidance, yield mapping and telematics, turning the combine into a data-gathering platform.

Manufacturers

Major manufacturers include John Deere, AGCO (Massey Ferguson, Fendt), CNH Industrial (Case IH, New Holland), CLAAS and Kubota.
