Islamic garden

An Islamic garden is a garden built in accordance with the traditions of Islam, drawing on Persian, and later Mughal, Timurid, Ottoman and Andalusian models. Its characteristic form is the charbagh (from Persian chahar bagh, "four gardens"): a quadrilateral garden divided by walkways and flowing water channels into four sections, representing the four elements or the four rivers of paradise described in the Qur'an, which promises believers "gardens beneath which rivers flow".

Origins and symbolism

The tradition descends from the ancient Persian paradise garden (pairidaeza, the origin of the word "paradise"), an enclosed, irrigated space contrasting with the surrounding arid land. Water, shade and enclosure are the defining elements: channels and pools cool the air and mirror the sky, while fruit trees and fragrant flowers provide sensory richness. Gardens served as earthly images of the paradise promised to the faithful and often accompanied palaces, mosques and tombs.

Regional developments

In Persia, gardens such as those of Isfahan and Shiraz — including the Eram Garden — refined the charbagh form. The Mughal emperors carried the style to India: Babur laid out gardens in Kabul and Agra, Humayun's Tomb in Delhi (c. 1570) set a charbagh around a mausoleum, and Shah Jahan's Taj Mahal (completed 1653) placed its tomb at the end of a four-part garden along the Yamuna River. The Shalimar Gardens in Lahore and Srinagar are further celebrated examples.

In the western Islamic world, the Alhambra and Generalife in Granada, Spain, developed courtyard gardens with rills, fountains and pavilions, while Ottoman gardens in Istanbul favoured terraces and tulip beds overlooking the Bosphorus. Tomb gardens, mosque courtyards and palace pleasure grounds all adapted the same vocabulary of water, geometry and shade.

Features

Typical elements include a high enclosing wall, a central pavilion or kiosk, axial water channels fed by qanat or aqueduct, pools and fountains, sunken flower beds, plane and cypress trees, and fruit-bearing plants such as orange, pomegranate and date palm. Geometry is usually rectilinear and symmetrical, reinforcing the garden's role as an ordered image of the cosmos.
