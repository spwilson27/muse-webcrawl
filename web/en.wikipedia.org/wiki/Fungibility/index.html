Fungibility

Fungibility is the property of a good or a commodity whose individual units are essentially interchangeable, each unit being indistinguishable from and mutually substitutable for another. For example, one kilogram of pure gold is equivalent to any other kilogram of pure gold, and one banknote of a given denomination is legally interchangeable with another of the same denomination.

Examples

Fungible items include currency, standardised commodities such as crude oil of a given grade, bushels of wheat of a stated grade, and shares of the same class in the same company. Money is the paradigmatic fungible good: it does not matter which particular coins or notes are surrendered in payment of a debt of a given amount.

Non-fungible goods

By contrast, goods with unique attributes — real estate parcels, works of art, collectible items, or a specific used car — are non-fungible: one unit cannot be substituted for another without changing what is owed. The distinction became widely discussed in the 2020s with the rise of non-fungible tokens (NFTs), blockchain tokens designed to be unique and non-interchangeable, in contrast to fungible cryptocurrencies such as bitcoin, each unit of which is interchangeable.

In law

In legal usage, fungible obligations can be discharged with any units of the specified kind and quality, and fungible goods held in bulk (as in a grain elevator) may be owned in undivided shares. The concept matters in contract law, securities regulation, commodities trading, and bankruptcy, where the fungibility of stored goods affects creditors' rights.

Degrees of fungibility

Fungibility is not always absolute: goods may be fungible within a grade or batch but not across them, and items nominally identical may become non-fungible through provenance — a banknote once owned by a celebrity, for instance, may command a collector's premium that ordinary notes do not.
