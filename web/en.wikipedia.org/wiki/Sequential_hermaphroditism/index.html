Sequential hermaphroditism

Clownfish are protandrous: all individuals mature as males, and the dominant individual in a group becomes female.

Sequential hermaphroditism (also called dichogamy in botany) is a type of hermaphroditism that occurs in many fish, gastropods, and plants. It involves the complete and irreversible replacement of the gonads: an individual is born as one sex and later changes into the other. Species that can change sex include species of parrotfish, wrasses, groupers, clownfish, and many others.

Types

Sequential hermaphroditism can be divided into three broad categories:

Protandry: the organism is born male and changes into a female. An example is the clownfish (subfamily Amphiprioninae), which live in groups with a strict dominance hierarchy: the largest fish is female, the second largest is the breeding male, and the others are non-breeding males. If the female dies, the breeding male changes sex and becomes the female.

Protogyny: the organism is born female and changes into a male. This is common in wrasses (family Labridae), where large terminal-phase males defend territories and harems of females.

Bidirectional sex change: the organism has both female and male reproductive organs and can change in either direction, as occurs in some coral-dwelling gobies.

Evolutionary explanation

The size-advantage model explains sequential hermaphroditism by noting that reproductive success scales with size differently in the two sexes. If large males monopolise matings, an individual gains by reproducing first as a female when small and as a male when large (protogyny). Conversely, if female fecundity increases steeply with size while male success does not, changing from male to female (protandry) is favoured.
