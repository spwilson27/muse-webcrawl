Kallmann syndrome

Kallmann syndrome (KS) is a genetic disorder characterised by the combination of congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism — a failure to start or complete puberty due to deficient gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) — and a partial or total loss of the sense of smell (anosmia or hyposmia).

Cause

During embryonic development, GnRH-producing neurons and olfactory neurons originate together outside the brain and normally migrate along the olfactory nerve into the hypothalamus. In Kallmann syndrome, mutations in genes such as ANOS1 (formerly KAL1), FGFR1, PROK2/PROKR2 and CHD7 disrupt this migration. The condition may be inherited in X-linked, autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive patterns, and is more commonly diagnosed in males. It was described by the German psychiatrist Franz Josef Kallmann in 1944.

Features and diagnosis

Beyond absent puberty and anosmia, some patients show mirror movements of the hands (bimanual synkinesis), absence of a kidney, cleft palate, hearing loss or dental agenesis, depending on the gene involved. Diagnosis rests on hormone levels, smell testing, MRI showing absent or hypoplastic olfactory bulbs, and genetic testing.

Treatment

Hormone replacement (testosterone or oestrogen/progestogen) induces secondary sexual characteristics and maintains bone health; fertility can often be achieved with gonadotropin therapy or pulsatile GnRH. There is no treatment for the anosmia itself.
