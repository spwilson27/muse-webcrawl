Cyrenaics

Aristippus of Cyrene, founder of the Cyrenaic school.

The Cyrenaics were a school of ancient Greek philosophy founded in the fourth century BC by Aristippus of Cyrene, a pupil of Socrates. The school is known for an early and uncompromising hedonism: it taught that bodily pleasure in the present moment is the only good, and pain the only evil. Cyrenaicism flourished for about a century before being eclipsed by Epicureanism, which offered a more systematic and moderate hedonism.

Doctrine

The Cyrenaics held a subjective theory of knowledge: we can know only our own immediate sensations ("affections"), not the external things that cause them. From this they concluded that the goal of life is the pleasure of the moment — particularly bodily pleasure, which they judged more intense and certain than mental pleasure — while future pleasures, being uncertain, should not be counted on. Unlike Epicurus, they emphasised active enjoyment over the mere absence of pain, and regarded gratitude, friendship, and reputation as valuable chiefly for the pleasure they bring.

Figures and branches

Aristippus's teaching was carried on by his daughter Arete and his grandson Aristippus the Younger. Later members modified the doctrine in divergent directions: Theodorus "the Atheist" dismissed gods and conventional morality; Hegesias argued that complete happiness is unattainable and earned the nickname "the Death-persuader"; and Anniceris softened the school's egoism by restoring value to friendship, family, and country.

Sources

No Cyrenaic writings survive; the school's doctrines are known from later summaries, especially Diogenes Laërtius's Lives of the Eminent Philosophers, and from hostile or neutral reports in other ancient authors.
