Bonnot Gang

The Bonnot Gang (French: La bande à Bonnot) was a French criminal anarchist group that operated in France and Belgium during the Belle Époque, from 1911 to 1912. Named after its nominal leader, Jules Bonnot, the gang pioneered the use of the automobile and repeating rifles in robbery — outgunning the police of the day — and its spectacular exploits and violent end gripped the French press.

Origins

The gang formed among illegalist anarchists around the journal L'Anarchie in Paris. Its members — including Octave Garnier, Raymond Callemin ("Raymond la Science"), Étienne Monier, Édouard Carouy and André Soudy — embraced "individual repossession": robbery as revolutionary expropriation of the bourgeoisie. Jules Bonnot, a chauffeur and ex-soldier who had fallen into crime in Lyon, joined them late in 1911 and supplied driving skill and daring.

The crime spree

On 21 December 1911 the gang robbed a Société Générale bank messenger in Paris, shooting him and escaping in a stolen Delaunay-Belleville — widely described as the first motorised bank robbery. Further robberies of banks, gun shops and wealthy targets followed in France and Belgium, and policemen and bystanders were killed. A huge manhunt ensued. Carouy was arrested; Monier and others were hunted across the country.

The end

In April 1912 police cornered Bonnot at Choisy-le-Roi; after a siege involving hundreds of police and soldiers, the house was dynamited and Bonnot died of his wounds, leaving a diary maintaining his outlaw philosophy. Garnier and René Valet died in a similar siege at Nogent-sur-Marne in May. The surviving members were tried in February 1913; Callemin, Monier, Soudy and Dieudonné were sentenced to death — Dieudonné was reprieved — and guillotined in April 1913.

Legacy

The gang's war with the state prompted the creation of France's first motorised police brigades under Georges Clemenceau's earlier reforms (the "Brigades du Tigre") and remains a touchstone in anarchist and popular culture, retold in memoirs, films and songs.
