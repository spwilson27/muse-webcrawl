The genrō (元老) were an unofficial group of retired elder statesmen in the Empire of Japan who served as informal, extraconstitutional advisers to the Emperor during the Meiji, Taishō, and early Shōwa eras. Drawn from the leaders of the Meiji Restoration, the genrō dominated Japanese politics behind the scenes for more than half a century, most importantly by recommending who should become Prime Minister.

History

The term was never a formal office. In 1885 a number of senior statesmen were given the title of genkun (adviser), and in 1889 several privy councillors received the designation genrō. By the 1890s the word referred to a small circle of senior Restoration leaders — men such as Itō Hirobumi, Yamagata Aritomo, Matsukata Masayoshi, Inoue Kaoru, Ōyama Iwao, and Saigō Tsugumichi — whose prestige derived from having created the modern Japanese state.

Because the Meiji Constitution was silent on how a prime minister was to be chosen, the genrō filled the vacuum: after a cabinet fell, they conferred and presented the Emperor with the name of the next premier. They also influenced foreign policy, military appointments, and the selection of senior officials.

Decline

The group's power rested entirely on personal authority, so it shrank as its members died. By the 1920s only Saionji Kinmochi remained, and he increasingly shared the nominating function with other court and party figures. Saionji, the last genrō, died in 1940, and with him the institution effectively ended. Historians have debated whether the genrō stabilised early constitutional government or retarded the growth of party democracy by keeping ultimate power outside the Diet.

List of genrō

Usually counted among the genrō are Itō Hirobumi, Kuroda Kiyotaka, Yamagata Aritomo, Matsukata Masayoshi, Inoue Kaoru, Saigō Tsugumichi, Ōyama Iwao, Katsura Tarō, and Saionji Kinmochi.
