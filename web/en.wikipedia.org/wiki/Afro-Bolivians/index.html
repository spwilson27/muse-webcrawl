Afro-Bolivians are Bolivians of predominantly African ancestry, descended from enslaved Africans brought to colonial Bolivia. The 2012 census counted about 23,330 Afro-Bolivians, concentrated in the subtropical Yungas valleys of La Paz Department — around Coroico, Chulumani and the village of Tocaña — with additional communities in Santa Cruz and Cochabamba.

History

Enslaved Africans were brought to the silver mines of Potosí from the 16th century; many died in the brutal conditions of the mines and the Potosí mint, where they worked coinage machinery. Survivors and later arrivals moved to the warmer Yungas to work on coca, coffee and citrus estates under the hacienda system. Slavery was abolished in 1851, but sharecropping (pongueaje) persisted until the agrarian reform following the 1952 revolution.

Culture and recognition

Afro-Bolivian culture is best known for the saya, a music and dance genre with drums, bells and call-and-response singing, revived in the 1980s by cultural movements that also campaigned for political recognition. The 2009 Bolivian constitution formally recognised Afro-Bolivians as one of the country's nations and peoples, and 23 September is celebrated as a national day of Afro-Bolivian culture. The community also maintains a ceremonial monarchy: Julio Pinedo, a descendant of an African prince brought as a slave according to community tradition, was recognised as King of the Afro-Bolivians in 1992.
