Woodblock printing in Japan

Woodblock printing in Japan (moku hanga) is a technique best known for its use in the artistic ukiyo-e genre of single-sheet prints and paintings, though it was also used very widely for printing books in the Edo period. The Japanese technique, adapted from Chinese woodblock printing (known in Japan by the 8th century), reached its artistic height in the 18th and 19th centuries with artists such as Hokusai and Hiroshige.

History

Among the earliest Japanese woodblock prints are the Hyakumantō Darani (c. 770 CE), miniature Buddhist charms printed in enormous numbers and placed in small pagodas — some of the oldest printed texts in the world. Woodblock printing spread through Buddhist publishing for centuries. In the Edo period (1603–1868), expanding urban literacy created a mass market for printed books and, from the late 17th century, for single-sheet pictures of the "floating world" (ukiyo-e): actors, courtesans, landscapes and scenes of daily life.

Technique

Traditional production divided labour among four specialists: the designer (artist), the carver, the printer and the publisher. The artist's drawing was pasted face-down on a block of fine-grained cherry wood and carved away, leaving the design's lines in relief for the key block (printed in black). Separate blocks were carved for each colour, with registration marks (kentō) cut into every block to align the impressions. The printer applied water-based pigments to the block, laid a dampened sheet of washi paper over it, and rubbed the back of the sheet with a pad called a baren to transfer the image. Special effects included bokashi (hand-applied colour gradation), embossing (karazuri), and the use of mica dust and metal powders.

Artists and schools

Key figures include Hishikawa Moronobu, who pioneered the single-sheet print; Suzuki Harunobu, who introduced full-colour nishiki-e printing in the 1760s; Kitagawa Utamaro, famed for studies of women; Tōshūsai Sharaku, known for intense actor portraits; and the landscape masters Katsushika Hokusai (Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, including The Great Wave off Kanagawa) and Utagawa Hiroshige (The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō).

Influence and later developments

Exported prints profoundly influenced European art in the later 19th century (Japonisme), shaping Impressionist and Post-Impressionist composition and flat colour. In the 20th century the shin-hanga ("new prints") movement revived the collaborative system for export markets, while sōsaku-hanga ("creative prints") artists cut and printed their own blocks. Moku hanga is still practised today, valued for its water-based, non-toxic process.
