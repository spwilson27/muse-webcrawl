Hybrid warfare

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hybrid warfare is a theory of military strategy in which a belligerent blends conventional warfare, irregular warfare, and cyberwarfare with other influencing methods, such as disinformation, diplomacy, lawfare, and foreign political interference. By combining kinetic operations with subversive efforts, the aggressor intends to avoid attribution or retribution and to exploit the vulnerabilities of the target.

Definition and characteristics

The concept was developed in the 2000s, notably by Frank Hoffman of the United States Marine Corps, who argued that future adversaries would employ a fused mixture of conventional weapons, irregular tactics, terrorism, and criminal behaviour in the same battlespace. Hybrid warfare is often associated with the "grey zone" between peace and war, in which actions fall below the threshold that would trigger a conventional military response or a formal declaration of war.

Common elements include: regular and irregular forces operating alongside one another; information operations and propaganda; cyberattacks on infrastructure; economic coercion; the use of proxy groups and unmarked personnel ("little green men"); and the exploitation of legal and political processes.

Applications and examples

The term gained wide currency after Russia's annexation of Crimea in 2014, in which unmarked Russian forces, local proxies, and an intensive information campaign preceded a disputed referendum. Russia's interference in foreign elections and the tactics of groups such as Hezbollah in the 2006 Lebanon War and the Islamic State have also been analysed as hybrid warfare. NATO and the European Union established centres and strategies to counter "hybrid threats," including the European Centre of Excellence for Countering Hybrid Threats in Helsinki (2017).

Debate

Critics argue that hybrid warfare is an old phenomenon under a new name — combinations of regular and irregular methods appear throughout military history — and that the concept's vagueness can make it analytically unhelpful. The related idea of the "Gerasimov doctrine," derived from a 2013 article by Russian general Valery Gerasimov, was later described by its populariser as overstated.
