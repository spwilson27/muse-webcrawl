Sixteen Tons

SongwriterMerle Travis (credited)
First recorded1946, Merle Travis
Hit versionTennessee Ernie Ford, 1955
GenreCountry, folk

"Sixteen Tons" is a song about a coal miner, based on life in the coal mines of Muhlenberg County, Kentucky. It was written and first recorded by Merle Travis in 1946 and released the following year on his album Folk Songs of the Hills. The song's most famous recording, by Tennessee Ernie Ford in 1955, spent eight weeks at number one on the Billboard country chart and seven weeks atop the pop chart, becoming one of the best-selling singles of the decade.

Content and themes

The refrain — "You load sixteen tons, what do you get? Another day older and deeper in debt. Saint Peter, don't you call me 'cause I can't go; I owe my soul to the company store" — laments the debt bondage of the truck system, under which miners were paid in company scrip redeemable only at high-priced company stores. Travis said the title line echoed a remark of his father, also a miner, and "I owe my soul to the company store" derived from a letter his brother wrote. The sixteen-ton figure referred to the tonnage a miner was expected to load by hand in a day.

Authorship dispute

Although Travis is officially credited, Kentucky miner George S. Davis claimed in later years that he wrote the song in the 1930s. The claim has never been legally resolved, and recordings continue to credit Travis.

Legacy

The song has been recorded by dozens of artists across genres, from Johnny Cash to Tom Morello, and Ford's version was inducted into the Grammy Hall of Fame and selected for the Library of Congress National Recording Registry. It remains a standard of American folk and country music and a widely quoted anthem of working-class hardship.
