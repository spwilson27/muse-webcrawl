The Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station is a United States scientific research station at the South Pole of the Earth, the southernmost place on the planet. It is located on the high plateau of Antarctica at an elevation of 2,835 metres (9,301 feet) above sea level. The station is named in honour of Roald Amundsen and Robert F. Scott, who reached the Pole in 1911 and 1912 respectively.

History

The original station was built for the International Geophysical Year and was occupied from January 1957. Because the ice sheet at the Pole moves about ten metres per year and snow accumulates steadily, the first station was gradually buried and crushed. It was replaced in 1975 by a geodesic dome station, 50 metres wide and 16 metres high, which sheltered a cluster of buildings. The dome was in turn superseded by the current elevated station, a modular building raised on stilts so that drifting snow passes beneath it; it was dedicated in January 2008 and the dome was dismantled by 2010.

Research

Research at the station includes astronomy and astrophysics, taking advantage of the clear, dry, stable air and months of continuous darkness. Major instruments have included the South Pole Telescope and the IceCube Neutrino Observatory, which uses a cubic kilometre of the ice cap as a detector. Studies in glaciology, geophysics and atmospheric science — including long-running measurements relevant to the ozone hole — are also carried out.

Life at the station

The station is resupplied during the austral summer (roughly November to February) by ski-equipped LC-130 Hercules aircraft landing on the Jack F. Paulus Skiway. The summer population is around 150; during the winter a crew of roughly 45 "winter-overs" remains in isolation through the six-month polar night, when temperatures can fall below −70 °C. The ceremonial pole, surrounded by the flags of the Antarctic Treaty signatories, stands near a marker for the geographic pole that is repositioned each New Year's Day to account for ice motion.
