Red Queen hypothesis

Image placeholder: the Red Queen, namesake of the hypothesis.

The Red Queen hypothesis is an evolutionary hypothesis proposing that organisms must constantly adapt, evolve and reproduce not merely to gain an advantage but simply to survive while pitted against ever-evolving opposing organisms in a changing environment. It is commonly used to explain the evolution of sexual reproduction and the persistent extinction rates observed in the fossil record.

Origin of the name

The name was proposed by Leigh Van Valen in 1973, taken from Lewis Carroll's Through the Looking-Glass, in which the Red Queen tells Alice: "it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place." Van Valen used it to describe his finding that the probability of extinction for a group of organisms is roughly constant over time.

Applications

The hypothesis is most famously applied to host-parasite coevolution: hosts evolve defences, parasites evolve counter-adaptations, and sexual reproduction generates the genetic variation that allows hosts to keep pace. It has also been invoked in discussions of predator-prey arms races, the maintenance of genetic diversity, and analogous competitive dynamics in economics and technology.
