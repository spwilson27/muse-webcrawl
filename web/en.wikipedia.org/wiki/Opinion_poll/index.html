Opinion poll

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An opinion poll, often simply referred to as a poll or a survey, is a research method used to collect public opinion data from a sample of a population. Typically designed to represent the opinions of a population by conducting a series of questions, opinion polls gauge public sentiment on a wide range of issues, including politics, social trends, and consumer behaviour.

Methodology

Pollsters select a sample intended to be representative of the target population, often using random sampling and weighting by demographic characteristics such as age, sex, education and region. Interviews may be conducted by telephone (live or automated), online, face-to-face or by mail. Results are reported with a margin of error reflecting sampling variability. Question wording, question order, non-response bias and the difficulty of identifying likely voters in election polls are persistent sources of error.

History

Straw polls conducted by newspapers appeared in the United States from the early nineteenth century. Scientific polling began in the 1930s with George Gallup, Archibald Crossley and Elmo Roper; Gallup's correct prediction of the 1936 US presidential election — in contrast to the Literary Digest poll's spectacular failure — established quota sampling and later probability methods as standard practice. Polls famously erred again in 1948 ("Dewey Defeats Truman"), and polling misses in recent elections have driven methodological change, including the shift from landline telephones to mixed-mode designs.

Uses and criticism

Opinion polls inform election forecasting, government policy, market research and academic study of public opinion. Critics argue that polls can influence the voters they measure (bandwagon and underdog effects), that media "horse race" coverage of polls crowds out policy reporting, and that published polls in some countries are subject to manipulation or legal restriction near election day; several countries ban publication of polls in the final days of a campaign.
