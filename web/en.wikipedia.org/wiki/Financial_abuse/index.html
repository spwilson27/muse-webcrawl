Financial abuse

Financial abuse (also called economic abuse) is a form of abuse in which one person controls another person's access to economic resources, diminishing the victim's capacity to support themselves and forcing dependence on the perpetrator. It is recognised as a pattern of coercive control in intimate relationships, and also occurs against older people and people with disabilities at the hands of family members, partners, or carers.

Forms

Financial abuse can include:

  • Controlling all household money and demanding detailed accounts of every purchase.
  • Preventing the victim from working, attending education, or keeping their own earnings.
  • Running up debts in the victim's name, or destroying their credit rating ("coerced debt").
  • Withholding money for essentials such as food, medicine, or children's needs.
  • Stealing income, savings, pensions, or benefits.
  • In elder abuse: misuse of a power of attorney, pressuring an older person to change a will, or skimming from their accounts.

Prevalence and effects

Research by domestic abuse organisations consistently finds that economic abuse accompanies the large majority of intimate partner abuse cases, and it is a leading reason victims feel unable to leave: without money, housing, or credit, escape is practically difficult and dangerous. Survivors may face long-term consequences including poverty, homelessness, damaged credit histories, and debt. Because financial abuse often leaves a paper trail rather than physical evidence, it historically attracted less legal attention than physical violence, though awareness has grown substantially.

Legal responses

Several jurisdictions have written economic abuse into law. England and Wales included it in the statutory definition of domestic abuse in the Domestic Abuse Act 2021, and the offence of coercive control (Serious Crime Act 2015) frequently encompasses financial control. Australia's family law framework recognises financial abuse as family violence, and many US states address it through domestic violence statutes, while financial exploitation of elders is separately criminalised in most states. Banks and financial institutions increasingly train staff to spot warning signs and to help customers secure accounts.

Support

Specialist charities advise survivors on separating finances safely, freezing or replacing cards and accounts, disputing coerced debts, and accessing benefits and emergency funds. Advocates caution that confronting a perpetrator about money can escalate risk, so safety planning is recommended before financial separation.
