Wastewater treatment

Secondary clarifiers separate treated water from biological solids.

Wastewater treatment is a process used to remove contaminants from wastewater and convert it into an effluent that can be returned to the water cycle. Once back in the water cycle, the effluent creates an acceptable impact on the environment and is also possible to be reused for various purposes (known as water reclamation). The treatment process takes place in a wastewater treatment plant, where pollutants are removed and broken down.

Stages of treatment

Preliminary treatment removes coarse material through bar screens and grit chambers, protecting downstream equipment. Primary treatment uses sedimentation tanks to settle out suspended solids and greases, typically removing roughly half the solids. Secondary treatment is biological: in the activated sludge process, air is blown into aeration basins where microbial flocs consume dissolved organic matter; trickling filters and rotating biological contactors grow microbes on fixed media instead. Clarifiers then separate the biomass. Tertiary treatment polishes the effluent — filtration, biological nutrient removal of nitrogen and phosphorus, and disinfection by chlorination, ultraviolet light or ozone — before discharge or reuse.

Sludge treatment

Solids removed from the wastewater, collectively called sludge or sewage sludge, are thickened, stabilized — often by anaerobic digestion, which produces methane-rich biogas — dewatered, and then disposed of or applied to land as biosolids, subject to regulation.

History and regulation

Modern treatment expanded rapidly in the late 19th and 20th centuries as cities built sewers and recognized the disease burden of untreated sewage. In the United States the Clean Water Act of 1972 required at least secondary treatment for municipal discharges; in the European Union the Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive of 1991 sets similar requirements. Increasing attention is now given to micropollutants such as pharmaceuticals and PFAS.
