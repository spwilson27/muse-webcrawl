Retrocession (reinsurance)

Retrocession is the practice by which a reinsurer passes on some or all of the risks it has assumed to another reinsurer, called a retrocessionaire. Just as reinsurance allows a primary insurer to cede part of its risk to a reinsurer, retrocession allows the reinsurer to further spread its own exposure, protecting its balance sheet against catastrophic losses.

Mechanics

A reinsurer that cedes risk is known as the retrocedent, and the party accepting the risk is the retrocessionaire. Retrocession contracts may be proportional (sharing premiums and losses in an agreed proportion) or non-proportional (such as excess-of-loss covers that respond when losses exceed a specified threshold). The global retrocession market has historically been centered in London (Lloyd's of London and the London market), Bermuda, and other reinsurance hubs.

Significance

Retrocession is an important mechanism for spreading peak risks, such as natural catastrophe exposure, across the global insurance market. However, chains of reinsurance and retrocession contributed to the "LMX spiral" in the London market in the late 1980s and early 1990s, in which the same risks were repeatedly reinsured within the market, concentrating rather than dispersing losses when major claims (such as the Piper Alpha disaster and Hurricane Andrew) occurred. Modern risk management and catastrophe modeling have sought to limit such accumulations.
