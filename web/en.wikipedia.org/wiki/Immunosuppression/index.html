Immunosuppression

Immunosuppression can be deliberate (therapeutic) or a consequence of disease

Immunosuppression is a reduction of the activation or efficacy of the immune system. Some portions of the immune system itself have immunosuppressive effects on other parts of the immune system, and immunosuppression may occur as an adverse reaction to treatment of other conditions. In general, deliberately induced immunosuppression is performed to prevent rejection of a transplanted organ or to treat autoimmune disease.

Deliberate immunosuppression

After an organ transplantation, the body will nearly always reject the new organ(s) unless immunosuppressive drugs are given, because the immune system recognises the graft as foreign tissue. Immunosuppressants such as corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors (ciclosporin, tacrolimus), antimetabolites (azathioprine, mycophenolate), and biologic agents are used in combination regimens. Immunosuppression is also used to treat autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, and Crohn's disease, and to prevent graft-versus-host disease after bone marrow transplantation.

Non-deliberate immunosuppression

Immunosuppression may result from diseases that attack the immune system, most notably HIV/AIDS, in which the virus destroys CD4+ T cells. Primary immunodeficiencies, malnutrition, certain cancers such as leukaemia and lymphoma, and treatments such as chemotherapy and radiation can also compromise immunity.

Complications

Because the immune system is weakened, immunosuppressed people are vulnerable to opportunistic infections by organisms that are usually harmless, as well as to the reactivation of latent infections such as tuberculosis and cytomegalovirus. Long-term immunosuppression also increases the risk of certain cancers, particularly skin cancers and lymphomas.
