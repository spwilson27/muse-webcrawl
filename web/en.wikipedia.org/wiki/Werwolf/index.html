Werwolf

Werwolf (pronounced [ˈveːɐ̯vɔlf], German for "werewolf") was a Nazi plan, developed in late 1944 and early 1945, to create a resistance force that would operate behind Allied lines as Germany was occupied, conducting guerrilla warfare, sabotage and assassinations against occupation forces and German collaborators.

Origins and organisation

The plan was directed by SS-Obergruppenführer Hans-Adolf Prützmann under Heinrich Himmler, inspired partly by Allied fears of a guerrilla campaign from an "Alpine redoubt" and by the example of Soviet partisans. Werwolf recruits, many of them teenage Hitler Youth members, were trained in sabotage, sniping and the use of cached weapons and explosives. Joseph Goebbels' propaganda ministry amplified the movement through "Radio Werwolf", which began broadcasting on 1 April 1945 and threatened continued resistance after defeat.

Activities and effects

Actual Werwolf operations were limited and fragmented. The most notorious act was the March 1945 assassination of Franz Oppenhoff, the Allied-appointed mayor of Aachen. Scattered attacks on Allied soldiers, supply depots and German officials accused of collaboration continued into the summer of 1945, and exaggerated fears of Werwolf activity influenced Allied occupation policy, prompting mass internments and harsh security measures. Historians generally conclude that Werwolf's real military impact was small compared with its propaganda value and the anxiety it generated.
