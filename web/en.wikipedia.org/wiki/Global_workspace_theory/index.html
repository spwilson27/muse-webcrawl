Global workspace theory

Global workspace theory (GWT) is a framework in cognitive science and neuroscience that models consciousness as information broadcast from a central "workspace" to many specialized, mostly unconscious processors. Proposed by cognitive psychologist Bernard Baars in his 1988 book A Cognitive Theory of Consciousness, it uses the metaphor of a theatre: consciousness is the brightly lit spot on the stage, attention is the spotlight, and a vast audience of unconscious processes receives whatever is shown.

The model

According to GWT, the brain contains many parallel, specialized networks that compete and cooperate outside awareness. When one coalition of processes wins access to the global workspace, its content is made globally available — to memory, language, planning, and action systems — and that global availability is what it means for the content to be conscious. Stanislas Dehaene and colleagues developed a neuronal version, the global neuronal workspace, proposing that long-range cortical connections, especially involving prefrontal and parietal cortex, implement the broadcast, and that conscious access corresponds to a late, sustained "ignition" of this network.

Evidence and criticism

The theory has been applied to phenomena such as inattentional blindness, masking, the attentional blink, and binocular rivalry, and it predicts the late event-related potential known as the P3b. Critics — including proponents of rival accounts such as integrated information theory and higher-order theories — debate whether global broadcast is truly constitutive of phenomenal experience or merely a mechanism of cognitive access, and large adversarial collaborations have recently tested GWT's predictions against those of its rivals. The theory also underlies proposals for machine consciousness in artificial intelligence.
