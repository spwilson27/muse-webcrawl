Employment Division v. Smith

Employment Division, Department of Human Resources of Oregon v. Smith, 494 U.S. 872 (1990), is a United States Supreme Court case that held that the state could deny unemployment benefits to a person fired for violating a state prohibition on the use of peyote, even though the use of the drug was part of a religious ritual. Although states have the power to accommodate otherwise illegal acts performed in pursuit of religious beliefs, they are not required to do so.

Background

Alfred Leo Smith and Galen Black were members of the Native American Church who were fired from their jobs as drug counsellors because they ingested peyote as part of a religious ceremony. The State of Oregon denied them unemployment benefits on the ground that they had been discharged for work-related misconduct. After litigation in the state courts, the case was heard twice by the U.S. Supreme Court before the final decision.

Decision

Justice Antonin Scalia delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in relevant part by five justices. The Court held that the Free Exercise Clause of the First Amendment permits a state to burden a religious practice through a law that is neutral and generally applicable: if prohibiting religious use of peyote is merely an incidental effect of a valid, general criminal law, the state need not justify the prohibition by a compelling state interest. The decision thus abandoned the balancing test of Sherbert v. Verner (1963) for neutral laws of general applicability, while preserving strict scrutiny for laws that target religion or in contexts such as unemployment claims involving individualised governmental assessment.

Reaction and aftermath

The decision was sharply criticised by religious organisations across the political spectrum. In direct response, Congress passed the Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 (RFRA), which restored the compelling-interest test by statute for federal law and purported to apply it to the states as well; the Court later struck down RFRA's application to the states in City of Boerne v. Flores (1997), though it remains applicable to the federal government. In 1994 Congress amended the American Indian Religious Freedom Act to protect the ceremonial use of peyote by members of the Native American Church.
