Appeal to novelty

The appeal to novelty (Latin: argumentum ad novitatem) is a logical fallacy in which it is prematurely claimed that a proposal is correct or superior merely because it is new and modern. It is the converse of the appeal to tradition, which holds that something is better because it is old.

Form and criticism

The fallacy has the form: "X is new; therefore X is better." Novelty alone is not evidence of merit — a new idea may be an improvement, but it may equally be untested, flawed, or a rebranding of something older. Marketing exploits the appeal to novelty with labels such as "new and improved," and technology discourse with hype cycles in which the newest release is assumed superior. Critics note, however, that the fallacy applies only when novelty is the sole or primary justification; citing new evidence or new capabilities is not fallacious.

Related concepts

Closely related ideas include neophilia, chronocentrism, and the bandwagon appeal. In fashion and consumer culture, planned obsolescence institutionalizes the assumption that newer is better.
