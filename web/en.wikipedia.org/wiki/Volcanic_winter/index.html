Volcanic winter

A volcanic winter is a reduction in global temperatures caused by volcanic ash and droplets of sulfuric acid obscuring the Sun and raising Earth's albedo after a large, particularly sulfur-rich explosive eruption. The cooling effects are primarily dependent upon the amount of sulfur-bearing gases injected into the stratosphere, where they form an aerosol veil that can persist for years.

Sulfur aerosols in the stratosphere reflect sunlight and cool the surface.

Historical examples

The eruption of Mount Tambora in April 1815 caused the "Year Without a Summer" (1816), with crop failures and food riots across the Northern Hemisphere. The 1883 eruption of Krakatoa lowered global temperatures for several years and produced spectacular sunsets worldwide. The Toba super-eruption about 74,000 years ago may have caused a far longer cooling episode and a genetic bottleneck in early human populations, though this hypothesis remains debated.

Mechanism

Sulfur dioxide converted to sulfate aerosols in the stratosphere — above the weather — is not washed out by rain and can circle the globe for one to three years. The 1991 Pinatubo eruption injected about 20 million tonnes of sulfur dioxide and cooled the planet by roughly 0.5 °C at its peak.
