History of the tuba

The history of the tuba covers the development of the lowest-pitched member of the modern brass family. Although instruments named tuba existed in Roman antiquity — straight trumpets used for signalling — the modern valved tuba dates only from the 1830s, making it the youngest of the standard orchestral brass instruments.

Antecedents

Before the tuba, orchestras and bands relied for a brass bass voice on the serpent, a winding wooden instrument with finger holes dating from around 1590, and its upright successors such as the ophicleide, a large keyed brass instrument invented by Jean Hilaire Asté (Halary) in 1817. Both had uneven tone and intonation, and composers and bandmasters sought a valved replacement after valves were patented in 1818 by Heinrich Stölzel and Friedrich Blühmel.

Invention

The first instrument recognisably a tuba was patented in Berlin on 12 September 1835 by the Prussian military bandmaster Wilhelm Friedrich Wieprecht and the instrument maker Johann Gottfried Moritz. Their "Bass-Tuba" in F had five valves and a broad conical bore. Moritz's manufacturing rights later passed to Carl Wilhelm Moritz. Adolphe Sax in Paris developed rival low brass, the saxhorn family, and the euphonium emerged as the tuba's tenor-voiced companion.

Adoption

Richard Wagner, after encountering valved brass in Paris, specified tubas for his operas and commissioned the Wagner tuba, a hybrid instrument, for the Ring cycle. Hector Berlioz revised the tuba part of his Symphonie fantastique, originally for ophicleide, and the tuba became standard in orchestras and in military and brass bands through the later 19th century. In the United States, John Philip Sousa promoted the sousaphone, a circular tuba with an upward bell developed in the 1890s for marching bands.

Modern forms

Modern tubas are built in F, E♭, C and B♭, with rotary or piston valves, typically four to six in number. They anchor the brass section in orchestral works by composers such as Bruckner, Mahler, Prokofiev and Ralph Vaughan Williams, whose Tuba Concerto (1954) was the first concerto for the instrument by a major composer. Prominent soloists include Arnold Jacobs, Harvey Phillips and Øystein Baadsvik.
