Shingeki

Shingeki (新劇, literally "new drama") is a genre in Japanese theatre that developed in the early 20th century as a reaction against the traditional forms of kabuki and nō. Influenced by modern realist Western theatre — particularly the works of Henrik Ibsen, Anton Chekhov, and George Bernard Shaw — shingeki sought to bring contemporary, realistic drama to the Japanese stage, with naturalistic acting, translated and original modern plays, and productions mounted in Western-style proscenium theatres.

History

The movement is generally traced to the founding of the Bungei Kyōkai (Literary Arts Society) in 1906 by Tsubouchi Shōyō, and to the Jiyū Gekijō (Free Theatre) founded in 1909 by Osanai Kaoru and Ichikawa Sadanji II, modeled on European free theatres. In 1924 Osanai and Hijikata Yoshi founded the Tsukiji Little Theatre (Tsukiji Shōgekijō) in Tokyo, which became the spiritual home of shingeki, staging translations of European plays and works by new Japanese playwrights in a purpose-built modern playhouse.

After the disruption of the Second World War, during which shingeki troupes faced government suppression for their leftist associations, the movement flourished in the postwar period. Major companies such as the Bungakuza (founded 1937), the Mingei Theatre (1950), and the Haiyūza (1944) became the leading shingeki troupes, training generations of actors who also staffed Japan's film and television industries.

Characteristics and legacy

Shingeki productions emphasized realistic sets, psychologically motivated acting in the Stanislavski tradition, and texts by modern playwrights, in deliberate contrast to the stylized performance conventions of kabuki. From the 1960s onward, younger artists reacting against shingeki's realism created the angura (underground) or "little theatre" movement (shōgekijō undō), led by figures such as Terayama Shūji, Kara Jūrō, and Suzuki Tadashi, though shingeki companies remain an established part of Japanese theatrical life.
