Human–computer interaction

HCI studies how people design, evaluate and use interactive computing systems

Human–computer interaction (HCI) is a multidisciplinary field of study focusing on the design of computer technology and, in particular, the interaction between humans (the users) and computers. While initially concerned with computers, HCI has since expanded to cover almost all forms of information technology design, including graphical user interfaces, websites, mobile devices, voice interfaces and ubiquitous computing.

History

HCI emerged as a distinct field in the early 1980s, drawing on cognitive psychology, computer science, design and human factors (ergonomics). Early landmarks include Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad (1963), Douglas Engelbart's 1968 demonstration of the mouse, hypertext and collaborative editing, and the commercialisation of the graphical user interface at Xerox PARC and in the Apple Macintosh (1984). Stuart Card, Allen Newell and Thomas Moran's The Psychology of Human–Computer Interaction (1983) helped define the field, and the ACM Special Interest Group on Computer–Human Interaction (SIGCHI) became its main professional community.

Principles and methods

Central concerns of HCI include usability, accessibility, user experience (UX) and interaction design. Researchers and practitioners use methods such as user-centred design, prototyping, usability testing, heuristic evaluation (for example Jakob Nielsen's ten heuristics), cognitive walkthroughs and field studies including ethnography. Influential models include Fitts's law for pointing, the Keystroke-Level Model, Norman's action cycle and the concept of affordances popularised by Donald Norman in The Design of Everyday Things.

Areas of study

Modern HCI covers topics such as information visualisation, computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW), social computing, tangible and embodied interaction, virtual and augmented reality, natural language and voice interfaces, recommender systems, and human–AI interaction. Accessibility research ensures systems can be used by people with diverse abilities, guided by standards such as the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG).
