Xanthos (Lycian: 𐊀𐊕𐊑𐊏𐊀 Arñna; Greek: Ξάνθος) was an ancient city in Lycia, in what is now south-western Turkey, near the modern village of Kınık. It was the capital and largest city of the Lycian League and is celebrated for its dramatic history of collective resistance and for its distinctive funerary monuments, including the Harpy Tomb and the Nereid Monument. Together with the nearby sanctuary of Letoon, it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1988.

History

Xanthos is mentioned by Homer as the source of Lycian allies of Troy. Herodotus recounts that around 540 BC, besieged by the Persian general Harpagus, the Xanthians burned their acropolis with their families and possessions inside and sallied out to die fighting, rather than submit. The city was later repopulated. Alexander the Great took Xanthos in 334 BC; Strabo reports a similar mass suicide during the civil wars after Caesar's assassination, when Brutus besieged the city in 42 BC. Under Rome the city prospered, acquiring a theatre, agora and other monumental buildings, and it continued into the Byzantine period before being abandoned under pressure from Arab raids.

Monuments

The Xanthians buried their dynasts in tall pillar tombs and house-shaped sarcophagi carved in local limestone. The most famous monuments were removed in the 1840s by the British explorer Charles Fellows and shipped to the British Museum, where the Nereid Monument — a 5th-century BC tomb temple decorated with sculptures of the Nereids — has been reconstructed. The Inscribed Pillar of Xanthos bears one of the longest texts in the Lycian language, an epitaph of a local dynast.

Archaeology

Excavations by French missions since the 1950s have uncovered the theatre, Roman and Byzantine buildings, mosaics and fortifications. The site lies on a steep hill above the Xanthos river (modern Eşen Çayı).
