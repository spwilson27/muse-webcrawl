Walter Eucken (17 January 1891 – 20 March 1950) was a German economist of the Freiburg school and father of ordoliberalism. His name is closely linked to the development of the "social market economy" (soziale Marktwirtschaft) that shaped post-war West German economic policy.

Life and career

Eucken was born in Jena, the son of the philosopher Rudolf Eucken, winner of the 1908 Nobel Prize in Literature. He studied economics at Kiel, Bonn and Jena, served as an officer in the First World War, and after holding a chair at Tübingen moved in 1927 to the University of Freiburg, where he remained for the rest of his life. During the Nazi period he was associated with resistance circles around the Freiburg School, including figures connected to the Bonhoeffer resistance; after the July 1944 plot against Hitler, his close associates were arrested, and Eucken himself was interrogated.

Eucken died suddenly of a heart attack in London in March 1950 while delivering a series of lectures at the London School of Economics, published posthumously as This Unsuccessful Age.

Ideas

His major work, Die Grundlagen der Nationalökonomie (Foundations of Economic Theory, 1940), developed a morphological method for analysing economic systems and the concept of economic order (Ordnung). Eucken argued that a free economy requires a strong state to set and enforce a competitive order — rules against monopolies and cartels, stable money, open markets, private property, freedom of contract, liability and constancy of economic policy — while refraining from discretionary intervention in economic processes. Together with Franz Böhm and Hans Großmann-Doerth he edited the yearbook ORDO (founded 1948), which gave ordoliberalism its name. His ideas strongly influenced Ludwig Erhard, the first economics minister of the Federal Republic of Germany.
