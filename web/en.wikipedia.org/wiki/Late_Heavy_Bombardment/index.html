The Late Heavy Bombardment (LHB), also known as the lunar cataclysm, is a hypothesised event in the early history of the Solar System, approximately 4.1 to 3.8 billion years ago (during the Hadean and early Archean eons of Earth), in which a large number of asteroids and comets collided with the terrestrial planets, including Earth and the Moon.

Evidence

The hypothesis rests mainly on radiometric dates of impact-melt samples returned by the Apollo missions, which cluster around 3.9 billion years, suggesting a spike in the impact rate long after the planets formed. The Moon's great impact basins — Imbrium, Serenitatis, Crisium, and Nectaris — date to this interval.

Cause

The leading explanation is the Nice model, in which the giant planets migrated outward, with Jupiter and Saturn crossing a mutual resonance that destabilised the outer Solar System and scattered planetesimals inward. Computer simulations show this process could have produced a sharp bombardment spike several hundred million years after planet formation.

Debate

Some researchers argue the apparent clustering of dates is a sampling artefact of material collected near the Imbrium basin, and that the impact flux instead declined monotonically. The debate remains active, informed by lunar samples, crater counts on Mars and Mercury, and the study of ancient terrestrial zircons.

Effects on Earth

The bombardment would have repeatedly melted parts of Earth's crust and vaporised oceans, with implications for the timing of the origin of life; the oldest evidence of life appears soon after the bombardment waned, around 3.8 billion years ago.
