Genetically modified crops

Genetically modified crops (GM crops) are plants used in agriculture whose DNA has been modified using genetic engineering methods. In most cases the aim is to introduce a new trait to the plant which does not occur naturally in the species. Examples include resistance to certain pests, diseases, or environmental conditions, reduction of spoilage, resistance to herbicides, or improving the nutrient profile of the crop.

History

The first genetically modified crop plant was tobacco, reported in 1983. The Flavr Savr tomato became the first commercially grown genetically engineered food approved for human consumption in 1994. From the mid-1990s, herbicide-tolerant soybean, insect-resistant maize and Bt cotton expanded rapidly, particularly in the United States, Brazil, Argentina, Canada and India. By 2019, GM crops were grown on approximately 190 million hectares in 29 countries.

Methods

Genetic modifications are introduced using techniques such as Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, biolistics (gene guns), electroporation, and, more recently, genome editing tools such as CRISPR-Cas9, which may produce gene-edited crops sometimes regulated separately from transgenic crops.

Traits

The most widely deployed traits are herbicide tolerance (notably to glyphosate) and insect resistance through expression of Bt toxins from Bacillus thuringiensis. Other traits include virus resistance (as in Hawaiian papaya), drought tolerance, and biofortification, such as Golden Rice engineered to produce vitamin A precursor beta-carotene.

Debate

There is a broad scientific consensus that currently marketed GM foods pose no greater risk to human health than conventional foods, but GM crops remain the subject of public controversy concerning environmental effects, corporate control of seed supply, labelling, and regulation, which differs sharply between jurisdictions such as the United States and the European Union.
