Biogerontology

Biogerontology is the sub-field of gerontology concerned with the biological aging process, its evolutionary origins, and potential means of intervention in the process. The term "biogerontology" was coined by S. Robert Arking and is sometimes used interchangeably with the terms experimental gerontology and biomedical gerontology. Biogerontology involves research into the causes, effects, and mechanisms of biological aging, with the goal of understanding — and possibly slowing, halting, or reversing — the processes that make organisms more vulnerable to disease and death over time.

History

Scientific study of aging dates to the late 19th and early 20th centuries, with figures such as Élie Metchnikoff, who coined the term "gerontology" in 1903. Leonard Hayflick's discovery in 1961 that normal human cells divide only a limited number of times (the Hayflick limit) provided one of the first cellular mechanisms of aging, later linked to telomere shortening. The identification of long-lived mutants in model organisms such as the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans in the 1980s and 1990s — including the age-1 and daf-2 mutations studied by Cynthia Kenyon and others — demonstrated that aging rates could be modulated by single genes.

Theories of aging

Biogerontologists have proposed many theories to explain aging, generally divided into damage-based and program-based theories. Damage-based theories include the free radical theory of aging (proposed by Denham Harman in 1956), the DNA damage theory, and theories involving accumulated cross-linking and somatic mutation. Program-based theories include programmed longevity, endocrine theory, and immunological theory. Evolutionary theories — such as the disposable soma theory of Thomas Kirkwood and the antagonistic pleiotropy theory of George C. Williams — explain why aging evolves despite its apparent disadvantages.

In 2013, López-Otín and colleagues published "The Hallmarks of Aging," identifying nine cellular and molecular hallmarks: genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis, deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, and altered intercellular communication. An updated 2023 paper expanded the list to twelve hallmarks.

Interventions

Research interventions that extend lifespan in model organisms include caloric restriction, genetic manipulation of nutrient-sensing pathways (such as insulin/IGF-1 and mTOR signalling), and pharmacological agents such as rapamycin and metformin. More recent approaches include senolytic drugs that clear senescent cells, partial cellular reprogramming using Yamanaka factors, and plasma dilution or young blood plasma experiments.

Biogerontologists

Notable researchers in the field include Leonard Hayflick, Cynthia Kenyon, Aubrey de Grey, David Sinclair, Judith Campisi, and Nir Barzilai. Professional organisations include the American Aging Association and the International Association of Gerontology and Geriatrics.
