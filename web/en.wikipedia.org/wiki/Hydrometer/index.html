A hydrometer is an instrument used to measure the relative density (specific gravity) of liquids — the ratio of the density of the liquid to the density of water. It is usually made of sealed glass, with a weighted bulb at the bottom (weighted with lead shot or mercury in older instruments) and a graduated stem at the top.

Principle

The hydrometer works on Archimedes' principle: a floating body displaces a weight of liquid equal to its own weight. In a denser liquid the instrument floats higher; in a less dense liquid it sinks deeper. The density is read directly from the scale on the stem where it crosses the liquid surface. Readings are temperature-dependent, so hydrometers are calibrated for a reference temperature, commonly 15.5 °C or 20 °C, and correction tables are used otherwise.

Specialised hydrometers

Many variants are named for their application: an alcoholmeter measures ethanol content; a saccharometer measures dissolved sugar (used by brewers and winemakers to follow fermentation and estimate alcohol by volume); a lactometer tests milk; a urinometer measures urine specific gravity in medicine; and battery hydrometers check the state of charge of lead–acid batteries via the density of the sulfuric acid electrolyte.

Scales

Various empirical scales are used instead of direct density, including the Baumé, Brix, Plato, Oechsle and API gravity scales. In soil mechanics, a hydrometer analysis determines the particle-size distribution of fine-grained soils by timing the settling of a suspension.

History

An early description of a hydrometer appears in a letter of Synesius of Cyrene (c. 402 AD) asking Hypatia of Alexandria to construct one; in modern times the instrument was refined by scientists such as Robert Boyle, who described a version in the 1670s.
