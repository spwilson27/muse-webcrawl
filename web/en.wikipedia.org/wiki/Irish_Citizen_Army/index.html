The Irish Citizen Army (ICA; Irish: Arm Cathartha na hÉireann) was a small paramilitary group of trained trade union volunteers from the Irish Transport and General Workers' Union (ITGWU), established in Dublin in 1913 to protect workers' demonstrations during the Dublin Lock-out. Led by James Connolly, it took part alongside the Irish Volunteers in the Easter Rising of 1916, and is regarded as the first workers' army of the modern era — its motto was that "the ownership of Ireland, moral and material, is vested of right in the people of Ireland."

Origins: the 1913 Lock-out

During the Lock-out of 1913, Dublin Metropolitan Police repeatedly baton-charged strikers' meetings, killing two workers on Bloody Sunday, 31 August 1913. In November 1913 the socialist leader James Larkin proposed forming a workers' defence force; Captain Jack White, a former British Army officer, organised and drilled the new body, which mustered several hundred members drawn largely from the ITGWU. Connolly and White intended the Citizen Army to defend pickets and meetings, and its members wore a uniform with a slouch hat and a badge of the Red Hand of Ulster — chosen to signify that it stood for all Irish workers.

Connolly takes command

When Larkin left for America in 1914, James Connolly took command of the ICA and of the ITGWU. Connolly reorganised the Army, issued it with a constitution committing it to establishing an Irish socialist republic, and steered it towards alliance with the separatist Irish Volunteers and the secret Irish Republican Brotherhood, though he resisted subordination: in January 1916 he reached an agreement with the IRB Military Council to rise jointly at Easter.

Easter Rising

About 220 Citizen Army members — including some 30 women of the women's section, among them Constance Markievicz — mobilised on Easter Monday, 24 April 1916. Connolly, appointed Commandant-General of the Dublin forces, directed the Rising from the General Post Office. A Citizen Army detachment under Michael Mallin and Markievicz occupied St Stephen's Green and then the Royal College of Surgeons. Another party briefly seized Dublin Castle's approaches. Connolly was badly wounded, and after the surrender he was executed by firing squad while strapped to a chair, on 12 May 1916; Mallin was also executed. Eleven Citizen Army members were killed in the fighting.

Later years

The ICA was reorganised after 1916 but played only a marginal role in the War of Independence, serving mostly as a guard of honour and in ceremonial functions. It took a neutral-to-anti-Treaty stance in the Civil War without active participation. In the 1930s it was briefly revived by left-wing republicans associated with the Republican Congress, holding a final parade in 1934; veterans paraded for the last time at commemorations in 1947. Its flag — the Starry Plough, introduced in 1914 — remains an emblem of Irish labour and republican socialism.
