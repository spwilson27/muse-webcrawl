Pecora Commission

The Pecora Commission was an inquiry begun on March 4, 1932, by the United States Senate Committee on Banking and Currency into the causes of the Wall Street Crash of 1929. It is named after Ferdinand Pecora, its chief counsel from January 1933, whose aggressive questioning of Wall Street bankers made the hearings a national sensation and built political support for the financial reforms of the New Deal.

Ferdinand Pecora conducting the Senate investigation, 1933.

Background

The Senate authorised the investigation in 1932, initially under chief counsel Claude Branch and then John Marrinan, with limited effect. In January 1933 the committee's incoming chairman, Duncan Fletcher, hired Ferdinand Pecora — a former New York assistant district attorney — as chief counsel. Pecora transformed the inquiry, subpoenaing bank records and putting the most powerful financiers in America on the stand.

The hearings

Pecora's examination of Charles E. Mitchell, head of National City Bank, revealed that Mitchell had sold stock short to his own wife to manufacture a tax loss and had received more than a million dollars in "loans" from the bank that were quietly forgiven; Mitchell resigned within days. The hearings then exposed J. P. Morgan Jr. and his partners' failure to pay income tax in some years, and the "preferred lists" by which Morgan distributed lucrative stock allocations to favoured clients including prominent politicians. Further testimony uncovered stock manipulation pools, conflicts of interest between banks' commercial and securities operations, and the financing of dubious foreign bonds sold to small investors.

Legacy

The revelations directly shaped landmark legislation: the Banking Act of 1933 (Glass–Steagall), which separated commercial from investment banking; the Securities Act of 1933; and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, which created the Securities and Exchange Commission. Pecora himself became a commissioner of the new SEC under its first chairman, Joseph P. Kennedy. The commission's name became a byword for congressional investigation of financial wrongdoing, invoked again after the 2008 financial crisis by the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission.
