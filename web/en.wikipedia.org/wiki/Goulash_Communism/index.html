Goulash Communism (Hungarian: gulyáskommunizmus), also known as Kádárism, was the variety of socialism implemented in the Hungarian People's Republic under János Kádár from the early 1960s until the end of communist rule in 1989. It was characterised by a gradual softening of political repression after the crushed Hungarian Revolution of 1956 and by market-oriented economic reforms intended to raise living standards — a tacit bargain in which citizens accepted one-party rule in exchange for consumer goods, relative tolerance and "the happiest barrack in the socialist camp."

Origins

Kádár, installed by the Soviet Union after 1956, initially ruled through reprisals, but by the early 1960s he had reversed the Rákosi-era slogan to "whoever is not against us is with us," declared amnesties, and loosened cultural and travel restrictions. Hungary became markedly more open than most Eastern Bloc states: citizens could travel to the West more freely, small private businesses were tolerated, and censorship, while real, was comparatively flexible under the "three T's" system of supported, tolerated and forbidden works (támogatott, tűrt, tiltott).

Economy

The centrepiece was the New Economic Mechanism of 1968, which introduced elements of market coordination, enterprise autonomy and price flexibility into the planned economy. The result was comparatively well-stocked shops and the highest standard of living in the Bloc — financed increasingly by Western loans. By the 1980s Hungary's hard-currency debt and slowing growth exposed the model's limits, but its reform experience made Hungary's transition after 1989 comparatively smooth.
