The yehu (Chinese: 椰胡; pinyin: yēhú; literally "coconut huqin") is a Chinese bowed string instrument in the huqin family. It is named after its resonator, which is made from a halved coconut shell. The yehu is used principally in Cantonese music and Cantonese opera, and in the music of Chaozhou (Teochew). A related instrument with a coconut body, the gambus椰胡-derived forms, also appears in Malaysian and Indonesian music.

Construction

The soundbox of the yehu is formed from half a coconut shell, over whose open face a thin soundboard — traditionally of paulownia wood, plywood, or occasionally snakeskin — is glued. A long, fretless hardwood neck rises from the shell. The instrument usually has two strings, originally of silk and now commonly of steel or nylon-wrapped wire, tuned in fourths or fifths, and two tuning pegs. It is played with a loose-haired bow whose hair passes between the strings, held vertically with the coconut body resting against the player's leg or held in the lap.

History and use

Instruments of this type spread with Chinese maritime trade across Southeast Asia. In Guangdong, the yehu became a mainstay of Cantonese ensembles in the early 20th century, often taking a high melodic role alongside the gaohu. Because the coconut resonator gives a mellow, nasal timbre distinct from the brighter erhu, the yehu was favoured for accompanying singing. In Chaozhou music it serves as one of the leading string instruments. Variants survive in Malaysia and Indonesia, where the coconut-bodied bowed lute is used in gambus and ghazal ensembles.
