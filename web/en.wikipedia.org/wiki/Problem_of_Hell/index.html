Problem of Hell

The problem of Hell is an ethical problem in the philosophy of religion, related to but distinct from the problem of evil. It asks how the existence of Hell — conventionally understood as a place of eternal conscious torment for the damned — can be reconciled with the belief in a God who is omnipotent, omniscient and perfectly good (all-loving). Critics argue that eternal punishment for finite sins committed in a finite life is disproportionate, and that a loving God would not create or sustain a realm of unending suffering.

Formulation

The problem is usually directed at the traditional doctrine held by many Christians (and, in other forms, in Islam) that the wicked are punished eternally. Its strands include:

  • Proportionality: no finite offence can merit infinite retribution.
  • Divine love: an all-loving being would seek the salvation of every person, and omnipotence guarantees eventual success — yet Hell implies permanent failure.
  • Freedom: the choices made in a short earthly life, under conditions of ignorance and unequal circumstance, seem an inadequate basis for irreversible eternal destiny.

Responses

Free will defence

Defenders such as C. S. Lewis argue that Hell is not imposed but chosen: the damned freely and persistently reject God, and God respects that freedom. In Lewis's image, "the doors of hell are locked on the inside."

Annihilationism

Annihilationists (conditional immortalists) hold that the unsaved are not tormented forever but cease to exist after judgement; eternal life is a gift given only to the saved. This view has been defended within evangelical theology by figures such as John Stott.

Universalism

Christian universalists argue that ultimately all persons will be reconciled to God, with Hell — if it exists — being purgative and temporary rather than eternal, a view associated with Origen and Gregory of Nyssa and defended in modern times by philosophers such as Thomas Talbott and theologian David Bentley Hart.

Other responses

Others reply that sin against an infinite God carries infinite demerit (Anselm), that the damned continue sinning eternally and so continue to be punished justly, or that talk of Hell should be read metaphorically rather than as literal everlasting torture.
