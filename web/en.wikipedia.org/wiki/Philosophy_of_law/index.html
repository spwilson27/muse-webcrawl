Philosophy of law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The philosophy of law or legal philosophy (often called jurisprudence in the broader sense) is the branch of philosophy concerned with the nature of law: what makes norms legal, how law relates to morality and power, and how legal reasoning works. Its perennial debate opposes natural-law theory (law's validity depends partly on moral content — Aquinas, Finnis) to legal positivism (validity rests on social sources — Bentham, Austin, Hans Kelsen, H. L. A. Hart), with Legal realism denying that either rules or morality explain outcomes as well as officials' behaviour does.

Modern sub-fields include the theory of adjudication (Ronald Dworkin's interpretivism), the philosophy of criminal law (retribution, deterrence, responsibility and excuses), of private law (corrective justice), of international law, and applied normative questions (obligation to obey the law, civil disobedience, punishment of the Nazi-era informers that framed the Hart–Fuller debate). Analytic jurisprudence since Hart professionalised the field around conceptual analysis, while interdisciplinary movements (law and economics, critical legal studies, feminist and critical race jurisprudence) challenged its autonomy — a plurality that itself mirrors law's position between philosophy, politics and practice.
