Density gradient

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A density gradient is a spatial variation in density over an area. The term is used in the natural sciences to describe varying density of matter, but can also be used in other fields. In laboratory practice, a density gradient is most commonly a column of liquid whose density increases steadily from top to bottom, used to separate particles, cells or molecules by centrifugation.

Density gradient centrifugation

In density gradient centrifugation, a sample is layered on top of a gradient medium — historically caesium chloride or sucrose solutions, later materials such as Percoll and iodixanol — and spun in an ultracentrifuge. Particles migrate until they reach the point where their own density equals that of the surrounding medium (isopycnic separation), or are separated by their sedimentation rate (rate-zonal separation).

The technique was famously used in the 1958 Meselson–Stahl experiment, which demonstrated semi-conservative replication of DNA: bacteria grown in heavy nitrogen (15N) were shifted to light nitrogen (14N), and the DNA formed bands in a caesium chloride gradient at positions confirming the mechanism.

Applications

  • Separating organelles, viruses and cells in biology
  • Purifying nucleic acids and plasmids
  • Isolating peripheral blood mononuclear cells (e.g. Ficoll gradients)
  • Measuring densities of polymers and minerals

In nature

Density gradients occur naturally in the oceans (pycnoclines), the atmosphere, and in stellar interiors, where they govern convection, stratification and mixing.
