Cuneiform

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A clay tablet inscribed with cuneiform signs beside a reed stylus]

Cuneiform is a logo-syllabic writing system invented by the Sumerians of Mesopotamia in the late 4th millennium BC — one of the earliest writing systems in the world — in which signs were impressed into wet clay with a cut reed stylus, producing the characteristic wedge-shaped (Latin cuneus, wedge) marks. Over three thousand years it was adapted for Akkadian, Babylonian, Assyrian, Hittite, Elamite, Urartian and Old Persian, recording everything from beer rations and contracts to the Epic of Gilgamesh and royal annals, before falling out of use by the 1st century AD. Its decipherment in the 19th century opened the documentary world of the ancient Near East.

Origins and development

Cuneiform grew from accounting tokens and pictographic tablets of Uruk (c. 3400–3300 BC) into a system of several hundred signs mixing word-signs and phonetic syllables, written in Sumerian's agglutinative grammar. Akkadian scribes adapted it to their Semitic language; scribal schools (edubba) trained pupils on sign lists, proverbs and mathematics. Millions of tablets survive — clay being nearly indestructible, especially when baked in the fires that destroyed the cities — in archives from Mari, Nineveh (Ashurbanipal's library) and Babylon.

Decipherment

Old Persian cuneiform, the simplest form, was cracked first (Grotefend, Rawlinson at Behistun in the 1830s–40s) and used as the key to Akkadian; in 1857 four scholars independently produced matching translations of the same text, confirming the decipherment. Sumerian, a language unrelated to any other, was recovered last. Reading the tablets revolutionised knowledge of Mesopotamian law, literature, astronomy and daily life — and revealed the antiquity of stories later echoed in the Bible, including a flood narrative strikingly parallel to Noah's.
