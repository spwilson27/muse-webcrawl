Precision-guided munition

A precision-guided munition (PGM), also called a smart weapon or smart munition, is a guided weapon intended to hit a specific target with a high probability, minimising collateral damage and the number of launches required. PGMs include laser-guided bombs, GPS-guided bombs such as the JDAM, imaging-infrared and radar-guided missiles, and guided artillery shells such as Excalibur.

Guidance methods

  • Laser guidance: the weapon homes on laser light reflected from a target designated by an aircraft or ground observer.
  • Satellite guidance: GPS/INS kits steer bombs to programmed coordinates, effective in all weather.
  • Electro-optical and imaging infrared: seekers recognise and lock onto the target's image.
  • Radar guidance: active or semi-active radar homing, used by anti-ship and air-to-surface missiles.

History

Early guided weapons appeared in the Second World War, such as the German Fritz X and American Azon bombs. Laser-guided bombs were used in the Vietnam War, notably destroying the Thanh Hóa Bridge in 1972 after years of unsuccessful conventional attacks. Precision weapons rose to prominence in the 1991 Gulf War and accounted for a growing share of munitions in subsequent conflicts, making possible much smaller circular error probable (CEP) — often only a few metres.

PGMs reduce the sorties and munitions needed per target and can lower unintended damage, but they are more expensive than unguided weapons and depend on targeting intelligence, designation and satellite signals that can be jammed.
