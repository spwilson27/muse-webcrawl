Rashomon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The bandit, the wife and the samurai in the sun-dappled grove: the same encounter replayed in incompatible versions]

Rashomon (Japanese: 羅生門) is a 1950 Japanese period drama film directed by Akira Kurosawa, adapted from Ryūnosuke Akutagawa's stories 'In a Grove' and 'Rashōmon'. A samurai is murdered and his wife assaulted in a forest; at the ruined Rashomon gate in Kyoto, during a rainstorm, a woodcutter and a priest retell the trial — in which the bandit Tajōmaru (Toshiro Mifune), the wife, the dead samurai (through a medium) and finally the woodcutter give four contradictory accounts, each self-serving, none reconcilable.

The film won the Golden Lion at Venice in 1951 and an honorary Academy Award, single-handedly opening Japanese cinema to the West — Kurosawa was unknown abroad before it. The 'Rashomon effect' entered world languages for the irreconcilability of honest-seeming testimony.

Technique

Kazuo Miyagawa's cinematography — shooting directly into the sun through forest canopy, dappled light as moral weather — was revolutionary. Kurosawa structured the flashbacks as lies told beautifully, each lit and staged as its teller's truth. The framing device of the gate (built full-size) and the rain, and the final act of compassion — the woodcutter adopting an abandoned baby — give despair a single, fragile answer.

Reception history

Japanese critics initially found it baffling and 'too Western'; Venice reversed the verdict in a year. It is regularly cited as the most influential film about truth ever made, and its name now serves courts, journals and psychology as shorthand. Remakes and homages range from The Outrage (1964) to episodes of world television without number.
