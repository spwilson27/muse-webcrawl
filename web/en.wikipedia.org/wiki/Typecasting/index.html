Typecasting

In film, television, and theatre, typecasting is the process by which a particular actor becomes strongly identified with a specific character, one or more particular roles, or characters having the same traits or coming from the same social or ethnic groups. Actors are said to be typecast when casting directors and producers repeatedly assign them similar parts, often because of a breakout role that defined their public image.

Effects on actors

Typecasting can be a double-edged sword. It provides steady employment and a recognizable brand, and some performers — such as character actors — build entire careers on it. However, many actors regard it as limiting or frustrating, fearing they will be passed over for more varied or prestigious roles. Some performers deliberately seek out roles radically different from their established type to escape it, while others embrace and parody their type.

Examples

Sean Connery famously struggled to escape identification with James Bond; Leonard Nimoy titled his first autobiography I Am Not Spock in response to being identified with the Star Trek character (his second was I Am Spock). Bela Lugosi became so associated with Dracula that he was buried in the character's cape. Conversely, actors such as John Wayne built durable stardom by consistently playing the same type of role.

Related concepts

Typecasting is related to stereotyping in casting, in which performers are selected because their appearance matches cultural expectations of a role. The term derives from the older theatrical practice of casting companies in which each actor was hired for a fixed "line of business" or stock character type.
