Bond (finance)

[Image: a vintage engraved government bond certificate with ornate border and coupons]

A bond is a fixed-income security: a loan made by an investor to a borrower — typically a government or a Corporation — in return for periodic interest payments (coupons) and repayment of the principal (face value) at a set maturity date. Bonds are issued in the primary market and traded over-the-counter in the secondary market; the global bond market, at well over one hundred trillion US dollars outstanding, is larger than the global stock market.

Yields and prices

A bond's yield relates its coupon and price to maturity. Because coupons are fixed, bond prices move inversely to market interest rates: when rates rise, existing bonds fall in price so that their yield matches new issues. Longer maturities and lower coupons imply greater price sensitivity (duration). The pattern of yields across maturities — the yield curve — is watched as a signal about growth and recession expectations.

Types and risk

Major categories include sovereign bonds (US Treasuries, UK gilts, German bunds, Japanese government bonds), municipal bonds, corporate bonds and mortgage-backed securities. Credit-rating agencies (Moody's, S&P, Fitch) grade issuers from AAA downwards; bonds below investment grade are classed as high-yield or "junk". Bond risk centres on credit/default risk, interest-rate risk and inflation risk. Central-bank purchases of bonds (quantitative easing) were a major policy tool after 2008, and government bond markets anchor the pricing of credit throughout the financial system.
