English is a West Germanic Indo-European language that emerged in early medieval England and has become the world's foremost lingua franca — the most spoken language overall when native and non-native speakers are combined, with well over a billion speakers, and the dominant language of international business, science, aviation, diplomacy and the Internet.

English evolved from the dialects of Germanic settlers (Angles, Saxons and Jutes) in post-Roman Britain, was transformed by Old Norse and especially Norman French influence, and spread worldwide through the British Empire and the later global influence of the United States.

History

Old English (c. 450–1100), the language of Beowulf, was heavily inflected and largely unintelligible to modern speakers. The Norman Conquest (1066) flooded English with French vocabulary and simplified its grammar, producing Middle English (Chaucer's language). Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700) — of Shakespeare and the King James Bible — saw the Great Vowel Shift and the standardising effects of print.

From the seventeenth century, colonisation carried English to North America, the Caribbean, Africa, South Asia, Australia and the Pacific, generating distinct varieties (American, Indian, Nigerian, Australian English and many more) and creoles.

English today

English has an exceptionally large vocabulary, drawn from Germanic roots, French, Latin and Greek, and hundreds of other languages; its spelling is famously irregular because it fossilised while pronunciation changed. It is the most commonly learned second language in the world and the required language of international aviation and maritime communication.

Linguists debate 'World Englishes' — whether English now belongs to its global users rather than its native speakers, who are a minority of all speakers — and how its dominance affects other languages, thousands of which are endangered.
