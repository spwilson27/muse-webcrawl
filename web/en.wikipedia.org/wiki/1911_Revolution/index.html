The 1911 Revolution, also known as the Xinhai Revolution (辛亥革命), was a revolution that overthrew China's last imperial dynasty, the Qing dynasty (1644–1912), and established the Republic of China. The revolution began with the Wuchang Uprising on October 10, 1911, and ended with the abdication of the Xuantong Emperor (Puyi) on February 12, 1912.

Background

By the early twentieth century the Qing dynasty was weakened by foreign defeats (the First Sino-Japanese War, the Boxer Rebellion), reform failures, and growing anti-Manchu nationalism. Revolutionary societies such as Sun Yat-sen's Tongmenghui agitated for a republic. A dispute over the nationalization of railways in Sichuan in 1911 — the Railway Protection Movement — diverted Qing troops and created the opening for revolutionaries in Hubei.

Wuchang Uprising

On October 10, 1911, soldiers of the New Army in Wuchang mutinied after a bomb accidentally exploded in the revolutionaries' headquarters in the Russian concession of Hankou, exposing their plans. Within weeks, province after province declared independence from Qing rule. In December, delegates of the independent provinces met in Nanjing and elected Sun Yat-sen provisional president; the Republic of China was proclaimed on January 1, 1912.

Abdication and aftermath

Sun negotiated with the Qing commander Yuan Shikai: in exchange for persuading the court to abdicate, Sun resigned the presidency in Yuan's favour. The Xuantong Emperor abdicated on February 12, 1912, ending over two thousand years of imperial rule in China. Yuan's subsequent authoritarianism and the fragmentation of the republic led to the Warlord Era.

October 10 — "Double Ten Day" — is celebrated as the national day of the Republic of China (Taiwan).
