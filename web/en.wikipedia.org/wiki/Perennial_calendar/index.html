Perennial calendar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A perennial calendar is a calendar that can be used for more than one year. Perennial calendars may be general, for a range of years, or specific to a single year class. In general use, a perennial calendar is a printed table or device that lets the user determine the day of the week for any date within a span of years, without printing a separate calendar for each year.

For the Gregorian calendar, the pattern of weekdays repeats exactly every 400 years, and the possible year calendars number only fourteen: seven for common years (one for each weekday on which 1 January can fall) and seven for leap years. A perennial calendar exploits this limited set, typically as a set of fourteen annual calendars indexed by a table of years, or as monthly tables combined with a year code.

Types

Table calendars

The most common form is a set of tables: one table assigns each year a dominical letter or a calendar number from 1 to 14; a second set of tables gives the twelve months arranged for each of the fourteen types. To find the weekday of a date, the user looks up the year's calendar type and then reads the corresponding month grid. Reference books such as almanacs and encyclopedias traditionally included such tables covering one or two centuries.

Mechanical and perpetual devices

Desk calendars with rotating discs or sliding strips are also called perpetual or perennial calendars. By setting the month and year, levers or windows align the correct arrangement of days. Many mechanical watches with a "perpetual calendar" complication track the date, day, month, and leap-year cycle automatically.

Algorithms

Several mental and written algorithms compute the weekday for a given Gregorian date and can be used to construct perennial calendars, including the Doomsday rule devised by John Horton Conway and Zeller's congruence. These methods assign codes to centuries, years, and months and reduce the calculation modulo seven.

Reform proposals

The term also applies to proposed calendar reforms — such as the World Calendar and the International Fixed Calendar — designed so that the calendar is literally identical every year, with each date always falling on the same weekday. Such "perennial" reform calendars achieve regularity by means of blank days or a leap week, but none has been adopted for civil use.
