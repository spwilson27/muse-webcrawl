Coping (psychology)

In psychology, coping is the conscious expenditure of effort to solve personal and interpersonal problems, and to seek to master, minimize, or tolerate stress and conflict. The influential transactional model of Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman (1984) defines coping as "constantly changing cognitive and behavioral efforts to manage specific external and/or internal demands that are appraised as taxing or exceeding the resources of the person."

Types of coping

  • Problem-focused coping — strategies aimed at changing the stressful situation itself, such as planning, seeking information, and taking direct action.
  • Emotion-focused coping — strategies aimed at regulating the emotional response, such as reappraisal, relaxation, seeking emotional support, or — less adaptively — avoidance and denial.
  • Meaning-focused coping — drawing on beliefs, values, and goals to sustain well-being under stress.
  • Social coping — seeking instrumental or emotional support from others.

Appraisal and effectiveness

Lazarus's theory emphasizes appraisal: a primary appraisal of whether an event is a threat, and a secondary appraisal of one's resources for dealing with it. No strategy is universally adaptive; effectiveness depends on the fit between the strategy and the controllability of the stressor, and rigid reliance on avoidance is associated with poorer outcomes. Coping research influences clinical interventions including stress-management training and cognitive-behavioral therapy.
