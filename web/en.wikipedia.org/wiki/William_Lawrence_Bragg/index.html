Sir William Lawrence Bragg (31 March 1890 – 1 July 1971) was an Australian-born British physicist and X-ray crystallographer, discoverer (1912) of Bragg's law of X-ray diffraction, which is basic for the determination of crystal structure. He was joint winner (with his father, William Henry Bragg) of the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1915, "for their services in the analysis of crystal structure by means of X-rays"; an important step in the development of X-ray crystallography.

Early life

Bragg was born in Adelaide, South Australia, where his father held a chair of mathematics and physics. He studied at the University of Adelaide and then at Trinity College, Cambridge. In 1912, following Max von Laue's discovery of X-ray diffraction by crystals, the 22-year-old Bragg derived the simple relation nλ = 2d sin θ — Bragg's law — that interprets diffraction spots as reflections from crystal planes.

Career

After war service in sound-ranging, Bragg became professor at the University of Manchester (1919) and later director of the National Physical Laboratory. In 1938 he succeeded Ernest Rutherford as Cavendish Professor at Cambridge, where he built a crystallography group that supported the work of Max Perutz and John Kendrew on protein structures and provided a home for the young James Watson and Francis Crick, whose DNA work drew on X-ray diffraction methods the Braggs pioneered. From 1954 he directed the Royal Institution, reviving its Christmas Lectures.

Legacy

At 25, Bragg remains the youngest person ever to receive a Nobel Prize in Physics. X-ray crystallography, the field he effectively founded, went on to reveal the structures of everything from table salt to proteins and viruses; the diffraction principles behind it link crystal studies to classical optics of the Huygens–Fresnel type.
