Patriarchate of Rome

The Patriarchate of Rome is the ecclesiastical jurisdiction associated with the Bishop of Rome — the Pope — in his capacity as one of the patriarchs of the ancient church. Within the classical Pentarchy, Rome ranked first in honour among the five great patriarchates (Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem), and its territory was understood to comprise the Christian West.

History

The prestige of the Roman see rested on its apostolic foundation by Saints Peter and Paul, its status as capital of the Roman Empire, and the ministry of its bishops. In the Latin Church the patriarchal aspect of the papacy was gradually overshadowed by the Pope's role as universal pontiff and, later, as head of the Diocese of Rome. The pope historically bore the title "Patriarch of the West"; Pope Benedict XVI discontinued that title in 2006, a decision that drew comment from Orthodox interlocutors. In 2024 Pope Francis restored "Patriarch of the West" among the historical titles listed for the Pope in the Annuario Pontificio.

Structure

Unlike the patriarchates headed by other patriarchs, the Patriarchate of Rome has no separate patriarchal synod or curia distinct from the Holy See itself; the Pope's patriarchal functions over the Latin Church are exercised through the same structures as his universal primacy. The patriarchal basilicas of Rome — St Peter's, St John Lateran, St Mary Major, and St Paul Outside the Walls — preserve in their traditional designation a memory of the patriarchal system.

Ecumenical significance

In dialogue between the Catholic and Orthodox churches, the role of Rome in the first millennium — a primacy of honour within a communion of patriarchates — is often discussed as a possible model, though the scope of Roman jurisdiction remains a central point of disagreement stemming from the East–West Schism.
