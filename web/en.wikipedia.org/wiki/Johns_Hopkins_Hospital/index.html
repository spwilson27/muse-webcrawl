The Johns Hopkins Hospital (JHH) is the teaching hospital and biomedical research facility of the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine in Baltimore, Maryland. Founded in 1889 from the bequest of the Baltimore merchant and banker Johns Hopkins, it was revolutionary for its time in combining patient care with university-based research and bedside teaching, and it is consistently ranked among the best hospitals in the United States.

History

Johns Hopkins left $7 million in his will (1873) — then the largest philanthropic bequest in American history — split between a university and a hospital. The hospital opened on 7 May 1889, designed by John Shaw Billings on advanced pavilion principles. Its founding "Big Four" physicians were pathologist William Henry Welch, surgeon William Stewart Halsted, internist William Osler and gynaecologist Howard Atwood Kelly. The School of Medicine followed in 1893, admitting women on equal terms from the start — a condition of fundraising led by Mary Elizabeth Garrett.

Hopkins pioneered practices now universal in medicine: the residency system of graduate training, the modern surgical residency under Halsted, rubber gloves and aseptic technique in surgery, and the first major textbook of modern internal medicine under Osler.

The modern hospital

The hospital anchors the Johns Hopkins medical campus in East Baltimore. Major additions include the Charlotte R. Bloomberg Children's Center and the Sheikh Zayed Tower, opened in 2012. Johns Hopkins Hospital operates a nationally designated trauma center, the Johns Hopkins Children's Center, and specialty institutes in oncology (Sidney Kimmel Comprehensive Cancer Center), neurology and neurosurgery, cardiology, psychiatry and many other fields. It was ranked first in the United States by U.S. News & World Report for 21 consecutive years (1991–2011) and remains at or near the top of national rankings.

Research milestones

Discoveries associated with the hospital include the first "blue baby" heart operation by Alfred Blalock and Helen Taussig (1944), the development of CPR, the isolation of restriction enzymes that launched genetic engineering (Nobel Prize, 1978), and work on HeLa cells, taken from patient Henrietta Lacks in 1951 and central to later debates about research ethics and consent.
