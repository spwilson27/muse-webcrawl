Bespoke tailoring

Bespoke tailoring is the craft of making clothing — most famously men's suits — fully by hand to an individual customer's measurements and specifications, from a pattern drafted uniquely for that client. The word bespoke derives from the verb bespeak, "to speak for something": cloth was said to be "spoken for" by the customer. Bespoke tailoring is most closely associated with Savile Row in London.

Process

The process begins with consultation on cloth and style, followed by detailed measurement. A cutter drafts a unique paper pattern and cuts the cloth; the garment is basted together for a first (skeleton) fitting, then a forward fitting, and finally a finish fitting, with the pattern adjusted at each stage. A full bespoke suit traditionally requires some 50 or more hours of handwork and takes several weeks to months to complete.

Bespoke, made-to-measure and ready-to-wear

Bespoke differs from made-to-measure, which modifies a standard block pattern to the customer's measurements and choice of options, and from ready-to-wear, sold off the rack in standard sizes. The Savile Row Bespoke Association sets criteria for its members, including a pattern made individually, substantial handwork, and a minimum number of hours of hand labour.

History

Savile Row became the centre of English tailoring in the 19th century, dressing military officers, aristocrats and statesmen; houses such as Henry Poole & Co., Huntsman, Gieves & Hawkes and Anderson & Sheppard shaped the structured English silhouette. Bespoke traditions also flourish in Naples (soft, unstructured shoulders), Milan, and increasingly in East and South Asia. Although the market contracted with casualisation, bespoke tailoring survives as the apex of the menswear trade and has influenced contemporary fashion.
