Soviet–Japanese Neutrality Pact

The Soviet–Japanese Neutrality Pact (Japanese: 日ソ中立条約, Nisso Chūritsu Jōyaku; Russian: Пакт о нейтралитете между СССР и Японией) was a non-aggression pact signed in Moscow on 13 April 1941 between the Soviet Union and the Empire of Japan, by foreign ministers Vyacheslav Molotov and Yōsuke Matsuoka. It came two years after the Soviet–Japanese border conflicts had ended in a Soviet victory at Khalkhin Gol.

Terms

The parties pledged to maintain friendly relations, respect each other's territorial integrity, and remain neutral if either party became the object of military action by one or more third powers. A separate declaration addressed the territories of the Mongolian People's Republic and Manchukuo, whose integrity each side undertook to respect. The pact was to run for five years, automatically renewable unless denounced one year before expiry.

Effects

The pact secured Japan's northern flank and helped enable its southward expansion into Southeast Asia and the Pacific, while allowing the Soviet Union, once Germany invaded in June 1941, to transfer substantial forces from Siberia and the Far East to the defence of Moscow — a redeployment made safer by intelligence from Richard Sorge confirming that Japan would strike south rather than north.

Repudiation

On 5 April 1945 the Soviet Union gave notice that it would not renew the pact. Having secretly agreed at Yalta to enter the war against Japan, the USSR declared the pact broken and invaded Manchuria on 9 August 1945 in Operation August Storm, overwhelming the Kwantung Army in the closing days of the Second World War.
