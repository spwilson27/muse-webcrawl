Horror fiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A gothic novel by candlelight: the page open at a woodcut of a ruined castle as a shadow crosses the wall]

Horror is a genre of fiction intended to disturb, frighten or unsettle its audience, drawing on fear of death, the unknown, the body and the supernatural. It descends from folklore and Gothic fiction (Horace Walpole's The Castle of Otranto, 1764) through the nineteenth century's great triad — Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818), Stevenson's Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde (1886), Bram Stoker's Dracula (1897) — and the cosmic dread of H. P. Lovecraft, into the modern boom led by Stephen King.

Horror is as much a mode as a genre: its literature crosses into film (where it is a commercial mainstay), comics, games and creepypasta, and its subgenres — supernatural, psychological, body horror, folk horror, cosmic horror, slasher — map the fears of their eras.

The literary line

The Gothic supplied the furniture (ruined castles, doubles, buried secrets); Poe made horror psychological and musical; M. R. James antiquarian ghost stories perfected the quiet scare; Lovecraft's Cthulhu mythos proposed horror from the cosmos's indifference rather than evil's attention. Shirley Jackson (The Haunting of Hill House, 'The Lottery') domesticated dread; Richard Matheson and then King — Carrie (1974) opening the modern era — made horror publishing's mass engine, its everyman prose carrying monsters into small American towns.

Why horror

Scholars read horror as the culture's rehearsed catastrophe: the Gothic processed revolution and empire; Lovecraft, race and science; the 1970s boom, Vietnam and suburban unease; body horror, disease and technology. Its fans prize exactly what repels others — the safe dose of the unsafe — and its writers' canon, once pulp, now sits in classics series: horror is the literature of what a society cannot say plainly.
