Single tax

The single tax is a proposal in which a single tax on the rental value of land — a land value tax — replaces all other taxes on income, sales, buildings, and trade. The idea was popularised by the American economist and social reformer Henry George in his best-selling book Progress and Poverty (1879), and the movement it inspired is known as Georgism.

Rationale

George argued that while individuals should own the wealth they create, the value of land derives from nature and from the growth of the surrounding community, not from the owner's labour; its rental value therefore rightfully belongs to society. Taxing land values, he held, would be efficient as well as just, because land is fixed in supply: unlike taxes on labour or capital, a land tax does not discourage production or investment. Revenue from the single tax would fund government, and some versions return surplus revenue to citizens as a dividend.

History

Antecedents of the idea appear in the French Physiocrats' impôt unique on land in the eighteenth century, and in remarks by John Locke, Adam Smith, and John Stuart Mill favouring the taxation of land rent. George's book sparked a worldwide movement: single-tax parties and leagues formed across the English-speaking world, George nearly won the 1886 New York mayoral election, and planned communities such as Fairhope, Alabama, and Arden, Delaware, were founded on single-tax principles. Elements of the policy survive in split-rate property taxes, used at times in Pittsburgh and other Pennsylvania cities, and in land-value taxation in places such as Denmark, Estonia, and parts of Australia.

Criticism

Critics argue that land values alone may be insufficient to fund a modern state, that assessing unimproved land value is difficult where land and improvements are sold together, and that a sudden shift would impose large transitional losses on current landowners. Supporters reply that modern assessment methods and phased introduction address these concerns.
