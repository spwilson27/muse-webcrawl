Shirin (Persian: شیرین; died 628) was a wife of the Sasanian king Khosrow II (r. 590–628) and one of the most celebrated women of Persian tradition. A Christian of Aramean or Persian background — sources differ — she rose from obscure origins to become Khosrow's favourite consort and a powerful figure at court.

Historical Shirin

Historical accounts describe Shirin as a Christian who retained her faith at the Zoroastrian court and used her influence to protect Christian communities and churches. The Byzantine historian Theophylact Simocatta and Syriac Christian sources mention her patronage of monasteries and her role in court politics during the reign of Khosrow II, including the great Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628. After Khosrow was overthrown and killed in 628, Shirin is said to have died shortly afterwards; some accounts report she took her own life rather than marry the usurper's successor.

In literature

Shirin became immortalised in Persian literature as the beloved of Khosrow in the romance Khosrow and Shirin, most famously versified by Nizami Ganjavi in his Khamsa (12th century). In the legend, the sculptor Farhad falls in love with Shirin and carves a channel through a mountain for her; Khosrow, jealous, tricks Farhad into believing Shirin has died, whereupon Farhad kills himself. The tragic love triangle of Khosrow, Shirin, and Farhad became one of the canonical romances of Persian, Turkish, and South Asian literature, and Shirin's name became a byword for beauty and devoted love.

Legacy

The town of Qasr-e Shirin ("Shirin's palace") in western Iran preserves her name. Her story has been retold in countless poems, miniature paintings, operas, and modern novels across the Persianate world.
