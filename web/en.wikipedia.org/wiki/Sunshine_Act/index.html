Sunshine Act

The Sunshine Act most commonly refers to the Government in the Sunshine Act of 1976, a United States federal law requiring that meetings of government agencies be open to public observation. The term "sunshine laws" is also used generically for open-meetings and open-records statutes at the state level, and a separate federal "Physician Payments Sunshine Act" governs disclosure of payments to doctors.

Government in the Sunshine Act (1976)

Enacted on 13 September 1976 as part of the post-Watergate wave of transparency reforms, the Act (5 U.S.C. § 552b) applies to federal agencies headed by a collegial body — multi-member commissions, boards, and authorities such as the SEC, FCC, and Federal Reserve Board. It requires that, with specified exceptions, "every portion of every meeting of an agency shall be open to public observation". Agencies must publicly announce meetings at least one week in advance in the Federal Register, and may close a meeting only when it falls within one of ten exemptions — for example, matters of national defence, personnel rules, trade secrets, personal privacy, or ongoing law-enforcement proceedings — and only by a recorded vote of a majority of the agency's members.

State sunshine laws

All fifty states have their own open-meeting and public-records laws, collectively called sunshine laws. Florida's Government-in-the-Sunshine Law, first enacted in 1967, is among the broadest, covering boards and commissions at state and local level and requiring that meetings at which official acts are taken be open, noticed, and minuted. State laws vary in scope, exemptions, and enforcement, but they share the principle associated with Justice Louis Brandeis that sunlight is "the best of disinfectants".

Physician Payments Sunshine Act

A distinct provision, enacted in 2010 as section 6002 of the Affordable Care Act, requires manufacturers of drugs, devices, biologicals, and medical supplies covered by Medicare, Medicaid, or CHIP to report payments and other transfers of value to physicians and teaching hospitals. The data are published by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services in the Open Payments database, intended to disclose financial relationships that could influence clinical decisions and research.
