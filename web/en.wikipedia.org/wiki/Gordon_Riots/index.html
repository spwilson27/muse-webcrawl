Gordon Riots

The Gordon Riots of 1780 were several days of rioting in London motivated by anti-Catholic sentiment. They began with a huge and orderly protest against the Papists Act of 1778, which was intended to reduce official discrimination against British Catholics enacted by the Popery Act of 1698. Lord George Gordon, head of the Protestant Association, warned that the Act would enable Catholics to join the British Army and plot treason. The protest led to widespread rioting and looting, including attacks on the homes and chapels of Catholics, on prisons — including the burning of Newgate Prison — and on the Bank of England.

An estimated 300 to 700 people died in the riots and the military repression that ended them. The riots are considered the most destructive urban disturbance in 18th-century London, causing damage estimated at £180,000.

Background

The Papists Act 1778 was the first significant measure of Catholic relief: it removed some penalties on Catholics who took an oath of allegiance, and was partly intended to encourage Catholic recruitment into the army during the American War of Independence. Lord George Gordon, an eccentric Scottish MP and president of the Protestant Association, organized a mass petition against the Act.

Course of the riots

On 2 June 1780, a crowd of around 40,000–60,000 people assembled in St George's Fields and marched on Parliament to present the petition. When Parliament refused to consider it immediately, the crowd attacked the coaches of MPs and peers, and violence spread. Over the following days mobs destroyed Catholic chapels (including embassy chapels of Sardinia and Bavaria), attacked the houses of prominent Catholics and judges, burned Langdale's gin distillery in Holborn — whose escaping spirits caught fire in the streets — and on "Black Wednesday," 7 June, attacked Newgate and other prisons, releasing the prisoners, and assaulted the Bank of England.

Suppression and aftermath

The government eventually called in some 12,000 troops; soldiers fired on crowds, killing hundreds. Order was restored by 9 June. Around 450 rioters were arrested and some 25 were hanged. Lord George Gordon was tried for high treason but acquitted, his defence arguing he had not intended the violence. The riots severely set back the cause of Catholic emancipation for decades and entered literature most famously in Charles Dickens's novel Barnaby Rudge (1841).
