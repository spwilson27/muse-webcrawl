Pierre Beaumarchais

Pierre-Augustin Caron de Beaumarchais
Born24 January 1732, Paris, France
Died18 May 1799, Paris
OccupationsWatchmaker, inventor, playwright, musician, diplomat, spy, arms dealer, publisher
Notable worksThe Barber of Seville, The Marriage of Figaro

Pierre-Augustin Caron de Beaumarchais (24 January 1732 – 18 May 1799) was a French polymath whose career spanned watchmaking, court intrigue, espionage, arms dealing, publishing and the theatre. He is best remembered for his Figaro plays — The Barber of Seville (1775), The Marriage of Figaro (1784) and The Guilty Mother (1792) — which mocked the privileges of the aristocracy on the eve of the French Revolution.

Early career

Born Pierre-Augustin Caron, the son of a Paris watchmaker, he invented a new escapement for watches that made them thinner and more accurate; when the royal watchmaker claimed the invention, the young Caron's vindication by the Academy of Sciences brought him to court. He bought a noble office and the name "de Beaumarchais", taught music to the daughters of Louis XV, and entered a series of business ventures and lawsuits, defeating the powerful judge Goëzman in a celebrated pamphlet campaign (the Mémoires) that made him a popular hero.

American Revolution

Beaumarchais persuaded the French government to support the American rebels secretly. Through a front company, Roderigue Hortalez et Cie, he channelled French and Spanish money, arms and supplies to the colonists — aid that helped arm the American army at Saratoga. He was never fully repaid and spent years petitioning Congress, a debt finally settled with his heirs only in the nineteenth century.

The Figaro plays

The Marriage of Figaro, written in 1778, was banned by Louis XVI, who declared that the Bastille would have to be destroyed before its performance could be harmless; after a long battle it premiered in 1784 to enormous success. Figaro's monologue attacking the privileges of birth anticipated revolutionary rhetoric — Napoleon later called the play "the Revolution already in action". The plays were adapted as operas by Rossini (The Barber of Seville, 1816) and Mozart (Le nozze di Figaro, 1786).

Later life

Beaumarchais edited a complete edition of Voltaire's works, welcomed the Revolution at first but fell under suspicion, was imprisoned during the Terror era and spent time in exile in Hamburg before returning to Paris, where he died in 1799.
