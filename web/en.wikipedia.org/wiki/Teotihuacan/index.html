Teotihuacan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Pyramid of the Sun viewed along the Avenue of the Dead at Teotihuacan]

Teotihuacan was an ancient Mesoamerican city in the Valley of Mexico, some 40 km north-east of modern Mexico City, which between about 100 BC and AD 650 grew into one of the largest cities in the world — with perhaps 125,000 inhabitants at its peak — and the dominant power in Mesoamerica. Its grid plan, the vast Pyramids of the Sun and the Moon along the Avenue of the Dead, and its apartment compounds with vivid murals mark a civilisation whose name, language and rulers remain unknown; 'Teotihuacan' ('place of the gods') is the name the Aztecs gave its ruins centuries later.

The city

Rebuilt on a precise grid after about AD 200, Teotihuacan housed its people in more than 2,000 one-storey apartment compounds, many painted with processions of deities, warriors and animals. The Pyramid of the Sun, the third-largest pyramid in the world by volume, and the Feathered Serpent Pyramid in the Ciudadela — adorned with hundreds of sculpted serpent heads and accompanied by mass sacrificial burials — anchor the ceremonial axis. Obsidian workshops, foreign quarters (Oaxacan, Maya, Gulf coast) and murals reveal a cosmopolitan trading metropolis.

Power and fall

Teotihuacan's armies and trade reached the Maya lowlands — in AD 378 a Teotihuacan-linked arrival ('Siyaj K'ak'') installed a new dynasty at Tikal — and its goods and gods (the Storm God, the Feathered Serpent) spread far and wide, though whether it ruled an empire is debated. Around AD 550 the city's ceremonial core was burned, apparently in internal revolt; population drained away over the next century. The Aztecs revered the ruins as the birthplace of the present sun and world.
