Kelso (Scots: Kelsae; Scottish Gaelic: Cealsaidh) is a market town in the Scottish Borders area of Scotland, lying on the banks of the River Tweed near its confluence with the River Teviot. It is widely regarded as one of the most attractive towns in the Borders, noted for its broad cobbled town square, elegant Georgian architecture and the romantic ruins of Kelso Abbey.

History

The town grew up around Kelso Abbey, founded in 1128 by David I for Tironesian monks who had first settled at Selkirk. The abbey became one of the richest and most powerful monastic houses in Scotland, and it was at Kelso that the young James III was crowned in 1460 following the death of his father James II at the siege of Roxburgh Castle. The town and abbey suffered heavily during the Anglo-Scottish wars and the Rough Wooing of the 1540s, when English forces burned the abbey.

Nearby stood the great medieval town and castle of Roxburgh, once one of Scotland's most important burghs, which was destroyed after its recapture from the English in 1460 and never rebuilt. Kelso later prospered as a market and coaching town, and Floors Castle, the seat of the Duke of Roxburgh and the largest inhabited castle in Scotland, was built just outside the town in the 1720s.

Landmarks

The town square, said to be the largest market square in Scotland, is surrounded by fine Georgian and Victorian buildings. Kelso Bridge, built by John Rennie and completed in 1803, was the model for Rennie's later Waterloo Bridge in London. The ruins of Kelso Abbey stand in the town centre, and the town is also home to Kelso Racecourse, a noted National Hunt course established in 1822.
