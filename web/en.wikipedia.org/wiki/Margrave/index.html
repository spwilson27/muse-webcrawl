Margrave

A margrave, lord of a border march

Margrave (from German Markgraf, "march count") was originally the medieval title of a military commander assigned to maintain the defence of one of the border provinces — a march — of the Carolingian Empire and later of the Holy Roman Empire. The feminine form is margravine; the territory ruled was a margraviate (or march).

Origins

Charlemagne created marches along the frontiers of his empire, including the Spanish March, and the office of their counts combined military, judicial and administrative authority greater than that of an ordinary count, since border regions faced constant threat. Under the Ottonian emperors, frontier margraviates such as the Northern March, the March of Brandenburg's predecessors, Meissen, Lusatia, and the Bavarian Eastern March (later Austria) became key instruments of expansion against Slavic and Magyar neighbours.

Elevation in rank

Within the Holy Roman Empire the title rose in prestige: several margraves became hereditary territorial princes, and the Margraves of Brandenburg were among the seven Prince-electors named in the Golden Bull of 1356. The Margraves of Baden, Brandenburg and Meissen (whose holders became Electors of Saxony) ruled major states. In England the roughly equivalent office was the Marcher Lord of the Welsh Marches, and in France the cognate title was marquis.

Later use

After the dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire in 1806, the title survived as a noble rank — below a duke and above a count — in German heraldry, and as a courtesy style in some ruling houses. Analogous frontier offices elsewhere included the Byzantine akritai commands and the Ottoman serhad beys.
