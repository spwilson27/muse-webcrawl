Overview

The Tropic of Capricorn is the southernmost circle of latitude at which the Sun can be directly overhead. This happens at the December solstice, when the Southern Hemisphere is tilted most fully towards the Sun. It lies at about 23.44° south of the Equator, mirroring the Tropic of Cancer in the north.

Between the two tropics lies the tropical zone, where the Sun passes through the zenith twice a year (or once, on either tropic itself).

Name

The line is named for Capricornus: when it was named in antiquity, the Sun stood in that constellation at the December solstice. Owing to precession, the December-solstice Sun now appears in Sagittarius, but the old name is retained.

Unlike its northern twin, the Tropic of Capricorn crosses mostly ocean and sparsely populated land, so it is marked by roadside monuments in far fewer places — famously along highways in Australia and southern Africa.

Geography

Travelling westward from the prime meridian, the tropic crosses Namibia, Botswana, South Africa, Mozambique, Madagascar, the Indian Ocean, Australia, the Pacific Ocean, Chile, Argentina, Paraguay and Brazil.

Like the Tropic of Cancer it is not fixed: as Earth's axial tilt decreases, the Tropic of Capricorn is drifting northward by about 15 metres a year.
