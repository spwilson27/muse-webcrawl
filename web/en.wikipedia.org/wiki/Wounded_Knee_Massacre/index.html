Wounded Knee Massacre

The Wounded Knee Massacre, also known as the Battle of Wounded Knee, was a massacre of Lakota people by soldiers of the United States Army. It occurred on December 29, 1890, near Wounded Knee Creek on the Pine Ridge Indian Reservation in South Dakota, following a botched attempt to disarm the Lakota camp. Approximately 250 to 300 Lakota men, women, and children were killed, along with 25 soldiers; many of the army casualties were believed to have been caused by friendly fire, as the shooting took place at close range in chaotic conditions.

Background

In 1890 the Ghost Dance movement, founded by the Paiute prophet Wovoka, spread among the Lakota, who faced starvation after government rations were cut and their land base reduced. Settlers and officials feared an uprising. The army moved thousands of troops into the Dakotas; the Standing Rock agent ordered the arrest of Sitting Bull, who was killed by Indian police on December 15. Chief Spotted Elk (Big Foot), ill with pneumonia, led his band of Miniconjou and refugee Hunkpapa toward Pine Ridge to seek safety. They were intercepted on December 28 by Major Samuel Whitside of the 7th Cavalry and escorted to a camp on Wounded Knee Creek.

The massacre

On the morning of December 29, Colonel James W. Forsyth arrived with the rest of the regiment and four Hotchkiss mountain guns positioned on the hill above the camp. During the disarmament a scuffle broke out — accounts describe a deaf Lakota man, Black Coyote, resisting giving up his rifle, which discharged. Soldiers opened fire, and the Hotchkiss guns fired into the camp and into fleeing families. Lakota men grabbed weapons and fought back at close quarters, but most were unarmed. Killings continued for hours along the creek and ravine; bodies of women and children were found miles from the camp. A blizzard struck that night, and the dead were later buried in a mass grave.

Twenty soldiers were awarded the Medal of Honor for the engagement, awards that Native American activists and members of Congress have repeatedly sought to rescind.

Aftermath and legacy

Wounded Knee is generally regarded as the end of the Indian Wars and of armed Lakota resistance. In 1973 the site was occupied for 71 days by activists of the American Indian Movement in protest at conditions on the reservation. In 1990, Congress passed a resolution expressing "deep regret" for the massacre on its centennial. The site is a National Historic Landmark, and the mass grave is marked by a monument erected in 1903.
