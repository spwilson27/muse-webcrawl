Cliometrics

Cliometrics (from Clio, the Greek muse of history, and -metrics, measurement), sometimes called the new economic history, is the systematic application of econometrics, economic theory, and other formal quantitative methods to the study of history (especially social and economic history).

Development

The field emerged in the United States in the late 1950s and 1960s, associated with scholars such as Robert Fogel and Douglass North. Landmark works include Fogel's Railroads and American Economic Growth (1964), which used counterfactual analysis to assess the contribution of railroads to the U.S. economy, and Fogel and Stanley Engerman's Time on the Cross (1974), a controversial quantitative study of American slavery. In 1993, Fogel and North shared the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for renewing research in economic history by applying economic theory and quantitative methods.

Methods and criticism

Cliometricians construct large historical datasets, build explicit models, and test hypotheses statistically, often using counterfactual reasoning. Critics have questioned the assumptions underlying such models and the reduction of historical complexity to measurable variables, but cliometric methods are now a standard part of economic history, institutionalised in the Cliometric Society and the journal Explorations in Economic History.
