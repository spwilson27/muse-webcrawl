Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) is a soft ionization technique used in mass spectrometry for the analysis of large biomolecules such as proteins, peptides, and polymers, which tend to fragment when ionized by conventional methods. In MALDI, the analyte is mixed with a large excess of a matrix compound — typically a small organic acid such as α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid or sinapinic acid — and co-crystallized on a metal target plate.

Mechanism

A pulsed laser, commonly a nitrogen laser at 337 nm or a frequency-tripled Nd:YAG laser at 355 nm, irradiates the crystals. The matrix absorbs the laser energy, desorbs from the surface, and carries the embedded analyte molecules into the gas phase, where proton transfer from the matrix ionizes them. The resulting ions are usually singly charged, which simplifies interpretation of the spectra. MALDI is most often coupled to time-of-flight (TOF) analysers, giving the technique the name MALDI-TOF MS.

History

The technique was developed in the 1980s. Koichi Tanaka demonstrated soft laser desorption of proteins using a metal powder matrix in 1987, work for which he shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Franz Hillenkamp and Michael Karas developed the organic-matrix approach and coined the term MALDI in 1985–1988.

Applications

MALDI-TOF is widely used for peptide mass fingerprinting in proteomics, for imaging mass spectrometry of tissue sections, for polymer analysis, and — notably in clinical microbiology — for rapid identification of bacteria and fungi from their characteristic spectral fingerprints.
