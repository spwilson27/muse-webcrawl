Sociolect

In sociolinguistics, a sociolect is a form of language (lect) or set of lexical items used by a socioeconomic class, profession, age group, or other social group. The term contrasts with dialect, which is associated primarily with regional variation, and with idiolect, the speech of an individual.

Concepts

Sociolects are marked by vocabulary, pronunciation, grammar, and patterns of usage that signal group membership and social identity. William Labov's studies of New York City English and of Martha's Vineyard demonstrated that speech variation correlates systematically with social class and aspiration.

Class and register

Basil Bernstein distinguished between "restricted" and "elaborated" codes associated with working-class and middle-class speech communities. Related concepts include register (variation by situation), jargon (occupational vocabulary), slang, and anti-languages used by marginal groups. The notion of sociolect overlaps with that of a social dialect and is central to the study of language and social stratification.

Examples

Examples often cited include Received Pronunciation as a prestige sociolect in Britain, African American Vernacular English in the United States, and the distinct speech of occupational groups such as lawyers, physicians, and sailors.
