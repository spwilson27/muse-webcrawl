Bantu Education Act, 1953

Enacted1953, Parliament of South Africa
Act no.Act No. 47 of 1953
Minister responsibleHendrik Verwoerd (Native Affairs)
PurposeSegregated, state-controlled schooling for Black South Africans
Repealed1979 (Education and Training Act), apartheid ended 1994

The Bantu Education Act, 1953 (Act No. 47 of 1953) was a South African apartheid law that brought the education of Black South Africans under central state control and designed it to prepare them for subordinate roles in the economy. It was a cornerstone of the apartheid system.

Provisions

The Act transferred control of "Bantu" schools from provincial administrations and mission churches to the Department of Native Affairs under Hendrik Verwoerd. Mission schools—the backbone of Black education—could receive state subsidies only if they handed over control; most were forced to sell or close. The curriculum emphasised vernacular instruction in early years, manual skills, and separate development, while state spending per Black pupil remained a small fraction of spending on white pupils, with funding partly tied to taxes paid by the Black community itself.

Aims and opposition

Verwoerd notoriously argued there was "no place for [the Bantu] in the European community above the level of certain forms of labour" and questioned teaching mathematics to children expected to work in manual jobs. The Act provoked boycotts led by the ANC and protests by teachers and churches; figures such as Z. K. Matthews resigned academic posts in protest.

Legacy

Deliberately underfunded and overcrowded schooling entrenched inequality for generations. Enforcement of Afrikaans as a medium of instruction under the system's successors triggered the Soweto uprising of 1976. The Act was replaced by the Education and Training Act of 1979, but unified non-racial education arrived only with the end of apartheid in 1994; disparities in school resources remain a central issue in South Africa today.
