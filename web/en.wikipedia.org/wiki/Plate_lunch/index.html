Plate lunch

The plate lunch (Hawaiian: pā mea ʻai) is a quintessential meal of Hawaiian cuisine, roughly analogous to Southern U.S. meat-and-three fare or a Japanese bento box, but distinct in its combination of ingredients. In its classic form it consists of two scoops of white rice, one scoop of macaroni salad, and a main entrée, all served on a single plate (today often a compartmented styrofoam or fibre takeout container). It is the signature offering of Hawaii's plate lunch restaurants and lunch wagons, and one of the most recognisable expressions of Hawaii's multicultural food culture.

Composition

The standard plate lunch has three components:

  • Two scoops of white rice — medium-grain rice, traditionally formed with an ice-cream scoop; for many diners everything is mixed together with the entrée's gravy or sauce.
  • Macaroni salad — a deliberately simple, mayonnaise-rich elbow-macaroni salad, typically seasoned only with salt, pepper and sometimes grated carrot or onion, and characteristically slightly overcooked so it is soft.
  • An entrée — drawn from Hawaii's many ethnic traditions.

Common entrées include chicken katsu (Japanese), kalua pork (Hawaiian), teriyaki beef (Japanese), shoyu chicken, hamburger steak with gravy, mahi mahi, garlic shrimp, Korean-style kalbi and meat jun, Filipino pork adobo and Chinese char siu. A mixed plate combines two or more entrées. Many plates are topped or drenched with brown gravy.

History

The plate lunch descends from the shared meals of Hawaii's sugar and pineapple plantation workers in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Labourers recruited from Japan, China, Korea, the Philippines, Portugal, Puerto Rico and elsewhere brought their own foods, which were exchanged and combined during communal lunch breaks — a practice reflected in the Japanese-influenced bento-style compartmenting and in the pan-ethnic menu that later appeared at lunch counters. After World War II, lunch wagons and drive-ins serving affordable, filling "plate lunches" spread across the islands, and chains such as Zippy's, L&L Drive-Inn (L&L Hawaiian Barbecue) and Rainbow Drive-In turned the format into an institution. L&L later exported the plate lunch to the U.S. mainland and beyond.

Related dishes

The most famous spin-off is the loco moco: a hamburger patty over rice, smothered in gravy and topped with a fried egg, said to have been created in Hilo in 1949. Other relatives include the saimin-and-plate-lunch combination at local diners and the "mini plate" (one scoop of rice and a smaller entrée) for lighter appetites.

Cultural significance

Food writers treat the plate lunch as edible evidence of Hawaii's plantation-era multiculturalism: a single plate on which Japanese, Filipino, Korean, Chinese, Portuguese, Native Hawaiian and American elements coexist. Its generous portions, low prices and working-class roots make it a marker of local identity, contrasted with resort and fine-dining cuisine. The dish has been celebrated by Hawaii-born chefs and was a favourite of public figures from the islands, helping carry it to national attention.
