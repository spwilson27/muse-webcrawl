Carlos Fermín Fitzcarrald López (6 July 1862 – 9 July 1897) was a Peruvian rubber baron and explorer of Irish-American descent who became one of the wealthiest and most notorious figures of the Amazon rubber boom. His exploits in the Peruvian Amazon—above all the portage of a steamship over a mountain ridge between two river basins—inspired Werner Herzog's 1982 film Fitzcarraldo.

Life

Fitzcarrald was born in San Luis, in the Ancash region of Peru, the son of an Irish-American sailor and a Peruvian mother. Drawn east by the rubber boom, he established himself in Iquitos and then deep in the jungle of the Ucayali basin, building a rubber empire worked by indigenous laborers under conditions of virtual enslavement that drew the attention of critics such as Roger Casement in the wider Amazonian context.

The Fitzcarrald Isthmus

Seeking the rich rubber stands of the Madre de Dios region, Fitzcarrald explored the headwaters between the Urubamba and Manú river systems. In 1894–1895 he had his steamship Contamana dismantled and carried in pieces across the narrow land bridge—now known as the Isthmus of Fitzcarrald—between the Serjali and Caspajali rivers, then reassembled on the other side. The feat opened the Madre de Dios basin to rubber extraction and made him, briefly, one of the richest rubber merchants in the Amazon.

Death and legacy

Fitzcarrald drowned in 1897 when his steamship Adolfito sank in rapids on the Urubamba River, along with his Bolivian business partner Antonio de Vaca Diez. Puerto Maldonado, capital of the Madre de Dios region, stands near the heart of his former territory. Herzog's film relocated the story and dramatized the ship-hauling episode, hauling a full steamship over a hill with the labor of hundreds of indigenous extras—a production nearly as legendary as its subject.
