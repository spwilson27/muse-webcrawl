Fractal dimension

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, a fractal dimension is a term invoked in the science of geometry to provide a rational statistical index of complexity detail of a pattern. A fractal dimension measures how the detail in a pattern changes with the scale at which it is measured. It is also a measure of the space-filling capacity of a pattern and tells how a fractal scales differently, and in a fractal dimension between, say, a one-dimensional line and a two-dimensional plane. For example, a coastline can have a fractal dimension of about 1.25: rougher and more space-filling than a smooth line, yet not filling a plane.

History

The term was developed largely from the work of Benoit Mandelbrot, building on earlier ideas of Lewis Fry Richardson, who noticed that the measured length of a coastline depends on the length of the measuring stick, and on Felix Hausdorff's 1918 definition of a dimension that need not be a whole number. Mandelbrot's 1967 paper "How Long Is the Coast of Britain?" popularised the idea that such objects are self-similar and have non-integer dimensions.

Definitions

Several distinct but related definitions are used:

  • Hausdorff dimension — the rigorous measure-theoretic definition, based on covering a set with small sets and finding the threshold at which the summed measure changes from infinite to zero.
  • Box-counting (Minkowski–Bouligand) dimension — computed by counting how the number of boxes of side ε needed to cover the set grows as ε shrinks: D = lim log N(ε) / log(1/ε). This is the most common definition in applied work because it can be estimated from empirical data.
  • Similarity dimension — for exactly self-similar objects composed of N copies scaled by a ratio r, D = log N / log(1/r). The Koch snowflake, made of 4 copies scaled by 1/3, has dimension log 4 / log 3 ≈ 1.262.
  • Correlation dimension and information dimension — generalisations used especially in the analysis of chaotic attractors in dynamical systems.

Examples

The Sierpiński triangle has Hausdorff dimension log 3 / log 2 ≈ 1.585; the Menger sponge ≈ 2.727. Fractal dimensions are used in practice to characterise coastlines, river networks, clouds, turbulence, stock-market fluctuations, medical images, and the branching structure of lungs and blood vessels.
