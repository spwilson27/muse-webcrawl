Pencil

A pencil is a writing and drawing implement with a core of solid pigment — traditionally graphite mixed with clay — enclosed in a protective casing, usually wood. As it is used, the core abrades and deposits a thin layer onto paper, producing a grey or black mark that can be erased.

History

The modern pencil followed the discovery of a pure graphite deposit at Borrowdale, England, in the 1560s. In 1795 Nicolas-Jacques Conté patented the process of mixing powdered graphite with clay and firing it, allowing hardness to be controlled. Pencil hardness is graded from soft, dark 9B through HB to hard, light 9H. The familiar yellow pencil dates to the late nineteenth century; the attached eraser was patented by Hymen Lipman in 1858.

Types

Variants include mechanical (propelling) pencils with refillable leads, colored pencils whose cores use wax-based pigments, carpenter's pencils with rectangular cores, and grease pencils. Despite the name, the "lead" in pencils has never been the metal lead, though ancient Romans did write with lead styli — the source of the term.
