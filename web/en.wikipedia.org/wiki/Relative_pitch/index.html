Relative pitch

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Relative pitch lets a listener derive any note from a single reference pitch

Relative pitch is the ability of a person to identify or re-create a given musical note by comparing it to a reference note and discerning the interval between them. Unlike absolute pitch (perfect pitch), in which a note is identified without any reference, relative pitch depends on the perception of intervals, scale degrees and chord functions — but it is a skill that can be developed through ear training by most musicians.

Abilities

Well-developed relative pitch typically includes identifying intervals (major second, perfect fifth, tritone, etc.), recognising chord qualities (major, minor, diminished, augmented) and inversions, hearing the function of a note within a key (as a movable-do scale degree), transcribing melodies and harmonies by ear, and sight-singing from notation given only a starting pitch. In tonal music, listeners perceive pitches relationally — as positions within a scale — so relative pitch is considered the core listening skill of musicianship.

Training

Ear-training methods include interval drills, chord and cadence recognition, dictation, and solfège systems such as movable-do solfège and scale-degree numbers. Research suggests relative pitch perception relies on learned categorical representations of intervals and tonal hierarchies; it improves with practice at all ages, though the accuracy achievable varies. Some possessors of absolute pitch report that it can interfere with relative tasks such as transposition, while others integrate both skills.
