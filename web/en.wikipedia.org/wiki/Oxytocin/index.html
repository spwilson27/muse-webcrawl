Oxytocin

Oxytocin is a peptide hormone and neuropeptide normally produced in the hypothalamus and released by the posterior pituitary gland. It plays a role in social bonding, reproduction, childbirth, and the period after childbirth. Oxytocin is released into the bloodstream as a hormone in response to sexual activity and during labour, and is also available in pharmaceutical form to facilitate childbirth.

Biochemistry

Oxytocin is a peptide of nine amino acids (a nonapeptide) with the sequence Cys–Tyr–Ile–Gln–Asn–Cys–Pro–Leu–Gly–NH2, cyclised by a disulfide bond. It differs from the related hormone vasopressin by only two amino acids. Its structure was determined and the peptide synthesised by Vincent du Vigneaud in 1953, work that contributed to his Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Physiological functions

In the body, oxytocin stimulates uterine contractions during labour (the basis of the positive-feedback Ferguson reflex) and milk ejection during breastfeeding. Within the brain it modulates social behaviour, including pair bonding, maternal behaviour, trust, and recognition of social cues; popular accounts sometimes call it the "love hormone", though its behavioural effects are context-dependent and more complex than the label suggests.

Medical use

Synthetic oxytocin (often under the name Pitocin) is on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines. It is used to induce or strengthen labour and to prevent and treat postpartum haemorrhage. Intranasal oxytocin has been investigated for psychiatric and social conditions, with mixed results.
