The Congress of Berlin (13 June – 13 July 1878) was a diplomatic conference to reorganise the states in the Balkan Peninsula after the Russo-Turkish War (1877–1878), which had been won by Russia against the Ottoman Empire. The meeting was attended by representatives of the United Kingdom, Austria-Hungary, France, the German Empire, Italy, Russia and the Ottoman Empire.

Chaired by German Chancellor Otto von Bismarck, who described himself as an "honest broker", the Congress revised the preliminary Treaty of San Stefano, which had created a large, Russian-dominated Bulgaria. The Great Powers, alarmed by the expansion of Russian influence, sought to restore a balance of power in the Balkans.

Background

The Treaty of San Stefano, signed in March 1878, ended the Russo-Turkish War on terms highly favourable to Russia. It created a large autonomous Bulgarian principality stretching from the Danube to the Aegean Sea, recognised the independence of Romania, Serbia and Montenegro, and ceded territory in the Caucasus to Russia. Britain and Austria-Hungary regarded the settlement as a threat: a large Bulgaria would be a Russian satellite dominating the Balkans and threatening the route to the Mediterranean.

The Congress

Delegates included Benjamin Disraeli, Earl of Beaconsfield, and the Marquess of Salisbury for Britain; Count Gyula Andrássy for Austria-Hungary; Prince Alexander Gorchakov for Russia; and William Henry Waddington for France. Bismarck presided and steered the negotiations through a series of prior agreements, including the secret Cyprus Convention between Britain and the Ottoman Empire.

Treaty of Berlin

The resulting Treaty of Berlin partitioned the San Stefano Bulgaria into three parts: a small autonomous Principality of Bulgaria north of the Balkan Mountains; the autonomous province of Eastern Rumelia under Ottoman sovereignty; and Macedonia, returned to direct Ottoman rule. Austria-Hungary received the right to occupy and administer Bosnia and Herzegovina, while Britain acquired Cyprus. Romania, Serbia and Montenegro were recognised as independent, though Romania was compelled to cede southern Bessarabia to Russia in exchange for the Dobruja.

Consequences

The settlement frustrated Russian ambitions and Bulgarian national aspirations, and sowed grievances that contributed to the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913 and ultimately to the First World War. Disraeli returned to London claiming to have brought "peace with honour".
