Prince-elector

The prince-electors (German: Kurfürsten, sing. Kurfürst) were the members of the electoral college that elected the ruler of the Holy Roman Empire. From the 13th century onwards, the right to vote was granted to a limited number of imperial princes; in 1356 the Golden Bull issued by Emperor Charles IV fixed the college at seven members, and its composition changed only a few times over the following centuries.

The Golden Bull of 1356

The Golden Bull named seven electors: three ecclesiastical — the Archbishops of Mainz, Trier and Cologne — and four secular: the King of Bohemia, the Count Palatine of the Rhine, the Duke of Saxony and the Margrave of Brandenburg. Elections were held at Frankfurt am Main, and the Archbishop of Mainz, as arch-chancellor of Germany, summoned and presided over the college.

Court offices

Each elector held a hereditary great office of the imperial court: the Archbishop of Mainz was Arch-Chancellor of Germany, Cologne of Italy, and Trier of Gaul/Burgundy; the King of Bohemia was Arch-Cupbearer, the Count Palatine Arch-Steward, the Duke of Saxony Arch-Marshal, and the Margrave of Brandenburg Arch-Chamberlain.

Later changes

During the Thirty Years' War the Palatinate's vote was transferred to Bavaria in 1623; the Peace of Westphalia (1648) created an eighth electorate for the restored Palatinate. A ninth electorate was created for Brunswick-Lüneburg (Hanover) in 1692. In 1777 Bavaria and the Palatinate were reunited under one Wittelsbach ruler, reducing the number of votes. The Reichsdeputationshauptschluss of 1803 reshaped the college, but the Empire itself was dissolved in 1806.

Electorates after 1806

The title "Elector" survived in the Electorate of Hesse (Hesse-Kassel), whose ruler retained the style of Kurfürst until the principality was annexed by Prussia in 1866, long after the title had lost its electoral function.
