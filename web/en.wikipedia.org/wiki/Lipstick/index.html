Lipstick is a cosmetic that applies colour, texture, and protection to the lips. Many types and colours of lipstick exist; as with most cosmetics, lipstick is typically worn by women, although it has also been used by men in various periods and cultures. The product is made of waxes, oils and pigments such as carmine and iron oxides, cast into a stick and applied from a tube.

History

Coloured lip preparations date back to antiquity: Mesopotamian women ground semi-precious stones to decorate their lips, ancient Egyptians used plant and mineral pigments (and occasionally toxic compounds), and lip colour was worn in classical Greece and Rome despite ambivalent moral commentary. In 16th-century England, Queen Elizabeth I popularised a look of bright red lips against a whitened face. Lipstick's respectability fluctuated: in the 19th century it was associated with actresses and prostitutes in much of Europe and America, but by the early 20th century commercial production and advertising made it mainstream. The French firm Guerlain was among the first to sell lipstick commercially in the 1880s, and in 1915 Maurice Levy patented a sliding metal tube in the United States, making the product portable. During the Second World War lipstick was treated as a morale-boosting essential, and shades such as "Victory Red" were marketed to servicewomen.

Composition and types

A typical lipstick combines waxes (such as beeswax, candelilla or carnauba) for structure, oils and emollients (such as castor oil and lanolin) for spread, and pigments and dyes for colour, plus antioxidants and preservatives; formulations range from matte and satin to glossy, sheer and long-wear "liquid lipsticks". Regulators restrict ingredients because small quantities are inevitably ingested, and permitted colorants are listed in food and drug regulations in most markets.

Cultural significance

Lipstick has served as a symbol in feminist and social history — worn defiantly by suffragettes in the 1910s, associated with flappers in the 1920s, and periodically reclaimed as a sign of glamour or rebellion. The so-called "lipstick effect" — the claim, popularised by Leonard Lauder of Estée Lauder, that lipstick sales rise during economic downturns as an affordable luxury — is often cited as a folk economic indicator, though its reliability is debated.
