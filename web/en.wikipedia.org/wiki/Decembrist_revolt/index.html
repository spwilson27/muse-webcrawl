The Decembrist revolt (Russian: Восстание декабристов, Vosstaniye dekabristov) was a failed military uprising against the Russian Empire on 26 December 1825 (14 December in the Julian calendar). Officers of the Imperial Russian Army — many of them veterans of the Napoleonic Wars who had absorbed liberal ideas in Western Europe — led about 3,000 soldiers onto Senate Square in Saint Petersburg to protest Nicholas I's assumption of the throne, demanding instead his brother Constantine and a constitutional government.

Background

Secret societies formed among guards officers after 1816: the Union of Salvation and Union of Welfare gave way to the Northern Society in Saint Petersburg and the more radical Southern Society in Ukraine, led by Pavel Pestel. Programs ranged from Nikita Muravyov's constitutional monarchy to Pestel's Russkaya Pravda, which envisaged a republic and the abolition of serfdom. The sudden death of Alexander I in November 1825 and the confused succession — Constantine had secretly renounced his claim — created the conspirators' opportunity.

The uprising

On the morning set for the oath to Nicholas, rebel officers led the Moscow Guards Regiment and others to Senate Square, where the troops stood in square formation for hours in the freezing cold. Their designated "dictator", Prince Sergei Trubetskoy, failed to appear. Negotiations collapsed; Count Mikhail Miloradovich, a popular general sent to persuade the soldiers, was shot. By late afternoon Nicholas ordered loyal artillery to fire grapeshot into the square, scattering the rebels; dozens were killed. A companion revolt of the Chernigov Regiment in Ukraine was crushed days later.

Aftermath

An investigative commission interrogated 579 people. Five leaders — Pestel, Kondraty Ryleyev, Sergei Muravyov-Apostol, Mikhail Bestuzhev-Ryumin, and Pyotr Kakhovsky — were hanged in July 1826; over a hundred officers were exiled to Siberian hard labor and settlement, many followed voluntarily by their wives, the celebrated Decembrist wives.

Legacy

Though a failure, the revolt became a founding myth of the Russian revolutionary tradition. Alexander Herzen and later generations of reformers and revolutionaries, including Lenin, honored the Decembrists as the first noble revolutionaries who "awoke" Russia's opposition movement.
