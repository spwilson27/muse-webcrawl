Charge (warfare)

Charge
TypeOffensive manoeuvre
Practised byInfantry, cavalry, armour
PurposeClose with and rout the enemy by shock and momentum

A charge is an offensive manoeuvre in battle in which combatants advance towards their enemy at their fastest possible pace in an attempt to engage in close combat. The charge is the dominant shock attack and has been the key tactic and decisive moment of many battles throughout history. Modern charges are usually executed by small groups against individual defensive positions, rather than as massed assaults across open ground.

Cavalry charges

Mounted charges were decisive from antiquity through the 19th century: Alexander's Companion cavalry, medieval knights with couched lances, Polish winged hussars at Vienna (1683), and Napoleonic cuirassiers all used the momentum and psychological shock of the charge. Famous examples include the Charge of the Light Brigade at Balaclava (1854), a disastrous frontal assault on artillery, and the last great cavalry charges of the First World War era.

Infantry charges

Infantry charges ranged from the Greek phalanx's advance to the bayonet charges of the 18th and 19th centuries and Japanese banzai charges of the Second World War. Their success depended on the defender's morale breaking before contact; disciplined firepower from muskets, rifles, and machine guns made frontal charges increasingly costly.

Decline and legacy

By the First World War, barbed wire, machine guns, and artillery made massed charges largely suicidal, and shock action passed to tanks and armoured vehicles. The word survives in doctrines of assault and in ceremonial contexts.
