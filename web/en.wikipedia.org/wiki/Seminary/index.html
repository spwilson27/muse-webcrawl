Seminary

A seminary, school of theology, theological seminary, or divinity school is an educational institution for educating students—often called seminarists or seminarians—in scripture, theology and ministry, so as to prepare them for ordination or for service in religious ministry.

History

The modern seminary system took shape in the Roman Catholic Church after the Council of Trent (1545–1563), whose decree Cum adolescentium aetas (1563) required every diocese to maintain a seminary for the formation of priests. Protestant traditions developed their own theological colleges and divinity schools, often attached to universities; in the United States, institutions such as Andover Theological Seminary (1808) and Princeton Theological Seminary (1812) became influential centres of ministerial education. Jewish seminaries, including the Jewish Theological Seminary of America, train rabbis and cantors.

Programme

Formation typically combines academic study—biblical languages and exegesis, systematic theology, church history and ethics—with spiritual formation, pastoral placements and supervised ministry. Catholic priestly formation is commonly organised around human, spiritual, intellectual and pastoral pillars. Accreditation in North America is provided by bodies such as the Association of Theological Schools.
