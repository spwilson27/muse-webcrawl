Privatization

Mass share flotations, such as that of British Telecom in 1984, popularised privatisation in Britain.

Privatization (also privatisation) is the transfer of ownership, property or business from a government to the private sector — including the sale of state-owned enterprises, the contracting-out of public services, and the conversion of public assets into private property. The opposite process is nationalisation; the partial return of services to direct public provision is sometimes called re-municipalisation. Privatisation is an instrument for moving an economy toward a market economy.

Methods

  • Share issue privatisation — selling shares in a state enterprise on a stock exchange, often deliberately priced to attract small investors.
  • Asset sale — selling an enterprise as a whole to a strategic buyer or management buyout.
  • Voucher privatisation — distributing shares or vouchers to the population free or cheaply, used on a vast scale in post-communist transitions.
  • Contracting out and public–private partnerships — private firms deliver public services under contract while the state retains ownership.

History

Although episodes of state asset sales are old, privatisation as a systematic programme is associated first with Chile in the 1970s and then, most influentially, with the British governments of Margaret Thatcher after 1979. The sales of British Telecom (1984), British Gas (1986), the electricity and water industries (1989–91) and later the railways were copied worldwide. After 1989 the post-communist states of Central and Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union privatised entire economies; Russia's voucher and "loans-for-shares" schemes transferred great industrial wealth to a small class of oligarchs and remain controversial. International lenders such as the World Bank and IMF frequently made privatisation a condition of assistance.

Arguments and criticism

Supporters cite greater efficiency and innovation under competition and profit discipline, relief for public budgets, the removal of political interference from commercial decisions, and wider share ownership. Critics respond that private monopolies can exploit consumers as readily as public ones, that natural monopolies and essential services are ill-suited to profit maximisation, that sales at undervalued prices squander public wealth, and that workers and service quality may suffer. Empirical studies find productivity gains most reliably where privatisation is combined with genuine competition and effective regulation.
