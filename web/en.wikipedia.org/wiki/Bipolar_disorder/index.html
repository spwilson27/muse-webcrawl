Bipolar disorder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bipolar disorder is a mental disorder characterised by episodes of mania (or its milder form, hypomania) alternating with episodes of depression. During mania, mood is abnormally elevated or irritable, energy and activity surge, sleep need falls, speech becomes pressured, judgement deteriorates and psychosis may occur; depressive episodes mirror major depression, with substantial suicide risk across the illness. Onset is usually in late adolescence or early adulthood, and lifetime prevalence is roughly 1–2%.

The condition (formerly 'manic depression', Kraepelin's term) has one of the strongest genetic components among psychiatric disorders, and involves circadian and signalling pathways in the brain. Episodes are often triggered by sleep disruption, stress, childbirth and substances.

Treatment

Lithium — in use since 1949 (Cade) — remains a gold-standard mood stabiliser and uniquely reduces suicide; alternatives include certain anticonvulsants and antipsychotics. Antidepressants alone can destabilise bipolar illness, so accurate diagnosis matters. Psychotherapies focused on rhythm regularity and early-warning signs, plus sleep protection, are core adjuncts.

Spectrum and creativity

Modern classifications describe a spectrum (bipolar I, II, cyclothymia). A statistical association with creative professions is often discussed — Kay Redfield Jamison's work is the popular reference — though most people with the disorder are not artists and most artists do not have it.
