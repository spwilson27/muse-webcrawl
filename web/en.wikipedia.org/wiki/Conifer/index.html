Conifers dominate the boreal forests of the Northern Hemisphere

Conifers are a group of cone-bearing seed plants, a subset of gymnosperms. Scientifically, they make up the division Pinophyta, also known as Coniferophyta or Coniferae. The division contains a single extant class, Pinopsida. All extant conifers are perennial woody plants with secondary growth. The great majority are trees, though a few are shrubs. Examples include cedars, Douglas-firs, cypresses, firs, junipers, kauri, larches, pines, hemlocks, redwoods, spruces, and yews.

As of 2024 there are seven families, 68 genera, and about 615 species of living conifers. Although the number of species is relatively small, conifers are of immense ecological importance: they dominate the vast boreal forest (taiga) biome, which occupies some 17 per cent of the Earth's land surface, and some conifers — the coast redwood, giant sequoia and bristlecone pine — include the tallest, largest and oldest individual living organisms on Earth.

Reproduction

Most conifers are monoecious, bearing separate male and female cones on the same tree. Pollen is carried by wind to the ovulate (female) cones, where fertilisation may be delayed for more than a year after pollination. Seeds are typically winged and wind-dispersed, though some (such as junipers, whose cones are modified into berry-like structures, and pinyon pines) rely on birds and mammals. Unlike flowering plants, conifers have naked seeds — the ovules are not enclosed in an ovary — a defining feature of the gymnosperms.

Distribution and ecology

Conifers occur on all continents except Antarctica. They predominate in cold-temperate and boreal climates and at high elevations, where needle-like or scale-like leaves with thick cuticles reduce water loss and shed snow. Southern Hemisphere conifer diversity is concentrated in the Podocarpaceae and Araucariaceae of Australasia and South America.

Economic importance

Conifers supply most of the world's softwood timber and pulpwood; plantation species such as radiata pine, loblolly pine and Norway spruce underpin global forestry. They are also grown as ornamentals and Christmas trees, and yield products including resin, turpentine, pine nuts and the anticancer drug paclitaxel (originally from the Pacific yew).
