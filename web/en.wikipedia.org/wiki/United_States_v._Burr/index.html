United States v. Burr

Case
Court: Circuit Court for the District of Virginia
Decided: 1807
Presiding: Chief Justice John Marshall
Verdict: Not guilty

United States v. Burr was the 1807 treason trial of former Vice President Aaron Burr in Richmond, Virginia, presided over by Chief Justice John Marshall. Burr was acquitted, and the case established a strict constitutional definition of treason.

The conspiracy

After killing Alexander Hamilton in an 1804 duel and leaving the vice presidency, Burr traveled west and entered into schemes — never fully clarified — involving General James Wilkinson and possibly plans to seize Spanish territory in Mexico or detach western lands from the United States. President Thomas Jefferson, informed by Wilkinson, proclaimed the conspiracy and ordered Burr's arrest.

The trial

Burr was indicted for treason, defined in Article III of the Constitution as levying war against the United States or adhering to their enemies, requiring two witnesses to the same overt act. Marshall ruled that Burr, who was not present at the alleged assemblage on Blennerhassett Island, could not be convicted of treason by constructive presence, and imposed exacting evidentiary standards. The jury acquitted Burr on September 1, 1807. A related subpoena duces tecum issued to Jefferson in the case became an early precedent on executive privilege.

Legacy

The trial remains a landmark in American law, cementing the difficulty of proving treason and shaping the law of conspiracy. Burr's career never recovered; he later practiced law quietly in New York.
