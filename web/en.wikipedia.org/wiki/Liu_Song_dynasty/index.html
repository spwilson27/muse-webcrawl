The Liu Song dynasty (420–479), also known as the Former Song, was the first of the four Southern dynasties of China during the Northern and Southern dynasties period. It was founded by Liu Yu, a general of humble origin who had risen through the Eastern Jin military, and its capital was Jiankang (present-day Nanjing). The dynasty ruled southern China while the Northern Wei controlled the north.

History

Liu Yu's northern expeditions of 409–417 briefly recovered Luoyang and Chang'an, but after he usurped the Jin throne in 420 the gains in the north were largely lost. His son, Emperor Wen (r. 424–453), presided over the prosperous Yuanjia era, marked by fiscal reform and cultural flourishing, though his attempts to reconquer the north from the Northern Wei ended in costly failure in 450–451, when Wei cavalry reached the Yangtze.

The later dynasty was notorious for bloody succession struggles. Emperor Xiaowu and his successor, the tyrannical Emperor Qianfei, were followed by Emperor Ming, who massacred many of his own nephews. After Ming's death the court fell under the control of the general Xiao Daocheng, who deposed the last Song emperors and in 479 founded the Southern Qi.

Culture

The Liu Song period saw major achievements in literature and scholarship: the historian Fan Ye compiled the Book of the Later Han; Liu Yiqing assembled the anecdote collection A New Account of the Tales of the World (begun under the Jin); the poet Xie Lingyun pioneered landscape poetry; and Liu Xie wrote the Wenxin diaolong, the great work of Chinese literary theory. Buddhism continued to expand in the south.
