Cervix

[Image: Diagram of the female reproductive system showing the cervix]

The cervix (Latin for "neck") is the lower, narrow part of the uterus that connects it to the vagina. Roughly cylindrical and about 2.5–3.5 cm long in adult women, it projects into the upper vagina; its central canal connects the uterine cavity with the vagina and allows the passage of sperm, menstrual blood and, during childbirth, the baby. The opening at the vaginal end is the external os; the opening into the uterus is the internal os.

Anatomy and function

The part of the cervix visible in the vagina (ectocervix) is covered by squamous epithelium, while the canal (endocervix) is lined by mucus-secreting columnar epithelium; the junction between them, the transformation zone, is the site of most cervical cancers. Cervical mucus changes across the menstrual cycle, becoming thin and stretchy around ovulation to permit sperm passage. During pregnancy the cervix remains firm and closed, sealed by a mucus plug; at labour it softens ("ripens"), effaces and dilates to about 10 cm.

Health

Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) is the main cause of cervical cancer, one of the most common cancers in women worldwide. Screening by Pap smear or HPV testing, and HPV vaccination, have greatly reduced its incidence where widely used. Other conditions include cervicitis, cervical ectropion, polyps and cervical weakness (insufficiency), which may be treated with cervical cerclage.
