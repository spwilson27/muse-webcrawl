Albumins are a family of globular proteins, the most common of which are the serum albumins. All of the proteins of the albumin family are water-soluble, moderately soluble in concentrated salt solutions, and experience heat denaturation. Albumins are commonly found in blood plasma and differ from other blood proteins in that they are not glycosylated.

Serum albumin

Serum albumin is the main protein of human blood plasma, making up roughly half of plasma protein content, at concentrations of about 35–50 g/L. It is synthesized in the liver as preproalbumin and then processed to the mature protein. Albumins bind water, cations (such as Ca2+, Na+ and K+), fatty acids, hormones, bilirubin, thyroxine (T4) and pharmaceuticals, and their main function is to regulate the oncotic pressure of blood — the osmotic pressure that keeps fluid within the circulation.

Low serum albumin (hypoalbuminemia) can result from liver disease, kidney disease (nephrotic syndrome), malnutrition or inflammation, and is associated with edema because fluid leaks from blood vessels into tissues. Clinically, human albumin solution is administered intravenously in some cases of shock, severe burns and hypoproteinemia.

Other albumins

Substances containing albumins, such as egg white, are called albuminoids. Ovalbumin, the main protein of egg white, is a storage albumin; lactalbumin occurs in milk, and plant seeds contain 2S albumins that serve as storage proteins. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) is widely used as a protein concentration standard in laboratory assays.

Etymology

The name derives from the Latin albus ("white"), via the German Albumin, referring to the white of an egg.
