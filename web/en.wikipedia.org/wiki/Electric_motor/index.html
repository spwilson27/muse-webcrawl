Electric motor

[Image: a cutaway electric motor showing copper windings, a central rotor and steel laminations]

An electric motor is an electrical machine that converts electrical energy into mechanical energy. Most electric motors operate through the interaction between the motor's magnetic field and electric current in a wire winding to generate torque on the motor's shaft — the reverse process, converting motion into electricity, is performed by a generator (dynamo or alternator).

Electric motors are everywhere: in industrial machinery, transport (electric vehicles, trains, ships), household appliances, pumps, fans, tools, computers and robots. They are typically far more efficient than internal combustion engines, converting around 80–95% of input electrical energy to motion in larger sizes.

History and types

The principles were demonstrated in the 1820s–30s (Faraday's rotation experiments; early motors by Jedlik and Davenport) and practical DC motors followed in the 1870s. Nikola Tesla's AC induction motor (1888), powered by alternating current, became industry's workhorse because it is rugged and needs no brushes or commutator.

Types include DC motors (brushed and brushless), AC induction and synchronous motors, stepper and servo motors for precision control, and linear motors. Modern drives pair motors with electronic controllers (variable-frequency drives, inverters) that vary speed and torque precisely — an efficiency revolution in industry and the heart of the electric car.
