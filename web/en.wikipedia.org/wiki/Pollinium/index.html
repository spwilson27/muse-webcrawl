Pollinium

A pollinium (plural: pollinia) is a coherent mass of pollen grains produced by a single anther that is transferred as one unit during pollination. Pollinia are characteristic of the orchids (Orchidaceae) and of the milkweed subfamily Asclepiadoideae within the Apocynaceae.

Structure

In orchids the pollen of each anther lobe is bound together into one or a few waxy or powdery masses. The pollinium is typically connected by a stalk (caudicle or stipe) to a sticky disc, the viscidium; together these structures form the pollinarium. When an insect visits the flower, the viscidium glues the whole pollinarium to its body, and the pollinia are carried to the stigma of the next flower visited.

Evolutionary significance

Pollinia make pollination highly efficient and targeted: a single insect visit can deliver enough pollen to fertilise the thousands of ovules in an orchid ovary. The precise mechanical fit between pollinaria and particular pollinators drove much of the celebrated diversity of orchid flowers, a connection Charles Darwin explored in his 1862 book On the Various Contrivances by which British and Foreign Orchids are Fertilised by Insects.
