Adenylyl cyclase (also spelled adenyl cyclase or adenylate cyclase) is an enzyme that catalyses the conversion of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) into cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), a ubiquitous second messenger, releasing pyrophosphate. It is a central component of G protein-coupled receptor signalling: when activated receptors stimulate the G protein Gs, its α subunit switches adenylyl cyclase on, raising intracellular cAMP, which in turn activates protein kinase A, EPAC proteins and cyclic-nucleotide-gated channels.

Structure and isoforms

Mammals have nine membrane-bound adenylyl cyclases (AC1–AC9), each with twelve transmembrane segments and two cytosolic catalytic domains that come together to form the active site. A tenth enzyme, soluble adenylyl cyclase (sAC), is not membrane-bound and is regulated by bicarbonate and calcium rather than G proteins; it acts as a cellular sensor in sperm and other cells. The different isoforms are distinguished by their regulation — for example AC1 and AC8 are stimulated by calcium/calmodulin, while AC5 and AC6 are inhibited by calcium and by Gi.

Regulation and pharmacology

The plant diterpene forskolin directly activates most membrane-bound isoforms and is widely used as a research tool to raise cAMP. Cholera toxin locks Gs in its active state, causing massive cAMP accumulation in intestinal cells and the watery diarrhoea of cholera, whereas pertussis toxin disables Gi, removing inhibition. Caffeine and related drugs raise effective cAMP signalling indirectly by inhibiting phosphodiesterases, the enzymes that break cAMP down.
