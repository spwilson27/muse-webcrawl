Tensegrity

Tensegrity, or tensional integrity, is a structural principle in which a system of isolated components under compression is suspended within a network of components under continuous tension, in such a way that the compressed members (usually bars or struts) do not touch each other while the prestressed tensioned members (usually cables) delineate the system spatially.

History

The concept was developed in the mid-20th century. Artist Kenneth Snelson produced early sculptures based on the principle beginning in 1948, while architect and designer Buckminster Fuller popularized the term "tensegrity" (a portmanteau of "tensional integrity") and explored its applications in geodesic structures and engineering.

Principles

In a tensegrity structure, stability arises from the balance of tension and compression rather than from the bending strength of individual elements. The structures are characteristically lightweight, resilient, and able to distribute loads throughout the whole system; increasing tension in one element affects forces across the entire structure.

Applications

Tensegrity principles have been applied in sculpture (such as Snelson's Needle Tower), architecture, deployable structures, robotics, and biomechanics. In cell biology, Donald Ingber proposed that tensegrity describes how the cytoskeleton maintains cell shape, with microtubules acting in compression and actin filaments in tension.
