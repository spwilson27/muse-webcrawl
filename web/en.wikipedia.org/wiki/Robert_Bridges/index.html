Robert Bridges

Born: October 23, 1844, Walmer, Kent, England
Died: April 21, 1930, Boars Hill, Oxford
Occupation: Poet, physician
Office: Poet Laureate of the United Kingdom (1913–1930)

Robert Seymour Bridges (October 23, 1844 – April 21, 1930) was an English poet who served as Poet Laureate from 1913 until his death. Trained as a physician, he is remembered for his lyric poetry, his experiments with prosody, and his role in bringing the work of his friend Gerard Manley Hopkins to posthumous publication.

Life

Bridges was educated at Eton and Corpus Christi College, Oxford, where he befriended Hopkins. He studied medicine at St Bartholomew's Hospital, London, and practiced as a physician until illness forced his retirement in 1882. Thereafter he lived at Yattendon in Berkshire and later at Boars Hill near Oxford, devoting himself to poetry and scholarship.

Works

Bridges's poetry, collected in volumes from 1873 onward, is classical in form and restrained in tone; his best-known lyrics include "London Snow" and the settings of his poems by composers such as Hubert Parry, Charles Villiers Stanford, and Gustav Holst. A keen prosodist, he developed a theory of "Neo-Miltonic syllabics" and wrote the influential study Milton's Prosody (1893). His long philosophical poem The Testament of Beauty (1929), published for his eighty-fifth birthday, became an unexpected bestseller. In 1916 he edited the war-spirituality anthology The Spirit of Man.

Perhaps his most consequential literary act was editing Hopkins's poems: Hopkins had sent him his manuscripts, and Bridges's 1918 edition, nearly thirty years after Hopkins's death, introduced the radically innovative poetry to the world.
