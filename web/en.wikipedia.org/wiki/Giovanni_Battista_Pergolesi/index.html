Giovanni Battista Pergolesi (4 January 1710 – 16 March 1736) was an Italian composer, violinist and organist of the Baroque and early Classical periods. Despite a career cut short by his death at the age of 26, he became one of the most celebrated composers of his generation, best known for the intermezzo La serva padrona (1733), a landmark of opera buffa, and for his Stabat Mater (1736), completed in the final weeks of his life.

Life

Pergolesi was born in Jesi, in the Papal States (now in the Marche region of Italy). He studied music locally before entering the Conservatorio dei Poveri di Gesù Cristo in Naples, where his teachers included Gaetano Greco and Francesco Durante. Graduating in 1731, he achieved early success with the sacred drama Li prodigi della divina grazia and the opera seria Salustia.

Appointed maestro di cappella to Prince Ferdinando Colonna Stigliano, Pergolesi produced the opera Lo frate 'nnamorato (1732) and, in 1733, Il prigionier superbo, whose comic intermezzo La serva padrona eclipsed the main work in fame. Following an earthquake in 1731, he was commissioned to write works including the Mass in F major. In 1734 he succeeded Francesco Mancini as deputy maestro of the royal chapel in Naples. His last opera, L'Olimpiade (1735), was written for Rome. Suffering from tuberculosis, he retired to the Franciscan monastery at Pozzuoli, where he completed the Stabat Mater and died in 1736.

Works and legacy

Pergolesi's output includes opere serie, intermezzi, oratorios, Masses, motets, cantatas, concertos and chamber music. His catalogue was long inflated by misattributions: publishers attached his famous name to hundreds of works by contemporaries, and the modern thematic catalogue (the "P." numbering) recognises only a fraction as authentic. Most famously, much of the music Igor Stravinsky used in the ballet Pulcinella (1920), then believed to be by Pergolesi, is now attributed to other composers such as Domenico Gallo — though the ballet permanently revived interest in Pergolesi's music.

La serva padrona, performed in Paris in 1752, ignited the Querelle des Bouffons, a pamphlet war between defenders of French and Italian opera that drew in figures such as Jean-Jacques Rousseau. The Stabat Mater, scored for soprano, alto, strings and continuo, became one of the most frequently printed compositions of the 18th century and was adapted by, among others, Johann Sebastian Bach.
