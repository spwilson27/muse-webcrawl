Nuclear fallout

Nuclear fallout is the residual radioactive material propelled into the upper atmosphere following a nuclear blast, so called because it "falls out" of the sky after the explosion and the shock wave have passed. It commonly refers to the radioactive dust and ash created when a nuclear weapon detonates. The amount and spread of fallout is a product of the size of the weapon, the altitude of its explosion, and the surface against which it detonates.

Types of fallout

Fallout may be local, deposited close to the burst site within hours; tropospheric, carried around a latitude band and deposited over weeks; or stratospheric (global), distributed worldwide over months to years. When a weapon is detonated at or near the ground (a surface burst), large quantities of soil and debris are vaporized, become radioactive, and fall back as comparatively coarse, highly radioactive particles. An air burst, by contrast, produces much less local fallout because the fireball does not touch the ground.

Composition and decay

Fallout consists of fission products (such as caesium-137, strontium-90, and iodine-131), unfissioned nuclear material, and neutron-activated environmental material. Its radioactivity decays rapidly at first: a useful rule of thumb is the "7:10 rule", whereby for every sevenfold increase in time after detonation, the radiation dose rate decreases by a factor of ten.

Effects

Exposure to fallout can cause acute radiation syndrome at high doses and increases the long-term risk of cancer. Fallout from atmospheric nuclear weapons testing conducted in the mid-20th century distributed radioactive material globally and contributed to the Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty of 1963, which banned atmospheric, underwater, and outer-space tests by its signatories. Notable fallout incidents include the contamination of the Japanese fishing vessel Daigo Fukuryū Maru (Lucky Dragon No. 5) by the Castle Bravo test in 1954.

Protection

Civil defense guidance emphasizes sheltering in the densest available building, remaining indoors for at least 24–48 hours while short-lived isotopes decay, and avoiding consumption of contaminated food and water.
