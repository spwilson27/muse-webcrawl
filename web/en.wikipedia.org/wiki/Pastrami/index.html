Pastrami

Pastrami is a form of smoked cured meat, typically made from beef brisket, which originated among Romanian Jews and is strongly associated with Jewish delicatessens in New York City. The raw meat is brined, partially dried, seasoned with herbs and spices, then smoked and steamed.

A pastrami sandwich, a hallmark of the New York Jewish deli.

History

The word "pastrami" derives from Romanian pastramă, a preserved meat descended in turn from the Turkish pastırma, made by air-drying seasoned beef. Romanian Jewish immigrants brought pastramă to the United States in the second half of the 19th century. In New York, Jewish delicatessens adapted the technique to the beef navel and brisket cuts, and pastrami on rye with mustard became an iconic sandwich. Institutions such as Katz's Delicatessen (founded 1888) popularised it.

Preparation

Traditional New York pastrami is made from the navel end of the brisket. The meat is cured in a brine with salt, sugar, and curing salts; coated with a rub of black pepper, coriander, garlic, mustard seed, and other spices; smoked for several hours; and finally steamed until tender before serving. Montreal-style smoked meat is a close relative with a distinct spice profile. Turkey pastrami is a common leaner variant.

Serving

Pastrami is usually sliced hot and served on rye bread with mustard. It also appears in the Reuben sandwich (with sauerkraut, Swiss cheese, and Russian dressing on grilled rye) and in the Rachel sandwich variant. "Pastrami" as an adjective also describes the spice treatment applied to other foods, such as pastrami salmon or pastrami-spiced burgers.
