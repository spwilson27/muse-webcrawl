In-group favoritism

In-group favoritism, sometimes called in-group–out-group bias, is a pattern of favouring members of one's own group over members of other groups. It appears in evaluations, attributions, and the allocation of resources: people tend to rate their own group more positively, excuse its members' failures, and reward them more generously. The effect has been demonstrated with groups based on nationality, ethnicity, religion, profession, and even arbitrary laboratory assignments.

Sports fandom is a familiar everyday setting for in-group favoritism.

Minimal group experiments

In the early 1970s, Henri Tajfel and colleagues showed that merely categorising strangers into meaningless groups — for example, by a coin toss or by stated preference for paintings by Klee versus Kandinsky — was enough to make participants allocate more rewards to members of their own group, sometimes even sacrificing absolute gain to maximise the difference between groups. These "minimal group paradigm" results suggested that social categorisation alone can trigger bias, without any history of conflict or material competition.

Explanations

Social identity theory explains in-group favoritism as a way of maintaining positive self-esteem: because people derive part of their identity from their groups, favouring the group enhances the self. Other accounts emphasise expectations of reciprocity — people assume in-group members will return favours — and the greater empathy and trust felt toward those seen as similar. Notably, in-group favoritism does not automatically imply hostility toward out-groups; research by Marilynn Brewer and others indicates that "in-group love" is more fundamental and more common than "out-group hate", though the two can combine into discrimination under threat or competition.

Consequences

In-group favoritism contributes to ethnic and religious conflict, workplace cliques, biased refereeing and hiring, and political polarisation. Interventions that recategorise people into a larger common group, or that create cross-cutting identities, can reduce it.
