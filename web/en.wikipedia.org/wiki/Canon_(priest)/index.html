Canon (priest)

A canon (from Latin canonicus, itself from Greek kanonikos, "relating to a rule") is a member of certain bodies of clergy attached to a cathedral or collegiate church, subject to a rule of life. In the Roman Catholic Church, Anglican Communion, and some other traditions, canons historically formed the chapter that governed a cathedral, elected bishops, and maintained the daily round of worship.

History

The institution emerged in the early medieval Church as communities of clergy living a common life under a rule (kanon) alongside a bishop's church. In the 8th century Chrodegang of Metz wrote a rule for such communities, and the Rule of Aachen (816) regularized the canonical life across the Frankish empire. Over time, cathedral chapters acquired corporate property (prebends), the right to elect bishops, and considerable political influence.

A distinction developed between secular canons, serving cathedrals and collegiate churches, and canons regular — such as the Augustinian canons and Premonstratensians — who combined the canonical life with a monastic rule and vows.

Modern usage

In the Roman Catholic Church, a chapter of canons maintains solemn worship in a cathedral or basilica, and the title of canon is also conferred honorarily on diocesan priests. Canon law provides for diocesan priests titled "canon" as members of cathedral or collegiate chapters.

In the Church of England, cathedral canons include residentiary canons, who work full-time at the cathedral, and non-residentiary or honorary canons and lay canons appointed from the wider diocese. Canons traditionally occupy named stalls in the choir and may wear distinctive dress, including a purple or red mozzetta in some churches.
