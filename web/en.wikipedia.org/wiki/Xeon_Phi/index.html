Xeon Phi was a series of x86 manycore processors designed and produced by Intel under its Many Integrated Core (MIC) architecture, intended as accelerators and processors for high-performance computing (HPC). Descended in part from Intel's Larrabee graphics project, Xeon Phi products combined dozens of comparatively simple x86 cores with wide 512-bit vector units on a single chip. The line was discontinued in 2018–2020 as Intel refocused its HPC strategy on conventional Xeon processors, FPGAs and GPU-based accelerators.

Generations

The first generation, Knights Corner (2012), shipped as a PCIe coprocessor card with up to 61 Pentium-derived cores and required a host Xeon system; programs were offloaded to it or run in symmetric mode over the PCIe bus. The second generation, Knights Landing (2016), could serve as a bootable host processor in its own right, with up to 72 Silvermont-derived cores, AVX-512 instructions, four hardware threads per core, and up to 16 GB of stacked MCDRAM high-bandwidth memory alongside conventional DDR4. A third generation, Knights Mill, added features aimed at machine-learning workloads. Knights Landing powered notable supercomputers including Cori at NERSC, Theta at Argonne, and (with Knights Corner) the Tianhe-2 and Stampede systems.

Programming model and competition

Xeon Phi's selling point was programmability: because the cores were x86 and could run Linux natively, existing C, C++, Fortran, OpenMP and MPI codes could be ported with far less rewriting than GPU computing with CUDA required. In practice, achieving peak performance demanded heavy vectorisation and careful memory management, narrowing the advantage. Facing improving GPUs and Intel's own adoption of AVX-512 in mainstream Xeons, the product line was wound down; its architectural legacy persists in AVX-512 and in Intel's tile-based designs.
