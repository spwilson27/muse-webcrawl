Geophysical survey (archaeology)

In archaeology, geophysical survey is ground-based physical sensing techniques used for archaeological imaging or mapping. Remote sensing and marine surveys are also used in archaeology, but are generally considered separate disciplines. Geophysical methods allow archaeologists to detect buried features such as walls, ditches, pits, hearths, and kilns without excavation, and are a standard, non-destructive part of archaeological evaluation and research.

Methods

  • Magnetometry measures small variations in the Earth's magnetic field caused by fired features, ferrous objects, and magnetically enhanced soils filling ditches and pits. Fluxgate gradiometers are the most common instruments.
  • Earth resistance survey passes an electrical current into the ground and measures resistance, which varies with moisture content; stone walls resist current while damp ditch fills conduct it.
  • Ground-penetrating radar transmits radio pulses into the ground and records reflections from buried interfaces, producing depth slices and profiles.
  • Electromagnetic induction and metal detection are used for conductivity and magnetic susceptibility mapping and for locating metal artefacts.

Applications

Geophysical surveys are used in advance of development-led excavation, for site prospection, and in research projects mapping entire landscapes — for example, surveys around Stonehenge that revealed numerous previously unknown monuments. Data are collected on grids, processed with specialist software, and interpreted as plans of anomalies, which can then be tested by targeted excavation or coring.

History

Archaeological geophysics developed from the 1940s and 1950s, when pioneers adapted proton magnetometers and resistivity equipment for archaeological use. The widespread adoption of fluxgate gradiometers and, later, multi-sensor cart systems and motorised arrays has made large-area survey routine in commercial and research archaeology.
