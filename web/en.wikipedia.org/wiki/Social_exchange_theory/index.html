Social exchange theory

Social exchange models relationships as negotiated exchanges of costs and rewards
Key figuresGeorge Homans, Peter Blau, John Thibaut, Harold Kelley, Richard Emerson
FieldSociology, social psychology
Key conceptsRewards and costs, comparison level, comparison level for alternatives, reciprocity, power-dependence
Seminal worksHomans, "Social Behavior as Exchange" (1958); Blau, Exchange and Power in Social Life (1964)

Social exchange theory is a sociological and social psychological perspective that explains social interaction and relationship stability in terms of negotiated exchanges between parties. Drawing on behaviourism and microeconomics, it proposes that people weigh the potential rewards and costs of relationships and interactions, and tend to pursue those that maximise benefits relative to costs.

Core assumptions

The theory assumes that relationships are interdependent, that social behaviour is a series of exchanges of material and non-material goods (such as approval, status, information and support), and that people generally seek reciprocity — a balance of giving and receiving over time. Unlike strict economic models, the "currency" of social exchange is often unspecified, and the obligation to reciprocate is enforced by trust and social norms rather than contracts.

Key developments

George C. Homans laid out the theory in "Social Behavior as Exchange" (1958) and Social Behavior: Its Elementary Forms (1961), proposing propositions about reinforcement and distributive justice. Peter Blau extended the analysis from dyads to larger social structures, showing how exchange generates power and status differentiation. John Thibaut and Harold Kelley, in The Social Psychology of Groups (1959), introduced the comparison level (the standard against which a relationship's rewards are judged) and the comparison level for alternatives (the lowest outcome a person will accept given available alternatives), foundational to later relationship science such as the investment model. Richard Emerson's power-dependence theory recast power as a property of exchange relations and networks.

Applications and criticism

Social exchange theory is widely used to study close relationships, marriage, organisational behaviour, leader–member exchange, and online communities. Critics argue that the theory can be overly individualistic and calculative, underplaying altruism, moral obligation, culture and genuine attachment; others reply that modern versions incorporate norms of fairness and reciprocity precisely to answer this charge.
