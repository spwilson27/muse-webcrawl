Power loom

A power loom is a mechanised loom powered by steam, water or electricity, designed to weave cloth automatically. Invented in 1785 by Edmund Cartwright, the power loom was one of the key developments in the industrialisation of textile manufacturing during the early Industrial Revolution, moving weaving from cottages into factories and eventually displacing the handloom weaver.

History

Cartwright, an Oxford academic and clergyman, patented his first loom in 1785 after hearing that spinning machinery had created a bottleneck in weaving. His early machines were crude, but a series of improvements by others — William Horrocks's variable-speed batten and metal gearing, Richard Roberts's self-acting iron loom of 1822, and the Lancashire loom of Kenworthy and Bullough (1842) — made power weaving reliable and economical. By the 1850s there were more than 250,000 power looms in Britain, concentrated in Lancashire, and handloom weaving had collapsed from a major trade to a marginal one, with great hardship for the weavers displaced — a grievance that fed Luddite and Chartist unrest.

Operation

A power loom automates the three primary motions of weaving: shedding (separating warp threads to form a gap), picking (driving the shuttle carrying the weft across), and beating-up (pushing the new weft thread into the cloth), together with let-off and take-up motions. Looms in a mill were driven in rows from overhead line shafts by leather belts. Later developments — the Northrop loom's automatic bobbin-changing mechanism of the 1890s — allowed one weaver to tend dozens of looms. In the 20th century, shuttleless looms (rapier, projectile, air-jet and water-jet) largely superseded the shuttle loom, hugely increasing speed.
