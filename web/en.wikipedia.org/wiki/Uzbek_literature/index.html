Uzbek literature


Manuscript of the works of Alisher Navoi

Uzbek literature refers to the body of literary works produced by Uzbek authors, principally in the Uzbek language and in its literary predecessor, the Chagatai Turkic language. It developed within the rich Persianate-Turkic culture of Central Asia, whose centres — Samarkand, Bukhara, Herat and Khiva — were among the great literary cities of the Islamic world.

Early Turkic foundations

The earliest monuments of Turkic writing in the region include Yusuf Khass Hajib's Kutadgu Bilig ("Wisdom which Brings Happiness", 11th century) and Ahmad Yasawi's Sufi poetry. The flowering of Chagatai literature came in the Timurid era: the poet Lutfiy and, above all, Alisher Navoi (1441–1501), whose five epic poems (the Khamsa) and his treatise Muhakamat al-Lughatayn — arguing the literary merits of Turkic against Persian — made him the founding figure of classical Uzbek literature. Babur, founder of the Mughal Empire, wrote his celebrated memoirs, the Baburnama, in Chagatai.

19th and early 20th centuries

In the khanates of the 19th century, court poets such as Muqimiy and Furqat combined classical forms with satire of contemporary life. The Jadid reform movement produced modern prose, drama and journalism; figures like Abdulla Qadiri, author of Oʻtkan kunlar ("Bygone Days", regarded as the first Uzbek novel), and the poet Cho'lpon reshaped Uzbek letters before perishing in the Stalinist purges of the 1930s.

Soviet and post-independence periods

Under the Soviet Union, Uzbek literature operated within socialist realism while poets like Gʻafur Gʻulom and Oybek dominated the canon; the script was changed from Arabic to Latin and then to Cyrillic. Since independence in 1991, Uzbekistan has returned toward a Latin-based alphabet, and contemporary writers have explored themes suppressed in the Soviet era, though freedom of expression remains constrained. Oral epic traditions (dastan), performed by bakhshi storytellers, continue alongside written literature.
