Citric acid is a weak organic acid with the formula C₆H₈O₇, found naturally in citrus fruits — especially lemons and limes (5–6% of their juice) — giving them their sour taste. It is an intermediate in the citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle), the metabolic pathway by which nearly all living organisms generate energy from carbohydrates, fats and proteins.

Citric acid is produced industrially on a huge scale (millions of tonnes per year) by fermenting sugars with the mould Aspergillus niger, a process commercialised from 1917–19 by James Currie at Pfizer, freeing supply from Italian lemon orchards. It is among the world's most used food additives (E330) — flavouring, preservative and acidity regulator in soft drinks, sweets and canned goods — and serves in cleaning products (descaling, chelating), cosmetics and pharmaceuticals. It was first isolated from lemon juice in 1784 by Carl Wilhelm Scheele.
