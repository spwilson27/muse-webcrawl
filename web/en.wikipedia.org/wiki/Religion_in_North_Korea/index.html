Religion in North Korea

There is no known official statistics for religion in North Korea. Officially, North Korea is an atheist state, although its constitution nominally guarantees freedom of religious belief. According to Western estimates and human rights organisations, the government severely restricts religious activity, and independent religious practice outside state-controlled organisations is suppressed.

Traditional religions

Historically, Korean religious life combined Korean shamanism, Buddhism, Confucianism and, from the late 19th century, Christianity; Pyongyang was once a major centre of Protestant missions, nicknamed the "Jerusalem of the East". Cheondoism, a native syncretic movement, retains a recognised presence and is associated with the Chondoist Chongu Party, a minor party in the ruling coalition.

State policy

After the founding of the state in 1948, religious communities were progressively dismantled under Kim Il-sung, with believers facing discrimination under the songbun classification system or persecution. Defectors and international bodies such as the UN Commission of Inquiry have reported severe punishment for unauthorised religious activity, especially Christianity. A handful of state-sanctioned churches and a Buddhist temple operate in Pyongyang, largely for show to foreign visitors. The personality cult surrounding the Kim family, framed by the Juche ideology, is often described by scholars as functioning like a state religion.
