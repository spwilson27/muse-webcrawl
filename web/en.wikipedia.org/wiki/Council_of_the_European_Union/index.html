Council of the European Union

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Council of the European Union, often referred to as the Council of Ministers or simply the Council, is an essential EU decision-making body: it represents the executive governments of the member states and, together with the European Parliament, adopts EU legislation and the budget. The Council meets in ten policy configurations (foreign affairs, economic and financial affairs, agriculture, justice and home affairs, and so on), composed of the responsible national ministers; a rotating Presidency (every six months, per member state) chairs most configurations, while the High Representative chairs Foreign Affairs.

Decision rules

Most legislation passes by qualified majority voting: at least 55 per cent of member states representing at least 65 per cent of the EU's population — a double majority balancing state equality with population weight. Sensitive areas (foreign policy, taxation, treaty matters) generally require unanimity, the source of recurring veto controversies and proposals to expand QMV. The Council's work is prepared by COREPER (the ambassadors' committee) and hundreds of working groups, making most "ministerial" decisions pre-cooked agreements — the so-called A points. Together with the Parliament's co-legislative role, the Council forms the EU's bicameral legislature in substance, though neither chamber fits the classic template: one represents citizens as Europeans, the other citizens through their states.
