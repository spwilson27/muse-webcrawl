Counterfeiting

[Image placeholder: counterfeit currency detection]

Counterfeiting is the production of imitations of something valuable — most notably currency, but also documents, branded goods, medicines, electronics and works of art — with the intention that they be taken for genuine. Counterfeit money has existed since the earliest coinage: plated coins appear in ancient Greek and Roman contexts, and counterfeiting of coin was treated as treason in medieval England, punishable by death. Governments have repeatedly used counterfeiting as a weapon of war, as in Britain's forgery of American Continental currency and Nazi Germany's Operation Bernhard, which forged Bank of England notes.

Currency

Modern banknotes incorporate security features intended to make counterfeiting difficult: watermarks, security threads, microprinting, intaglio printing, colour-shifting inks, transparent windows in polymer notes, and the EURion constellation, which causes some copiers and software to refuse reproduction. Estimates of counterfeit US currency in circulation are generally below 0.01 percent of genuine notes; the United States Secret Service was originally founded in 1865 primarily to suppress counterfeiting. "Superdollars" — extremely high-quality counterfeit $100 bills — have attracted particular attention from investigators.

Goods and medicines

The OECD has estimated the trade in counterfeit and pirated goods at several hundred billion dollars per year, roughly 2–3 percent of world trade. Counterfeit pharmaceuticals are a particular public-health concern, especially in low- and middle-income countries, where substandard or falsified medicines may contain wrong or absent active ingredients.
