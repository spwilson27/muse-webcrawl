Thallium poisoning

Thallium poisoning is poisoning due to exposure to thallium and its compounds, which are among the most toxic heavy-metal poisons. Thallium salts are tasteless, odourless, and readily absorbed; historically they were used in rat poisons and insecticides, which led to many accidental and deliberate poisonings, and thallium has a notorious history as a murder weapon, sometimes nicknamed "the poisoner's poison."

Causes and mechanism

Thallium behaves chemically like potassium in the body and is taken up by cells in place of potassium ions, disrupting potassium-dependent processes and damaging mitochondria. Exposure may occur through ingestion, inhalation, or skin contact, in industrial settings, from contaminated food or water, or through deliberate administration.

Signs and symptoms

Early symptoms are gastrointestinal — nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and constipation. Within days a painful peripheral neuropathy develops, followed about two to three weeks after exposure by hair loss (alopecia), a characteristic feature. Dark bands on the nails (Mees' lines), confusion, and damage to the heart, liver, kidneys, and nervous system may occur; severe poisoning can be fatal.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is confirmed by measuring thallium in urine or blood, which is normally present only in trace amounts. The specific antidote is Prussian blue (ferric hexacyanoferrate), taken orally, which binds thallium in the gut and interrupts its enterohepatic circulation, greatly increasing faecal elimination. Supportive care and, in early severe cases, haemodialysis may also be used.

Notable cases

Graham Frederick Young, a British serial poisoner, used thallium to murder several people in the 1960s and 1970s. Thallium poisoning also features in Agatha Christie's novel The Pale Horse, which has been credited with helping real-life readers recognize cases of poisoning.
