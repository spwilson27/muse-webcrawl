Caddoan languages

RegionGreat Plains, United States
LanguagesCaddo, Pawnee, Arikara, Wichita, Kitsai
Family typeLanguage family

The Caddoan languages are a family of Native American languages spoken on the Great Plains of the United States, in present-day Oklahoma, Texas, Kansas, Nebraska, and the Dakotas. The family comprises five languages: Caddo, Pawnee, Arikara, Wichita, and Kitsai.

All Caddoan languages are severely endangered; Wichita and Kitsai lost their last fully fluent native speakers in the 21st and 20th centuries respectively, while Caddo, Pawnee, and Arikara retain small numbers of speakers and active revitalisation programmes.

Classification

Caddo, spoken in Oklahoma, forms its own branch (Southern Caddoan). The Northern Caddoan branch divides into Wichita and Kitsai, and the Pawnee–Arikara group, whose two languages descend from a single proto-language and were once considered dialects.

Features

Caddoan languages are polysynthetic, building complex words from many morphemes, and are known for intricate verb morphology, pitch accent in some languages, and elaborate consonant alternations that made Arikara historically difficult for outsiders to learn.

History

Speakers of Caddoan languages were agricultural peoples of the Plains — builders of mound centres such as Spiro in the Mississippian world (in the Caddo case) and earth-lodge farmers along the Missouri and its tributaries — and played central roles in Plains trade and diplomacy before displacement to reservations.
