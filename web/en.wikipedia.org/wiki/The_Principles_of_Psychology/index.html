The Principles of Psychology is a two-volume work on psychology by American philosopher and psychologist William James, published in 1890 by Henry Holt and Company. James had contracted to write the book in 1878, and its composition consumed twelve years; the result — some 1,400 pages — fused the laboratory psychology then emerging in Germany, British associationism, French clinical work and James's own pragmatist philosophy into the single most influential textbook in the early history of American psychology. An abridged one-volume edition, Psychology: Briefer Course (1892), known to generations of students as "the Jimmy" (as the Principles was "the James"), carried its ideas far more widely.

Key ideas

The work is remembered for several doctrines that became canonical:

  • The stream of consciousness. James argued that mental life is not a chain of discrete ideas, as associationists held, but a continuous, personal, ever-changing stream, in which "transitive" states of relation and tendency are as real as the "substantive" resting places of thought. The phrase entered the language through this book and later shaped modernist literature.
  • The James–Lange theory of emotion (developed from his 1884 paper): we do not tremble because we are afraid; we feel afraid because we tremble — emotion is the perception of bodily changes triggered by an exciting object.
  • Habit. A celebrated chapter treats habit as the mechanism by which repeated action carves pathways in the nervous system, and urges early formation of useful habits: "The great thing, then, in all education, is to make our nervous system our ally instead of our enemy."
  • The self. James distinguishes the "I" (the knower) from the "Me" (the known self — material, social and spiritual), and proposes a theory of self-esteem as the ratio of success to pretensions.
  • Attention, memory, perception and will are treated in chapters that long served as standard references, including the distinction between primary and secondary memory and the account of the "fringe" of consciousness.

Reception

The Principles was recognised at once as a masterpiece of science writing as much as of psychology; reviewers compared its literary quality to Darwin's. It established James — Harvard's first professor of psychology, though he always styled himself a philosopher — as the leading American psychologist of his generation and served as the founding document of functionalism. Although behaviourism later displaced its introspective method, later psychologists from Gordon Allport to contemporary cognitive scientists have acknowledged its permanent insights, and modern paperback reprints remain in print.
