Ukrainization (Ukrainian: Українізація) refers to policies promoting the Ukrainian language and culture. The term is used principally for (1) the Soviet policy of the 1920s in the Ukrainian SSR, implemented as part of the all-Union policy of korenizatsiya ("indigenisation"), and (2) the language and cultural policies of independent Ukraine, especially after 2014.

Ukrainian-language schooling expanded rapidly during the 1920s Ukrainization campaign.

Soviet Ukrainization, 1923–1933

Adopted in 1923, Ukrainization sought to win the loyalty of the Ukrainian peasantry and intelligentsia by making Ukrainian the language of government, education, and publishing in the Ukrainian SSR. Under People's Commissar of Education Mykola Skrypnyk (from 1927), the share of Ukrainian-language schools rose above 80 per cent, Ukrainian newspapers and book publishing flourished, and administrative staff were required to learn Ukrainian. The policy accompanied a flowering of Ukrainian literature and scholarship known as the "Executed Renaissance" generation.

From the early 1930s the policy was reversed: Skrypnyk was denounced and died by suicide in 1933, Ukrainian cultural figures were arrested and executed, orthographic reforms bringing Ukrainian closer to Russian were imposed, and Russification resumed — coinciding with the Holodomor famine of 1932–33.

Post-independence Ukrainization

After 1991, Ukrainian was confirmed as the sole state language. Language legislation — notably the 2017 education law and the 2019 law "On Ensuring the Functioning of Ukrainian as the State Language" — extended Ukrainian-language requirements in media, services, and public life. Following Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022, surveys recorded a marked voluntary shift toward the use of Ukrainian in everyday life, including among historically Russian-speaking citizens; de-Russification of place names and removal of Russian imperial and Soviet symbols also accelerated.
