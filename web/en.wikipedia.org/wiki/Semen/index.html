Semen

Semen, also known as seminal fluid, is a bodily fluid that contains spermatozoa. It is secreted by the gonads and other sexual organs of male or hermaphroditic animals and can fertilize the female ovum. In humans, seminal fluid contains multiple components besides sperm: proteolytic enzymes and fructose promote the survival of spermatozoa and provide a medium through which they can move or "swim".

Physiology

Human semen is produced by several glands. Spermatozoa, formed in the testes, make up only a few percent of the volume. The bulk of the fluid comes from the seminal vesicles, which contribute fructose-rich alkaline fluid; the prostate gland, which adds a milky fluid containing enzymes such as prostate-specific antigen (PSA), citric acid, and zinc; and the bulbourethral (Cowper's) glands, which secrete a lubricating mucus.

Characteristics

A typical human ejaculate is 2 to 5 millilitres in volume and is slightly alkaline (pH about 7.2–7.8). After ejaculation the semen briefly coagulates and then liquefies, a change driven by prostate enzymes. Semen quality — including sperm concentration, motility, and morphology — is assessed in semen analysis for fertility evaluation; the World Health Organization publishes reference values for these parameters.

Health aspects

Semen can transmit sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, when mucous membranes are exposed. Allergic reactions to seminal plasma, though rare, are also documented. Semen collected from humans and animals is widely used in assisted reproduction and animal breeding through artificial insemination, and can be preserved by cryopreservation.
