Panamá Viejo

Panamá Viejo (English: Old Panama), also written Panama Viejo, is the remaining part of the original Panama City, the oldest European settlement on the Pacific coast of the Americas. It was founded by the Spanish on 15 August 1519 by the conquistador Pedro Arias Dávila, and served as a starting point for expeditions that conquered the Inca Empire in Peru, as well as a transit point for gold and silver being shipped to Spain through the isthmus.

History

The settlement grew into a prosperous city of some 10,000 inhabitants at its height, with a cathedral, convents, a hospital and busy warehouses. It was also a port of the transatlantic slave trade. The city suffered fires and earthquakes over the years, and was repeatedly threatened by pirates and indigenous attacks.

On 28 January 1671, the Welsh privateer Henry Morgan attacked with a force of about 1,400 men; the city was destroyed — whether by a fire ordered by the Spanish governor or set during the sack remains debated — and Panama City was rebuilt and formally established two years later on a new site about 8 kilometres southwest, the present-day Casco Viejo. The original site was abandoned.

The ruins today

The ruins, spread over about 28 hectares, include the cathedral tower — the city's emblem — along with remains of convents, churches, houses and the royal houses. Together with the historic district of the new city, Panamá Viejo was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1997 (extended in 2003). A museum on the site presents the history of the settlement.
