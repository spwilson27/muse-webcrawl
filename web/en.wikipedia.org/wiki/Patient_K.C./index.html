Patient K.C.

Kent Cochrane (1951 – 27 March 2014), widely known in the scientific literature as Patient K.C., was a Canadian man whose extensive memory impairments following a motorcycle accident made him a landmark case study in neuropsychology and the science of memory.

Biography and injury

Cochrane was born in Toronto in 1951. In October 1981, aged 30, he crashed his motorcycle, suffering severe traumatic brain injury. Surgery removed damaged tissue including much of both hippocampi and parts of the medial temporal lobes. He recovered physically but was left with profound amnesia.

Memory profile

K.C. had both severe anterograde amnesia — he could not form new episodic memories — and retrograde amnesia for personal episodes across his whole life. Remarkably, his semantic memory for facts learned before the accident was largely preserved: he knew facts about the world and about his own life as facts, but could not re-experience any personal event. He described his mental state as like being permanently in a moment with no past episodes attached.

Scientific significance

Studied for over three decades, principally by Endel Tulving and colleagues, K.C. provided key evidence for the distinction between episodic and semantic memory, and for the idea that episodic memory supports mental time travel — imagining one's personal future as well as recollecting the past, both of which K.C. could not do. His case complemented that of Patient H.M.
