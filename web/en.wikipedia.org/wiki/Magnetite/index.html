Magnetite is a mineral and one of the main iron ores, with the chemical formula Fe3O4. It is one of the oxides of iron, and is ferrimagnetic; it is attracted to a magnet and can be magnetized to become a permanent magnet itself. Naturally magnetized pieces of magnetite, called lodestone, are the material with which humanity first discovered the property of magnetism. With the exception of extremely rare native iron deposits, magnetite is the most magnetic of all the naturally occurring minerals on Earth.

Occurrence

Magnetite occurs in igneous and metamorphic rocks and in banded iron formations, where it forms one of the principal ore layers. Major deposits are mined in Sweden (Kiruna), Australia, South Africa, Russia, and the United States. It commonly forms octahedral crystals and massive granular aggregates, and it is a frequent constituent of black beach sands.

Properties and uses

Magnetite contains both ferrous (Fe2+) and ferric (Fe3+) iron and has the inverse spinel crystal structure. It is an important ore of iron for steelmaking; because it is magnetic, it can be concentrated from low-grade ores by magnetic separation. Magnetite is also used as a dense aggregate in heavy concrete, as a pigment, as a catalyst in the Haber process, and in ferrofluid and biomedical applications in nanoparticle form.

Biological occurrence

Magnetite crystals occur in the tissues of many organisms, from magnetotactic bacteria — which use chains of magnetite crystals to orient themselves in the Earth's field — to the beaks of homing pigeons and trace amounts in the human brain.
