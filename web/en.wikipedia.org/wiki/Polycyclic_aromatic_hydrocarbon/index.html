Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon

A polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) is a chemical compound composed of two or more fused aromatic rings, containing only carbon and hydrogen. The simplest examples are naphthalene (two rings), anthracene and phenanthrene (three rings); larger members include pyrene, chrysene, benzo[a]pyrene and coronene.

Sources

PAHs are formed by the incomplete combustion of organic matter. Natural sources include forest fires and volcanoes; anthropogenic sources include coal and wood burning, vehicle exhaust, tobacco smoke, industrial processes and charred or grilled food. They are found in coal tar, crude oil and creosote, and spread widely through air, soil and water.

Health and environment

Several PAHs are known or suspected carcinogens. Benzo[a]pyrene, classified by the IARC as carcinogenic to humans, is metabolised in the body to a diol epoxide that binds to DNA. Occupational exposure historically caused scrotal cancer in chimney sweeps, one of the first recognised occupational cancers. Because PAHs persist in sediments and bioaccumulate, regulators monitor a standard set — the US EPA's 16 priority PAHs — in environmental and food testing.

Beyond Earth

Spectroscopic evidence indicates PAHs are abundant in the interstellar medium, where they produce characteristic infrared emission bands, and they have been detected in meteorites and comets. See also aromatic hydrocarbon.
