Learning curve

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A learning curve is a graphical representation of the relationship between learning and experience, showing how proficiency at a task increases (or the cost of producing a unit decreases) as practice or production accumulates. The concept was first described quantitatively by Hermann Ebbinghaus in 1885 in the context of memory, and was applied to manufacturing by Theodore Paul Wright in 1936, who found that the unit cost of building aircraft fell by a constant percentage each time cumulative production doubled.

Forms

Learning curves typically follow a power law: performance improves rapidly at first and then more slowly, approaching an asymptote. A "steep" learning curve in common usage—often used to mean a task is difficult to learn—mathematically indicates rapid progress once learning begins. In economics, the related experience curve describes cost declines across an entire industry, and Wright's law has been used to model cost reductions in technologies such as solar photovoltaic modules and lithium-ion batteries.

Applications

Learning curves are used in education, psychology, industrial engineering, economics, and machine learning (where "learning curve" plots model performance against training-set size). Unit man-hours, defect rates, and production costs are routinely forecast using learning rates, commonly in the range of 70–95%.
