Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD or A1AD) is a genetic disorder that may result in lung disease or liver disease. It is caused by mutations in the SERPINA1 gene, which encodes the protein alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT), a protease inhibitor produced mainly in the liver whose principal function is to protect the lungs from the enzyme neutrophil elastase.

Signs and symptoms

In the lungs, deficiency of AAT permits elastase to destroy alveolar walls, producing panacinar emphysema, typically with onset of breathlessness and wheezing between the ages of 25 and 50, markedly worsened by cigarette smoking. In the liver, accumulation of misfolded mutant AAT protein within hepatocytes can cause neonatal jaundice, hepatitis, cirrhosis, and an increased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma. A skin condition, panniculitis, occurs rarely.

Genetics

The disorder is inherited in an autosomal codominant fashion. The normal allele is designated PiM; the most common deficient alleles are PiS and PiZ. Individuals with the PiZZ genotype have the most severe deficiency, with circulating AAT levels around 10–15% of normal. AATD is one of the most common serious hereditary disorders in people of Northern European descent, yet it remains substantially underdiagnosed.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by measurement of the serum AAT level together with phenotyping or genotyping. Treatment of lung disease resembles that of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease generally: smoking cessation is paramount, inhaled bronchodilators are used, and intravenous augmentation therapy with pooled human AAT is available in some countries for selected patients with emphysema. Liver disease is managed supportively; transplantation is the definitive treatment for advanced liver failure.
