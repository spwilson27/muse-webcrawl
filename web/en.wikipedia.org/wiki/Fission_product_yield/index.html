Fission product yield

Nuclear fission splits a heavy nucleus into two, and sometimes three, lighter nuclei, the fission products, releasing energy and neutrons. The fission product yield is the fraction of fission events that produce a given nuclide, usually expressed as a percentage per fission. Yields are conventionally given either as the independent yield of a nuclide as formed directly in fission, or as the cumulative yield after radioactive decay of its precursors.

Mass yield curve

For thermal-neutron fission of uranium-235, the yield curve plotted against mass number is a distinctive double-humped ("camel") curve: fission is asymmetric, with peaks near mass numbers 95 and 137, corresponding to fragments bound by the particularly stable proton and neutron shells near tin (Z = 50) and the N = 82 neutron shell. The deep valley between the peaks, near symmetric fission (mass ~117), has yields roughly a thousand times lower. As the energy of the neutron causing fission increases, the valley fills in and the fission becomes more symmetric.

Significance

Fission product yields determine the composition of spent nuclear fuel and nuclear fallout. High-yield, long-lived products such as caesium-137 and strontium-90 (each with yields near 6% and half-lives of about 30 years) dominate the medium-term radioactivity and heat of spent fuel. Some products, notably xenon-135, are strong neutron absorbers ("reactor poisons") that affect reactor operation. Fission yields are measured experimentally and compiled in evaluated nuclear data libraries used in reactor physics, waste management, safeguards and nuclear forensics.

Chain yields

Because the immediate fission fragments are neutron-rich and beta-decay along isobaric chains toward stability, the cumulative yield of a stable or long-lived nuclide at the end of a chain is approximately the total yield of that mass number. This "chain yield" varies smoothly and is the quantity tabulated for most practical applications.
