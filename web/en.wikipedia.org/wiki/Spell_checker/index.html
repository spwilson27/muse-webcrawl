Spell checker

A spell checker is a software application or feature that flags words in a document that may not be spelled correctly. Spell checkers are standard in word processors, email clients, web browsers and mobile keyboards, typically underlining suspect words and offering suggested corrections.

How it works

At its simplest, a spell checker compares each word in a text against a dictionary of correctly spelled words; words not found are flagged. To suggest corrections, programs use techniques such as edit distance (the number of insertions, deletions, substitutions or transpositions needed to transform one string into another), phonetic algorithms such as Soundex, and word frequency data. Modern systems use context: grammar checkers and statistical or neural language models can detect real-word errors, such as "their" for "there", which dictionary lookup alone cannot catch.

History

Spell checking programs appeared on mainframe computers in the 1970s; one of the earliest was SPELL at Stanford. The Unix program spell, written by Stephen C. Johnson and others at Bell Labs, popularised the approach, and ispell and its successors aspell and hunspell became widely used in open-source software.

Limitations

Spell checkers struggle with proper names, technical vocabulary, neologisms and homophones, and may produce misleading corrections — a phenomenon known as the Cupertino effect, named after cases where "cooperation" was automatically replaced by "Cupertino".
