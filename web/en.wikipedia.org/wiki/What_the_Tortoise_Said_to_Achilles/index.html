"What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" is a brief philosophical dialogue by Lewis Carroll, published in the journal Mind in 1895. Drawing on Zeno's paradox of Achilles and the tortoise, it presents a foundational puzzle for logic: the distinction between the premises of an argument and the rules of inference used to draw conclusions from them.

The dialogue

Achilles has caught up with the Tortoise and shows him Euclid's First Proposition in the form of premises A ("things that are equal to the same are equal to each other") and B ("the two sides of this triangle are things equal to the same") and the conclusion Z ("the two sides of this triangle are equal to each other"). The Tortoise accepts A and B but refuses to accept Z until Achilles writes down a further hypothetical premise, C: "if A and B are true, Z must be true."

Achilles complies — but the Tortoise then accepts A, B, and C while still withholding Z, demanding a further premise D stating that if A, B and C are true, Z must be true. The notebook fills with an endless sequence of premises, and the conclusion is never reached.

Significance

The dialogue dramatises an infinite regress: a rule of inference such as modus ponens cannot itself simply be added as another premise, for the new premise would still have to be applied. Logicians and philosophers — including Bertrand Russell and later commentators such as Peter Winch and Timothy Smiley — have used the piece to argue that rules of inference belong to the practice of reasoning rather than to its stock of truths, a point echoed in Wittgenstein's rule-following considerations. The story is frequently reprinted in collections on the philosophy of logic.
