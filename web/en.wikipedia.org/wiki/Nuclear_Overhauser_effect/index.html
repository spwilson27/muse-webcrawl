Nuclear Overhauser effect

The nuclear Overhauser effect (NOE) is the transfer of nuclear spin polarization from one population of spin-active nuclei to another via cross-relaxation. It is a phenomenon observed in nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, first predicted by Albert Overhauser in 1953 for electrons and extended to nuclei shortly afterwards.

Principle

When one resonance is saturated by irradiation, the intensities of resonances of nuclei close in space (typically within about 5 ångströms) change, because the two spins relax one another through dipole–dipole coupling. The magnitude of the effect depends on the inverse sixth power of the internuclear distance, making the NOE a sensitive molecular ruler for short distances.

Applications

In organic chemistry, one-dimensional NOE difference experiments help assign stereochemistry and conformation. In structural biology, the two-dimensional NOESY experiment (nuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy) generates cross-peaks between protons that are close in space, providing the distance restraints used to calculate three-dimensional structures of proteins and nucleic acids in solution. Related experiments include ROESY (rotating-frame NOE) for medium-sized molecules and transferred NOE for ligand binding studies.

Steady-state and transient NOE

In the steady-state NOE, one signal is saturated for a long period and the enhancement of another is measured; in transient experiments such as NOESY, magnetisation is transferred during a mixing time, and build-up curves can be converted into approximate interproton distances.
