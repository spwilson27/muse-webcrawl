Freezing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia · For other uses, see Freezing (disambiguation).

Freezing, or solidification, is a phase transition in which a liquid turns into a solid when its temperature is lowered to or below its freezing point. For most substances the freezing point coincides with the melting point: pure water freezes at 0 °C (32 °F, 273.15 K) at standard atmospheric pressure. During freezing, a substance releases its latent heat of fusion, which is why the temperature of a freezing mixture stays constant until solidification is complete. Almost all known liquids freeze; the notable exception is liquid helium, which remains liquid under its own vapour pressure down to absolute zero and solidifies only under applied pressure.

Process

Freezing usually begins with nucleation: the formation of tiny crystalline seeds, either spontaneously in the bulk liquid (homogeneous nucleation) or around impurities and surfaces (heterogeneous nucleation). In the absence of nucleation sites a liquid can be cooled below its freezing point without solidifying — a metastable state called supercooling — after which freezing, once triggered, proceeds rapidly and the temperature rebounds to the freezing point. As crystals grow, dissolved substances are largely excluded from the solid, so the remaining liquid becomes more concentrated and its freezing point falls (freezing-point depression), an effect exploited in freeze distillation and responsible for the relative freshness of sea ice compared with seawater.

Effects and applications

Water is unusual in expanding by about 9 percent on freezing, because ice is less dense than liquid water; this bursts pipes and containers, drives frost weathering of rock, and makes ice float, insulating lakes so aquatic life survives winter. Freezing is a cornerstone of food preservation: commercial freezing (pioneered by Clarence Birdseye in the 1920s) halts microbial growth and slows chemical change, while modern blast, plate, and cryogenic freezers minimise ice-crystal damage to food texture. Related techniques include freeze-drying (lyophilisation), cryopreservation of biological specimens, and the use of freezing in medicine (cryosurgery) and industry.
