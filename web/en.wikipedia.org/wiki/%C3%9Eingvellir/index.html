Þingvellir (Icelandic: [ˈθiŋkˌvɛtlɪr̥], anglicised as Thingvellir) is a national park in the municipality of Bláskógabyggð in southwestern Iceland, about 40 km northeast of the capital Reykjavík. It is a site of historical, cultural and geological significance and is one of the most popular tourist destinations in Iceland, forming part of the Golden Circle route.

History

Þingvellir was the site of the Althing, the national parliament of Iceland, which was founded there in 930 AD. Sessions of the assembly were held at the site until 1798. Chieftains and their followers travelled from across the country to gather on the plains, where the Law Speaker recited the laws from the Law Rock (Lögberg). The location was chosen in part because of its accessibility and the availability of water and grazing. When Iceland came under Norwegian rule in 1262, the assembly continued in a reduced form, and the site later served as a court venue into the 18th century.

The park was established in 1930, coinciding with the 1,000th anniversary of the Althing. In 2004 it was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site for its cultural landscape and its testimony to the medieval Icelandic Commonwealth.

Geology

Þingvellir lies in a rift valley created by the drifting apart of the North American and Eurasian tectonic plates along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. The valley floor is cut by numerous fissures, the largest of which is Almannagjá, a canyon through which the Öxará river was diverted in the early days of the settlement. Another notable fissure, Silfra, is filled with exceptionally clear glacial meltwater and is a popular site for snorkelling and diving between the two continental plates. Lake Þingvallavatn, Iceland's largest natural lake, borders the park to the south.

Features

  • Lögberg – the Law Rock, where the Law Speaker presided over the Althing.
  • Öxarárfoss – a waterfall on the Öxará river at the head of Almannagjá.
  • Silfra – a water-filled fissure known for its visibility of around 100 metres.
  • Þingvallakirkja – a small church on the site, the present building dating from 1859.
  • Drekkingarhylur – the "drowning pool" used historically for executions.
