Haleem (Urdu: حلیم) is a type of stew widely popular in the Middle East, Central Asia, and the Indian subcontinent. Although the dish varies by region, it invariably consists of wheat or barley, meat (usually beef or mutton, sometimes chicken), lentils and spices, slow-cooked for many hours and pounded or blended into a thick paste-like consistency. It is especially associated with the holy month of Ramadan and with Muharram, and in Hyderabad, India, its Ramadan version is so celebrated that "Hyderabadi haleem" has been granted a Geographical Indication (GI) tag.

History

Haleem descends from harees (also written harisa), an ancient dish of pounded wheat and meat mentioned in Arabic cookbooks as early as the 10th century (Ibn Sayyar al-Warraq's Kitab al-Tabikh) and reputed to have been a favourite of the Prophet Muhammad. The dish spread with Arab traders and Muslim rule across Persia and into South Asia, where it acquired lentils, locally grown spices and regional variations during the Mughal period and in the courtly cuisine of Hyderabad under the Nizams. Hyderabadi tradition traces its haleem to the twentieth-century kitchens of the Nizam's era, where it became the standard dish with which to break the Ramadan fast.

Preparation

Haleem is traditionally cooked in large cauldrons over a wood fire for seven to twelve hours. Meat is stewed with cracked wheat, barley and lentils (chana dal, urad dal and others), ginger, garlic, turmeric, red chilli, cumin, coriander and other spices, then pounded with wooden beaters until the meat fibres dissolve into the grains, giving the dish its characteristic glutinous texture. It is served garnished with fried onions (birista), julienned ginger, fresh coriander and mint, green chillies, lemon wedges and a drizzle of ghee. Variants include khichra (a less-blended cousin in which the meat remains in pieces) and vegetarian versions made for those who do not eat meat.

Regional varieties

  • Hyderabadi haleem – made with goat meat and pounded wheat, GI-tagged in 2010 as the first non-vegetarian Indian dish to receive the status; prepared at famous outlets such as Pista House during Ramadan.
  • Pakistani haleem – commonly made with beef or chicken and a mix of lentils, eaten year-round as a main course with naan.
  • Iranian and Arab harees/harisa – simpler wheat-and-meat porridge, often sweetened or seasoned mildly.
  • Bangladeshi haleem – sold as a street food and Ramadan staple, often with a thinner consistency and topped with boiled egg.

Cultural significance

Because it is nutritious, filling and easy to share, haleem is a communal food: mosques and charities distribute it at iftar during Ramadan, and in Hyderabad the Ramadan haleem industry employs thousands seasonally. Its slow-cooked, one-pot character makes it a symbol of patience and of shared religious festivity across South Asian Muslim communities.
