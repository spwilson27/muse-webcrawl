Fly tying

Fly tying
PurposeCreating artificial flies for fly fishing
ToolsVise, bobbin, scissors, hackle pliers, whip finisher
MaterialsFeathers, fur, hair, thread, synthetic fibers, hooks

Fly tying is the process of producing an artificial fly used by fly fishing anglers to catch fish. A tier secures a hook in a vise and binds materials—feathers, fur, hair, thread, and synthetics—to it with fine thread, constructing an imitation of an insect, baitfish, or other prey item. Fly tying is practiced both as a practical means of supplying flies and as a craft hobby in its own right.

Tools and materials

The basic tools are a vise to hold the hook, a bobbin to dispense thread, sharp scissors, hackle pliers, and a whip-finish tool for the final knot. Traditional materials include rooster hackle feathers, deer and elk hair, rabbit and dubbing furs, peacock herl, and silk or nylon thread; modern tiers also use foam, rubber legs, flash fibers, and UV-cure resins.

Fly types

Patterns are broadly grouped as dry flies, which float on the surface to imitate adult insects; nymphs and wet flies, fished below the surface to imitate immature stages; streamers, which imitate baitfish and leeches; and saltwater and bass patterns. Famous patterns include the Adams, Elk Hair Caddis, Woolly Bugger, Hare's Ear, and Pheasant Tail nymph.

History

Descriptions of dressed hooks date to late antiquity, and Juliana Berners' 15th-century Treatyse on Fysshynge wyth an Angle lists fly patterns. The craft was codified in the 19th century by writers such as Frederic M. Halford for dry flies and G. E. M. Skues for nymphs, and expanded greatly in the 20th century with new materials and regional schools of design.
