Archaeoceti ("ancient whales"), or Zeuglodontes in older literature, is a paraphyletic group of primitive cetaceans that lived from the Early Eocene to the late Oligocene, roughly 53 to 23 million years ago. They represent the transition of whales from land-dwelling, even-toed ungulate ancestors to fully aquatic marine predators, and they include the direct ancestors of modern baleen and toothed whales (Neoceti).

Evolution and anatomy

Archaeocetes are united by primitive features retained from their terrestrial ancestors: heterodont dentition with distinct incisors, canines, premolars and multi-cusped molars; well-developed hind limbs in the earliest forms; and nostrils positioned partway along the snout rather than at the top of the head as in modern whales. Their ears already show the characteristic cetacean involucrum of the auditory bulla, a feature used to identify even fragmentary fossils as whales.

Families

Five families are generally recognized. The Pakicetidae, such as Pakicetus from Pakistan, were wolf-sized terrestrial or freshwater animals of the early Eocene. The Ambulocetidae included Ambulocetus natans, the "walking whale", a large amphibious predator. The Remingtonocetidae were long-snouted forms of the Indo-Pakistani seas. The Protocetidae, including Rodhocetus and Maiacetus, spread across the world's oceans while retaining functional hind limbs. Finally the Basilosauridae — among them the serpentine, 15–18 m Basilosaurus and the smaller Dorudon — were fully aquatic, with tiny, externally reduced hind limbs incapable of supporting the body on land.

Extinction

By the end of the Eocene, basilosaurids had given rise to the first members of the crown group Neoceti. The last archaeocetes disappeared in the Oligocene as ocean circulation changed following the opening of the Southern Ocean gateways and the onset of Antarctic glaciation, reshaping the marine ecosystems they inhabited.
