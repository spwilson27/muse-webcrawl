Tie-dye

Tie-dye is a term used for a number of ancient resist-dyeing techniques and the products of those processes. The process of tie-dye typically consists of folding, twisting, pleating or crumpling a fabric or garment and binding with string or rubber bands, followed by the application of dye or dyes. The manipulations of the fabric before the application of dye are called resists, as they partially or completely prevent the applied dye from coloring the fabric.

History

Resist dyeing with bindings is ancient and widespread: early examples survive from pre-Columbian Peru (500–810 AD), and related techniques include shibori in Japan, practiced since the eighth century, bandhani in India, and adire in West Africa. In the United States, tie-dye was popularised in the 1960s counterculture and the hippie movement, aided by the introduction of affordable fibre-reactive dyes such as Procion, and became an emblem of psychedelic style, associated with musicians such as Janis Joplin and the Grateful Dead. It had an earlier American vogue in the 1920s and has repeatedly returned to fashion since.

Techniques

Characteristic patterns include spirals, peace signs, mandalas, stripes and mottled "crumple" effects, produced by different folding and binding methods. Modern craft tie-dye usually uses fibre-reactive dyes fixed with soda ash, which bond permanently to cotton and other cellulose fibres and give the bright, wash-fast colours typical of the style. Variants such as ice dyeing, in which powdered dye is applied over ice and allowed to melt through the fabric, produce watercolour-like effects.
