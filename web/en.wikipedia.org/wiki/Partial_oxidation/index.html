Partial oxidation

Partial oxidation (POX) is a type of chemical reaction that occurs when a substoichiometric fuel–air mixture is partially combusted in a reformer, creating a hydrogen-rich syngas. It is an exothermic process, in contrast to steam reforming, which is endothermic. For a hydrocarbon CnHm, the idealised reaction is CnHm + (n/2) O2 → n CO + (m/2) H2.

Process

In industrial practice, natural gas, heavy oil residues or coal are reacted with a limited amount of oxygen (and often steam) at temperatures of 1,300–1,500 °C and elevated pressure. The Shell Gasification Process and the Texaco (GE) process are widely licensed implementations. Because the reaction is exothermic and fast, partial oxidation reactors are compact and respond quickly to load changes, but they consume oxygen from an air separation plant and produce a syngas with a lower H2/CO ratio than steam reforming.

Applications

The syngas produced is used for hydrogen manufacture, ammonia and methanol synthesis, Fischer–Tropsch liquids, and integrated gasification combined-cycle (IGCC) power generation. Catalytic partial oxidation (CPOX) over noble-metal catalysts offers millisecond contact times and is studied for fuel processing in fuel-cell systems.
