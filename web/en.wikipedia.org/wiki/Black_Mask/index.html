Black Mask was an American pulp magazine founded in 1920 by journalist H. L. Mencken and drama critic George Jean Nathan, initially to help finance their literary magazine The Smart Set. Under editor Joseph T. "Cap" Shaw (1926–1936), it became the cradle of the hardboiled detective school, publishing Dashiell Hammett, Raymond Chandler, Erle Stanley Gardner, and Carroll John Daly, and serialising Hammett's The Maltese Falcon in 1929–30.

History

Mencken and Nathan sold the magazine after only eight issues, and it passed through several publishers before reaching its golden age under Shaw, who demanded lean, realistic crime stories with authentic dialogue. The magazine launched or developed many defining hardboiled characters, including Hammett's Continental Op and Sam Spade, Daly's Race Williams, and Gardner's early stories that preceded Perry Mason. Raymond Chandler published his first short story, "Blackmailers Don't Shoot," in its pages in 1933.

Decline and legacy

Competition from paperback novels, comic books, and rival digests eroded the pulp market after the Second World War; Black Mask shrank in format and ceased publication in 1951. Its clipped prose style profoundly influenced American crime fiction and film noir, and later revivals of the title have appeared as anthologies.
