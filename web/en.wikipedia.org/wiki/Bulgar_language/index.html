Bulgar (also spelled Bolgar, Bulghar, or Bulgaro-Turkic) is an extinct Turkic language of the Oghuric branch, spoken by the Bulgars in the medieval states of Volga Bulgaria and, earlier, in the steppe regions north of the Black and Caspian Seas. It should not be confused with modern Bulgarian, which is a Slavic language that took its name from the Bulgar state founded on the Danube. Bulgar is known from a small corpus of inscriptions, loanwords in neighbouring languages, and names preserved in historical sources.

Classification

Bulgar belongs to the Oghuric (or Lir-Turkic) branch of Turkic, distinguished from Common Turkic by sound correspondences such as r where Common Turkic has z, and l where Common Turkic has sh (rhotacism and lambdacism). The only living language of this branch is Chuvash, spoken in the middle Volga region, which is generally considered to descend from a language closely related to — or a continuation of — Volga Bulgar.

Sources

Evidence for Bulgar is fragmentary. It includes Bulgar words and titles recorded by Byzantine, Arab, and Persian authors; loanwords in Hungarian, Slavic languages, and the languages of the Volga region; and, most importantly, the tombstone inscriptions of Volga Bulgaria from the 13th and 14th centuries, written in Arabic script. These epitaphs, deciphered by scholars of Turkic epigraphy, provide the bulk of the attested vocabulary and grammar.

Danube Bulgar

The Bulgars who founded the First Bulgarian Empire on the Danube in the 7th century were gradually Slavicised, and their Turkic language left few traces beyond names, titles (such as khan and boyar-related terms), and a handful of inscriptions and calendar terms. The so-called Nominalia of the Bulgarian khans preserves Bulgar month names that have been used to reconstruct aspects of the Bulgar calendar.

Volga Bulgar

Volga Bulgaria, centred on the middle Volga and Kama rivers, adopted Islam in the early 10th century and flourished as a trading state until the Mongol invasions of the 1230s. Volga Bulgar remained in use for some time under the Golden Horde, as the tombstone corpus shows, before being displaced by Kipchak Turkic (the ancestor of Tatar) by roughly the 14th century. The Chuvash, who preserved an Oghuric language in the same region, are often linked by scholars to a Bulgar-speaking population.
