The 24 Hours of Le Mans (French: 24 Heures du Mans) is an endurance motor race held annually near Le Mans, France, since 1923. It is the world's oldest active endurance race and, with the Indianapolis 500 and the Monaco Grand Prix, forms the informal Triple Crown of Motorsport.

Format

Teams of three drivers per car compete over 24 hours on the 13.626 km Circuit de la Sarthe, a mix of public roads and dedicated track. The winner is the car covering the greatest distance in 24 hours. Classes have included top-tier prototypes — now Hypercars — and grand tourers.

History

Organised by the Automobile Club de l'Ouest, the race emphasised reliability and efficiency as much as speed, spurring innovations such as disc brakes, aerodynamic bodywork, and hybrid powertrains. The 1955 race was marred by the worst accident in motorsport history, when Pierre Levegh's car crashed into the crowd, killing over 80 spectators. Famous eras include Bentley's 1920s wins, the Ford–Ferrari duel of the 1960s, Porsche's dominance, Audi's diesel and hybrid era, and Toyota's wins before Ferrari's return to overall victory in 2023–2024.

Traditions

Traditions include the Le Mans start (used until 1969), the winner's podium champagne spray popularised by Dan Gurney in 1967, and the French tricolour waved to start the race. The race typically takes place in mid-June, with night driving a defining challenge.
