Choriocarcinoma


Placeholder image: histology of choriocarcinoma

Choriocarcinoma is a highly malignant cancer of trophoblastic origin. Most cases are gestational, arising in the uterus in association with a pregnancy, a hydatidiform mole, a miscarriage or a normal delivery; non-gestational choriocarcinoma can also arise in the ovary or testis as a component of a germ cell tumour. The tumour is notable for its early and widespread blood-borne metastasis, and for its secretion of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), which serves as a sensitive tumour marker.

Signs and symptoms

Gestational choriocarcinoma typically presents with abnormal vaginal bleeding after a pregnancy or molar evacuation. Because the tumour invades blood vessels early, metastases to the lungs (causing cough and haemoptysis), vagina, liver and brain are common at presentation.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on a markedly elevated and rising serum hCG level in the appropriate clinical setting, together with imaging. Histology shows sheets of syncytiotrophoblast and cytotrophoblast without chorionic villi, with prominent haemorrhage and necrosis.

Treatment

Choriocarcinoma was one of the first cancers to be cured by chemotherapy. In the 1950s, Min Chiu Li and colleagues at the National Cancer Institute demonstrated complete remissions using methotrexate, a landmark in medical oncology. Low-risk gestational disease is treated with single-agent methotrexate or actinomycin D; high-risk or metastatic disease is treated with combination regimens such as EMA-CO (etoposide, methotrexate, actinomycin D, cyclophosphamide and vincristine). Cure rates exceed 90% overall, and fertility can usually be preserved.
