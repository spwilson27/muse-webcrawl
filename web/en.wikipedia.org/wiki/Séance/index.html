A séance or seance (/ˈseɪ.ɑːns/; French: [seɑ̃s]) is an attempt to communicate with spirits. The word means "session" in French and derives from the Old French seoir, "to sit". In English, the word came to be used specifically for a meeting of people who gather to receive messages from ghosts or to listen to a spirit medium discourse with or relay messages from spirits.

History

Séances became a widespread social and religious practice with the rise of modern Spiritualism, conventionally dated to the Fox sisters' "Hydesville rappings" of 1848 in New York. Victorian and Edwardian séances typically took place in darkened parlours around a table, with sitters holding hands while a paid medium produced phenomena: table-turning and rapping, automatic writing, trance speech, spirit trumpets, and materialisations of ectoplasm. Interest surged again after the First World War, when many bereaved families sought contact with the dead; Arthur Conan Doyle was a famous public advocate, while his friend Harry Houdini devoted years to exposing fraudulent mediums.

Scepticism and fraud

Scientific investigators and stage magicians demonstrated that many séance phenomena could be duplicated by trickery — concealed accomplices, thread, telescopic rods, and the ideomotor effect behind table-turning, which Michael Faraday demonstrated experimentally in 1853. Believers reply that genuine mediumship survives alongside fraud. The séance persists today in Spiritualist churches, private circles, and popular culture, including the use of Ouija boards.
