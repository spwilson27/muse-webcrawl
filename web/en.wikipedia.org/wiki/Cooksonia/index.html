Cooksonia is an extinct group of primitive land plants, treated as a genus, although probably not a natural (monophyletic) grouping. The earliest Cooksonia date from the middle of the Silurian period, around 433 million years ago; the group continued into the Early Devonian. Cooksonia fossils are distributed globally, but most specimens come from Britain, where the genus was first discovered.

The genus was named in honour of Isabel Cookson, an Australian botanist and collaborator of William H. Lang, who described the genus in 1937.

Description

Known species were small, only a few centimetres tall. Their stems (axes) were leafless, smooth and branched dichotomously (dividing into two equal branches). At the tips of the branches sat sporangia — spore-forming structures — which in most species were flattened discs or kidney-shaped, distinguishing Cooksonia from earlier spore producers. Some specimens preserve a central strand of water-conducting tissue, making Cooksonia pertoni among the earliest known plants with xylem, i.e. a true vascular plant; other species may have had only a simpler conducting strand or none, and stomata have been reported in at least one species.

Significance

Cooksonia is widely regarded as one of the earliest land plants and a transitional form between the bryophytes and the vascular plants. Its simple body plan — upright branching axes with terminal sporangia — became the template from which more complex Devonian plants evolved, including those with true roots, leaves and wood.
