The Waitangi Tribunal (Māori: Te Rōpū Whakamana i te Tiriti o Waitangi) is a standing New Zealand commission of inquiry established under the Treaty of Waitangi Act 1975. It inquires into claims by Māori that acts or omissions of the Crown were contrary to the principles of the Treaty of Waitangi — the founding document signed in 1840 between the British Crown and Māori chiefs — and recommends remedies. Its findings, with limited exceptions, are recommendatory rather than binding on the government.

History and powers

As created in 1975, the Tribunal could consider only claims arising after 1975. Amendments in 1985 extended its jurisdiction back to 1840, opening the way to the great historical inquiries into land confiscation (raupatu), the loss of rivers, fisheries, and other resources. Claims are heard by panels of three to seven members drawn from a membership of up to twenty, appointed for their knowledge of Māori culture, law, and history; hearings are commonly held on marae, and proceedings are conducted with both legal formality and tikanga (Māori custom).

Major inquiries and reports

Landmark early reports included the Motunui claim (1983), the Manukau Harbour report (1985), and the Te Reo Māori report (1986), which found the Māori language to be a taonga (treasure) protected by the Treaty and led to te reo becoming an official language. The Tribunal's reports provide the evidential basis for the historical Treaty settlements negotiated by the Office of Treaty Settlements, through which iwi receive apologies, financial redress, and return of significant sites. The multi-year district inquiry programme and the kaupapa (thematic) inquiries — such as the Health Services and Outcomes inquiry (Wai 2575) — continue into the twenty-first century. By the 2020s more than 2,500 claims had been registered with the Tribunal.
