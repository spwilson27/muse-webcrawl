Polynomial

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The graph of a cubic polynomial curve crossing the x-axis three times]

In mathematics, a polynomial is a mathematical expression consisting of indeterminates (variables) and coefficients, involving only addition, subtraction, multiplication, and non-negative integer exponents of variables — for example, 3x² − 2x + 5.

Polynomials are classified by degree (the highest exponent) and number of terms (monomial, binomial, trinomial). They are among the simplest and most useful functions: smooth everywhere, easy to compute and differentiate, and dense in the continuous functions (Weierstrass approximation theorem).

Roots and factorisation

Finding a polynomial's roots (where it equals zero) drove centuries of algebra: quadratic formula in antiquity, cubic and quartic formulas in the 16th century (Cardano, Ferrari), and the Abel–Ruffini theorem (1824) proving no general radical formula exists for degree five or higher. The fundamental theorem of algebra guarantees every degree-n complex polynomial has exactly n complex roots counted with multiplicity (proved by Gauss, 1799).

Polynomial division, the factor and remainder theorems, and (over the reals) partial-fraction methods are school-level staples; computationally, polynomial arithmetic and interpolation support error-correcting codes and cryptography.

Applications

Taylor polynomials approximate functions; splines piece polynomials together for curves in design and animation; orthogonal polynomial families (Legendre, Chebyshev, Hermite) pervade physics and numerical analysis.
