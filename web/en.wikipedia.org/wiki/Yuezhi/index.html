Yuezhi

The Yuezhi migrated from the Gansu corridor to Bactria in the 2nd century BC.

The Yuezhi (Chinese: 月氏; pinyin: Yuèzhī) were an ancient people first described in Chinese sources as nomadic pastoralists living in a grassland region in the far west of modern Gansu Province in China, between the Qilian Mountains and Dunhuang. After being defeated by the Xiongnu in the 2nd century BC, the majority of the Yuezhi migrated westward across Central Asia and eventually settled in Bactria, where one of their clans, the Kushans, founded the Kushan Empire.

Origins and defeat by the Xiongnu

Chinese accounts describe the Yuezhi as prosperous and powerful traders — they were associated with the supply of jade to early Chinese states — until a series of conflicts with the Xiongnu. Around 176 BC the Xiongnu under Modu Chanyu inflicted a major defeat on them, and under Modu's successor the Yuezhi king was killed and his skull reportedly made into a drinking cup. These defeats drove the main body of the Yuezhi, known as the Greater Yuezhi, on a long migration westward; a smaller group, the Lesser Yuezhi, moved south into the Tibetan borderlands.

Migration and Bactria

The westward migration is documented in the accounts of the Chinese envoy Zhang Qian, who set out in 138 BC to seek a Yuezhi alliance against the Xiongnu and reached them more than a decade later. The Yuezhi displaced the Sakas from the Ili valley, then moved south in turn, crossing the Oxus (Amu Darya) and conquering Bactria around 130 BC. By Zhang Qian's visit they had settled north of the Oxus with their court there, ruling Bactria through a system of five chiefdoms (xihou). About a century later, the Kushan chiefdom under Kujula Kadphises united the five and founded the Kushan Empire, which came to rule much of Central Asia and northern India.

Identity and legacy

The ethnic and linguistic identity of the Yuezhi remains debated. Many scholars associate them with the Tocharians of the Tarim Basin or with speakers of Indo-European languages; in Greco-Roman sources, peoples connected with the conquest of Bactria appear under names such as the Tochari and Asii. Whatever their precise origins, the Yuezhi migration reshaped the political map of ancient Central Asia and opened the routes that became the Silk Road.
