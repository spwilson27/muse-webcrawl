Mediterranean Basin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The Mediterranean Sea and the lands surrounding it

The Mediterranean Basin, also known as the Mediterranean region, is the region of lands around the Mediterranean Sea that have mostly a Mediterranean climate, with mild to cool, rainy winters and warm to hot, dry summers. The region is home to characteristic Mediterranean vegetation, including evergreen scrublands known as maquis or chaparral.

The basin spans parts of three continents — Southern Europe, Western Asia and North Africa — and has been a crossroads of trade, migration and empire since antiquity, hosting Phoenician, Greek, Roman and many other civilisations.

Geography and climate

The Mediterranean climate is characterised by dry summers and wet winters, a regime produced by the seasonal shift of subtropical high-pressure systems. Similar climates occur in only a few other parts of the world, including California, central Chile, the Western Cape of South Africa and southwestern Australia, and these regions share many plant families adapted to summer drought and fire, making the Mediterranean Basin one of the world's recognised biodiversity hotspots.

Biodiversity

The Mediterranean Basin is one of the world's most significant centres of plant diversity and endemism, with tens of thousands of plant species, roughly half of them found nowhere else. Intensive human use over millennia, together with modern urbanisation, tourism and climate change, places heavy pressure on its ecosystems.

History

As the heartland of classical antiquity, the basin saw the rise of some of the earliest complex societies, the spread of agriculture from the Fertile Crescent, and the Roman Empire's unification of the entire sea — called by the Romans Mare Nostrum, "our sea". In later centuries the basin was contested by Byzantine, Arab, Ottoman and European powers and remained a central artery of world trade.
