Manila galleon trade
RouteManila (Philippines) ↔ Acapulco (New Spain)
Active1565–1815
ShipsSpanish galleons, typically one or two sailings per year

The Manila galleons (Spanish: Galeón de Manila) were Spanish trading ships that sailed the Pacific Ocean between Manila in the Philippines and Acapulco in New Spain (Mexico) for two and a half centuries, from 1565 to 1815. The route, pioneered by Andrés de Urdaneta, who discovered the return passage (the tornaviaje) using the North Pacific gyre, formed the first permanent link in a truly global trading network.

The trade

Eastbound galleons carried Chinese silk, porcelain, ivory, spices and other Asian goods, purchased in Manila from Chinese merchants, to Acapulco, where they were sold at a great trade fair and carried overland to Veracruz for shipment to Spain. Westbound ships carried principally Mexican and Peruvian silver, which flowed through Manila into China, fuelling the Chinese silver economy. The trade also exchanged plants, animals and people, spreading American crops such as maize, sweet potato and chilli to Asia and Asian goods and techniques to the Americas.

Voyages and losses

The eastbound crossing, riding trade winds south of Hawaii, took about three months; the return via the northern Kuroshio current could take six months and was notoriously gruelling, with crews suffering scurvy and starvation. Galleons were also lost to storms, wrecked on Pacific coasts, or captured — Thomas Cavendish took the Santa Ana in 1587 and George Anson captured the Nuestra Señora de la Covadonga in 1743. The trade ended in 1815 when Mexican independence disrupted the route; the last galleon reached Manila in 1815.
