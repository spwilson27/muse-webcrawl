Patrick Sarsfield, 1st Earl of Lucan (c. 1655 – 21 August 1693) was an Irish soldier and a leading figure of the Jacobite cause during the Williamite War in Ireland (1689–1691). Celebrated in Irish popular memory as one of the most dashing cavalry commanders of his age, he is best remembered for the daring raid that destroyed the Williamite siege train at Ballyneety in 1690, for his defence of Limerick, and for leading the Flight of the Wild Geese after the Treaty of Limerick in 1691.

Early life

Sarsfield was born at Lucan, County Dublin, into a family of mixed Old English and Gaelic Irish descent. His father, Patrick Sarsfield of Lucan, had lost much of the family estate during the Cromwellian confiscations, though the family recovered part of its property at the Restoration. The younger Patrick was sent to France as a young man and served as an officer in French and English regiments, gaining a reputation for courage and reckless bravery. He fought for Charles II's forces and, under James II, rose in the Irish army; he commanded a troop at the Battle of Sedgemoor in 1685, where he was wounded fighting the Monmouth rebels.

Williamite War in Ireland

When James II landed in Ireland in 1689, Sarsfield became one of his most energetic officers. He sat in the Patriot Parliament of 1689 as member for County Dublin and was promoted rapidly through the Jacobite army. He helped secure Connacht for the Jacobites, capturing Sligo, and was a central figure in the defence of the west of Ireland after the Jacobite defeat at the Battle of the Boyne in July 1690.

The ride to Ballyneety

Sarsfield's most famous exploit came in August 1690, when William III was besieging Limerick. Guided by a deserter remembered as "Galloping Hogan," Sarsfield led a picked force of about 600 horsemen across the River Shannon at Killaloe and through the Silvermine Mountains to Ballyneety, where the Williamite siege train — artillery, pontoons and stores, reportedly including 12,000 barrels of powder — lay lightly guarded. On the night of 10–11 August his men surprised the camp, spiked or destroyed the guns, blew up the ammunition and withdrew almost without loss. The raid forced William to raise the siege weeks later and became the stuff of Irish legend; a popular ballad has William exclaim on hearing the explosion that his men had been ruined by Sarsfield.

Aughrim and the Treaty of Limerick

After the Jacobite defeat at the Battle of Aughrim in July 1691, where the Marquis de St Ruth was killed, Sarsfield rallied the retreating army and organised the second defence of Limerick. As the senior surviving commander he negotiated the Treaty of Limerick, signed on 3 October 1691, whose military articles allowed Jacobite soldiers to sail for France and enter French service, and whose civil articles promised Catholics security of property and worship — promises soon broken by the Irish Parliament, a breach remembered in Ireland as "the broken treaty."

Exile and death

Created Earl of Lucan by the exiled James II, Sarsfield sailed from Ireland in December 1691 with some 14,000 soldiers — the Flight of the Wild Geese — and entered French service as a lieutenant-general. He fought in Flanders during the Nine Years' War and was mortally wounded at the Battle of Landen on 29 July 1693. According to a famous tradition, as he lay dying at Huy he pressed his hand to his wound and cried, "Oh, if this were only for Ireland!" He died on 21 August 1693 and was buried in the church of St Martin at Huy.

Legacy

Sarsfield remains one of the great romantic heroes of Irish nationalist memory. Sarsfield's Bridge and the Treaty Stone in Limerick commemorate the events of 1691, and his name was given to military units and GAA clubs across Ireland. His reputed dying words became a byword for the sacrifices of the Irish military diaspora in continental service.
