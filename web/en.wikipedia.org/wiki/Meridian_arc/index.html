Meridian arc

In geodesy and navigation, a meridian arc is the distance between two points with the same longitude on the surface of the Earth, or more precisely the arc of the meridian ellipse between them. Measurements of meridian arcs over different latitudes historically provided the data needed to determine the shape and size of the Earth, and the difference in latitude between the endpoints is obtained by astronomical observation.

History

Eratosthenes (3rd century BCE) produced an early estimate of Earth's circumference by comparing shadows at Syene and Alexandria. The French Academy of Sciences expeditions of the 1730s — to Peru (modern Ecuador), led by Pierre Bouguer and Charles Marie de La Condamine, and to Lapland, led by Pierre Louis Maupertuis — measured arcs near the equator and near the pole, confirming Newton's prediction that Earth is an oblate spheroid flattened at the poles. During the French Revolution, Delambre and Méchain measured the Dunkirk–Barcelona arc, which was used to define the metre as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator.

Formula

For a reference ellipsoid with semi-major axis a and eccentricity e, the meridional radius of curvature at geodetic latitude φ is M(φ) = a(1 − e²) / (1 − e² sin²φ)3/2, and the meridian arc from the equator to latitude φ is the integral of M over latitude, expressible as an infinite series in powers of e². For the WGS 84 ellipsoid, the quarter meridian (pole to equator) is 10,001,965.729 m. A degree of latitude corresponds to roughly 111 km, varying from about 110.6 km at the equator to 111.7 km near the poles.

Famous arcs

  • Struve Geodetic Arc (1816–1855) — a chain of survey triangulations stretching from Norway to the Black Sea, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
  • Great Trigonometrical Survey of India — measured the "Great Arc" of India under William Lambton and George Everest.
