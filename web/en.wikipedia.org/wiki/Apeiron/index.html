Apeiron

Apeiron (ἄπειρον) is a Greek word meaning "(that which is) unlimited", "boundless", "infinite", or "indefinite", from ἀ- a-, "without", and πεῖραρ peirar, "end, limit".

Apeiron is a cosmological theory created by Anaximander (c. 610 – c. 546 BC), a Pre-Socratic Greek philosopher. According to his theory, apeiron is the principle or origin (archē) of all things: a boundless, indefinite, and eternal substance from which all things arise and to which they return.

Anaximander's doctrine

Anaximander held that the first principle could not be any definite element such as water (the principle proposed by his teacher Thales), because no single element could account for its opposites. Instead, he posited the apeiron — something unlimited in extent and duration and indeterminate in kind — which "encompasses all the worlds". From it the opposites (hot and cold, wet and dry) separate out, producing the cosmos; things perish back into the apeiron "according to necessity, for they pay penalty and retribution to each other for their injustice according to the assessment of Time", in the one surviving fragment attributed to him.

Later use

The term was discussed extensively by Aristotle in his Physics, where it is treated in connection with the infinite. In later thought the apeiron has been variously interpreted, and it influenced subsequent ideas of an indeterminate underlying reality, including the Platonic receptacle and, in modern comparisons, concepts such as the quantum vacuum.
