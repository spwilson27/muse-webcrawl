Mesta

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Mesta (Spanish: Honrado Concejo de la Mesta de Pastores, "Honourable Council of the Mesta of Shepherds") was a powerful association of sheep ranchers in the medieval Crown of Castile. It was established in 1273 by Alfonso X, who brought together existing local associations of migratory shepherds, and it survived, with gradually diminishing power, until its dissolution in 1836.

Transhumance and privileges

The Mesta organised the transhumance of millions of merino sheep between summer pastures in the northern mountains and winter grazing in the southern plains of Extremadura, La Mancha, and Andalusia, along protected drove roads called cañadas. Its royal privileges included exemption of flocks from local tolls and grazing dues, legal protection of the cañadas against enclosure, and its own itinerant magistrates (alcaldes entregadores) to settle disputes. In return, wool — Spain's premier export, much of it shipped to Flanders and Italy — yielded the Crown substantial and reliable tax revenue, which helps explain royal support for the pastoralists against farming interests.

Conflicts and decline

The Mesta's privileges made it a perennial enemy of settled farmers, whose crops were damaged by migrating flocks and who resented the legal presumptions favouring sheep-owners, and of towns that sought to enclose common lands. Historians have debated the Mesta's role in retarding Spanish agriculture and contributing to deforestation and soil erosion. From the 16th century onward its membership and power waned as wool prices, market conditions, and royal policies changed; the liberal governments of the 19th century abolished it in 1836, replacing it with a general stockbreeders' association.
