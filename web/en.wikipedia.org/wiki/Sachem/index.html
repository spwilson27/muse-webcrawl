Sachem

Sachem
OriginAlgonquian languages of New England
MeaningChief or leader of a Native American tribe or confederation
Related termSagamore (often a lesser chief)

A sachem was the chief or leader of a Native American tribe or confederation, particularly among the Algonquian-speaking peoples of New England. The word derives from an Eastern Algonquian term (compare Massachusett sâchem) and was adopted into colonial English usage in the 17th century.

A sachem led by persuasion and consensus as much as by command.

Role

Sachems led communities such as the Massachusett, Wampanoag, Narragansett, Mohegan and Pequot. Their authority combined hereditary succession — which could pass through women as well as men — with the need to maintain the support of councillors and community members. Sachems allocated land use, received tribute, conducted diplomacy and war, and concluded treaties with neighbouring peoples and, later, with European colonists. Well-known sachems in colonial history include Massasoit (Ousamequin) of the Wampanoag, Canonicus of the Narragansett, and Uncas of the Mohegan.

Later usage

The word survived in American place names and institutional titles: in the Tammany Hall political organisation of New York City, the leader was styled the Grand Sachem, and members of its council were called sachems. Some modern tribal governments in New England continue to use the title for their leaders.
