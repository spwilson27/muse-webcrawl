Huronian glaciation
Timingc. 2.45 – 2.22 billion years ago
Eon/eraProterozoic / Palaeoproterozoic (Siderian–Rhyacian)
Named forLake Huron region, Canada

The Huronian glaciation is the oldest and longest known ice age on Earth, occurring roughly 2.45 to 2.22 billion years ago during the Palaeoproterozoic era. It is named for the Huronian Supergroup of rocks north of Lake Huron in Canada, where glacial deposits were first recognised. Glacial sediments of this age are also found in South Africa (the Makganyene diamictite) and Western Australia, indicating glaciation at low palaeolatitudes and suggesting the Earth may have become a "snowball Earth", largely or entirely covered in ice.

Causes

The glaciation coincides with the Great Oxidation Event, when photosynthetic cyanobacteria raised atmospheric oxygen levels. The oxygen would have destroyed atmospheric methane — a potent greenhouse gas — triggering global cooling under the relatively faint young Sun. Volcanic quiescence and weathering of newly emplaced large igneous provinces may have contributed by drawing down carbon dioxide.

Evidence and extent

Evidence includes diamictites (unsorted glacial sediments), dropstones in marine shales, and striated pavements in the Huronian Supergroup (including the Gowganda Formation), the Transvaal Supergroup of South Africa and the Hamersley Range of Australia. Palaeomagnetic data from the Makganyene diamictite indicate deposition near the equator, supporting a global, snowball-scale event.

Subsequent events

At least three glacial episodes are recognised within the Huronian interval. The ice age ended as volcanic carbon dioxide accumulated in the atmosphere during the long volcanic suppression of weathering by ice cover, eventually producing a hothouse rebound. The Huronian predates by more than a billion years the better-known Cryogenian snowball Earth episodes (Sturtian and Marinoan).
