Lambeth Palace is the official London residence of the Archbishop of Canterbury. It is situated in north Lambeth, on the south bank of the River Thames, a short distance upstream of the Palace of Westminster on the opposite shore.

The palace has been the London home of the archbishops for some 800 years. Its oldest surviving parts — the chapel and its crypt — date from the early thirteenth century, while the Great Hall was rebuilt after the Civil War and the residential ranges were remodelled in the nineteenth century by Edward Blore. The palace grounds, including Lambeth Palace Library, occupy about ten acres of gardens among the largest private gardens in London.

History

Archbishops acquired the manor of Lambeth in the late twelfth century, when its position across the river from Westminster made it a convenient base for attending the royal court. Archbishop Stephen Langton consecrated the palace chapel in the early 1200s. During the Peasants' Revolt of 1381 the palace was attacked and Archbishop Simon Sudbury was murdered. The Morton Gatehouse, built around 1490 under Archbishop John Morton, remains the palace's most recognisable entrance.

During the English Civil War the palace was seized and the Great Hall dismantled; after the Restoration, Archbishop William Juxon rebuilt the hall in a conservative Gothic style. The palace hosted the Lambeth Conferences of Anglican bishops from 1867 onwards and was damaged by bombing in the Second World War before restoration.

Lambeth Palace Library

Founded in 1610 by Archbishop Richard Bancroft, the library is one of the earliest public libraries in England and holds the historic collections of the archbishops, including manuscripts such as the Lambeth Apocalypse and the Mac Durnan Gospels. In 2021 the library moved into a purpose-built building at the edge of the palace gardens.

Grounds

The palace gardens, parts of which are open to the public on community days, include a fig tree traditionally said to descend from one planted by Cardinal Pole in the sixteenth century, and extensive planting maintained continuously for centuries.
