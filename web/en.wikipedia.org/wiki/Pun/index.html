Pun

A pun, also known as a paronomasia, is a form of word play that exploits multiple meanings of a term, or of similar-sounding words, for an intended humorous or rhetorical effect. These ambiguities can arise from the intentional use of homophonic (same-sounding), homographic (same-spelled), metonymic, or figurative language. Puns are used in many forms of comedy, literature, advertising, and everyday speech.

Types

  • Homophonic puns use words that sound alike: "Time flies like an arrow; fruit flies like a banana."
  • Homographic puns use words spelled the same but with different meanings.
  • Homonymic puns exploit words that are both spelled and pronounced alike.
  • Compound puns combine several puns in one statement.
  • Visual puns use images or typography to suggest the double meaning.

History

Puns are ancient: they appear in Sumerian and Egyptian texts, in the Hebrew Bible, and in Sanskrit literature, where the technique of śleṣa (double meaning) was highly developed. In Latin rhetoric the figure was called paronomasia. Shakespeare was a prodigious punster, with hundreds of puns across his plays; John Donne and James Joyce also made wordplay central to their work. In Chinese, homophonic puns are embedded in cultural practices, such as giving fish at New Year because the word for fish (yú) sounds like the word for surplus.

Reception

Puns are sometimes dismissed — Samuel Johnson called punning a low form of wit — yet they remain a staple of headlines, jokes, and advertising slogans, and competitions such as the O. Henry Pun-Off World Championships celebrate the form. The characteristic groan response to a pun is itself a recognized social ritual.
