Indium tin oxide

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Indium tin oxide (ITO) is a ternary composition of indium, tin and oxygen in varying proportions. Typically composed of about 74% indium, 18% oxygen and 8% tin by weight, it is the most widely used transparent conducting oxide: thin films of ITO are optically transparent and electrically conductive, a rare combination that makes them essential in displays and touchscreens.

Chemical compositionIn2O3 with ~10 wt% SnO2
AppearancePale yellow to greenish solid; transparent in thin films
Melting point1,800–2,200 °C (sublimes/decomposes)
Electrical resistivity~10−4 Ω·cm (thin film)
Band gap~3.5–4.3 eV

Properties and preparation

ITO is an n-type semiconductor whose conductivity arises from tin dopants and oxygen vacancies donating free electrons. Films are deposited by sputtering, electron-beam evaporation or chemical vapour deposition onto glass or plastic substrates. Typical films 100–300 nm thick transmit more than 80% of visible light while providing sheet resistances of tens of ohms per square.

Uses

ITO coatings are used in liquid-crystal displays, OLEDs, plasma displays, touch panels, smart windows and thin-film solar cells, as well as in aircraft windshields for defrosting, electromagnetic shielding, and antistatic coatings. Because indium is scarce and expensive—ITO consumes the majority of world indium production—research into alternatives such as aluminium-doped zinc oxide, silver nanowires, carbon nanotubes, graphene and conductive polymers is active, though none has fully displaced ITO.

Health concerns

Occupational exposure to indium compounds in ITO production and recycling has been linked to "indium lung", a serious interstitial lung disease, leading to exposure limits and monitoring in manufacturing.
