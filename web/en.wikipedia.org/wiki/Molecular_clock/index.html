Molecular clock


A phylogram whose branch lengths are scaled by molecular-clock divergence times.

The molecular clock is a figurative term for a technique in molecular evolution that uses the mutation rate of biomolecules to deduce the time in prehistory when two or more life forms diverged. The biomolecular data used for such calculations are usually nucleotide sequences for DNA, RNA, or amino acid sequences for proteins.

The idea was first proposed in 1962 by Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling, who noticed that the number of amino acid differences in haemoglobin between different lineages changes roughly linearly with time, as estimated from fossils. They generalised this to the hypothesis that the rate of evolutionary change of any specified protein was approximately constant over time and over different lineages.

Neutral theory

The molecular clock hypothesis provided early support for the neutral theory of molecular evolution, developed by Motoo Kimura, which holds that most mutations are selectively neutral and accumulate at a rate governed mainly by the mutation rate and genetic drift rather than by natural selection.

Use and limitations

Molecular clocks are used in phylogenetics to date speciation events, calibrate trees against fossil evidence, and reconstruct the timing of radiations and biogeographic events. A famous application supported an African origin of modern humans by dating the common ancestor of human mitochondrial DNA ("Mitochondrial Eve").

In practice, substitution rates vary between genes, lineages and time periods, so modern methods use "relaxed clocks" and Bayesian models that allow rates to vary across the tree, anchored by fossil calibrations.
