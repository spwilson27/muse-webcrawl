A rabbi (Hebrew: רַבִּי) is a spiritual leader or religious teacher in Judaism. One becomes a rabbi by being ordained by another rabbi (semikhah) following a course of study of Jewish texts such as the Talmud. The basic form of the rabbi developed in the formative era of Rabbinic Judaism — the era of the Pharisees and the Talmud — when learned teachers gathered students to discuss and debate the application of the Torah.

Historical development

After the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, Judaism reorganised around synagogues, study, and prayer, and the rabbis — the sages of the Mishnah and Talmud — became the custodians of Jewish law (halakha) and tradition. In medieval communities the rabbi functioned as judge, teacher, and legal authority; the title rav was used in Babylonia and rabbi in the Land of Israel. Figures such as Rashi, Maimonides, and the Vilna Gaon shaped Jewish learning through their commentaries and codes.

The modern congregational rabbi — preacher, pastor, and communal leader — emerged in 19th-century Europe and North America, influenced by the Protestant ministry and by the founding of rabbinical seminaries such as Hebrew Union College (1875, Reform), the Jewish Theological Seminary (1886, Conservative), and Berlin's Orthodox seminaries.

Roles and functions

Rabbis typically teach Torah, rule on questions of Jewish law, lead or support synagogue worship, officiate at life-cycle events (weddings, funerals, bar and bat mitzvahs), provide pastoral counselling, and represent the community publicly. In Israel, the state-recognised Chief Rabbinate oversees matters such as marriage, divorce, and kashrut certification. Distinctive roles include the Hasidic rebbe (a dynastic spiritual leader), the dayan (judge on a rabbinical court), and the posek (decisor of halakha).

Women rabbis

Reform, Reconstructionist, and Conservative movements began ordaining women in the late 20th century — Sally Priesand became the first woman ordained by a seminary in 1972 — and women rabbis now serve across the non-Orthodox world. Orthodox ordination of women remains contested, though some institutions have created titles such as maharat for women in clergy roles.
