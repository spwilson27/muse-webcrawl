Concept map

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A concept map or conceptual diagram is a graphical tool for organising and representing knowledge. A concept map typically represents ideas and information as boxes or circles (concepts), connected by arrows or lines labelled with linking words or phrases that specify the relationship between concepts, so that each concept–link–concept triple reads as a proposition ("birds — have — wings").

Development

Concept mapping was developed in the 1970s by Joseph D. Novak and his research team at Cornell University, as a way to represent children's understanding of science concepts, grounded in David Ausubel's theory of meaningful learning. Novak's work held that learning proceeds by assimilating new concepts into existing cognitive structures, and that maps make those structures visible. The CmapTools software, developed at the Institute for Human and Machine Cognition (IHMC), popularised digital concept mapping.

Structure and construction

Concept maps are usually hierarchical, with the most general concept at the top, and are built in response to a focus question. Good maps use labelled linking phrases to form propositions, include concrete examples, and contain cross-links between different branches, which Novak regarded as evidence of creative, integrated understanding. Construction steps typically include selecting a topic, ranking concepts from general to specific, arranging them, and iteratively adding links and examples.

Uses

Concept maps are used in education (as learning and assessment tools), in knowledge management and expert elicitation, in brainstorming and curriculum design, and in research synthesis. They are distinguished from mind maps, which are usually free-form, single-centred, and lack labelled links and propositions, and from argument maps, which represent reasoning rather than conceptual structure.

Evidence

Meta-analyses of classroom studies find moderate positive effects of concept mapping on knowledge retention and transfer compared with passive study, particularly when students construct maps themselves rather than only studying provided maps.
