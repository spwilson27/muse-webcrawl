Station (Australian agriculture)

In Australia, a station is a large landholding used for livestock production, typically cattle or sheep grazing. The term is equivalent to the North American ranch, but Australian stations are often far larger, sometimes exceeding a million acres (over 4,000 square kilometres), making some of them among the largest agricultural properties in the world.

Terminology

Grazing properties in Australia are usually divided into sheep stations and cattle stations, depending on the stock carried. The manager's residence is called the homestead, and workers — historically called jackaroos and jillaroos in training — live in quarters near the homestead. A station may also be called a run or property.

History

Stations grew out of the pastoral expansion of the nineteenth century, when squatters occupied Crown land beyond the limits of official settlement and later converted their occupation into pastoral leases. The wool industry drove much of this expansion, and by the late 1800s vast sheep stations dominated the interior plains.

Scale and operation

The largest stations, such as Anna Creek Station in South Australia, cover areas larger than some small nations. Operations typically involve mustering stock by motorbike, vehicle or helicopter, maintaining bores and fences, and moving livestock by road train to market.
