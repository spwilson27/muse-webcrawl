The Vandals were a Germanic people who first inhabited what is now southern Poland and are best known for establishing a kingdom in North Africa in the 5th century that included the Roman province of Africa and the islands of Sicily, Sardinia, Corsica, Malta, and the Balearics. Under their king Genseric, they invaded Roman Gaul, crossed into Spain, and in 429 crossed the Strait of Gibraltar into Africa, capturing Carthage in 439. In 455 they famously sacked Rome.

Migration

On the last night of 406, amid a freezing of the Rhine, Vandals (in two branches, the Hasdingi and Silingi) together with Alans and Suebi crossed the river into Gaul, devastating as they went, and entered Spain in 409. Pressure from the Visigoths destroyed the Silingi and most Alans; the survivors joined the Hasdingi under Genseric, who in 429 led the whole people — perhaps 80,000 including families — across to Africa, exploiting Roman civil war. By 442 the western Roman Empire recognized Vandal possession of Africa Proconsularis, the empire's granary.

The Vandal Kingdom

Ruling from Carthage, the Vandals built a formidable navy, raided Mediterranean coasts, and in June 455 occupied and plundered Rome for two weeks — an event later remembered as emblematic of barbarian destructiveness, though contemporary sources report the sack was comparatively orderly, with Pope Leo I negotiating to spare the city's inhabitants and buildings. The Arian Vandal kings at times persecuted Nicene Christians. The kingdom weakened in the later 5th century; in 533–534 the Byzantine general Belisarius, sent by Justinian I, defeated kings Gelimer and reconquered North Africa in the Vandalic War, ending the Vandal kingdom and the Vandals as a distinct people.

Legacy

The name "Vandal" later acquired its modern sense of willful destroyer — the word "vandalism" was coined in 1794 by the Abbé Grégoire to describe damage to art during the French Revolution — an association historians consider exaggerated relative to other sacks of Rome.
