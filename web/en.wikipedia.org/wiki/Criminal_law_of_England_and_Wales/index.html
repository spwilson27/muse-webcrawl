Criminal law of England and Wales

The Central Criminal Court (Old Bailey) in London

The criminal law of England and Wales is the body of law governing crimes and their punishment within the legal jurisdiction of England and Wales. Unlike Scotland and Northern Ireland, which have separate legal systems, England and Wales share a single common-law system in which criminal offences derive both from statute and from the common law developed by the courts.

Sources and general principles

There is no single criminal code: offences are scattered across centuries of statutes and case law, a situation repeatedly criticised by the Law Commission, which has proposed codification. Conviction generally requires proof beyond reasonable doubt of a guilty act (actus reus) together with a guilty state of mind (mens rea), subject to defences such as self-defence, duress, insanity, and automatism. Some offences impose strict liability, requiring no mens rea as to one or more elements. The age of criminal responsibility is ten years.

Principal offences

Offences against the person include murder (which carries a mandatory life sentence), manslaughter, and the offences in the Offences against the Person Act 1861, such as wounding and assault occasioning actual bodily harm. Property offences are largely statutory: theft, robbery, and burglary under the Theft Act 1968, fraud under the Fraud Act 2006, and criminal damage under the Criminal Damage Act 1971. Sexual offences were comprehensively reformed by the Sexual Offences Act 2003. Other areas include public order offences, drug offences under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971, terrorism offences, and road traffic offences.

Procedure and enforcement

Most prosecutions are brought by the Crown Prosecution Service (established 1986), which applies a two-stage test of evidential sufficiency and public interest. Cases begin in the magistrates' courts: summary offences are tried there, while indictable offences are sent to the Crown Court for trial by jury. Appeals lie to the Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) and ultimately the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom. Sentencing follows guidelines issued by the Sentencing Council.
