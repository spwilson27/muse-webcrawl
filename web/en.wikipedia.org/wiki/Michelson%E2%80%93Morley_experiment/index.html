Michelson–Morley experiment

Michelson–Morley experiment
Performed: 1887
Location: Cleveland, Ohio
Result: Null — no aether wind detected

The Michelson–Morley experiment was an attempt to measure the relative motion of matter through the stationary luminiferous aether, the hypothetical medium thought to carry light waves. Conducted in 1887 by Albert A. Michelson and Edward W. Morley, it is often called the most famous failed experiment in physics.

Method

The experiment used an interferometer that split a beam of light into two perpendicular paths, reflected them back, and recombined them. If Earth moved through the aether, the light travelling along the direction of motion should take a different time than light travelling across it, shifting the interference fringes as the apparatus rotated. Multiple reflections increased the effective path length to about 11 metres.

Results

No significant fringe shift was observed — far less than the roughly 0.4 of a fringe expected. The null result contradicted aether-drag and stationary-aether theories and became one of the key experimental foundations for Einstein's special theory of relativity (1905), which dispensed with the aether altogether. Later repetitions with modern optical resonators have constrained any anisotropy of the speed of light to extraordinary precision.
