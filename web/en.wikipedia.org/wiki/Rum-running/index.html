Rum-running

Rum-running, or bootlegging, is the illegal business of transporting (smuggling) alcoholic beverages where such transportation is forbidden by law. The term rum-running is most often applied to smuggling by sea, particularly across the Maritime border during Prohibition in the United States (1920–1933), while bootlegging more often refers to smuggling over land.

Prohibition era

With the Eighteenth Amendment and the Volstead Act in force from 1920, foreign ships laden with liquor anchored just outside U.S. territorial waters — first three miles, later twelve — along a stretch of the Atlantic coast known as "Rum Row". Small, fast boats ran the liquor ashore at night to waiting trucks. Suppliers operated from Canada, the Caribbean (notably Cuba and the Bahamas), and the French islands of Saint-Pierre and Miquelon off Newfoundland, whose warehouses boomed during the period.

Enforcement and the rum fleet

The United States Coast Guard, greatly expanded for the task, patrolled Rum Row with cutters and destroyers, while rum-runners replied with faster boats, radio networks, and code systems. Famous vessels included the Canadian schooner I'm Alone, sunk by the Coast Guard in 1929, an incident that caused a diplomatic dispute with Canada. Well-known figures of the trade included Bill McCoy, whose unadulterated cargo gave rise to the expression "the real McCoy", and Gertrude Lythgoe.

End of the trade

The repeal of Prohibition by the Twenty-first Amendment in December 1933 ended the great rum-running era almost overnight. The term survives in the names of regattas, museums in the Bahamas and Nova Scotia, and the maritime folklore of the North Atlantic coast.
