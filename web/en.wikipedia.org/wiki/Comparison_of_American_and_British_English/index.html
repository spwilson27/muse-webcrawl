Comparison of American and British English

[Image: American and British English]

The English language was introduced to North America through British colonisation in the early 17th century, and the forms of English spoken in the United States and the United Kingdom have since diverged in pronunciation, vocabulary, spelling and some aspects of grammar, while remaining mutually intelligible. This article compares American English (AmE) and British English (BrE).

Vocabulary

Some words have different meanings in the two varieties, and many everyday objects have different names:

Divergences also include "false friends" such as pants (trousers in AmE, underwear in BrE) and football, which refers to different sports.

Spelling

American spelling was influenced by Noah Webster's dictionaries (from 1828), which promoted simplified forms: color for colour, center for centre, defense for defence, traveled for travelled, and check for cheque. British spelling generally retains the -our, -re, -ise/-isation and double-consonant conventions, although Oxford spelling uses -ize in many words.

Grammar and pronunciation

Differences include the use of the present perfect with just and already (more common in BrE), collective nouns taking singular verbs in AmE but often plural in BrE ("the team are"), the mandative subjunctive (stronger in AmE), and rhoticity: most American accents pronounce the /r/ after vowels, whereas Received Pronunciation and most accents of England and Wales do not. Vocabulary, date formats (month/day/year vs day/month/year) and punctuation conventions also differ in detail.
