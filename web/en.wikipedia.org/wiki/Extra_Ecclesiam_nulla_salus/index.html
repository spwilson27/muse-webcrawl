Extra Ecclesiam nulla salus

The doctrine holds that salvation is found through the Church alone.

Extra Ecclesiam nulla salus (Latin for "outside the Church there is no salvation") is a phrase referring to the doctrine that the Church is necessary for salvation. It traces to St. Cyprian of Carthage in the 3rd century, who wrote that one "cannot have God for his Father who has not the Church for his mother," and it was later affirmed in strong terms by the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) and by Pope Boniface VIII's bull Unam sanctam (1302).

Interpretation

The Catholic Church's modern teaching, expressed in the Second Vatican Council's Lumen gentium (1964) and the Catechism of the Catholic Church, holds that the phrase is directed at those who, knowing the Church to be necessary, refuse to enter or remain in it. The Church teaches that people who are ignorant of the Gospel without fault of their own, but who seek God sincerely, may attain salvation — through a grace that comes from Christ and is related to the Church in ways known to God. Baptism of desire and invincible ignorance are traditional qualifications developed by theologians over the centuries.

Other perspectives

Protestant traditions vary: confessional Lutheran and Reformed teaching historically used the phrase of the visible church, while many evangelicals apply its substance to faith in Christ rather than to any institution. Eastern Orthodox teaching likewise asserts that the Church is where salvation is found, while leaving the fate of those outside to God's mercy. A rigorist reading of the doctrine, associated with Fr. Leonard Feeney, was rejected by the Holy Office in 1949.
