Gokaidō

PeriodEdo period (1603–1868)
OriginNihonbashi, Edo (Tokyo)
RoutesTōkaidō, Nakasendō, Kōshū Kaidō, Ōshū Kaidō, Nikkō Kaidō

The Gokaidō (Japanese: 五街道, "five routes") were the five major roads of Edo-period Japan, administered by the Tokugawa shogunate and radiating from Nihonbashi in Edo (modern Tokyo). Built up from 1601 under Tokugawa Ieyasu, the network served the movement of officials, couriers and the great processions of the sankin-kōtai system, under which feudal lords travelled regularly between their domains and the shogun's capital.

The five routes

The most important was the Tōkaidō, running along the Pacific coast to Kyoto with 53 post stations, immortalised in Hiroshige's woodblock print series The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō. The Nakasendō took an inland mountain route to Kyoto with 69 stations. The Kōshū Kaidō ran west to Kai Province and joined the Nakasendō; the Ōshū Kaidō ran north to Mutsu Province; and the Nikkō Kaidō led to the shrines of Nikkō, where Ieyasu was enshrined.

Administration and travel

Along the routes the shogunate maintained post towns (shukuba) providing horses, porters, inns and food, as well as checkpoints (sekisho) that monitored travellers, particularly women and firearms moving in and out of Edo. Milestones, usually mounds planted with trees called ichirizuka, marked distances along the roads. Much of the Gokaidō network underlies modern Japanese national highways, and many post towns survive as tourist destinations.
