Lithification

Lithification (from the Greek word lithos, meaning "rock" and the Latin-derived suffix -ific) is the process by which loose sediments are converted into solid sedimentary rock. It is a central part of diagenesis and involves two main processes: compaction and cementation.

Compaction

As sediment accumulates, the weight of overlying material (the overburden) compresses the grains below, squeezing out pore water and reducing pore space. Fine-grained sediments such as mud can lose a large proportion of their original volume in this way, eventually forming shale.

Cementation

Groundwater moving through buried sediment carries dissolved minerals—commonly calcite, silica (quartz), or iron oxides—which precipitate in the pore spaces between grains and bind them together. The type of cement strongly influences the hardness and durability of the resulting rock: silica-cemented sandstones are typically much harder than calcite-cemented ones.

Stages

Lithification begins shortly after burial and may continue over millions of years as sediments are buried more deeply and exposed to higher temperatures and pressures. In organic-rich sediments, the same burial history drives the transformation of organic matter into kerogen and, ultimately, petroleum.
