The Political Constitution of the Mexican Republic of 1857 (Spanish: Constitución Política de la República Mexicana de 1857) was the liberal constitution promulgated on 5 February 1857 during the presidency of Ignacio Comonfort. Drafted by a constituent congress elected after the Revolution of Ayutla overthrew Antonio López de Santa Anna, it incorporated the liberal Reform Laws and established individual rights, federalism and the separation of church and state. Conservative opposition to the constitution provoked the Reform War (1858–1861) and helped set the stage for the French intervention in Mexico. It remained Mexico's fundamental law, with interruptions, until replaced by the Constitution of 1917.

Background

Following the Plan de Ayutla of 1854 and Santa Anna's final exile, the liberal government of Juan Álvarez and then Ignacio Comonfort enacted a series of Reform Laws: the Ley Juárez (1855), abolishing clerical and military fueros; the Ley Lerdo (1856), ordering the sale of corporate property held by the Church and indigenous communities; and measures regulating parish fees and civil registration. A constituent congress convened in February 1856 to give these reforms constitutional form.

Contents

The constitution comprised 128 articles in eight titles. It guaranteed freedom of speech, press, assembly, petition and movement, abolished slavery and noble titles, and outlawed monopolies and imprisonment for civil debt. It confirmed the Ley Lerdo's ban on ecclesiastical and municipal corporations holding real estate not directly used for their purposes, mandated free primary education, and established a federal republic of states with a unicameral Congress — a feature later reversed by the restoration of the Senate in 1874. Significantly, the congress declined to proclaim complete religious toleration, but the document omitted Catholicism as the state religion, an omission conservatives bitterly resented.

Opposition and the Reform War

The Church threatened excommunication for officials who swore allegiance to the constitution. In December 1857 General Félix María Zuloaga proclaimed the Plan de Tacubaya, repudiating the charter; Comonfort briefly adhered to the coup before resigning. Benito Juárez, as president of the Supreme Court, assumed the constitutional presidency and led the liberal government from Veracruz through the Reform War, eventually triumphing in 1861.

Legacy

Suspended in practice during the Second Mexican Empire of Maximilian I, the constitution was restored in 1867 and governed Mexico through the Restored Republic and the Porfiriato. Amendments under Porfirio Díaz progressively weakened its anti-reelection provisions. The revolution of 1910 was initially waged in its defence under Francisco I. Madero's slogan of "effective suffrage, no re-election", but the Constitutional Congress of 1916–1917 replaced it with a new, socially oriented charter.
