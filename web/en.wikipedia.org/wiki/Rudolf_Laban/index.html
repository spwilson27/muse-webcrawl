Rudolf Laban

Rudolf Laban (15 December 1879 – 1 July 1958), known in England as Rudolf von Laban, was an Austro-Hungarian-born dance artist and theorist who is regarded as a founding figure of modern dance in Europe and the pioneer of movement analysis. He invented Labanotation, one of the most widely used systems of recording dance and movement, and developed the framework of "effort" and spatial harmony (choreutics) that still underpins dance education, actor training and movement therapy.

Life

Laban was born in Pressburg (now Bratislava) into a military family of Hungarian and French descent. He studied architecture and painting in Paris before turning to movement and dance, founding schools and movement choirs across Germany and Switzerland. At Monte Verità in Ascona during the First World War he worked with Mary Wigman and other pioneers of expressive dance (Ausdruckstanz). In the 1920s his dance companies toured widely, and in 1928 he published his notation system in Schrifttanz.

Appointed to senior posts in German state theatres after 1930, Laban initially worked within the Nazi cultural system, but his mass movement spectacle for the 1936 Berlin Olympics was banned by Goebbels, and he left Germany in 1937. He settled in England in 1938, where, with Lisa Ullmann, he founded the Laban Art of Movement Guild and, in 1946, the Art of Movement Studio in Manchester. His wartime work analysing factory workers' movements with F. C. Lawrence led to influential studies of industrial effort and time-and-motion efficiency.

Theories

Laban's system analyses movement through four components—body, effort, shape and space. His "effort" theory describes movement dynamics through weight, time, space and flow, combining into eight basic effort actions such as punch, slash, glide and float. Labanotation records movement on a three-line staff read from bottom to top, specifying direction, level, timing and body part. His ideas profoundly influenced modern dance pedagogy, the training of actors, dance therapy and later fields such as ergonomics and nonverbal communication research.
