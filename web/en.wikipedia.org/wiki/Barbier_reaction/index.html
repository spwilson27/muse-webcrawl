Barbier reaction

The Barbier reaction is an organometallic reaction in organic chemistry in which an alkyl halide, a carbonyl compound (aldehyde or ketone) and a metal react in a single pot to give a primary, secondary or tertiary alcohol after work-up. It is named after the French chemist Philippe Barbier, who introduced the method in 1899.

Relationship to the Grignard reaction

The Barbier reaction is closely related to the Grignard reaction developed by Barbier's student Victor Grignard, but differs in procedure: in the Grignard reaction the organomagnesium reagent is prepared first and then added to the carbonyl compound, whereas in the Barbier reaction the organometallic species is generated in situ in the presence of the carbonyl. The one-pot Barbier procedure tolerates some functional groups that would not survive a preformed Grignard reagent and can be more forgiving of moisture.

Metals and variants

Magnesium was Barbier's original metal, but modern variants commonly employ zinc (as in the Barbier-type allylations related to the Reformatsky reaction), indium, tin, samarium diiodide, lithium or aluminium, and the reaction can even be run in water with indium or zinc. Intramolecular Barbier reactions are used to close rings in the synthesis of natural products.
