Buffet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: A buffet table with rows of serving dishes]
A typical self-service buffet spread

A buffet is a system of serving meals in which food is placed in a public area where diners serve themselves. A buffet offers a wide selection of dishes from which guests choose, typically for a fixed price, and is a popular format in restaurants, hotels, catered events, and celebrations. The word comes from the French buffet, originally the sideboard or counter on which food and drink were displayed.

History

The buffet format has roots in the Swedish smorgasbord and in French service styles in which dishes were set out on a side table. The modern all-you-can-eat restaurant buffet was popularised in the United States in the mid-20th century; Las Vegas casino manager Herb McDonald is often credited with introducing the 24-hour "Buckaroo Buffet" in 1946, priced at one dollar, as a way to keep gamblers on the premises. The format spread widely through hotel dining, cruise ships, and casual restaurant chains.

Types

Buffets may be organised by cuisine (for example, a Chinese or Indian buffet), by meal (breakfast buffets are standard in hotels), or by theme. In an "all-you-can-eat" buffet, customers pay a single price and may return to the serving line repeatedly. Other buffets charge by weight or by plate. Serving stations sometimes include a carvery, a salad bar, or made-to-order stations such as omelette or pasta bars. Chafing dishes, sneeze guards, and heat lamps are used to keep food at safe serving temperatures.

Restaurant buffets

Buffets are economical for operators because they require fewer servers and allow batch preparation, but they raise challenges of food waste and food safety, since dishes may sit out for extended periods and are handled by many diners. Health regulations in many jurisdictions require sneeze guards, temperature monitoring, and limits on how long food may be held.

Home and event buffets

For private gatherings, a buffet allows guests to eat at their own pace and choose freely among dishes, and lets hosts serve large numbers without formal table service. Finger buffets, common at British functions, consist of items that can be eaten without cutlery.
