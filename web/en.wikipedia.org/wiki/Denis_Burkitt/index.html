Denis Burkitt

Denis Parsons Burkitt (28 February 1911 – 23 March 1993) was an Irish surgeon who made important contributions to health in the developing world, above all the identification of the childhood cancer now known as Burkitt lymphoma, and, later, pioneering advocacy of dietary fibre as protection against diseases of the colon.

Life and career

Born in Enniskillen, Northern Ireland, Burkitt studied medicine at the University of Edinburgh after abandoning engineering, having lost an eye in a childhood accident. During the Second World War he served with the Royal Army Medical Corps in Africa, and after the war he joined the Colonial Medical Service in Uganda, working at Mulago Hospital in Kampala.

Burkitt lymphoma

In 1957 Burkitt treated a child with swellings of the jaw and recognised a distinctive, rapidly growing tumour of children in equatorial Africa. He mapped its distribution across Africa — the "lymphoma belt" — and its dependence on climate led to the hypothesis of an insect-borne viral cause. Tissue samples sent to England enabled Anthony Epstein and Yvonne Barr to discover the Epstein–Barr virus in tumour cells in 1964, the first virus shown to be associated with a human cancer. Burkitt also showed the lymphoma was often curable with chemotherapy, remarkable for so aggressive a malignancy.

Dietary fibre

Returning to Britain in 1966 to work for the Medical Research Council, Burkitt compared diets and disease patterns worldwide and argued that low-fibre diets were linked to colorectal cancer, diverticular disease, appendicitis, and other "Western diseases". His 1979 book Don't Forget Fibre in Your Diet helped popularise high-fibre diets.
