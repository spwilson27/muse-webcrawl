Matter wave

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Electron diffraction pattern of concentric rings from a crystalline sample]
Electron diffraction rings: direct evidence of matter waves.

Matter waves are a central part of the theory of quantum mechanics, being half of Wave–particle duality. All matter exhibits wave-like behaviour: for instance, a beam of electrons can be diffracted just like a beam of light. The wavelength of a particle, the de Broglie wavelength, is λ = h/p — Planck's constant divided by the particle's momentum.

The hypothesis was proposed by Louis de Broglie in his 1924 doctoral thesis. It was confirmed for electrons in 1927 by Davisson and Germer's crystal diffraction experiments and independently by G. P. Thomson passing electrons through thin films; de Broglie received the 1929 Nobel Prize in Physics. In the Bohr model, de Broglie waves closing on themselves around the nucleus explain quantised orbits; in modern terms matter waves are the wave functions of particles, evolving by the Schrödinger equation.

Scale and observation

Because h is tiny, matter wavelengths are appreciable only for light particles at low momenta: thermal electrons have wavelengths near atomic spacings (hence electron microscopes and neutron diffraction as structural tools), while a thrown baseball's wavelength (~10⁻³⁴ m) is unmeasurably small. Interference of matter waves — in the Double-slit experiment and in atom interferometers — is now a precision measurement technology sensitive to gravity, rotation and fundamental constants.
