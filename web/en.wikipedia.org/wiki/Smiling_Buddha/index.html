Smiling Buddha (MEA designation: Pokhran-I) was the code name of India's first successful nuclear weapon test, conducted on 18 May 1974 at the Pokhran Test Range in the Thar Desert of Rajasthan. The device was detonated underground by the Indian Army under the direction of the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC). India described the test as a "peaceful nuclear explosion" (PNE).

Background

India's nuclear programme began under physicist Homi J. Bhabha in the 1940s. After the Sino-Indian War of 1962 and China's first nuclear test in 1964, pressure grew for an Indian deterrent. Prime Minister Indira Gandhi authorised development of a test device around 1971–72. The project was kept secret, involving a small team of scientists led by Raja Ramanna at BARC, with plutonium produced by the CIRUS research reactor at Trombay.

The device and test

The device was an implosion-type fission weapon using plutonium, roughly 1.25 metres in diameter and weighing about 1,400 kg. It was detonated at a depth of about 107 metres at 8:05 a.m. Indian Standard Time on 18 May 1974 — the festival day of Buddha Purnima, which inspired the code name; the message sent to signal success was reported as "The Buddha has smiled". The official Indian yield estimate was 12 kilotons, while independent Western estimates have generally been lower, in the range of 2 to 13 kilotons.

Reactions and consequences

The test drew international criticism and reshaped global non-proliferation policy. Canada, which had supplied the CIRUS reactor and heavy water arrangements, suspended nuclear cooperation with India; Pakistan accelerated its own weapons programme under Zulfikar Ali Bhutto. The Nuclear Suppliers Group was founded in 1975 largely in response to the test. India maintained that the explosion was for peaceful purposes and declined to sign the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, which it considered discriminatory.

Aftermath

India conducted no further tests for 24 years, until the Pokhran-II tests of May 1998 (code-named Operation Shakti), after which it declared itself a nuclear-weapon state. India subsequently adopted a declared doctrine of credible minimum deterrence with a no-first-use policy.
