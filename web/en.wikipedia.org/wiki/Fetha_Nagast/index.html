Fetha Nagast

The Fetha Nagast (Ge'ez: ፍትሐ ነገሥት, "Law of the Kings") is a legal code compiled in Ethiopia around 1240 by the Coptic Christian writer Abul Virtu (Abū l-Faḍāʾil ibn al-ʿAssāl), translated from Arabic into Ge'ez in the 15th century. For centuries it served as the supreme law of Ethiopia, blending canon law with civil, criminal, and social provisions drawn from the Bible, early church canons, and Byzantine legal traditions.

Structure and content

The work is divided into two parts. The first deals with ecclesiastical matters: the hierarchy of the church, sacraments, fasting, and the duties of clergy, drawing on the Apostolic Canons and councils such as Nicaea. The second part covers the law of laypeople and the state: kingship, judges, marriage, inheritance, contracts, property, slavery, and criminal punishments. Its sources include the Syro-Roman law book, the canons attributed to the Roman emperors, and Arabic Christian compilations.

History of use

Tradition holds that the Ge'ez translation was made at the request of the Ethiopian court in the 15th century, and from at least the 16th century onward Ethiopian emperors and judges administered justice in its name. Although many of its provisions were applied flexibly and alongside local custom, the Fetha Nagast retained immense prestige as a symbol of legitimate, divinely sanctioned law. Emperor Haile Selassie promulgated modern codes in the mid-20th century — the Penal Code of 1957 and the Civil Code of 1960 — which formally superseded it, yet the Fetha Nagast continued to inform Ethiopian legal culture and is still consulted in some ecclesiastical and customary contexts.

Legacy

Scholars regard the Fetha Nagast as one of the most remarkable transmissions of Mediterranean legal culture into sub-Saharan Africa, and it remains a central text for the study of Ethiopian history, the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and comparative law.
