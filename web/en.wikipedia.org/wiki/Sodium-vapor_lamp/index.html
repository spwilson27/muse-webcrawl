Sodium-vapor lamp

A sodium-vapor lamp is a gas-discharge lamp that uses sodium in an excited state to produce light. There are two varieties: low-pressure sodium (LPS) lamps emit a nearly monochromatic yellow-orange light at 589 nm and were for decades the most electrically efficient artificial light source available, while high-pressure sodium (HPS) lamps broaden the spectrum to a golden-white light with better colour rendering at somewhat lower efficacy. Sodium lamps were the dominant technology for street lighting, motorways, tunnels and industrial lighting through the later 20th century, and are now being widely replaced by LED lamps.

Low-pressure sodium

LPS lamps discharge through sodium vapour at low pressure in a U-shaped tube of borosilicate glass with an infrared-reflecting outer jacket to retain heat. Because their light is almost single-wavelength, colour discrimination under LPS is essentially impossible, but their high efficacy and good performance in fog made them standard for roads; their narrow spectrum also minimises interference with astronomical observation, and they were favoured near observatories. Warm-up takes several minutes, during which the lamp glows red from a neon–argon starting gas before the sodium vapourises.

High-pressure sodium

HPS lamps, developed in the 1960s, run sodium vapour at high pressure inside a translucent alumina arc tube (ordinary glass is attacked by hot sodium). Pressure broadening spreads the emission, giving a broader golden-white spectrum and acceptable colour rendering, at efficacies still far above incandescent lamps. HPS became the default for street lighting from the 1970s onward and remains common in horticultural grow lighting because of its strong output in wavelengths usable by plants.

Replacement by LED

Falling LED prices, better colour rendering, instant start, dimmability and directional control have driven mass replacement of sodium street lighting since the 2010s; the change is visible from orbit as cities shift from orange to whiter light, a transition debated by astronomers and ecologists concerned about light pollution and spectrum.
