Cottontail rabbits are the roughly twenty species in the genus Sylvilagus of the Leporidae family, found in the Americas. They are named for the distinctive short, fluffy white underside of the tail, which resembles a ball of cotton when the rabbit runs. Cottontails are small to medium-sized rabbits, generally 25–50 cm long, with grizzled brown or gray-brown upperparts, long ears, and large hind feet.

Distribution and habitat

The genus ranges from southern Canada through the United States and Mexico into Central and northern South America. The best-known species is the eastern cottontail (Sylvilagus floridanus), widespread in eastern and south-central North America and commonly seen in meadows, brushy areas, orchards, and suburban gardens. Western species include the desert cottontail (S. audubonii) and the mountain cottontail (S. nuttallii). Unlike the European rabbit, cottontails do not dig extensive burrow systems or live in social colonies; they rest in shallow ground depressions called forms and nest in fur-lined ground nests.

Behavior and reproduction

Cottontails are herbivores, grazing on grasses and forbs in summer and browsing bark and twigs in winter. They are crepuscular, most active at dawn and dusk, and rely on camouflage and zigzag flight to escape predators, which include hawks, owls, foxes, coyotes, and domestic dogs and cats. Females are famously prolific, producing several litters a year of typically three to eight young; the altricial kits are born blind and hairless in a shallow nest and are independent within a few weeks. The New England cottontail (S. transitionalis) has declined sharply due to habitat loss and competition with the introduced eastern cottontail and is a focus of conservation efforts.
