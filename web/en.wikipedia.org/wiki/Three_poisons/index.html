Three poisons

Three poisons
Sanskrit: triviṣa
Pali: kilesa (defilements)
Members: moha (delusion), raga (attachment), dvesha (aversion)
Tradition: Buddhism

In Buddhism, the three poisons (Sanskrit: triviṣa; Tibetan: dug gsum) are the three root kleshas, or mental afflictions, held to keep beings trapped in saṃsāra, the cycle of rebirth. They are moha (delusion or ignorance), raga (attachment, greed, or sensual desire), and dvesha (aversion, hatred, or anger). The three poisons are considered the root cause of suffering (dukkha) and correspond to the ignorance identified in the doctrine of dependent origination.

At the centre of the bhavacakra (wheel of life), a pig, a snake, and a rooster represent ignorance, aversion, and attachment.

Symbolism

In the bhavacakra, the three poisons are depicted at the very centre of the wheel as three animals chasing one another in a circle: a pig or boar (delusion/ignorance), a snake (aversion/hatred), and a rooster or pigeon (attachment/greed). Each animal bites the tail of the one in front, symbolising how the three afflictions mutually reinforce and perpetuate the cycle of existence.

Opposites and antidotes

The three poisons are contrasted with the three wholesome roots (kuśala-mūla): non-delusion (wisdom, prajñā), non-attachment (generosity, dāna), and non-aversion (loving-kindness, mettā). Buddhist practice — ethical conduct, meditation, and the cultivation of insight — is aimed at weakening and ultimately uprooting the poisons, leading to nirvana. In the Mahāyāna tradition the poisons may also be described as transformable into wisdoms.
