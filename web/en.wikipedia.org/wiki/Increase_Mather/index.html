Increase Mather (June 21, 1639 – August 23, 1723) was a New England Puritan clergyman in the Massachusetts Bay Colony and president of Harvard College for twenty years. He was influential in the administration of the colony during a time when the United States was first being formed, in negotiating a new charter for Massachusetts, and in the early history of Harvard.

The son of minister Richard Mather, Increase Mather graduated from Harvard in 1656 and received his master's degree after study at Trinity College, Dublin. He served as pastor of Boston's Second Church (the Old North Church congregation) for more than fifty years, becoming the most prominent minister of his generation in New England.

Harvard and the charter

Mather served as president of Harvard College from 1685 to 1701 (initially as rector), the last clergyman and the first American-born person to lead the institution. After the Glorious Revolution and the overthrow of Governor Edmund Andros in 1689, Mather travelled to London as the colony's agent and negotiated the new Massachusetts charter of 1691, which combined Massachusetts Bay with Plymouth Colony but also imposed a royally appointed governor and religious toleration — concessions many Puritans resented.

The witchcraft trials

Mather's role in the Salem witchcraft trials of 1692–93 was ambivalent. His book Cases of Conscience Concerning Evil Spirits (1693) argued that spectral evidence was unreliable and that it were better "ten suspected witches should escape than one innocent person be condemned," a position that helped turn opinion against the trials. At the same time he defended the judges and never fully repudiated the proceedings; his son Cotton Mather was more closely associated with the trials in the public mind.

Works and legacy

A prolific author, Mather published more than 130 works, including sermons, histories of King Philip's War, and An Essay for the Recording of Illustrious Providences (1684), a collection of remarkable occurrences that fed his interest in natural philosophy; he was a fellow of the Royal Society's intellectual circle and one of colonial America's first men of science. He died in Boston in 1723.
