Brit milah

The brit milah (Hebrew: בְּרִית מִילָה, "covenant of circumcision"), also known by the Ashkenazi pronunciation bris, is the Jewish religious ceremony in which a baby boy is circumcised. According to the Torah (Genesis 17), circumcision is the sign of the covenant between God and Abraham and his descendants, and the ceremony is traditionally performed on the eighth day of the boy's life, as commanded in Leviticus 12:3.

The ceremony

The circumcision is performed by a mohel, a practitioner trained in the religious and surgical aspects of the rite. The baby is held by a sandek (often a grandfather or respected elder), who sits in a special chair known as the Chair of Elijah. The ceremony includes blessings, the circumcision itself, the giving of the boy's Hebrew name, and a festive meal (seudat mitzvah) for family and guests.

The brit milah is performed on the eighth day even if it falls on the Sabbath or a festival, but it is postponed if the baby's health is in any doubt.

Components

Traditional practice includes milah (cutting of the foreskin) and periah (uncovering the glans). A third element, metzitzah (suction of blood from the wound), historically performed orally (metzitzah b'peh), has become controversial in some communities because of infection risks, and many mohelim use a sterile tube or gauze instead.

Significance and variations

The ceremony is one of the most widely observed rituals in Judaism, practised across denominations and by many otherwise non-observant families. Reform and other liberal movements train mohelim and mohalot of all genders and recognise equivalent ceremonies for girls (brit bat or simchat bat). A small minority of Jewish parents decline circumcision and hold alternative naming ceremonies (brit shalom).
