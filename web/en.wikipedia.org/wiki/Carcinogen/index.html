Carcinogen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A carcinogen is any substance, radionuclide or radiation that promotes carcinogenesis — the formation of cancer. This may be due to the ability to damage the genome or to disrupt cellular metabolic processes. Several radioactive substances are considered carcinogens, but their carcinogenic activity is attributed to the radiation they emit. Common examples of non-radioactive carcinogens are inhaled asbestos, certain dioxins, benzene, and constituents of tobacco smoke.

Mechanisms

Genotoxic carcinogens bind directly to DNA or are metabolised into reactive intermediates that form DNA adducts, producing mutations in oncogenes and tumour-suppressor genes if repair fails. Non-genotoxic carcinogens act by other routes: chronic inflammation, hormonal effects, immunosuppression or cytotoxicity followed by regenerative proliferation. Co-carcinogens and promoters do not cause cancer on their own but enhance the effect of other agents. The distinction matters for regulation, because a threshold dose is generally assumed for non-genotoxic agents but not for genotoxic ones.

Classification

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classifies agents into groups: Group 1 (carcinogenic to humans — including tobacco smoke, asbestos, plutonium, processed meat and sunlight), Group 2A (probably carcinogenic), Group 2B (possibly carcinogenic), and Group 3 (not classifiable). The classification reflects strength of evidence, not level of risk. The US National Toxicology Program and the EU's CLP Regulation maintain parallel lists with legal effect in occupational safety.

History

The link between environment and cancer was recognised as early as 1775, when Percivall Pott attributed scrotal cancer in chimney sweeps to soot. Coal tar was shown to induce skin tumours in rabbits in the 1910s, and the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon benzo[a]pyrene was isolated from it in the 1930s.
