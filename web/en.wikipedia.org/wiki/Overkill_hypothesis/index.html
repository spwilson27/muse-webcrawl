Overkill hypothesis

The overkill hypothesis proposes that human hunting was a primary cause of the extinctions of large mammals (megafauna) at the end of the Pleistocene, as human populations spread into previously uninhabited continents and islands.

Development

The idea was developed most forcefully by palaeontologist Paul S. Martin from the 1960s, who noted that extinctions in the Americas, Australia and on oceanic islands closely followed the first arrival of humans, and that large, slow-breeding animals with no evolutionary experience of human predators would have been especially vulnerable — a "blitzkrieg" model of rapid extinction.

Debate

Critics point to climate change at the end of the last glacial period as a competing or interacting cause, note the scarcity of unambiguous kill sites for many extinct species, and observe that Africa, where megafauna evolved alongside hominins, retained far more of its large mammals. Modern research increasingly favours a combination of human pressure and climatic stress, with the balance differing by region.
