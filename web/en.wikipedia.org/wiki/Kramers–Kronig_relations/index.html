Kramers–Kronig relations

The Kramers–Kronig relations (also called Kramers–Kronig dispersion relations) are bidirectional mathematical relations connecting the real and imaginary parts of any complex function that is analytic in the upper half-plane. The relations are often used to relate the real and imaginary components of response functions in physical systems. The real part and imaginary part are not independent of each other, but can be reconstructed from each other given the requirement of causality.

History

The relations were derived independently in the mid-1920s by Hans Kramers and Ralph Kronig, who applied them to the dispersion of X-rays and light: the refractive index (real part) and the absorption coefficient (imaginary part) of a material cannot be chosen independently. They are a special case of the Hilbert transform, and equivalent results appear across physics wherever linear, causal response is described.

Formulation

For a complex function χ(ω) = χ1(ω) + iχ2(ω) of a real variable ω, analytic in the upper half-plane and vanishing sufficiently fast at infinity, the relations take the form of principal-value integrals:

χ1(ω) = (1/π) P ∫ χ2(ω′)/(ω′ − ω) dω′,   χ2(ω) = −(1/π) P ∫ χ1(ω′)/(ω′ − ω) dω′,

where P denotes the Cauchy principal value. The mathematical basis is Cauchy's integral theorem applied in the upper half-plane: causality — the response cannot precede its cause — forces the response function to be analytic there.

Applications

The relations are used throughout physics and engineering: in optics, to obtain the refractive index from absorption spectra (and vice versa); in electrical engineering, connecting the magnitude and phase of causal network functions and filters; in electrochemistry and impedance spectroscopy; and in particle physics, where dispersion relations relate scattering amplitudes to total cross-sections. They also serve as a consistency check on measured spectra, since experimental data that violate the relations cannot come from a linear causal system.
