Meteora

The Meteora (Greek: Μετέωρα, "suspended in the air") is a rock formation in Thessaly, Greece, hosting one of the largest and most precipitously built complexes of Eastern Orthodox monasteries, second in importance only to Mount Athos. The monasteries are built on immense natural sandstone pillars at the northwestern edge of the Plain of Thessaly, near the town of Kalambaka. Meteora is included in the UNESCO World Heritage List.

History

Hermit monks began settling the caves and fissures of the rocks as early as the 9th–11th centuries. In the 14th century, the monk Athanasios Koinovitis founded the Great Meteoron monastery on the broadest of the pillars, and the community grew as monks sought refuge from Ottoman expansion and political instability. At their peak in the 16th century there were 24 monasteries; six remain active today — Great Meteoron, Varlaam, Rousanou, St. Nicholas Anapausas, St. Stephen, and Holy Trinity.

Access

Originally the monasteries could be reached only by rope ladders and nets hauled up by winches; supplies and visitors were lifted in baskets. Steps carved into the rock and bridges were added in the 20th century, making the surviving monasteries accessible to pilgrims and tourists. St. Stephen's is reached by a small bridge, while others require climbing well over a hundred steps.

Geology

The pillars consist of sandstone and conglomerate formed about 60 million years ago as delta deposits, later uplifted and weathered into vertical columns and rounded towers rising up to about 300–500 metres above the plain.
