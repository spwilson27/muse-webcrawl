Landed gentry

Overview
Class:British social class
Basis:Ownership of landed estates and rental income
Reference work:Burke's Landed Gentry

The landed gentry, or simply the gentry, is a largely historical British social class of land owners who lived entirely from rental income, or at least had a country estate. Though distinct from, and socially below, the peerage and titled nobility, gentry families often intermarried with them and provided the backbone of local government — as justices of the peace, militia officers, members of Parliament and county administrators — from the Tudor period into the 19th century.

Composition and status

The gentry ranged from wealthy magnates with great estates to modest squires. Recognised markers of gentry status included ownership of a country house and estate, a coat of arms granted or confirmed by the College of Arms, a university or public-school education, and a lifestyle that did not require manual labour or trade — though younger sons routinely entered the professions, the clergy, the armed forces or colonial service. Reference works such as Burke's Landed Gentry documented the pedigrees of such families, and land tenure arrangements — entails, settlements and primogeniture — were designed to keep estates intact across generations.

Decline

The agricultural depression of the late 19th century, followed by death duties introduced in 1894, falling rents, the loss of heirs in the First World War and rising taxation, broke up many estates in the great land sales of 1918–1921 and afterwards. The class's political dominance faded with the expansion of the franchise and professional county administration, although gentry families and their houses remain a visible feature of British rural life, and comparable landed classes existed in Ireland, continental Europe and China.
