A birth defect, also known as a congenital disorder or congenital anomaly, is a condition present at birth, regardless of its cause. Birth defects may result in disabilities that can be physical, intellectual, or developmental, ranging from mild to severe. They are broadly divided into structural defects, such as congenital heart defects and neural tube defects, and functional or metabolic disorders, such as Down syndrome and phenylketonuria. Birth defects affect an estimated 3 to 6 percent of infants worldwide and are a leading cause of infant mortality.

Causes

Known causes include single-gene mutations, chromosomal abnormalities such as trisomy 21, and multifactorial combinations of genes and environment. Environmental agents called teratogens — including alcohol (fetal alcohol spectrum disorders), certain medications such as thalidomide and isotretinoin, and infections such as rubella and Zika virus — can disrupt development, with effects depending on the timing of exposure. Nutritional deficiencies, notably of folate, cause neural tube defects. In a large proportion of cases, no specific cause is identified.

Examples

The most common severe birth defects are heart defects, neural tube defects (spina bifida and anencephaly), and Down syndrome. Others include cleft lip and palate, limb reduction defects, gastroschisis, and congenital infections. Clubfoot and congenital hip dislocation are relatively common and treatable structural anomalies.

Prevention, screening, and treatment

Prevention strategies include folic acid fortification and supplementation (which has sharply reduced neural tube defects in many countries), rubella vaccination, iodine sufficiency, and avoiding alcohol and known teratogens in pregnancy. Screening includes prenatal ultrasound, maternal serum testing, and newborn screening panels that detect treatable metabolic disorders. Treatments range from corrective surgery, such as for congenital heart defects, to lifelong medical management and early intervention services.
