Amblyopia

Amblyopia, commonly called lazy eye, is a disorder of sight in which the brain fails to fully process input from one eye, favouring the other eye, even though the affected eye itself is structurally normal. It results from disrupted visual development in early childhood. Amblyopia is the most common cause of decreased vision in a single eye among children and younger adults, affecting roughly 1–5% of the population.

Causes

Amblyopia develops when the visual pathways of one eye receive consistently poorer input during the critical period of visual development. The main categories are strabismic amblyopia (from a squint, where the brain suppresses the misaligned eye), refractive amblyopia (from uncorrected unequal refractive error, anisometropia, or high bilateral error), and deprivation amblyopia (from obstruction of vision, e.g. by a congenital cataract or drooping eyelid).

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is made by measuring reduced best-corrected visual acuity in one eye that cannot be explained by structural abnormality. Screening of preschool children with photoscreeners or acuity charts is recommended because early detection greatly improves outcomes.

Treatment

Treatment first corrects the underlying cause: glasses for refractive error, surgery for cataract or strabismus. The stronger eye is then penalised — by patching or atropine drops — to force the brain to use the weaker eye. Treatment is most effective before about age seven but older children and even adults can improve, particularly with newer binocular and perceptual-learning therapies.
