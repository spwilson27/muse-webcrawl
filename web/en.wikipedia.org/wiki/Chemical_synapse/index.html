Chemical synapse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chemical synapses are biological junctions through which neurons signal to each other and to non-neuronal cells such as muscles and glands. They allow neurons to form circuits in the central nervous system and are crucial to computation in the brain, as well as to learning and memory. At a chemical synapse, an arriving action potential triggers the presynaptic neuron to release chemical messengers — neurotransmitters — which cross the synaptic cleft and bind receptors on the postsynaptic cell.

Anatomy

A typical chemical synapse consists of a presynaptic bouton containing mitochondria and synaptic vesicles filled with neurotransmitter; an active zone where vesicles dock and fuse; a synaptic cleft about 20–40 nanometres wide; and a postsynaptic density rich in receptors and scaffolding proteins. An adult human brain contains on the order of 100 trillion synapses.

Mechanism

Depolarization of the presynaptic terminal opens voltage-gated calcium channels. The resulting Ca2+ influx drives synaptic vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane via SNARE proteins (including synaptobrevin, syntaxin and SNAP-25), a process sensed by synaptotagmin. Released neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds ionotropic receptors (ligand-gated ion channels, producing fast excitatory or inhibitory postsynaptic potentials) or metabotropic receptors (G protein-coupled receptors, producing slower, modulatory effects). Transmission is terminated by enzymatic breakdown (e.g. acetylcholinesterase), reuptake into the presynaptic terminal, or diffusion.

Synaptic plasticity

The strength of chemical synapses can change with activity. Long-term potentiation and long-term depression at glutamatergic synapses — dependent on NMDA and AMPA receptors — are leading cellular models of learning and memory. Short-term plasticity, such as facilitation and depression, arises from changes in release probability and vesicle availability.

History

The synapse was named by Charles Sherrington in 1897. Otto Loewi's 1921 experiments demonstrating chemical transmission ("Vagusstoff") and the later work of Henry Dale, Bernard Katz and others established the chemical nature of most synaptic transmission, in contrast to direct electrical synapses at gap junctions.
