Enzyme replacement therapy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) is a medical treatment that replaces an enzyme which is deficient or absent in patients with certain genetic diseases, most notably the lysosomal storage diseases. The replacement enzyme is typically produced by recombinant DNA technology and administered by intravenous infusion, from where it is taken up by cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis (often through mannose-6-phosphate receptors) and delivered to lysosomes.

History and applications

The concept was proposed by Christian de Duve in 1964. The first successful ERT was developed for Gaucher's disease: alglucerase (Ceredase), derived from human placenta, was approved in 1991, followed by the recombinant product imiglucerase (Cerezyme) in 1994. ERTs have since been approved for Fabry disease, Pompe disease, several mucopolysaccharidoses, and other conditions.

Limitations

ERT is expensive — often costing hundreds of thousands of dollars per patient per year — requires lifelong regular infusions, and cannot effectively cross the blood–brain barrier, limiting its benefit for neurological manifestations. Patients may also develop antibodies against the infused enzyme. Alternative approaches include substrate reduction therapy, pharmacological chaperones, and gene therapy.
