Ophiocordyceps unilateralis

Scientific classification
Kingdom: Fungi
Division: Ascomycota
Class: Sordariomycetes
Order: Hypocreales
Family: Ophiocordycipitaceae
Genus: Ophiocordyceps
Species: O. unilateralis

Ophiocordyceps unilateralis, commonly known as the zombie-ant fungus, is an insect-pathogenic fungus discovered by the British naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace in 1859, and currently found predominantly in tropical forest ecosystems. It infects ants of the tribe Camponotini, including carpenter ants, and its full pathogenesis is characterised by altering the behaviour of the host ant in a way that facilitates the distribution of the fungus's spores.

Life cycle

Spores land on a foraging ant and penetrate its exoskeleton. Inside, the fungus grows as free-floating yeast-like cells, spreading through the body and consuming soft tissues while leaving vital organs intact. When the fungus is ready to reproduce, it manipulates the ant to leave its colony, climb vegetation, and bite down on the underside of a leaf or twig — the "death grip" — in a location with temperature and humidity suited to fungal growth.

Host manipulation

The manipulation is an example of an extended phenotype: fungal cells surround the ant's muscle fibres and form networks that coordinate the host's movements, while the brain itself remains largely uninvaded. The fungus secretes compounds that affect the host's nervous system and muscles, producing convulsions and the final biting behaviour, which is often synchronised to solar noon.

Reproduction and ecology

After the ant dies, a stalk (stroma) grows from its head and produces a fruiting body that releases spores onto the forest floor below, creating an infectious "killing field" beneath the elevated corpse. Dense aggregations of such dead ants are known as graveyards. The fungus is itself attacked by hyperparasitic fungi, which can castrate the fruiting body and limit its spread, stabilising the host–parasite system.

In culture

The fungus inspired the fungal infection in the video game and television series The Last of Us, although the real fungus poses no threat to humans.
