Forestry in India

Forestry in India is a significant rural industry and a major environmental resource. India is one of the ten most forest-rich countries of the world, with forest and tree cover amounting to roughly a quarter of its geographical area — around 80 million hectares according to recent India State of Forest Reports.

History

Organized forestry in India began under British rule with the appointment of Dietrich Brandis as the first Inspector General of Forests in 1864 and the Indian Forest Acts of 1865 and 1878, which reserved extensive forests for state control and timber production, especially teak and sal. The Indian Forest Service was created to administer the estate. After independence, the National Forest Policy of 1952, and its revision in 1988, shifted emphasis toward conservation, ecological stability, and the needs of forest-dwelling communities.

Administration and law

Forests are administered by the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change and state forest departments, staffed by the Indian Forest Service. Key legislation includes the Indian Forest Act 1927, the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980, and the Forest Rights Act 2006, which recognized the rights of Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers. The Forest Survey of India publishes a biennial assessment of forest cover.

Forest types and conservation

India's forests range from tropical rainforest in the Western Ghats and Northeast, through moist and dry deciduous teak and sal forests of the central plateau, to Himalayan coniferous forests and alpine scrub. They harbour much of the world's wild tiger population and other megafauna. The country maintains a large network of national parks, tiger reserves under Project Tiger (launched 1973), and wildlife sanctuaries.
