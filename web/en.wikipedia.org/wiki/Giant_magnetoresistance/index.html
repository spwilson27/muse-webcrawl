Giant magnetoresistance

Giant magnetoresistance (GMR) is a quantum mechanical magnetoresistance effect observed in thin-film structures composed of alternating ferromagnetic and non-magnetic conductive layers. The effect manifests itself as a significant decrease (typically 10–80%) in electrical resistance in the presence of a magnetic field. When the magnetisations of adjacent ferromagnetic layers are antiparallel, scattering of conduction electrons is high and resistance is maximal; an applied field aligns the layers, reducing scattering and resistance.

Discovery

GMR was discovered independently in 1988 by groups led by Albert Fert at the University of Paris-Sud, working with iron/chromium multilayers, and Peter Grünberg at the Jülich Research Centre in Germany, working with trilayer structures. Grünberg's group filed the first patent. Fert and Grünberg shared the 2007 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery. The effect is a founding phenomenon of spintronics, which exploits the electron's spin as well as its charge.

Mechanism

Conduction electrons carry spin up or spin down. In a ferromagnet, electrons whose spin is aligned with the local magnetisation scatter weakly, while oppositely aligned electrons scatter strongly (the two-current model). With antiparallel layer magnetisations, both spin channels encounter a strongly scattering layer, so total resistance is high; with parallel alignment, one channel passes through nearly unimpeded and short-circuits the other, so resistance is low.

Applications

GMR was commercialised with extraordinary speed. IBM introduced read heads based on the related spin valve structure in hard disk drives in 1997, enabling dramatic increases in areal density and the miniaturisation of storage. GMR sensors are also used in automotive systems, industrial position sensing, biosensors and non-volatile magnetic random-access memory research.
