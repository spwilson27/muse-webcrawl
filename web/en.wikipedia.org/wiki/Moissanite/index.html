Moissanite

Moissanite is the naturally occurring mineral silicon carbide (SiC), discovered in 1893 by the French chemist Henri Moissan in material from the Canyon Diablo meteorite crater in Arizona. Natural moissanite is exceedingly rare, so virtually all moissanite sold today is laboratory-grown. It is prized as a gemstone resembling diamond and as an industrial abrasive and semiconductor material.

Occurrence

Moissan first identified the mineral in meteorite fragments; natural crystals have since been found only in tiny quantities in meteorites, kimberlites, and a few other exotic settings. Silicon carbide was, however, synthesized industrially from 1891 by Edward Acheson's process and sold as the abrasive "Carborundum".

Gemstone use

Gem-quality silicon carbide, marketed from the late 1990s, rivals diamond in brilliance and exceeds it in dispersion ("fire"), while costing far less. It is nearly as hard as diamond and highly durable, though its double refraction and slightly different optical character allow gemmologists to distinguish it. As diamond simulants, moissanite gemstones are marketed as an economical and ethically uncomplicated alternative.

Industrial importance

Beyond gems, silicon carbide is a wide-bandgap semiconductor used in high-power, high-temperature electronics, as well as in abrasives, cutting tools, ceramic composites, and LED production — applications that dwarf its gemstone market in economic significance.
