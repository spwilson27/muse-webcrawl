Occupational hearing loss

Occupational hearing loss (placeholder image)

Occupational hearing loss (OHL) is hearing loss that occurs as a result of occupational hazards, such as excessive noise and ototoxic chemicals. Noise is a common workplace hazard, and recognized as the risk factor for noise-induced hearing loss. Continuous exposure to loud noise can result in significant disability, including tinnitus and inability to hear speech. Hearing loss caused by noise typically affects frequencies around 3,000–6,000 Hz, producing the characteristic "notch" on an audiogram.

Noise-induced hearing loss

Occupational noise-induced hearing loss develops gradually from chronic exposure to sound levels at or above 85 dB(A) over an eight-hour time-weighted average, though impulsive noise can damage hearing immediately. Industries with high risk include mining, construction, manufacturing, agriculture, transportation, and music. An estimated tens of millions of workers worldwide are exposed to hazardous noise; in the United States, about 22 million workers are exposed each year, and occupational hearing loss remains one of the most common work-related illnesses.

Prevention

Prevention follows the hierarchy of controls: eliminating or reducing noise at the source through engineering controls, administrative controls such as rotating workers, and hearing protection devices such as earplugs and earmuffs. Regulations by bodies such as OSHA and NIOSH in the United States, and the EU Physical Agents (Noise) Directive in Europe, set exposure limits and require hearing conservation programs with audiometric monitoring.
