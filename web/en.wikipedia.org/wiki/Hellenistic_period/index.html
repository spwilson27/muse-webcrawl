Hellenistic period

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Pharos lighthouse of Alexandria, one of the Seven Wonders, on a Roman coin]

The Hellenistic period covers Mediterranean and West Asian history from the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC to the Roman conquest of Egypt in 30 BC. Alexander's empire was divided among his generals (the Diadochi) into rival kingdoms — the Ptolemaic kingdom in Egypt, the Seleucid Empire in Asia, Antigonid Macedon and later Attalid Pergamon — which spread Greek language, cities and culture from Egypt to Afghanistan while absorbing local traditions. It was an age of great cities (Alexandria above all), royal libraries and museums, and advances in mathematics, astronomy and engineering.

Kingdoms and culture

After decades of Diadochi wars, three durable kingdoms emerged. Ptolemaic Alexandria, with its Museum and Library and the Pharos lighthouse, became the intellectual capital of the world; Euclid wrote his Elements there, Eratosthenes measured the Earth, and Aristarchus proposed a heliocentric cosmos. Seleucid kings founded cities across Iran and Mesopotamia; in Bactria, Greco-Bactrian and Indo-Greek kings carried Hellenic art as far as India, where it shaped Buddhist sculpture.

Roman conquest

Rome defeated Macedon (Pydna, 168 BC), absorbed Pergamon by bequest (133 BC), destroyed the Seleucid remnant when Pompey annexed Syria (64 BC), and took Egypt after Actium (31 BC) and the deaths of Antony and Cleopatra (30 BC). Hellenistic culture, however, conquered its conquerors: Greek remained the lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean, and Hellenistic science, art and kingship profoundly shaped the Roman world and early Christianity.
