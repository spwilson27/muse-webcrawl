Mitsubishi A6M Zero

The Mitsubishi A6M "Zero" is a long-range carrier-based fighter aircraft formerly manufactured by Mitsubishi Aircraft Company, a part of Mitsubishi Heavy Industries. It was operated by the Imperial Japanese Navy (IJN) from 1940 to 1945. The A6M was designated as the Mitsubishi Navy Type 0 carrier fighter (零式艦上戦闘機, rei shiki kanjō sentōki), or the Mitsubishi A6M Rei-sen. The A6M was usually referred to by its pilots as the Reisen (zero fighter), "0" being the last digit of the imperial year 2600 (1940) when it entered service.

Design and development

Designed by a team led by Jiro Horikoshi to a demanding 1937 navy specification, the Zero combined a very light airframe — built extensively from extra super duralumin — with a low-powered but efficient Nakajima Sakae radial engine. The result was an aircraft with exceptional range (over 3,100 km with a drop tank), superb manoeuvrability at low and medium speeds, and a heavy armament of two 20 mm cannon and two rifle-calibre machine guns. To save weight, the design omitted armour protection for the pilot and self-sealing fuel tanks, weaknesses that later proved fatal.

Operational history

The Zero debuted in combat over China in August 1940, where it swept aside opposing fighters. In the opening months of the Pacific War it was the finest carrier fighter in the world, playing a central role in the attack on Pearl Harbor, the Indian Ocean raid, and the battles of the Coral Sea and Midway. Its extraordinary range and turning ability shocked Allied pilots, who had underestimated Japanese aviation.

The turning point came in June 1942, when US forces recovered a nearly intact Zero from Akutan Island in the Aleutians. Flight testing revealed its weaknesses: sluggish controls at high speed, poor dive performance, and extreme fragility. Armed with this knowledge and with new fighters such as the F6F Hellcat and F4U Corsair, Allied pilots adopted tactics — energy fighting and the Thach weave — that neutralised the Zero's advantages. Japan's failure to field a successor in numbers, and the loss of its experienced pilots, left the ageing Zero outclassed. In the final year of the war many were expended in kamikaze attacks.

Legacy

Nearly 11,000 Zeros of all variants were built, more than any other Japanese aircraft of the war. The type remains the most famous Japanese fighter of the Second World War, and a small number of survivors are preserved in museums, with a handful still airworthy.
