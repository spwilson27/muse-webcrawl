Fireside poets

The Fireside poets — also known as the schoolroom or household poets — were a group of 19th-century American poets associated with New England. They took their name from the practice of families reading their poems aloud beside the fireside, and for generations their works were memorised by American schoolchildren. The group is generally taken to include Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, William Cullen Bryant, John Greenleaf Whittier, James Russell Lowell and Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr.

Characteristics

The Fireside poets wrote in conventional forms — regular metre, rhyme and stanza patterns inherited from British models — and treated familiar, often moralising subjects: nature, patriotism, family life, death and consolation, and American history and legend. Their poetry was meant to be both entertaining and edifying, and it achieved an unprecedented popular readership for American verse, rivalling the popularity of British poets such as Tennyson and Wordsworth.

Principal figures

Henry Wadsworth Longfellow (1807–1882) was the most famous American poet of his century, best known for The Song of Hiawatha (1855), Evangeline (1847) and "Paul Revere's Ride" (1860). William Cullen Bryant (1794–1878), author of "Thanatopsis", was also a leading journalist as editor of the New York Evening Post. John Greenleaf Whittier (1807–1892), a Quaker abolitionist, wrote Snow-Bound (1866) and many anti-slavery poems. James Russell Lowell (1819–1891) wrote The Biglow Papers and served as editor of The Atlantic Monthly. Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. (1809–1894), a physician and essayist, coined the name of The Atlantic and wrote "Old Ironsides".

Decline and reassessment

The Fireside poets' dominance faded with the rise of modernism in the early 20th century; critics dismissed them as genteel and derivative compared with Walt Whitman and Emily Dickinson, their contemporaries who were long less widely read. Later scholars have reassessed their cultural role in building an American national literature and a mass readership for poetry, and poems such as Lowell's and Whittier's abolitionist verse have drawn renewed attention.
