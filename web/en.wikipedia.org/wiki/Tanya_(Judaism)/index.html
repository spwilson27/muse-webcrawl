Tanya (Judaism)

The Tanya (Hebrew: תניא), formally known as Likkutei Amarim ("Collected Sayings"), is the foundational work of Chabad Hasidic philosophy. It was written by Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi (1745–1812), the founder of the Chabad movement, and first published in 1796. The work's name derives from its opening word, citing a Talmudic passage beginning Tanya ("It was taught in a baraita").

Contents

The Tanya consists of five parts. The first and longest, the Sefer Shel Beinonim ("Book of the Intermediates"), presents Chabad's psychology of the soul: every Jew possesses two souls — a divine soul (nefesh Elokit) and an animal soul (nefesh habehamit) — locked in lifelong struggle. It defines three classes of person: the tzadik (righteous), the rasha (wicked), and the beinoni (intermediate), arguing that the beinoni — who never sins in deed, though his inner struggle continues — is the attainable ideal for most people. The remaining parts treat the unity of God (Sha'ar HaYichud VeHaEmunah), repentance (Iggeret HaTeshuvah), and include letters of guidance and consolation.

Influence

The Tanya is studied daily by Chabad Hasidim as part of the Chitas cycle and has been published in thousands of editions, including special printings in thousands of locations worldwide by Chabad emissaries. It is regarded as the "written Torah" of Chabad Hasidism, complementing the oral teachings of its rebbes.
