Hill lists in the British Isles

Hill lists in the British Isles are catalogues of hills and mountains classified by height, topographic prominence, and other criteria, used by hillwalkers and "peak baggers" who aim to climb every summit on a given list. Lists are defined for Britain and Ireland and combine strict metrical thresholds with measures of relative prominence so that subsidiary bumps are separated from independent peaks.

Ben Nevis (1,345 m), the highest summit in the British Isles and the best-known Munro

Principal lists

  • Munros — Scottish mountains over 3,000 feet (914.4 m), first listed by Sir Hugh Munro in 1891. Walkers who complete them are "Munroists".
  • Corbetts — Scottish peaks between 2,500 and 3,000 feet with a prominence of at least 500 feet, listed by John Rooke Corbett.
  • Grahams — Scottish peaks between 600 and 762 metres with a prominence of at least 150 metres.
  • Furths — mountains in England, Wales and Ireland over 3,000 feet, "furth" of Scotland.
  • Hewitts — hills in England, Wales and Ireland over 2,000 feet with a prominence of at least 30 metres.
  • Wainwrights — the 214 fells of the Lake District described by Alfred Wainwright in his Pictorial Guides.
  • Marilyns — any hill in Britain and Ireland with a prominence of at least 150 metres, regardless of height; the list underpins the prominence-based classifications used throughout these islands.

Prominence and the Database of British and Irish Hills

Modern lists depend heavily on prominence — the height of a summit relative to the surrounding terrain — to define independent peaks. The Database of British and Irish Hills (DoBIH), compiled from survey data and maintained by volunteer surveyors using GPS measurement, provides the standard data for most classifications and has repeatedly redefined lists as measurements improved, moving hills between categories and prompting debates among list-keepers and completers.

Peak bagging culture

Completing recognised lists is a long-established pursuit: the first Munro round was completed in 1901 by the Reverend A. E. Robertson, and record-breaking continuous rounds — such as the winter Munro round and the fastest self-propelled rounds — attract wide attention. Registers of completers are maintained for the major lists by bodies such as the Scottish Mountaineering Club.
