Microfossil

A microfossil is a fossil that is generally between about 0.001 mm and 1 mm in size, whose study requires the use of a microscope. Microfossils are among the most abundant and informative fossils in the geological record, because enormous numbers of them can be recovered from small samples of sedimentary rock. The discipline devoted to them is called micropaleontology.

Types

Microfossils may be the shells (tests) of protists such as foraminifera and radiolaria; the frustules of diatoms; plates of coccolithophores; the carapaces of tiny crustaceans called ostracods; the tooth-like elements of the extinct conodont animals; microscopic parts of larger organisms such as sponge spicules and fish teeth; and organic-walled remains including pollen, spores, dinoflagellate cysts, and acritarchs. They may be calcareous, siliceous, phosphatic, or organic in composition, which determines how they are extracted from rock — by washing, sieving, or dissolution in acids.

Significance

Because many microfossil species were widespread, evolved rapidly, and are preserved in huge numbers, they are ideal index fossils for biostratigraphy: dating and correlating rock layers. The petroleum industry relies heavily on foraminifera and palynomorphs to correlate strata in drill cores. Microfossils from deep-sea sediment cores are also central to palaeoclimate research, since the species composition and the isotopic chemistry of foraminiferal shells record past ocean temperatures and ice volumes. Some of the earliest evidence for life on Earth, more than three billion years old, consists of microfossil-like structures in ancient cherts.
