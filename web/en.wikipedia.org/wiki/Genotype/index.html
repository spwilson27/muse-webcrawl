The genotype of an organism is its complete set of genetic material. The term can also refer to the two alleles inherited at a particular genetic locus. The genotype, together with environmental and developmental factors, determines the organism's observable characteristics, its phenotype.

Genotype and phenotype

The terms genotype and phenotype were introduced by Danish botanist Wilhelm Johannsen in work published between 1909 and 1911, transforming the study of heredity by separating the hereditary constitution of an organism from its appearance. A single genotype can give rise to different phenotypes in different environments (phenotypic plasticity), and many different genotypes can produce the same phenotype — for example, because a recessive allele is masked by a dominant one, so that carriers of genotype Aa look identical to AA individuals.

Notation and determination

Genotypes at a locus are conventionally written as pairs of letters (AA, Aa, aa) for diploid organisms; AA and aa are homozygous, Aa is heterozygous. An organism's genotype can be determined by genotyping assays and DNA sequencing, by test crosses in classical genetics, or by studying the genotypes of offspring. In humans, genome-wide association studies relate genotypic variation at millions of single-nucleotide polymorphisms to traits and diseases.

Further concepts

Related ideas include the genotype–phenotype map describing how genetic variation translates into traits, the norm of reaction (the range of phenotypes one genotype produces across environments), and genotypic frequency in population genetics, where the Hardy–Weinberg principle predicts genotype proportions from allele frequencies.
