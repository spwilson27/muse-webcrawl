Nuclear binding energy

Nuclear binding energy is the minimum energy required to disassemble the nucleus of an atom into its constituent protons and neutrons, or equivalently the energy released when those nucleons combine to form the nucleus. It arises from the mass defect: a bound nucleus has less mass than the sum of its free nucleons, the difference corresponding to the binding energy through Einstein's relation E = mc².

The binding energy curve

Binding energy per nucleon rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks near iron-56 and nickel-62 (about 8.8 MeV per nucleon), and declines slowly for heavier nuclei. This curve explains the energy released both by fusion of light elements — the process powering stars — and by fission of very heavy nuclei such as uranium-235.

Calculation

The binding energy is computed from the measured atomic masses: the mass defect Δm is multiplied by c². Semi-empirical mass formula (liquid drop model) terms — volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry and pairing — approximate binding energies across the chart of nuclides and explain the shape of the curve.
