Caesarea Philippi was an ancient Roman city located at the southwestern base of Mount Hermon, near one of the main sources of the Jordan River. The site, known today as Banias, lay near the spring of the Banias River and was famous in antiquity for its sanctuary of the god Pan.

History

The settlement was originally called Paneas (or Panias), after the half-goat, half-man deity Pan, who was worshipped at a grotto beside the spring that emerged from a cave in the cliff face. Herod the Great built a temple of white marble to Augustus near the sanctuary. On Herod's death, the district passed to his son Herod Philip II, who enlarged the city around 2–1 BC and renamed it Caesarea in honour of the emperor Augustus; it became known as Caesarea Philippi ("Philip's Caesarea") to distinguish it from Caesarea Maritima on the Mediterranean coast.

In the New Testament

In the Synoptic Gospels, the region of Caesarea Philippi is the setting of Peter's confession of faith: Jesus asks his disciples "Who do people say that I am?" and Peter answers, "You are the Christ." Matthew's account (16:13–20) adds the saying "You are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church," giving the site enduring significance in Christian tradition, particularly for the Roman Catholic doctrine of the primacy of Peter.

Later history

The city remained an important cult and administrative centre under the Romans and Byzantines; bishops of Paneas attended early church councils. It later passed through Muslim and Crusader hands — the Crusaders built the nearby fortress of Subeibe (Nimrod Fortress) to guard the road to Damascus. The town declined in the later Middle Ages.

Site today

The archaeological site, within the Banias nature reserve, preserves the sanctuary of Pan with its carved niches, the remains of Herod's palace complex, Roman and Byzantine streets, and the spring that feeds the Banias stream, one of the three principal sources of the Jordan River.
