Disgorgement

Disgorgement is a remedy in law requiring a party who has been unjustly enriched to give up the profits or benefits obtained through wrongful conduct. Rather than compensating a victim for losses (as damages do) or punishing the wrongdoer (as penalties do), disgorgement focuses on stripping the wrongdoer of ill-gotten gains, thereby preventing unjust enrichment and deterring future violations.

In United States securities law

Disgorgement is most prominently used by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) in enforcement actions for violations of the federal securities laws, such as insider trading, accounting fraud, and unregistered securities offerings. In such cases the SEC may seek disgorgement of profits gained or losses avoided, plus prejudgment interest, often alongside civil monetary penalties.

The scope of the remedy has been shaped by the Supreme Court of the United States. In Kokesh v. SEC (2017), the Court held that SEC disgorgement constitutes a "penalty" subject to the five-year statute of limitations in 28 U.S.C. § 2462. In Liu v. SEC (2020), the Court held that disgorgement remains permissible as equitable relief under the Securities Exchange Act, provided it does not exceed the wrongdoer's net profits and is awarded for the benefit of victims. Congress subsequently addressed the issue in the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2021, which amended the Exchange Act to expressly authorize the SEC to seek disgorgement and extended the limitations period for scienter-based claims.

Other contexts

Disgorgement also appears in contexts such as breach of fiduciary duty, intellectual property infringement, and restitution cases generally, and equivalent remedies exist in other legal systems. In English law, the related concept is an "account of profits".
