The Critique of Pure Reason (German: Kritik der reinen Vernunft) is a book by the German philosopher Immanuel Kant, first published in 1781 with a substantially revised second edition in 1787. It is one of the most influential works in the history of philosophy and the first of Kant's three Critiques, followed by the Critique of Practical Reason (1788) and the Critique of Judgment (1790).

Overview

Kant investigates the basis and scope of human knowledge, asking how synthetic a priori judgments — propositions that are informative yet known independently of experience, such as those of mathematics and metaphysics — are possible. His "Copernican revolution" in philosophy proposes that objects must conform to our knowledge rather than our knowledge conforming to objects: the mind actively structures experience.

Structure and doctrines

Transcendental Aesthetic

Space and time are not concepts abstracted from experience but pure forms of sensible intuition — a priori conditions under which anything can appear to us at all.

Transcendental Analytic

The understanding supplies twelve categories (including causality and substance) which the mind imposes on the manifold of intuition, making objective experience possible. Kant distinguishes phenomena (things as they appear) from noumena (things in themselves), which cannot be known.

Transcendental Dialectic

Kant argues that reason, when it seeks the unconditioned, falls into illusion: the paralogisms of rational psychology, the antinomies of cosmology, and the ideal of God. The traditional proofs of God's existence are criticised; metaphysics as knowledge of the supersensible is shown to exceed the bounds of possible experience.

Editions and terminology

Citations use "A" for the first edition (1781) and "B" for the second (1787), e.g. A51/B75: "Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind."

Reception

Initially received with confusion, the Critique soon transformed philosophy, giving rise to German idealism (Fichte, Schelling, Hegel) and shaping epistemology, philosophy of science, and later critical theory. Its labyrinthine style has made it famously difficult, yet it remains a cornerstone of modern philosophy.
