Single-session therapy

FieldPsychotherapy
Key figureMoshe Talmon
FormatOne planned session

Single-session therapy (SST) is a form of psychotherapy deliberately conducted as a single encounter, on the understanding that it may be the only session a client attends. Developed most prominently by Moshe Talmon in the 1980s and 1990s, it structures the meeting to maximise therapeutic benefit in one sitting while leaving the door open for further help.

Background

Talmon's research at Kaiser Permanente found that the most common number of therapy sessions attended was one, and that many clients reported significant improvement after that single visit. SST reframes this pattern: rather than treating a lone session as dropout, it plans therapy around it.

Practice

Techniques emphasise rapid engagement, identifying a clear focus, drawing on the client's own resources and strengths, and ending with concrete steps. Walk-in and by-appointment single-session services are now common in community mental-health and university counselling settings.

Evidence

Follow-up studies report that a substantial proportion of clients, often around 60-80 percent, consider their single session sufficient for the concern they brought, with satisfaction rates comparable to longer therapies for suitable problems.
