Singularity (mathematics)

Singularity
FieldMathematical analysis; complex analysis; algebraic geometry
DefinitionA point at which a mathematical object is not well-behaved (undefined, not differentiable, degenerate)
Examples1/x at x = 0; |x| at x = 0; cusp of y² = x³
Complex typesRemovable singularity, pole, essential singularity
Related theorySingularity theory (Thom, Arnold)

In mathematics, a singularity is a point at which a mathematical object — a function, curve, or other structure — is not well-defined or fails to behave regularly, for example because it is undefined, infinite, not differentiable, or otherwise exceptional. Singularities are often the most interesting points of a mathematical object, and their classification is a central theme in analysis, geometry and mathematical physics.

The function 1/x has a singularity at x = 0, where it diverges to infinity from both sides.

Examples in real analysis

The function f(x) = 1/x is singular at x = 0, where it is undefined and its values grow without bound nearby. The absolute-value function f(x) = |x| is continuous everywhere but singular at 0 in the sense of failing to be differentiable there. Catastrophe theory and singularity theory study how such exceptional points appear and bifurcate in families of functions.

Complex singularities

In complex analysis, isolated singularities of a holomorphic function are classified into three types: removable singularities (such as sin z / z at 0, which can be filled in to give a holomorphic function), poles (such as 1/z² at 0, where values diverge in a controlled Laurent-series fashion), and essential singularities (such as e1/z at 0), near which, by the Casorati–Weierstrass and Picard theorems, the function comes arbitrarily close to every complex value. In algebraic geometry, a singular point of a curve or variety is one where the tangent space is not well-defined, such as the cusp of y² = x³ at the origin; resolving such singularities is a major achievement of modern geometry.
