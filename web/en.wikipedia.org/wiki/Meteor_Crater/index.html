Meteor Crater, also known as Barringer Crater, is a meteorite impact crater located approximately 37 miles (60 km) east of Flagstaff and 18 miles (29 km) west of Winslow in the desert of northern Arizona, United States. The site was formerly known as the Canyon Diablo Crater, and fragments of the meteorite are officially called the Canyon Diablo Meteorite.

Despite its importance as a geological site, the crater is not protected as a national monument, a status that would require federal ownership. It was designated a National Natural Landmark in November 1967.

Formation

The crater was created about 50,000 years ago during the Pleistocene epoch, when the local climate on the Colorado Plateau was cooler and damper. The object that excavated the crater was a nickel-iron meteorite about 160 feet (50 meters) across. The impact energy has been estimated at about 10 megatons of TNT. The meteorite was mostly vaporized upon impact, leaving few remains in the crater.

Geology

The crater lies at an elevation of 1,719 m (5,640 ft) above sea level. It is about 1,200 m (3,900 ft) in diameter, some 170 m (560 ft) deep, and is surrounded by a rim that rises 45 m (148 ft) above the surrounding plains. The center of the crater is filled with 210–240 m (690–790 ft) of rubble lying above crater bedrock.

Discovery and investigations

The crater came to the attention of European-American scientists after its discovery by settlers in the 19th century. In 1903, mining engineer and businessman Daniel M. Barringer suggested that the crater had been produced by the impact of a large iron meteorite. Barringer's company, the Standard Iron Company, staked a claim and attempted to mine the meteorite for iron, but the main mass had largely vaporized and the venture was unsuccessful. Barringer spent 27 years trying to convince the scientific community of the crater's meteoritic origin.

Confirmation of the impact origin came in the 1960s through the work of Eugene Merle Shoemaker, whose discovery of the high-pressure minerals coesite and stishovite at the site provided key evidence of an impact event.

Recent history

During the 1960s and 1970s, NASA astronauts trained in the crater to prepare for the Apollo missions to the Moon. Today the crater is owned by the Barringer family through their Barringer Crater Company and is operated as a tourist attraction with a visitor center on the northern rim.
