Grading in education

Grading in education is the process of applying standardized measurements for varying levels of achievement in a course. Grades are assigned as letters (usually A through F) or as a range of numbers (e.g., 1 to 100), by a percentage, or as descriptors ranging from excellent to poor, depending on the educational system and level of study.

History

The earliest university grading systems used verbal descriptions. In the United States, the letter grade scale was introduced at Mount Holyoke College in 1897, assigning grades from A (excellent) through E (failure) based on percentage ranges. By the mid-twentieth century, the five-letter scale had become standard in American schools, with F denoting failure.

Grade point average

The grade point average (GPA) is a calculation of the average of all of a student's grades over a given period. On the common four-point scale, an A corresponds to 4.0, a B to 3.0, a C to 2.0, a D to 1.0, and an F to 0.0. Weighted GPAs may award extra points for advanced or honours courses.

Systems by country

Many European countries use numeric scales, such as the German 1-to-6 scale (where 1 is best), the French 0-to-20 scale, and the Italian 0-to-30 scale for university examinations. In the United Kingdom, undergraduate degrees are classified as first-class, upper second-class, lower second-class, and third-class honours. India commonly uses a 10-point CGPA scale in higher education.

Criticism

Critics argue that grades can reduce intrinsic motivation, encourage competition over learning, and poorly capture complex achievement. Alternatives include standards-based grading, narrative evaluations, mastery learning, and pass/fail systems, used by some schools and medical programmes.
