Lynn Margulis

Lynn Margulis (born Lynn Alexander; March 5, 1938 – November 22, 2011) was an American evolutionary biologist, University Professor at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, and the primary modern proponent of the endosymbiotic theory of the origin of eukaryotic organelles. Her 1967 paper "On the Origin of Mitosing Cells," published under the name Lynn Sagan, proposed that mitochondria and chloroplasts originated as free-living bacteria taken up by early cells; initially ridiculed or ignored, the theory became textbook orthodoxy as molecular evidence accumulated.

Career

Margulis earned her bachelor's degree from the University of Chicago at age 18, a master's in genetics and zoology from the University of Wisconsin–Madison, and a PhD from UC Berkeley in 1966. After teaching at Brandeis University, she joined Boston University in 1966 and moved to the University of Massachusetts Amherst in 1988, where she remained until her death. Her book Origin of Eukaryotic Cells (1970) laid out her serial endosymbiotic theory in detail.

Endosymbiotic theory

Building on earlier work by Konstantin Mereschkowski and Ivan Wallin, Margulis argued that mitochondria descend from aerobic bacteria and chloroplasts from photosynthetic cyanobacteria. The discovery that both organelles possess their own DNA and bacterial-type ribosomes strongly vindicated the theory. Her further claim that eukaryotic flagella derive from symbiotic spirochaetes was not generally accepted, though Margulis defended it throughout her career. She championed symbiosis and symbiogenesis as major drivers of evolution alongside mutation and natural selection.

Gaia hypothesis

With British scientist James Lovelock, Margulis developed the Gaia hypothesis, proposing that the Earth functions as a self-regulating system in which the biosphere, especially the microbial world, actively maintains conditions suitable for life. She identified the atmospheric and sedimentary roles of micro-organisms as central to this regulation.

Recognition and controversy

Margulis was elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 1983 and received the National Medal of Science from President Bill Clinton in 1999; she also shared the Darwin–Wallace Medal of the Linnean Society in 2008. In later years several of her heterodox positions — including scepticism about HIV as the cause of AIDS and her support of a petition questioning aspects of the 9/11 attacks — drew strong criticism from peers. She died of a haemorrhagic stroke in 2011. Her son Dorion Sagan, a science writer, collaborated with her on several books.
