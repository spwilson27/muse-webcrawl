Transaminase

In biochemistry, a transaminase is an enzyme that catalyzes a type of reaction between an amino acid and an α-keto acid, known as transamination. Specifically, this reaction involves transferring an amino group from the amino acid to the keto acid, forming a new amino acid and a new keto acid. Transaminases require the coenzyme pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), a vitamin B6 derivative.

Mechanism

The reaction proceeds in two half-steps via a Schiff base (internal aldimine) between PLP and a lysine residue in the active site; the amino acid substrate replaces the lysine, is deaminated to its keto acid, and the coenzyme (now pyridoxamine phosphate) aminates a second keto acid to release the product amino acid.

Clinical significance

Two transaminases dominate clinical testing: alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST). Both are normally concentrated inside cells, especially hepatocytes; elevated blood levels indicate cell damage, most commonly liver injury such as viral hepatitis, fatty liver disease or drug toxicity, while AST also rises after heart or skeletal-muscle damage. Transaminase levels are a standard part of liver function tests.

Examples

Beyond ALT and AST, the family includes branched-chain amino acid transaminases, ornithine aminotransferase and the GABA transaminase targeted by the drug vigabatrin.
