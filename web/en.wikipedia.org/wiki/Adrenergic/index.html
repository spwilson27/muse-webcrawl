Adrenergic

FieldNeuroscience, pharmacology
NeurotransmittersAdrenaline (epinephrine), noradrenaline (norepinephrine)
ReceptorsAlpha (α) and beta (β) adrenergic receptors

Adrenergic is an adjective describing cells, nerve fibers, receptors, or drugs that are activated by, or release, the catecholamines adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine). The term derives from "adrenaline." Adrenergic transmission is a central part of the sympathetic nervous system and underlies much of the body's "fight or flight" response.

Adrenergic neurons and transmission

Most postganglionic sympathetic neurons are adrenergic, releasing noradrenaline from varicosities at their terminals. Adrenaline is released into the bloodstream chiefly by the adrenal medulla. After release, catecholamines are removed by reuptake into the nerve terminal and by enzymatic degradation by monoamine oxidase (MAO) and catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT).

Receptors and drugs

Catecholamines act on adrenergic receptors — G protein-coupled receptors divided into alpha (α1, α2) and beta (β1, β2, β3) families. Drugs that mimic catecholamines are called sympathomimetics or adrenergic agonists (e.g., salbutamol, phenylephrine), and those that block them are adrenergic antagonists, including the widely used beta blockers. The contrasting system that uses acetylcholine is called cholinergic.
