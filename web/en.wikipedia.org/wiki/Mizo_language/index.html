The Mizo language, also known as Lushai, is a Tibeto-Burman language spoken natively by the Mizo people of Mizoram in north-eastern India, and by related communities in Myanmar's Chin State and the Chittagong Hill Tracts of Bangladesh. It is the lingua franca of Mizoram and one of the state's official languages, used in education, administration, media and literature.

History

Mizo was first reduced to writing in the 1890s by Christian missionaries, notably J. H. Lorrain and F. W. Savidge, who devised a phonetic orthography based on the Latin alphabet using Hunterian transliteration principles. The missionaries also compiled the first dictionaries and grammars, and the language of their publications, based on the Duhlian dialect, became the standard literary form.

Before the arrival of a written standard, the Mizo tribes spoke a cluster of related dialects; the central Duhlian dialect spread widely through trade, intermarriage and mission schooling.

Phonology and grammar

Mizo is a tonal language with several contrasting tones that distinguish word meanings, although tone is not marked in the standard orthography. It is an agglutinative language with a subject–object–verb word order, postpositions rather than prepositions, and a rich system of verbal particles marking aspect, negation and mood.

Nouns do not inflect for gender or number in the Indo-European manner, and plural and case relations are expressed through particles and word order.

Literature and media

Mizo literature developed from early mission-era hymnals and translations, including a full Bible translation, into a modern body of poetry, novels and drama. The language is served by daily newspapers such as Vanglaini, founded in 1978, and by radio and television broadcasting from Aizawl. Mizo is taught in schools throughout Mizoram and studied at university level.
