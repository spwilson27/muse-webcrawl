Tenderloin

Tenderloin may refer to a cut of meat, or — most famously — to a neighbourhood name given to red-light and vice districts in several American cities. The term is generally traced to New York police captain Alexander S. "Clubber" Williams, who, on being transferred in 1876 to a Manhattan precinct rich in graft, is said to have remarked that he had been eating chuck steak but would now eat tenderloin.

A street in the Tenderloin

New York City

New York's Tenderloin was an entertainment and red-light district in Manhattan in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, roughly bounded by 23rd Street to the south, 42nd Street to the north, Fifth Avenue to the east and Seventh Avenue to the west. It was known for its theatres, saloons, gambling houses and brothels, and for endemic police corruption.

San Francisco

The Tenderloin of San Francisco is a neighbourhood in the downtown area, roughly bordered by Geary Street, Market Street, Van Ness Avenue and Taylor Street. Long one of the city's poorest districts, it is known for its single-room-occupancy hotels, its large Southeast Asian refugee community, its history of vice and jazz clubs, and more recently for its concentration of homelessness and drug use alongside active community organisations and arts venues. Much of the district is listed on the National Register of Historic Places as the Uptown Tenderloin Historic District.

The meat

As a cut, tenderloin is the long, narrow muscle (the psoas major) along the spine of beef, pork and other animals, prized as the most tender cut; beef tenderloin is the source of filet mignon and of Chateaubriand.
