An autobiography, sometimes informally called an auto, is a self-written account of one's own life. The word derives from the Greek autos (self), bios (life) and graphein (to write); it first appeared in English in the late eighteenth century. Unlike a biography, which is written by another person, an autobiography presents the author's retrospective narrative of their experiences, with the author, narrator and protagonist understood to be the same person — the convention theorised by Philippe Lejeune as the "autobiographical pact".

Distinctions

Autobiography differs from memoir in scope: an autobiography typically covers the author's whole life or a formative arc of development, whereas a memoir focuses on a particular period, theme or set of events. Diaries and journals, written contemporaneously rather than retrospectively, are related forms sometimes published as autobiographical sources.

History

Spiritual autobiography has ancient roots: Augustine's Confessions (c. 397–400) established a model of introspective self-examination, and Margery Kempe's fifteenth-century Book is an early English example. The genre expanded with print culture and rising literacy; notable landmarks include Benvenuto Cellini's Vita, Rousseau's Confessions (1782), slave narratives such as Frederick Douglass's Narrative (1845), and, in the modern era, bestselling political and celebrity memoirs. Questions of memory's reliability, self-fashioning and factual accuracy are persistent critical themes.
