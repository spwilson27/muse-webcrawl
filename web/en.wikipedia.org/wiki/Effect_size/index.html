Effect size

Two overlapping distributions; the effect size measures their separation

In statistics, an effect size is a value measuring the strength of the relationship between two variables in a population, or a sample-based estimate of that quantity. Unlike p-values, effect sizes do not depend on sample size, so they convey the practical magnitude of a finding and can be compared across studies, for example in meta-analysis.

Types

Effect sizes fall into families. The difference family expresses the difference between group means in standard-deviation units, most famously Cohen's d:

d = (M1 − M2) / SDpooled

The correlation family includes the Pearson correlation coefficient r and the coefficient of determination r2. The categorical family includes the odds ratio, relative risk, and measures based on chi-squared tests. Hedges' g is a bias-corrected variant of Cohen's d used for small samples.

Interpretation

Jacob Cohen proposed rough conventions for d: 0.2 is "small," 0.5 "medium," and 0.8 "large," while cautioning that these benchmarks are arbitrary and should yield to context and subject-matter knowledge. Modern reporting guidelines recommend always presenting effect sizes with confidence intervals alongside significance tests, because a statistically significant result with a large sample may have a trivially small effect.

History

Although earlier statisticians used standardized measures, Cohen's 1969 and 1988 books on statistical power analysis popularized effect size reporting, and meta-analysis from the 1970s onward made comparable effect sizes essential for research synthesis.
