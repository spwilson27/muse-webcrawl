Inverse problem


Inverse problems work backwards from effects to causes.

An inverse problem in science is the process of calculating from a set of observations the causal factors that produced them: for example, calculating an image in X-ray computed tomography, source reconstruction in EEG, or calculating the density of the Earth from measurements of its gravity field.

Overview

Inverse problems contrast with forward problems, in which a model of a system is used to predict its observable effects. The inversion starts with the effects and attempts to recover the causes. According to the criteria of Jacques Hadamard, many inverse problems are ill-posed: a solution may not exist, may not be unique, or may not depend stably on the data, so that tiny measurement errors cause huge errors in the result.

Methods

Because of ill-posedness, inverse problems are usually handled with regularisation — adding prior information or constraints, as in Tikhonov regularisation — or within a Bayesian framework that quantifies uncertainty in the inferred parameters. Discrepancy principles and penalties that favour simple or sparse solutions are common tools.

Applications

Inverse problems arise throughout science and engineering: medical imaging (CT, MRI and electrical impedance tomography), geophysical prospecting and seismology, non-destructive testing, remote sensing, image deblurring, and parameter estimation in physics and finance.
