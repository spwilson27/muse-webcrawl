Great Filter

The Great Filter: one or more steps toward a visible civilization must be extremely improbable.

The Great Filter is a concept in astrobiology and futurology, proposed by the economist Robin Hanson in 1996, which proposes that some stage in the evolution of life — from the formation of habitable planets to the emergence of an advanced, detectable technological civilization — is extremely unlikely or difficult, and that this bottleneck explains the observed absence of extraterrestrial civilizations despite the enormous number of stars and planets. It is one proposed resolution of the Fermi paradox.

The argument

Hanson listed a sequence of steps that must be completed for a star system to produce a civilization visible across interstellar distances: a habitable planet, the emergence of self-replicating molecules, simple and then complex cells, sexual reproduction, multicellular life, intelligence, technology, and finally colonization of the galaxy. Because we observe no one else having completed the sequence, at least one step must be a "filter" that almost all life fails. The filter might lie behind humanity — life itself or eukaryotes might be vanishingly rare — or ahead of us, implying that technological civilizations almost always destroy themselves or fail to expand.

Implications

The concept shapes discussion of existential risk: discovering simple life on Mars or Europa would suggest the filter is still ahead, which Hanson described as bad news, whereas finding the universe truly sterile would imply humanity has already passed the hardest steps. Critics note the argument depends on assumptions about detection, the timescales of colonization, and a single observed sample of life.
