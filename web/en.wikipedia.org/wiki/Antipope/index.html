Antipope

An antipope (Latin: antipapa) is a person who makes an illegitimate claim to be Bishop of Rome and leader of the Catholic Church, in opposition to the pope canonically recognized at the time. Antipopes arose from disputed papal elections, doctrinal conflicts, and the interference of secular rulers, and in some periods commanded the allegiance of substantial parts of Christendom.

History

The first antipope is generally considered to be Hippolytus (217–235), elected in protest against Callixtus I, though he was later reconciled with the Church. Rival papal claimants were frequent during the political chaos of the tenth and eleventh centuries, and Holy Roman Emperors repeatedly installed their own candidates. The most serious crisis was the Western Schism (1378–1417), when rival popes at Rome, Avignon, and (from 1409) Pisa divided Europe; it was resolved by the Council of Constance, which deposed or accepted the resignations of the claimants and elected Martin V.

Later claimants

Felix V (1439–1449), elected by the Council of Basel, is usually regarded as the last historical antipope. In the modern era, small sedevacantist and conclavist groups have occasionally elected their own "popes," who are not recognized by the Catholic Church or by most historical lists. The number of antipopes recognized by historians varies, generally between 30 and 40.
