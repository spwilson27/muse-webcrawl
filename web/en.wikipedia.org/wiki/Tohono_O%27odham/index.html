Tohono O'odham
Regions: Arizona, U.S.; Sonora, Mexico
Language: O'odham (Tohono O'odham dialect)
Meaning of name: "Desert People"

The Tohono O'odham (O'odham: Tohono Oʼodham, "Desert People") are a Native American people of the Sonoran Desert, residing primarily in southern Arizona in the United States and northern Sonora in Mexico. They were historically known as the Papago, a name they have since replaced with their own endonym.

History and culture

The Tohono O'odham are descendants of the ancient Hohokam culture and developed sophisticated desert agriculture, including floodwater (ak-chin) farming of drought-adapted crops such as tepary beans, corn and squash. Spanish missionaries, including Eusebio Kino, established missions among them in the late 17th century; the mission church of San Xavier del Bac near Tucson remains a famous landmark.

Nation

The federally recognised Tohono O'odham Nation governs a reservation of about 4,400 square miles in Arizona — one of the largest in the United States — whose southern boundary with Mexico cuts through traditional homelands, a source of continuing political and cultural challenges.
