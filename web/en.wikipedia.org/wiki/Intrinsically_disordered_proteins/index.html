Intrinsically disordered proteins

An intrinsically disordered protein (IDP) is a protein that lacks a fixed or ordered three-dimensional structure, typically in the absence of its macromolecular interaction partners. IDPs range from fully unstructured sequences to proteins containing intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) alongside folded domains, and they challenge the classical structure–function paradigm that tied protein activity to a single stable fold.

Characteristics

Disordered sequences are enriched in charged and polar amino acids (such as glutamate, lysine, and proline) and depleted in bulky hydrophobic residues that drive folding. They are especially common in eukaryotes, where a large fraction of the proteome contains long disordered regions, and are strongly associated with cell signalling, transcription, and regulation.

Functions

Flexibility lets IDPs bind multiple partners with high specificity and low affinity, fold upon binding to a target ("coupled folding and binding"), and present short linear motifs and sites for post-translational modification. Disordered regions also drive the formation of biomolecular condensates through liquid–liquid phase separation. Dysregulation of IDPs is implicated in disease: tau and alpha-synuclein are disordered proteins that aggregate in neurodegenerative disorders.

Study

Because IDPs do not crystallise and appear as ensembles, they are studied with NMR spectroscopy, small-angle X-ray scattering, single-molecule fluorescence, and computational predictors of disorder.
