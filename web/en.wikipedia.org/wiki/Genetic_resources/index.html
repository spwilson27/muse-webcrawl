Genetic resources
DefinitionGenetic material of actual or potential value
Key treatiesConvention on Biological Diversity (1992); Nagoya Protocol (2010); Plant Treaty (2001)
StorageGene banks, seed vaults, cryopreservation

Genetic resources are genetic material of actual or potential value — the heritable material contained in plants, animals, fungi, and microorganisms that is useful, or may become useful, to humanity. The term is central to international law on biodiversity, agriculture, and biotechnology, where it frames questions of conservation, access, and the sharing of benefits arising from use.

Seed collections in gene banks preserve genetic resources for future use.

Conservation

Genetic resources are conserved in situ, in their natural habitats or on farms where landraces continue to evolve, and ex situ, in collections such as the world's roughly 1,750 gene banks, botanic gardens, and facilities like the Svalbard Global Seed Vault, which holds backup seed samples from crop collections worldwide. Crop wild relatives and traditional landraces are especially valued as sources of traits — disease resistance, drought tolerance — for breeding.

Access and benefit-sharing

The Convention on Biological Diversity (1992) affirmed states' sovereign rights over their genetic resources, replacing the older assumption that they were the common heritage of humankind. The Nagoya Protocol (adopted 2010, in force 2014) sets rules for access and benefit-sharing, while the International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture (2001) maintains a multilateral system for major food crops. Debate continues over "digital sequence information" — genetic data shared as sequences rather than physical samples.
