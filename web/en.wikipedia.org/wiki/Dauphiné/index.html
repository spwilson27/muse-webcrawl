Dauphiné

The Dauphiné is a former province in southeastern France, roughly corresponding to the present departments of Isère, Drôme and Hautes-Alpes. Its capital was Grenoble (earlier Vienne). Its name derives from the dolphin (dauphin) on the coat of arms of its rulers, the Counts of Albon.

History

After the fall of Rome the area passed through Burgundian, Frankish and imperial hands. From the 11th century the Counts of Albon, styling themselves Dauphins of Viennois, built a quasi-independent principality within the Holy Roman Empire. In 1349 the childless Humbert II sold the Dauphiné to King Philippe VI of France, on condition that the heir to the French throne bear the title Dauphin of France—a title used until 1830. The province kept its own estates and parliament in Grenoble until the French Revolution abolished provinces in 1790.

Geography

The Dauphiné spans the Rhône valley and the Dauphiné Alps, including the Écrins massif and Barre des Écrins (4,102 m), and areas such as Oisans, Queyras, Diois and the Vercors plateau—famous for Resistance activity in the Second World War.

Culture

The region gives its name to gratin dauphinois (sliced potatoes baked in milk/cream), pommes dauphine, and the Critérium du Dauphiné cycling race, a key Tour de France preparation event. Grenoble hosted the 1968 Winter Olympics.
