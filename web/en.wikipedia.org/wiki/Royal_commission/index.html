Royal commission

A royal commission is a major ad-hoc formal public inquiry into a defined issue in some monarchies. They have been held in the United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, New Zealand, Saudi Arabia, South Africa and other countries with Commonwealth ties. A royal commission is similar in function to a commission of inquiry (or, less commonly, a judicial inquiry) found in other countries such as Ireland, South Africa, and Hong Kong.

In republics, an equivalent entity may be termed a presidential commission.

Powers and procedure

A royal commission is one of the highest-profile inquiries a government can undertake. A commission's terms of reference are set by the appointing authority, typically the head of state acting on the advice of ministers. Commissioners are usually eminent figures such as judges, academics or senior public figures, and are frequently empowered to summon witnesses, compel evidence, and hold public hearings.

Because royal commissions sit outside the normal machinery of government, they can investigate matters that are politically sensitive or that involve allegations of corruption, maladministration or institutional failure.

Examples

Notable royal commissions include the Royal Commission into Aboriginal Deaths in Custody in Australia (1987–1991), the Royal Commission into the Banking and Financial Services Industry in Australia (2017–2019), the Canadian royal commissions on bilingualism and biculturalism, and various British royal commissions on subjects ranging from labour laws to university education.
