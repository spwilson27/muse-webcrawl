Choroid plexus

The choroid plexus (or plica choroidea) is a plexus of cells in the ventricles of the brain that produces cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). A choroid plexus is present in each of the four ventricles: the two lateral ventricles, the third ventricle, and the fourth ventricle.

Structure

The choroid plexus consists of a layer of cuboidal epithelial cells surrounding a core of capillaries and loose connective tissue. The fenestrated capillaries allow fluid to move into the interstitial space, while tight junctions between the epithelial cells form the blood–cerebrospinal fluid barrier, controlling what enters the CSF.

Function

The choroid plexuses produce most of the cerebrospinal fluid — roughly 400 to 600 millilitres per day in an adult human, several times the total CSF volume. The CSF cushions the brain, removes metabolic waste, and distributes signaling molecules. The choroid plexus also acts as an immune interface and a site of transport of nutrients and drugs.

Clinical significance

Choroid plexus papillomas and carcinomas are rare tumors that can overproduce CSF or block its flow, causing hydrocephalus. Calcification of the choroid plexus is a common incidental finding on brain imaging.
