Issue network

An issue network: a web of mutually interested actors connected by a shared policy concern.

An issue network is a concept in political science and public policy analysis describing an alliance of various interest groups and individuals who unite to promote a common cause or agenda in a way that influences government policy. The term was coined by the American political scientist Hugh Heclo in his 1978 essay "Issue Networks and the Executive Establishment," published in the volume The New American Political System.

Heclo proposed issue networks as an alternative to the older iron triangle model, which described policy making as dominated by a closed, stable trio of congressional committees, executive agencies, and interest groups. In contrast, issue networks are large, fluid, and open: participation shifts constantly, members are united less by material interest than by shared knowledge and concern about a particular policy area, and no single actor controls outcomes.

Characteristics

Issue networks typically include members of Congress and their staff, civil servants, journalists, academics, consultants, activists, and representatives of interest groups. Because membership is based on expertise and intellectual commitment, power within a network is dispersed and reputations for knowledge matter more than formal authority. Heclo argued that this fluidity makes issue networks harder to steer but also more permeable to new ideas.

Influence on policy studies

The concept helped move policy analysis away from purely institutional descriptions toward an understanding of policy communities and knowledge-based influence. It is frequently discussed alongside related ideas such as policy networks, epistemic communities, and advocacy coalitions. Critics have noted that the term's flexibility can make it hard to specify exactly who belongs to a network or to test its explanatory power.
