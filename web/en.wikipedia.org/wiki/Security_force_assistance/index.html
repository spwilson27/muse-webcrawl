Security force assistance

Security force assistance (SFA) is the set of activities by which one state's military and security institutions help build, train, advise, and equip the armed forces and security services of a partner nation, so that the partner can provide its own security — and, by extension, advance the assisting state's strategic interests without committing its own combat forces at scale.

Activities

SFA spans the generation of forces (recruiting, organisation, doctrine, and professional military education), training and advising down to tactical units, provision of equipment and logistics, and development of ministerial and institutional capacity such as budgeting, personnel systems, and maintenance. In United States doctrine, SFA is executed largely by the Army's Security Force Assistance Brigades (SFABs), activated from 2017, together with special operations forces and security cooperation programmes run through combatant commands.

History and rationale

Advisory missions date to the 19th century, but modern SFA was shaped by the Cold War — notably American assistance to South Vietnam — and expanded greatly after 2001, with large-scale efforts to build Iraqi and Afghan security forces. Proponents argue that capable partner forces prevent instability and reduce the need for direct intervention. Critics point to mixed results: in Afghanistan, two decades of assistance ended with the rapid collapse of the Afghan National Defense and Security Forces in 2021, prompting debate about dependency, corruption, and whether externally designed institutions can succeed without local political legitimacy.

Related concepts

SFA overlaps with foreign internal defense (assistance to a government facing insurgency), security cooperation (the broader set of defence engagements), and train-and-equip programmes funded through foreign military financing.
