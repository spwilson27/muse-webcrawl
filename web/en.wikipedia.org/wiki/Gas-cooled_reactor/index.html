Gas-cooled reactor

A gas-cooled reactor uses gas as the primary coolant to transfer heat from the core.

A gas-cooled reactor (GCR) is a nuclear reactor that uses graphite as a neutron moderator and a gas — carbon dioxide or helium — as the coolant. Gas-cooled reactors have been developed principally in the United Kingdom and France, and are noted for their ability to operate at high temperatures, which improves thermal efficiency.

History

The earliest gas-cooled power reactors were the British Magnox stations, which used natural uranium metal fuel clad in a magnesium alloy and carbon dioxide coolant. France developed a parallel line of reactors, the UNGG type. The Magnox design was followed in Britain by the advanced gas-cooled reactor (AGR), which uses enriched uranium dioxide fuel in stainless steel cladding and operates at higher gas temperatures and pressures, achieving better efficiency.

Design

In a gas-cooled reactor, coolant gas is circulated through the core by large blowers, absorbing heat from the fuel. The hot gas then passes through heat exchangers (boilers) inside or adjacent to the pressure vessel, raising steam to drive conventional turbo-generators. Graphite moderator blocks form the core structure, with channels for fuel elements and control rods.

High-temperature designs

Helium-cooled designs, such as the high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) and the very-high-temperature reactor (VHTR), use ceramic-coated particle fuels and are capable of outlet temperatures approaching 1,000 °C, enabling process-heat applications and efficient hydrogen production. Experimental reactors of this type have operated in Germany, the United States, Japan, and China.
