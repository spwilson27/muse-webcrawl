Kinect is a line of motion sensing input devices developed by Microsoft, first released in November 2010 for the Xbox 360 video game console. Using a depth camera, RGB camera, and microphone array, Kinect allowed users to control games and applications through natural user interface gestures and spoken commands, without a handheld controller.

History

Kinect was unveiled at E3 2009 under the codename "Project Natal". The first-generation sensor used structured-light depth technology licensed from the Israeli company PrimeSense. It launched on November 4, 2010 in North America and sold eight million units in its first 60 days, earning a Guinness World Record as the fastest-selling consumer electronics device at the time. A Windows version and software development kit followed in 2011–2012.

A second-generation Kinect, using time-of-flight depth sensing, was bundled with the Xbox One at its 2013 launch. The requirement that the sensor be connected proved controversial with consumers, and Microsoft unbundled it in 2014. Production of the Xbox One Kinect ended in 2017, and the device's technology was adapted into products such as the Windows Hello cameras, HoloLens, and the Azure Kinect developer kit (2019).

Technology

The original Kinect combines an infrared projector and monochrome CMOS sensor to build a depth map of the room, a colour camera, a four-microphone array, and a motorized tilt base. Software tracks up to six people and 20 joints per skeleton, enabling full-body gameplay. The Xbox One sensor increased resolution to a 1080p colour camera and a 512×424 time-of-flight depth sensor, adding heart-rate and facial-expression estimation.

Games and applications

Launch titles included Kinect Adventures!, Kinect Sports, and Dance Central. Beyond gaming, the sensor was widely adopted by researchers and hobbyists for robotics, 3D scanning, medical rehabilitation, and computer vision, thanks to its low cost and the availability of open-source drivers.

Reception and legacy

Kinect was praised for popularising controller-free gaming but criticised for latency, the space required for play, and privacy concerns arising from an always-connected camera and microphone. Its depth-sensing approach influenced later computer-vision products across the industry.
