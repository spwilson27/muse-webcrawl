Case method

The case method is a teaching approach in which students build skills in analytical thinking and reflective judgment by engaging in discussion of cases — written descriptions of real or hypothetical situations, often presenting a decision-maker facing a problem or opportunity. Developed at Harvard Law School under Christopher Columbus Langdell in the 1870s and adopted by Harvard Business School in the 1920s, the method is now widely used in law, business, medicine, public policy and education.

Approach

Rather than passively receiving lectures on theory, students prepare a case in advance and, under the instructor's questioning, must analyse the facts, identify the issues, weigh alternative courses of action and defend a decision. In business schools the case study method is designed to simulate the ambiguity of real management: cases rarely have a single correct answer, and grades often depend substantially on the quality of class participation.

Criticism and variants

Critics argue the method can privilege rhetoric over rigour, depend on anecdote, and under-represent issues such as ethics or diversity. Variants include the incident method, role-playing, and decision-forcing cases in military education. In law, the related casebook method focuses on extracting legal rules from appellate opinions.
