Chemin des Dames

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The ridge road of the Chemin des Dames with the Aisne valley below]

The Chemin des Dames (literally, "the Ladies' Path") is a ridge road in the Aisne department of northern France, running roughly 30 kilometres along a chalk plateau between the Aisne and Ailette valleys, between Laon and Soissons. It owes its name to the daughters of Louis XV, Mesdames Adélaïde and Victoire, who used the route in the eighteenth century on their way to visit their former governess at the Château de la Bove.

First World War

The ridge's commanding position made it one of the most bitterly contested sectors of the Western Front. German forces seized it after the First Battle of the Aisne in 1914 and fortified it with deep quarries and tunnels such as the Caverne du Dragon. In April 1917 General Robert Nivelle launched his great offensive along the Chemin des Dames, promising a breakthrough in 48 hours; instead the attack gained little ground at a cost of well over 100,000 French casualties in the first days, contributing directly to the French Army mutinies of 1917. In May 1918 the ridge was the axis of the German spring offensive (the Third Battle of the Aisne), which briefly carried German forces back to the Marne before the Allied counteroffensives of the summer retook the plateau.

Memorials

Today the road is lined with cemeteries, monuments, and the museum at the Caverne du Dragon, which preserves the vast underground quarry used by both armies.
