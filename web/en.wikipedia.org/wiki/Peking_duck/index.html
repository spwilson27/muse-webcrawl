Peking duck

Peking duck served with pancakes, scallions, and sauce.

Peking duck is a famous duck dish from Beijing (Peking) that has been prepared since the imperial era. It is prized for its thin, crisp skin, which is served with little meat and eaten with spring onion, cucumber, sweet bean sauce or hoisin sauce, and thin pancakes.

History

Roast duck was served at the imperial court during the Yuan dynasty, and a complete recipe appears in a 1330 manual of imperial dietary medicine by Hu Sihui. During the Ming dynasty the dish became firmly associated with the capital. In the Qing era it spread from the court to the public; Beijing restaurants such as Bianyifang (founded 1416) and Quanjude (founded 1864) became famous for their versions. Peking duck is now regarded as one of China's national dishes and is served at state banquets.

Preparation

Specially bred ducks are slaughtered after about 65 days. Air is pumped under the skin to separate it from the fat, the bird is scalded, coated with a maltose syrup glaze, and hung to dry, sometimes for 24 hours. It is then roasted—traditionally in a closed or hung oven over fruitwood fires—until the skin is glossy, amber, and crisp while the meat remains tender. Two historic methods persist: the closed-oven style of Bianyifang and the hung-oven style of Quanjude.

Serving

The duck is traditionally carved in front of diners. The crisp skin is often eaten first, sometimes dipped in sugar or garlic sauce; slices of meat and skin are then wrapped in steamed pancakes with scallion brushes, cucumber sticks, and sweet bean sauce. The remaining carcass is commonly made into a milky soup, and other dishes may be prepared from leftovers.
