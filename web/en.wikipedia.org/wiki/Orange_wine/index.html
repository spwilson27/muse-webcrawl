Orange wine, also called skin-contact white wine or amber wine, is a type of wine made from white grape varieties in which the grape skins are not removed during fermentation, as is usual for white wine, but remain in contact with the juice for days, weeks, or even months. This process gives the wine its characteristic deep orange-amber colour, greater tannin, and a fuller, more textured flavour profile than conventional white wine.

History

The technique is ancient: winemakers in present-day Georgia have fermented white grapes on their skins in clay vessels called qvevri buried in the ground for thousands of years. The style nearly vanished under industrial winemaking but was revived in the 1990s and 2000s by producers in the Friuli-Venezia Giulia region of Italy and across the border in Slovenia, notably Joško Gravner and Stanko Radikon. From there it spread through the natural wine movement to producers worldwide.

Production

White grapes are crushed and fermented with their skins, seeds, and sometimes stems, much as red wine is made. Extended maceration extracts phenolic compounds — tannins, colour pigments, and flavour precursors — producing wines that can be dry, structured, and notably aromatic, with notes of dried fruit, tea, nuts, and spices. Many orange wines are made with minimal intervention: wild yeasts, no fining or filtration, and little or no added sulphur.
