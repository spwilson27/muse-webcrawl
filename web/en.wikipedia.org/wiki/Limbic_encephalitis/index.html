Limbic encephalitis

Limbic encephalitis is a form of encephalitis characterised by inflammation localised predominantly to the limbic system — the medial temporal lobes, hippocampus, amygdala and related structures. It typically develops subacutely over weeks to a few months.

Signs and symptoms

The hallmark features are short-term memory impairment, disorientation and confusion, often accompanied by seizures (frequently temporal-lobe or faciobrachial dystonic seizures in LGI1-antibody disease) and psychiatric symptoms such as agitation, hallucinations, depression or personality change. Because psychiatric features may dominate at first, patients are sometimes initially misdiagnosed with a primary psychiatric illness.

Causes

Most cases are autoimmune. Paraneoplastic limbic encephalitis is associated with underlying cancer — classically small-cell lung cancer with anti-Hu antibodies, and testicular tumours with anti-Ma2 antibodies. Non-paraneoplastic forms are associated with antibodies against cell-surface proteins, including the voltage-gated potassium channel complex (LGI1 and CASPR2) and the NMDA receptor. Infectious causes, especially herpes simplex virus, must be excluded, since HSV encephalitis also targets the temporal lobes.

Diagnosis and treatment

MRI commonly shows T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in the medial temporal lobes; EEG may show temporal slowing or epileptiform activity, and CSF often shows mild inflammatory changes. Treatment combines immunotherapy (corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange, with rituximab or cyclophosphamide in refractory cases) and, in paraneoplastic disease, treatment of the underlying tumour.
