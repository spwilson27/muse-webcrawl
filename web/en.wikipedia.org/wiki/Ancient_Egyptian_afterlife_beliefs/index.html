Ancient Egyptian afterlife beliefs

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ancient Egyptian afterlife beliefs were centred around a variety of complex rituals and concepts concerning death and the soul. Egyptians believed that the human soul was made up of multiple parts — including the ka (life force), the ba (personality or soul that could travel between worlds), and the akh (the transfigured, effective spirit) — and that, after death, the deceased journeyed through the Duat (the underworld), faced judgement, and, if justified, lived on in the Aaru, the "Field of Reeds", a paradisiacal mirror of Egypt along the Nile.

The journey through the Duat

Each night the sun god Ra travelled through the Duat, battling the serpent Apep, and the dead were thought to accompany or follow his barque. Funerary texts — the Pyramid Texts of the Old Kingdom, the Coffin Texts of the Middle Kingdom, and the New Kingdom Book of the Dead and underworld books such as the Amduat — provided spells, maps, and declarations to guide and protect the deceased.

Judgement and the weighing of the heart

In the Hall of Two Truths, the deceased made the "negative confession" — a declaration of innocence of 42 sins — before Osiris, god of the dead, and a tribunal of gods. The heart of the deceased was then weighed on a balance against the feather of Maat, goddess of truth and cosmic order, while Anubis supervised the scales and Thoth recorded the result. A heart heavy with sin was devoured by the monster Ammit, condemning the soul to annihilation; the justified were declared "true of voice" (maa-kheru) and admitted to the afterlife.

Mummification and funerary practice

Because the soul was believed to need its body, Egyptians developed mummification: the corpse was dried with natron, the organs preserved in canopic jars (the heart left in place as the seat of thought), and the body wrapped and placed in a tomb equipped with food, furniture, shabti figurines to perform labour, and amulets such as the heart scarab. The "Opening of the Mouth" ceremony restored the senses of the deceased for life in the next world. These practices evolved over three thousand years and gradually spread from royalty to the wider population.
