The Corries were a Scottish folk group that emerged from the Scottish folk revival of the 1960s. Founded as the Corrie Folk Trio in 1961, the group became a duo of Roy Williamson (1936–1990) and Ronnie Browne from 1966 until Williamson's death in 1990, and was enormously popular in Scotland for its lively performances of traditional and original songs.

History

The group began as a trio with Bill Smith, expanded briefly to a quartet with singer Paddie Bell as the Corrie Folk Trio and Paddie Bell, and recorded several albums for the Waverley label. After Smith and Bell left, Browne and Williamson continued as The Corries, achieving wide popularity through concert tours, television appearances on BBC Scotland series such as The Corries and Other Folk, and dozens of albums.

Music

The Corries' repertoire mixed traditional ballads, Jacobite songs, Burns settings, and Williamson's own compositions, including "Flower of Scotland", written in the mid-1960s. The song — one of Scotland's unofficial national anthems — was sung at sporting events by Scotland rugby and football supporters and was formally adopted by the Scotland rugby team at the 1990 Five Nations Championship. Williamson designed and built unusual multi-necked instruments called combolins, combining guitar, mandolin, and bandurria-type necks, to reduce the need to switch instruments on stage.

Legacy

After Williamson's death from a brain tumour in 1990, Browne continued performing solo. The Corries remain among the best-selling acts in Scottish folk music, with an extensive catalogue of albums documenting hundreds of traditional songs.
