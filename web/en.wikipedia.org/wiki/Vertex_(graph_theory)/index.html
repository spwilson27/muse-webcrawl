Vertex (graph theory)

In mathematics, and more specifically in graph theory, a vertex (plural vertices) or node is the fundamental unit of which graphs are formed: an undirected graph consists of a set of vertices and a set of edges (unordered pairs of vertices), while a directed graph consists of a set of vertices and a set of arcs (ordered pairs of vertices).

A graph with six vertices and seven edges

Definitions and types

Two vertices are said to be adjacent if they are joined by an edge, and each is a neighbour of the other. The degree of a vertex is the number of edges incident to it; in directed graphs one distinguishes in-degree and out-degree. A vertex of degree zero is isolated, and a vertex of degree one is a leaf (or pendant vertex). A vertex is a cut vertex (articulation point) if removing it increases the number of connected components of the graph.

Vertices and edges form the basis of numerous further concepts: a path is a sequence of distinct vertices each adjacent to the next; vertices can be partitioned, coloured, or arranged into subgraphs, and entire algorithmic disciplines (network flow, shortest paths, graph traversal) are described in terms of vertices and their relationships.

Vertex labels and structure

Vertices may be labelled — given distinguishing names — and may carry additional attributes such as weights, colours or coordinates in geometric and chemical graphs. In the adjacency matrix representation of a graph with n vertices, rows and columns correspond to vertices and entries record adjacency. The arrangement of vertices in the plane in a drawing of a graph (its embedding) underlies planar graph theory and graph visualisation software.
