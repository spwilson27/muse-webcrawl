The Panathenaea (Greek: Παναθήναια) was the most important festival of ancient Athens, held in honour of the city's patron goddess, Athena. A Lesser Panathenaea was celebrated annually, and a Greater Panathenaea every four years with expanded games, drawing visitors from across the Greek world.

The procession and the peplos

The heart of the festival was a great procession (pompē) through the city to the Acropolis on 28 Hekatombaion, traditionally Athena's birthday. It carried a new embroidered robe — the peplos — woven by Athenian women and girls (the Ergastinai and Arrhephoroi) to clothe the ancient olive-wood statue of Athena Polias in the Erechtheion. Animals for sacrifice — the "hundred oxen" (hekatomb) — accompanied the procession, and meat was distributed to the citizens. The procession is famously depicted on the Parthenon frieze.

The games

The Greater Panathenaea, reorganised in 566 BC, included athletic contests modelled on the Olympic Games, held in the Panathenaic Stadium, as well as musical and rhapsodic competitions (including recitations of Homer), torch races, a boat race and a mock cavalry display. Victors received large quantities of olive oil in painted Panathenaic amphorae, crowns of olive, and cash equivalents; the games were "crown games" with prizes of substantial value.

History

Tradition credited the festival to the mythical king Erichthonius or to Theseus. It flourished under Peisistratus and the democracy, and continued to be celebrated under Roman rule, with benefactions from figures such as Herodes Atticus, into late antiquity.
