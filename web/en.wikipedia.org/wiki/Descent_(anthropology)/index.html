Descent (anthropology)

In anthropology, descent refers to the socially recognised links between ancestors and descendants — the way in which membership of a kin group, and often rights to property, office, and residence, are transmitted across generations. Descent theory was a central concern of British social anthropology in the mid-20th century, particularly in the study of kinship systems in Africa.

Types of descent

  • Patrilineal descent — membership and inheritance pass through the male line; children belong to their father's group.
  • Matrilineal descent — descent is traced through the female line; children belong to their mother's group, and authority may be exercised by the mother's brother.
  • Bilateral (cognatic) descent — kinship is reckoned through both parents, producing kindreds rather than bounded descent groups.
  • Double descent — different rights and obligations are transmitted patrilineally and matrilineally for different purposes.

Descent groups

A descent group is a social group whose members claim descent from a common ancestor. Where the ancestor is traced through known genealogical links, the group is a lineage; where descent is simply claimed or stipulated, it is a clan. Unilineal descent groups were analysed by anthropologists such as E. E. Evans-Pritchard (on the Nuer) and Meyer Fortes (on the Tallensi) as "corporate" groups holding property and jural rights in common, providing a framework for political organisation in stateless societies.
