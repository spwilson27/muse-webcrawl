Franco Modigliani (18 June 1918 – 25 September 2003) was an Italian-American economist at the MIT Sloan School of Management. He received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1985 for his pioneering analyses of saving (the life-cycle hypothesis) and of financial markets.

Work

The life-cycle hypothesis (developed with Richard Brumberg) holds that individuals plan consumption and saving over their lifetimes, accumulating wealth while working and drawing it down in retirement, implying that aggregate saving depends on growth and demographics rather than only on income. With Merton Miller, Modigliani proved the Modigliani–Miller theorems (1958–1961): under idealized conditions, a firm's market value is independent of its capital structure and dividend policy — a conceptual bedrock of modern corporate finance. He was a leading figure of the "neoclassical synthesis", helping to develop the IS–LM framework into large-scale Keynesian macroeconometric models (the MPS model used by the Federal Reserve).

Life

Born in Rome to a Jewish family, Modigliani fled Fascist Italy's racial laws in 1939 and completed his doctorate at the New School in New York in 1944. He taught at Carnegie Mellon, Northwestern, and from 1962 at MIT. He was an adviser to the U.S. Treasury and the Federal Reserve, and a prominent public critic of some European monetary policies late in life.
