Hall–Petch relationship

The Hall–Petch relationship (also called the Hall–Petch equation) is an empirical relationship in materials science between the grain size of a polycrystalline material and its yield strength. It states that the yield stress σy increases with the inverse square root of the average grain diameter d: σy = σ0 + ky d−1/2, where σ0 is a friction stress and ky is the Hall–Petch slope.

History

The relationship was published independently by E. O. Hall (1951) and N. J. Petch (1953), based on measurements on mild steel and other metals. It explained why fine-grained materials are generally stronger than coarse-grained ones of the same composition.

Mechanism and limits

The strengthening is attributed to grain boundaries impeding dislocation motion: dislocations pile up at a boundary, and smaller grains mean shorter pile-ups, requiring higher stress to propagate yielding into the next grain. At extremely small grain sizes (roughly below 10–20 nm), many materials show a breakdown known as the inverse Hall–Petch effect, where further refinement softens rather than strengthens.
