TypeNoodle soup
Place of originHawaii, United States
CreatedEarly 20th century, plantation era

Saimin is a noodle soup dish developed in Hawaii from the cuisines of the immigrant communities who came to work on the islands' sugar and pineapple plantations. Soft wheat noodles are served in a hot dashi-based broth and topped with ingredients such as sliced green onions, kamaboko (fish cake), char siu pork, sliced Spam, or wontons. It is considered the signature comfort food of Hawaii and a classic example of the islands' multicultural food culture.

History

Saimin's name is generally derived from Chinese words for "thin noodles" (sai, thin; min, noodle), reflecting its Chinese noodle roots, while the broth owes much to Japanese dashi and its toppings to Filipino, Korean, Portuguese, and Native Hawaiian influences. During the plantation era it was sold cheaply at stands and eaten by workers of all backgrounds after shifts and football games. In the mid-20th century saimin stands flourished in Honolulu; McDonald's restaurants in Hawaii added saimin to their menus in the 1960s, a local specialty rarely seen elsewhere in the chain.

Preparation and variations

The noodles are fresh wheat noodles, chewier than ramen and usually un-alkalised or lightly so. The clear broth is typically built on dried shrimp, shiitake, or bonito dashi. Common variants include wonton saimin, fried saimin (pan-fried drained noodles), and "saimin deluxe" with an egg. Instant dried saimin packets are a Hawaiian pantry staple.
