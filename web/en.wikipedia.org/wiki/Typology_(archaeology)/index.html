Typology (archaeology)

Image placeholder: typology (archaeology)

In archaeology, typology is the classification of artefacts according to their physical characteristics — form, material, decoration and technology — into types, and the arrangement of those types in sequences that are taken to reflect change over time. Typology was one of the founding methods of scientific archaeology and remains central to dating and comparing assemblages.

Development

The method was pioneered in nineteenth-century Scandinavia. Christian Jürgensen Thomsen's Three-Age System (stone, bronze, iron) provided a first framework, and Oscar Montelius refined typology into a systematic technique, arranging objects such as brooches and axes into developmental series. In Britain, Augustus Pitt Rivers applied typological series to his excavations, and Flinders Petrie's sequence dating of Egyptian pottery (1899) ordered graves by the changing forms of their pots, introducing seriation.

Method

Typologists assume that artefacts of a culture and period share recognisable styles, and that styles change gradually, so that objects can be ordered from earlier to later forms. Types defined on one site can then date contexts elsewhere where the same types occur — the basis of cross-dating. Combined with stratigraphy and absolute methods such as radiocarbon dating, typology still underpins ceramic and lithic studies worldwide.

Criticism

Twentieth-century critics, notably processual and post-processual archaeologists, questioned whether types are real categories of past makers or constructs of the analyst, and whether stylistic change is always gradual and unilinear. Modern approaches often use statistical and attribute-based methods alongside intuitive typology, and treat types as analytical tools rather than discovered truths.
