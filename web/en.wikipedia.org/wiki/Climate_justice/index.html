Climate justice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Protesters at a climate justice demonstration

Climate justice is a term and a movement that frames climate change as an ethical and political issue, rather than one that is purely environmental or physical. It argues that the countries and communities least responsible for greenhouse-gas emissions — often in the Global South, along with indigenous peoples and marginalised groups — suffer its worst effects, and therefore that climate action must address fairness, human rights, and historical responsibility.

Core principles

  • Common but differentiated responsibilities: wealthy industrialised nations, having contributed most historical emissions, should lead in cutting emissions and financing adaptation.
  • Procedural justice: affected communities should have a voice in climate decision-making.
  • Distributive justice: the costs and benefits of climate action should be shared fairly, both within and between nations.
  • Loss and damage: compensation for harms that cannot be adapted to, formalised in the loss and damage fund agreed at COP27.

History

The concept grew out of the environmental justice movement of the 1980s–90s and the 2002 Bali Principles of Climate Justice. It gained prominence through youth movements such as Fridays for Future and litigation like Urgenda v. Netherlands (2019).

Criticism

Critics argue the concept is politically vague and can complicate negotiations; supporters counter that ignoring equity has itself stalled progress.
