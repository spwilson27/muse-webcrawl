Cyclodextrin

The toroidal structure of a cyclodextrin, with a hydrophobic cavity.

Cyclodextrins are a family of cyclic oligosaccharides made of glucose subunits joined in a ring. The most common are α-, β-, and γ-cyclodextrin, containing six, seven, and eight glucose units respectively. They are produced from starch by enzymatic conversion using cyclodextrin glycosyltransferase.

Structure and properties

A cyclodextrin molecule has a truncated-cone (toroidal) shape with a hydrophilic outer surface and a relatively hydrophobic central cavity. This structure lets cyclodextrins form inclusion complexes with guest molecules that fit into the cavity, changing the guest's solubility, stability, and reactivity — a classic example of host–guest chemistry.

Applications

Cyclodextrins are widely used in pharmaceuticals to increase the solubility and bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs; in foods and cosmetics to stabilise flavours and fragrances and mask odours; and in chemistry as catalysts, separations media, and molecular containers. Chemically modified derivatives, such as hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin and the synthetic drug sugammadex (a modified γ-cyclodextrin used to reverse neuromuscular blockade), extend their range of use.
