Russian architecture
OriginsKievan Rus', Byzantine influence
Key periodsMedieval Rus'; Muscovite; Imperial (Baroque, Neoclassical); Soviet (Constructivism, Stalinist); Post-Soviet

The architecture of Russia follows a tradition that stretches from the wooden building of the Slavs and the stone churches of Kievan Rus' through the distinctive styles of the medieval Russian principalities, the westernising imperial era, and the grand projects of the Soviet Union to the present day. Christianisation in 988 brought Byzantine forms, which Russian builders adapted with local materials and climate in mind.

Medieval and Muscovite periods

Early Rus' produced the great cathedrals of Kiev (Saint Sophia, 1037) and Novgorod, and the white-stone churches of Vladimir-Suzdal with their carved façades and slender drum domes. After the Mongol period, Moscow's ascendancy brought the Kremlin cathedrals, built with Italian engineers, and a flowering of native forms: tented roofs, tiered kokoshnik gables, and the riotous polychromy of Saint Basil's Cathedral (1555–1561). Wooden architecture — from peasant izbas to the multi-domed churches of Kizhi — developed in parallel in the north.

Imperial era

Peter the Great founded Saint Petersburg in 1703 as a planned European capital and imported Baroque, later restrained into the Neoclassicism of Catherine the Great's reign (the work of Quarenghi, Cameron, and Rossi). The nineteenth century added Russian Revival and Byzantine Revival styles, eclecticism, and, around 1900, the Style Moderne (Art Nouveau) of Fyodor Shekhtel.

Soviet and post-Soviet periods

The 1920s made the USSR a laboratory of avant-garde Constructivism (Melnikov, the Vesnin brothers, Ginzburg's Narkomfin Building) and of visionary projects such as Tatlin's Monument to the Third International. Under Stalin, avant-garde work was suppressed in favour of monumental Socialist Classicism — the "Seven Sisters" skyscrapers and the Moscow Metro. Khrushchev's 1955 decree against ornament launched mass prefabricated housing (the khrushchyovka), and late-Soviet modernism produced large-scale civic ensembles. Since 1991, commercial development, church reconstruction (notably the Cathedral of Christ the Saviour, rebuilt 1995–2000), and megaprojects such as the Lakhta Center in Saint Petersburg have reshaped Russian cities.
