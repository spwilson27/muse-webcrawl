Hongi Hika (c. 1772 – 6 March 1828) was a New Zealand Māori rangatira (chief) and war leader of the Ngāpuhi iwi. One of the first Māori leaders to engage extensively with Europeans, he promoted trade and protected early missionaries, while also using European muskets to wage campaigns against rival iwi that had a devastating effect on Māori society during the period known as the Musket Wars.

Early life and rise

Hongi Hika was born near Kaikohe in the Bay of Islands region. He rose to prominence as a war leader of Ngāpuhi, particularly after the battle of Moremonui in 1807–08, where Ngāpuhi suffered heavy losses to Ngāti Whātua despite having a few muskets. Hongi established a base at Kerikeri and encouraged trade with visiting ships, exchanging flax, timber and provisions for European goods — above all, muskets.

Visit to England

In 1820 Hongi travelled to England with the missionary Thomas Kendall and the chief Waikato. He was presented at court to King George IV, assisted Kendall and the linguist Samuel Lee at Cambridge with a grammar and vocabulary of the Māori language, and was given many gifts. On the return journey, in Sydney, Hongi exchanged the gifts for some 300 muskets — a transaction that transformed the military balance in New Zealand and later drew criticism of the missionaries who had accompanied him.

The Musket Wars campaigns

From 1821 Hongi led a series of large expeditions (taua) against iwi to the south — including the capture of Mauinaina and Mokoia pā at Panmure, the defeat of Ngāti Maru at Te Totara, and campaigns against Te Arawa at Rotorua in 1823 and Ngāti Whātua. Motivated by utu (reciprocity/revenge) for earlier defeats, these raids killed thousands and displaced many communities, setting off migrations and conflicts across the North Island.

Death

In January 1827 Hongi was shot through the lung during fighting at Whangaroa; he survived for more than a year before dying on 6 March 1828. His deathbed instructions urged his people to protect the missionaries and not to resist Europeans by force, warning that his own people would be unable to withstand European power.
