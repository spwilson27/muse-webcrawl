Filariasis

Filariasis is a parasitic disease caused by infection with filarial nematode worms of the superfamily Filarioidea. These thread-like roundworms are transmitted to humans through the bites of infected blood-feeding arthropods, principally mosquitoes and black flies. The most important form is lymphatic filariasis, caused mainly by Wuchereria bancrofti and Brugia species, which can produce the disfiguring swelling known as elephantiasis.

Types

  • Lymphatic filariasis — Wuchereria bancrofti (about 90% of cases), Brugia malayi, and Brugia timori; adult worms live in the lymphatic system.
  • Subcutaneous filariasis — Loa loa (African eye worm), Mansonella streptocerca, and Onchocerca volvulus, which causes river blindness (onchocerciasis).
  • Serous cavity filariasis — Mansonella perstans and Mansonella ozzardi, usually mild or asymptomatic.

Life cycle

During a blood meal, an infected arthropod deposits infective third-stage larvae on the skin; these enter through the bite wound and migrate to their tissue of residence, where they mature into adults. Adults produce sheathed or unsheathed first-stage larvae called microfilariae, which circulate in the blood — often with nocturnal periodicity matching vector feeding habits — or migrate in the skin, ready to be taken up by another vector. Many filariae harbour the bacterial endosymbiont Wolbachia, which the worms require for fertility and survival and which contributes to inflammatory pathology.

Signs and symptoms

Most infections are asymptomatic, though they still damage the lymphatics. Acute episodes feature fever, lymphangitis, and lymphadenitis ("filarial fever"). Chronic lymphatic disease produces lymphoedema, hydrocele, and in severe cases elephantiasis of the limbs, breasts, or genitals. Tropical pulmonary eosinophilia is an immune hyper-response syndrome seen in some patients.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis historically relied on finding microfilariae in a night blood smear; antigen detection tests for W. bancrofti are now standard. Treatment uses combinations of diethylcarbamazine (DEC), ivermectin, and albendazole; doxycycline, which kills the worms' Wolbachia symbionts, is an effective macrofilaricidal adjunct. Surgery can help in hydrocele.

Epidemiology and control

Lymphatic filariasis is endemic in tropical regions of Africa, Asia, the Pacific, and parts of the Americas, and is classified by the World Health Organization as a neglected tropical disease. The Global Programme to Eliminate Lymphatic Filariasis, launched in 2000, uses annual mass drug administration to interrupt transmission, alongside morbidity management for those with chronic disease.
