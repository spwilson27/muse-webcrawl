Cholinergic


Placeholder image: a cholinergic synapse

In general, the word cholinergic refers to the neurotransmitter acetylcholine (ACh). A neuron, nerve fiber, receptor or drug is described as cholinergic if it uses, responds to, releases, mimics or otherwise relates to acetylcholine. The term is widely used in neuroscience, pharmacology and medicine.

The cholinergic system is one of the major modulatory neurotransmitter systems in the nervous system. In the peripheral nervous system, acetylcholine is the transmitter at the neuromuscular junction, in autonomic ganglia, and at postganglionic parasympathetic synapses. In the central nervous system, cholinergic projections arising from the basal forebrain and brainstem modulate arousal, attention, learning and memory.

Cholinergic receptors

Acetylcholine acts on two broad classes of receptors:

  • Nicotinic receptors — ligand-gated ion channels found at the neuromuscular junction, in autonomic ganglia and in the brain. They are named for their sensitivity to nicotine.
  • Muscarinic receptors — G protein-coupled receptors (subtypes M1–M5) found in the heart, smooth muscle, glands and the central nervous system. They are named for their sensitivity to muscarine.

Cholinergic drugs

Drugs that enhance cholinergic transmission are called cholinomimetics or parasympathomimetics. They include direct agonists such as pilocarpine and bethanechol, and indirect agents, the acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, which prevent the breakdown of acetylcholine. The latter group includes physostigmine, neostigmine, and the drugs donepezil, rivastigmine and galantamine used in the treatment of Alzheimer's disease.

Conversely, drugs that block cholinergic transmission are called anticholinergics. They include atropine, scopolamine, and many antihistamines and tricyclic antidepressants with antimuscarinic side effects.

Clinical significance

Degeneration of cholinergic neurons in the basal forebrain is a feature of Alzheimer's disease and underlies the "cholinergic hypothesis" of the disorder. In myasthenia gravis, autoantibodies attack nicotinic receptors at the neuromuscular junction. Organophosphate insecticides and nerve agents such as sarin exert their toxicity by irreversibly inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, producing a cholinergic crisis of excessive salivation, lacrimation, miosis, bronchoconstriction and muscle paralysis.
