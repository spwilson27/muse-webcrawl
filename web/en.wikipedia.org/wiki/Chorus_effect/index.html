Chorus effect


Placeholder image: a guitar chorus pedal

The chorus effect (or chorusing) is an audio effect in which a sound is duplicated, with the copies slightly delayed and continuously varied in pitch, and then mixed with the original. The result imitates the natural shimmering and thickening produced when several voices or instruments — a choir, or chorus — perform the same part together, none of them perfectly synchronised or in tune.

How it works

In an electronic chorus unit, the input signal is split. One path passes through unchanged; the other passes through a short delay line (typically 20–30 milliseconds) whose delay time is modulated by a low-frequency oscillator (LFO) at roughly 0.1–8 Hz. Varying the delay varies the pitch of the delayed copy by the Doppler effect, and mixing the two signals produces a moving comb-filter pattern of notches in the frequency spectrum together with the perceived doubling. Stereo chorus units use two modulated delays, often in opposite phase, to widen the stereo image.

Chorus is closely related to flanging, which uses shorter delays and often feedback, and to phasing, which uses all-pass filters rather than delay.

History and use

The effect was popularised in the 1970s by the Roland Jazz Chorus amplifier (JC-120, 1975), with its built-in "Chorus Ensemble" circuit, and by the Boss CE-1 Chorus Ensemble pedal (1976) and the compact CE-2 (1979). Chorus became a signature sound of 1980s pop and rock guitar — heard on recordings by The Police, The Cure and many others — and a defining feature of the Roland Juno series of synthesisers. It is widely used on electric guitar, bass, synthesisers and vocals.
