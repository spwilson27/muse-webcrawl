Shavuot (Hebrew: שָׁבוּעוֹת, "Weeks") is a Jewish holiday that occurs on the sixth day of the Hebrew month of Sivan (corresponding to late May or early June). One of the three pilgrimage festivals (shalosh regalim), it originally marked the wheat harvest in the Land of Israel and the offering of the first fruits (bikkurim) at the Temple in Jerusalem. In rabbinic tradition it also commemorates the anniversary of the giving of the Torah at Mount Sinai.

Origins and dating

The Torah commands the counting of seven weeks (the Counting of the Omer) from the second day of Passover; the festival falls on the fiftieth day, hence its Greek name Pentecost ("fiftieth"), later adopted for the Christian feast. In the Bible Shavuot is called the Feast of Weeks, the Festival of Reaping, and the Day of the First Fruits.

Customs

  • Tikkun Leil Shavuot — staying up all night studying Torah, a custom popularised by the Kabbalists of Safed
  • Book of Ruth — read in synagogue, fitting for its harvest setting and Ruth's acceptance of the Torah
  • Dairy foods — cheesecake and blintzes are eaten; explanations include the laws of kashrut being newly received and the Torah being compared to milk and honey
  • Greenery — homes and synagogues are decorated with plants and flowers, recalling Mount Sinai blooming
  • Confirmation — in some movements, Shavuot is the occasion for confirmation ceremonies for teenagers

The Ten Commandments

The Torah reading for the first day includes the Exodus account of the revelation at Sinai and the Ten Commandments, during which the congregation traditionally stands. In Israel the holiday lasts one day; in the diaspora two days are observed.

Agricultural significance

In the modern State of Israel, Shavuot's harvest aspect has been revived by kibbutzim and moshavim, which hold first-fruits parades and agricultural festivals; it is also a public holiday.
