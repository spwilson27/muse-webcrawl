Liquid cooling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Liquid cooling is the reduction of heat in electronic and mechanical devices through exploiting the properties of liquids. Liquids have a much higher capacity to carry heat away from a source than air, so liquid cooling systems can dissipate large heat loads in compact volumes and at lower noise levels than air cooling.

Principle and components

A typical liquid cooling loop consists of a cold plate or water block in thermal contact with the heat source (such as a CPU or GPU), a pump that circulates coolant, tubing, and a radiator where heat is transferred to the surrounding air, often assisted by fans. The coolant — usually distilled water with additives, or a dielectric fluid in immersion systems — absorbs heat at the block and releases it at the radiator. Because water's specific heat capacity is about four times that of air per unit mass (and far greater per unit volume), small coolant flows move substantial heat.

Applications

Liquid cooling is standard in internal combustion engines, where coolant circulates through jackets around the cylinders. In computing it was used in mainframes from the 1960s (such as IBM's Thermal Conduction Modules) and has become common in high-performance PCs for overclocking, in gaming consoles, and increasingly in data centres, where direct-to-chip and immersion cooling improve energy efficiency by reducing reliance on air conditioning.
