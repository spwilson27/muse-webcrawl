The San Joaquin Valley (/ˌsæn hwɑːˈkiːn/ SAN whah-KEEN) is the southern half of California's Central Valley. It stretches roughly from the Sacramento–San Joaquin River Delta in the north to the Tehachapi Mountains in the south, bounded by the Sierra Nevada to the east and the California Coast Ranges to the west. The valley is one of the most productive agricultural regions in the world, as well as a major oil-producing area, and it contains several of California's fastest-growing cities.

Geography

The valley floor is a broad, flat alluvial plain formed by sediment carried from the Sierra Nevada. The San Joaquin River drains the northern portion northward to the Delta, while the southern portion is an endorheic basin historically draining into Tulare Lake and Buena Vista Lake, now largely farmed lakebeds. The climate is semi-arid Mediterranean, with hot, dry summers and cool, foggy winters; the dense ground fog is known locally as Tule fog.

Economy

Agriculture dominates the economy: the valley produces grapes, almonds, pistachios, citrus, tomatoes, cotton, dairy products, and a large share of the United States' fruits and vegetables. Large-scale irrigation, supplied by federal and state water projects including the Central Valley Project and Friant Dam, made intensive farming possible. Petroleum extraction around Bakersfield — including the Kern River and Midway-Sunset oil fields — has been significant since the late 19th century.

Issues

The region faces persistent air pollution from agricultural dust, vehicle emissions, and stagnant winter air, contributing to high rates of asthma. Groundwater overdraft has caused land subsidence, and drought and water-allocation conflicts between farmers, cities, and environmental needs are recurring political issues. Seasonal re-flooding of the Tulare Lake bed has occurred in exceptionally wet years, notably 2023.
