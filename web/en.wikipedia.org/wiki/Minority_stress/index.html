Minority stress

Minority stress describes the high levels of stress faced by members of stigmatised minority groups. The concept, developed most influentially by psychologist Ilan Meyer, holds that disparities in mental and physical health between minority and majority populations can be explained in part by excess exposure to social stress — prejudice, discrimination, and stigma — above and beyond general life stressors.

The model

Meyer's model distinguishes distal stressors, such as experiences of violence and discrimination, from proximal stressors, such as expectations of rejection, concealment of one's identity, and internalised stigma (for example, internalised homophobia). These processes, combined with general stressors and moderated by coping and community support, are proposed to explain elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and suicidality observed among sexual minorities and other stigmatised groups.

Research and extensions

The framework has been extended to racial and ethnic minorities, transgender and gender-diverse people, and people with disabilities, and underlies public-health interventions aimed at reducing stigma and fostering supportive environments rather than treating individuals alone.
