Justinian I

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Justinian I (482 – 14 November 565), also known as Justinian the Great, was Eastern Roman (Byzantine) emperor from 527 to 565. A Latin-speaking Illyrian of peasant origin who rose through his uncle Justin I, he sought to restore the Roman Empire's lost western provinces, reformed administration and law, and left monuments — above all Hagia Sophia in Constantinople (built 532–537) — that defined Byzantine civilisation.

His enduring legacy is the Corpus Juris Civilis (529–534), compiled under the jurist Tribonian: the Code (imperial statutes), the Digest or Pandects (the distilled opinions of classical jurists), the Institutes (a student textbook) and the later Novels. The Corpus preserved Roman law and, rediscovered in eleventh-century Italy, became the foundation of the civil-law tradition across continental Europe and, through it, much of the world. Justinian's reign also brought the reconquest of North Africa, Italy and part of Spain (at ruinous cost), the Nika riots (532) suppressed with great bloodshed with his wife Theodora's support, plague (541–42), and wars with Persia.

Assessment

The near-contemporary historian Procopius left two irreconcilable portraits — the official Wars and Buildings, and the venomous Secret History — and Justinian's reputation has oscillated accordingly between great lawgiver and restorer and overreaching autocrat whose reconquests exhausted the empire he bequeathed.
