Full titleMedical Education in the United States and Canada: A Report to the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching

The Flexner Report is a book-length landmark report of medical education in the United States and Canada, written by Abraham Flexner and published in 1910 under the aegis of the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching. Many aspects of the present-day American medical profession stem from the Flexner Report and its aftermath.

Flexner, a schoolteacher and educational theorist rather than a physician, visited all 155 medical schools then operating in the United States and Canada. He found many of them scandalously inadequate: tiny proprietary schools with no laboratories, no hospital access, lax or nonexistent admission requirements, and curricula taught by part-time lecturers. He held up the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine as the model institution, with its university affiliation, full-time faculty, laboratory-based preclinical teaching and clinical clerkships.

Abraham Flexner (1866–1959), author of the report.

Recommendations

Among its principal recommendations, the report urged that medical schools:

  • Require a college degree, or at least substantial college-level science, for admission
  • Adopt a four-year curriculum: two years of laboratory sciences followed by two years of clinical instruction in teaching hospitals
  • Be integrated into universities and employ full-time faculty
  • Be subject to strengthened state licensing and accreditation

Effects

In the decades following the report, roughly half of American medical schools closed or merged. Proprietary schools largely disappeared, standards rose sharply, and American medicine was reorganised along scientific, university-based lines. The report is credited with transforming North American medical education into a rigorous, research-oriented enterprise.

Criticism

The report has also been criticised for its severe social consequences. Of the seven medical schools then training African American physicians, only two — Howard University College of Medicine and Meharry Medical College — survived, contributing to a long-term shortage of Black doctors. Most schools for women also closed. Critics further argue that Flexner's model contributed to the rising cost and specialisation of medicine and marginalised approaches such as osteopathy and eclectic medicine.
