Trial

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, a trial is a coming together of parties to a dispute to present information (in the form of evidence) in a tribunal, a formal setting with the authority to adjudicate claims or disputes. Trials decide criminal prosecutions and civil claims, and are governed by rules of procedure and evidence designed to ensure fairness and reliable fact-finding.

Types

In adversarial systems (typical of common law), parties investigate, present evidence and cross-examine witnesses before a passive judge — and often a jury, which decides facts while the judge rules on law. In inquisitorial systems (typical of civil law), judges take an active role in investigating and questioning. Bench trials dispense with juries. Most disputes in fact settle before trial; the trial's "shadow" shapes settlement bargaining.

Stages

A criminal trial generally proceeds through arraignment and pleas, pre-trial motions, jury selection, opening statements, the prosecution case, the defence case, closing arguments, jury instructions, deliberation and verdict, followed by sentencing and possible appeal. Safeguards include the presumption of innocence, the prosecution's burden of proof beyond reasonable doubt, exclusionary rules for improperly obtained evidence, public proceedings, and the right to confront witnesses. Civil trials apply a lower standard (balance of probabilities / preponderance) and typically end in damages or other remedies.
