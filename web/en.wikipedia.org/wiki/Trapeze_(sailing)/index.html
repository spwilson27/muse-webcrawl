Trapeze (sailing)

In sailing, the trapeze is a wire that comes from a point high on the mast, usually where the shrouds are fixed, down to a hook on a harness worn by a crew member. The crew stands on the side of the boat, near the sheerstrake, hooked into the trapeze wire, and leans out over the water, using their body weight to balance the heeling force of the wind on the sails.

Purpose

The trapeze allows a sailor's weight to be positioned much further outboard than hiking out in the cockpit, greatly increasing the righting moment they can generate. This lets a small dinghy or catamaran carry more sail and sail faster and more upright in stronger winds. Many high-performance classes permit one trapeze; some, such as the 49er skiff and many racing catamarans, permit both helm and crew to trapeze ("double trapeze").

Equipment and technique

The sailor wears a trapeze harness, typically with a spreader bar and hook, and clips onto a ring on the trapeze wire; handles and adjustable systems let the sailor move in and out and vary height above the water. Rules changes in the early 2000s required quick-release hooks after safety incidents in which sailors became trapped under capsized boats.

History

Forms of trapezing appeared experimentally in the 1930s; the device was popularized after the Second World War, notably by Peter Scott and John Winter in Britain, whose use on a Thames A Class Rater caused controversy, and the trapeze was banned in some classes for a time before becoming standard on performance dinghies such as the International 505 and Flying Dutchman in the 1950s.
