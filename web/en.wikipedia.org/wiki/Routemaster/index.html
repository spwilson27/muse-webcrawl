Routemaster

AEC Routemaster Manufacturer: AEC / Park Royal Vehicles
In service: 1956–2005 (regular service)
Length: 27 ft 6 in (RM) / 30 ft (RML)
Engine: AEC AV590 or Leyland O.600 diesel
Capacity: 64 seats (RM)
Operator: London Transport / successors

The AEC Routemaster is a front-engined double-decker bus designed by London Transport and built by the Associated Equipment Company (AEC) and Park Royal Vehicles. Introduced in 1956 and entering regular service in February of that year, it became one of the most famous and enduring symbols of London. Its most distinctive feature was the open rear platform, which allowed passengers to board and alight quickly, including at traffic lights and in slow-moving traffic.

Design

The Routemaster was designed to replace London's ageing fleet of trolleybuses and older motor buses. It used lightweight aluminium construction and advanced features for its time, including independent front suspension, power steering, an automatic gearbox, and a heating system for the saloon. The half-cab design placed the driver in a separate cab, communicating with the conductor—who collected fares and supervised the open platform—by bell signals.

The standard RM was 27 feet 6 inches long and seated 64 passengers. The longer RML variant, at 30 feet, seated 72 and became the most numerous type on central London routes in later years. A small number of forward-entrance and coach variants (RMF, RMC and RCL) were also built.

Service history

Nearly 2,900 Routemasters were built between 1954 and 1968. They dominated London bus services for half a century, outliving several intended successors because of their reliability, ease of maintenance and popularity with passengers and crews. The type survived the deregulation era and privatisation of London bus services, with refurbished examples—including new engines—working into the 21st century.

Regular Routemaster operation in London ended on 9 December 2005, when the last buses ran on route 159. The withdrawal was driven by accessibility requirements, as the open platform and high floor could not accommodate wheelchairs, and by the cost of crewing buses with conductors. Heritage services using Routemasters continued on selected routes for a further period, and many examples survive in preservation in Britain and around the world.

Legacy

The Routemaster's design influenced its eventual successor, the New Routemaster introduced in 2012, which revived the open rear platform concept (later enclosed with doors) and the rounded styling cues of the original. The bus remains a cultural icon, frequently depicted alongside black cabs and red telephone boxes as a symbol of London.
