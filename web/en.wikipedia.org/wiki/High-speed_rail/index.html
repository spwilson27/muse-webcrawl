High-speed rail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A white high-speed train with a long aerodynamic nose at a station platform]
A high-speed train in Japan.

High-speed rail (HSR) is a type of rail transport using trains that run significantly faster than those of traditional rail, typically at 250 km/h (155 mph) or more on purpose-built lines. Japan\'s Shinkansen opened the era in 1964; France\'s TGV, Germany\'s ICE, and above all China\'s vast network followed, and China now operates roughly two-thirds of the world\'s high-speed track.

Definitions

Definitions vary: the European Union uses 250 km/h for new lines and 200 km/h for upgraded ones, while other bodies accept 200 km/h. Systems use dedicated, grade-separated alignments with gentle curves, continuous welded rail and advanced signalling, often with in-cab displays instead of lineside signals.

History

The Tōkaidō Shinkansen opened in 1964 at 210 km/h, cutting Tokyo–Osaka times dramatically. France set a still-standing steel-wheel speed record of 574.8 km/h with a modified TGV in 2007. China opened its first true high-speed line in 2008 and built the largest network in little over a decade.

Technology

High-speed trains are usually electric multiple units with distributed traction, streamlined noses to manage tunnel pressure waves and noise, and regenerative braking. Experimental maglev trains in Japan have reached 603 km/h in testing.

Impact

HSR competes strongly with air travel on journeys of roughly 2–4 hours, often winning majority market share between major city pairs, with high capacity, city-centre access and lower per-passenger emissions than flying.
