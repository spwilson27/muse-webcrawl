Carl Richard Woese (15 July 1928 – 30 December 2012) was an American microbiologist and biophysicist. Woese is famous for defining the Archaea (a new domain of life) in 1977 through his work on phylogenetic taxonomy of 16S ribosomal RNA, a technique that revolutionised microbiology. He also originated the RNA world hypothesis in 1967.

With George Fox, Woese showed that the organisms then called archaebacteria were no more closely related to ordinary bacteria than to eukaryotes, leading to the Three-domain system (1990): Bacteria, Archaea, Eucarya. The proposal was initially resisted — sometimes bitterly — by mainstream biologists, but molecular phylogeny made it foundational; Woese's later work attacked the Darwinian assumption of a single last universal common ancestor, proposing communal, gene-swapping progenote collectives. He received the Crafoord Prize (2003) and the National Medal of Science (2000).

Ribosomal phylogeny

Woese's insight was that the small-subunit ribosomal RNA, universal and slowly evolving, is a molecular chronometer recording the deepest divergences of life. Cataloguing its oligonucleotide fragments through the 1970s was laborious manual work; the resulting tree revealed the archaea and, later, the bacterial radiation itself, and it remains the scaffold of microbial systematics even as whole-genome trees refine it.
