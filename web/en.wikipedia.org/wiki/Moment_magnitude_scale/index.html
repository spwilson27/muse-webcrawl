The moment magnitude scale (denoted explicitly with Mw or Mw, and generally implied with use of a single M for magnitude) is a measure of an earthquake's magnitude ("size" or strength) based on its seismic moment. It was proposed by Hiroo Kanamori and developed by Thomas C. Hanks and Kanamori in 1979 as a successor to the Richter scale that does not saturate for the largest earthquakes.

Definition

The seismic moment M0 is proportional to the area of the fault that ruptured multiplied by the average displacement and the rigidity of the rock. Moment magnitude is defined by Mw = (2/3)(log10 M0 − 9.1), with M0 measured in newton-metres. The constants were chosen so that moment magnitudes roughly agree with earlier scales, such as local (Richter) magnitude, for small and medium earthquakes while continuing to grow for great earthquakes, where older scales level off.

Energy

Because the scale is logarithmic, each whole-number increase corresponds to about 31.6 times more energy released. An earthquake of Mw 9 releases roughly a thousand times the energy of an Mw 7. The largest instrumentally recorded earthquake, the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile, measured Mw 9.5; the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake in Japan measured Mw 9.0–9.1.

Usage

Moment magnitude is the standard scale used by seismological agencies such as the United States Geological Survey for reporting significant earthquakes worldwide, and the magnitudes quoted in news reports of major earthquakes are almost always moment magnitudes. For small local earthquakes, local and duration magnitudes are still computed, but they are calibrated for consistency with Mw.
