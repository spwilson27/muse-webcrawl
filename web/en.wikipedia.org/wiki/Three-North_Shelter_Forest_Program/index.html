Three-North Shelter Forest Program

The Three-North Shelter Forest Program (Chinese: 三北防护林; pinyin: Sānběi Fánghùlín), also known as the Green Great Wall, is a series of human-planted windbreaking forest strips (shelterbelts) in China designed to hold back the expansion of the Gobi Desert and to reduce dust storms. Begun in 1978 and scheduled to continue until 2050, it is considered the largest ecological engineering project in the world, covering the "Three Norths" — north, northeast and northwest China.

Background

Decades of overgrazing, fuelwood collection and cultivation on marginal land accelerated desertification in northern China, and severe dust storms regularly affected Beijing and other cities. In 1978 the Chinese government launched the program to create shelterbelts across some 551 counties in 13 provincial-level regions.

Progress

By the 2010s the program reported hundreds of thousands of square kilometres of new forest and improved grassland. China's total forest coverage rose substantially during the program's life, and studies have credited the shelterbelts with reducing the frequency of dust storms reaching the capital. The project is organised in eight phases, with later phases emphasising native species and natural regeneration over monoculture plantations.

Criticism

Ecologists have criticised early phases for planting water-hungry monocultures, especially poplars, with high mortality rates and falling water tables in arid regions. Critics argue that fencing off degraded grassland to allow natural recovery — an approach increasingly adopted — is often more effective than planting trees where rainfall cannot support them.
