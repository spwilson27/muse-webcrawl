Fitz Henry Lane

Fitz Henry Lane (December 19, 1804 – August 14, 1865) was an American painter and printmaker of the luminism movement, known for his serene, precisely rendered paintings of harbours, ships and coastal scenes, principally of Gloucester and Boston, Massachusetts, and the coast of Maine. Born Nathaniel Rogers Lane in Gloucester, he later legally changed his name to Fitz Henry Lane; he was crippled in childhood, probably by polio, and pursued art as a career suited to his limited mobility.

Life and career

Lane apprenticed in lithography in Boston in the 1830s, working for the Pendleton lithography firm and producing marine views and cityscapes. By the early 1840s he had turned to oil painting, influenced by the English marine painter Robert Salmon. In 1848 he returned to Gloucester, where he built a granite house and studio overlooking the harbour. From there, often working with his student and assistant Mary Mellen, he produced the calm, glowing harbour scenes that define American luminism — works noted for glassy water, crystalline light, minute detail and a contemplative stillness.

Works

His paintings include The Western Shore with Norman's Woe (1862), Boston Harbor (c.1850–55), Owl's Head, Penobscot Bay, Maine (1862), numerous views of Gloucester Harbor, and ship portraits commissioned by merchants and captains. The Cape Ann Museum in Gloucester holds the largest collection of his work. Although modestly regarded in his lifetime, Lane was rediscovered in the twentieth century and is now considered, with Martin Johnson Heade and John Frederick Kensett, a leading figure of luminism.
