Yucatec Maya language

Yucatec Maya (endonimo: Maaya t'aan) is a Mayan language spoken in the Yucatán Peninsula and northern Belize. It is the direct descendant of the language of the Classic-period Maya civilisation and one of the largest indigenous languages of Mexico, with roughly 800,000 speakers. In Mexico it is commonly called simply Maya. Yucatec is a living language taught in schools in Yucatán state and broadcast on radio, though it faces pressure from Spanish.

Classification and history

Yucatec belongs to the Yucatecan branch of the Mayan family, most closely related to Itza, Mopan and the nearly extinct Lacandon. Before the Spanish conquest it was written in Maya hieroglyphic script; after the conquest, Franciscan friars devised a Latin orthography, and colonial-era texts such as the Books of Chilam Balam preserve its literary tradition. Bishop Diego de Landa's Relación de las cosas de Yucatán is an important early source.

Phonology and grammar

Yucatec has five vowel qualities, each contrasting in length and tone, and a set of ejective consonants written with an apostrophe (k', t', ts', ch'). It distinguishes plain and glottalised vowels, which can be distinguished in the modern standard orthography. Word order is basically verb–object–subject, and the language is ergative–absolutive in its person marking, using two sets of pronominal markers (Set A and Set B). Classifiers, especially numeral classifiers, are prominent.

Status

Yucatec Maya is co-officially recognised in Mexico under the General Law of Linguistic Rights of the Indigenous Peoples. It has a standard orthography maintained by INALI, a Wikipedia edition, and a growing presence in film and popular music, although intergenerational transmission is weakening in urban areas.
