A jirga (Pashto: جرګه, "assembly") is a traditional assembly of leaders that makes decisions by consensus according to the teachings of Pashtunwali, the Pashtun social code. Primarily held among the Pashtun people of Afghanistan and Pakistan's Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan, jirgas resolve disputes between individuals and communities, from land and water conflicts to criminal matters, and are attended — traditionally — by male tribal elders.

Functioning

A jirga seeks unanimous consensus rather than a majority vote. Proceedings are egalitarian in form: participants sit in a circle with no designated head. Elders hear both sides, deliberate, and announce a decision intended to be acceptable to all parties; enforcement relies on social sanction and fines (nagha). A smaller executive body, the maraka, may implement decisions. Temporary truces (tiga, "stone") may be declared while a jirga deliberates.

Loya jirga

A loya jirga ("grand assembly") convenes representatives from across Afghanistan for questions of national importance. Loya jirgas famously chose Ahmed Shah Durrani as king in 1747, adopted constitutions (1964, 2004), and approved the post-2001 political order, including the 2002 emergency loya jirga after the fall of the Taliban and the 2020 assembly on prisoner releases.

Criticism

Human-rights advocates criticise some jirga rulings that violate national law or women's rights, such as swara — giving girls in marriage to settle feuds — which is illegal in Pakistan but persists in places. Jirgas are banned or restricted by Pakistani courts, yet they remain a practical source of justice in regions where the state is weak. Women are generally excluded as members, though they may petition a jirga.
