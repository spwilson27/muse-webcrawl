Creation–evolution controversy

The creation–evolution controversy centres on the origins of life and the universe.

The creation–evolution controversy (often termed the creation vs. evolution debate or the origins debate) involves an ongoing, recurring cultural, political, and theological dispute about the origins of the Earth, of humanity, and of other life. In Euro-American geographies, the conflict is primarily between proponents of religion-based "creation science" and advocates of evolution, though the underlying question concerns the nature of religious literalism and the role of science in public education and society.

Critics of creationism, such as the vast majority of the scientific community, state that the scientific consensus on evolution is clear, and that what creationists call evidence against evolution has been thoroughly addressed. Advocates of creationism argue their worldview deserves equal standing, often framing the issue as one of academic freedom and fairness in science education.

History

The controversy began in the 1860s following the publication of Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859). Early opposition came from religious figures such as Bishop Samuel Wilberforce, who debated Thomas Henry Huxley in 1860. In the United States, the dispute became a major public issue in the early 20th century, culminating in the Scopes "Monkey Trial" of 1925 in Dayton, Tennessee, in which teacher John Scopes was convicted of teaching evolution in violation of the Butler Act.

Later legal battles shaped the landscape: Epperson v. Arkansas (1968) struck down laws banning the teaching of evolution; Edwards v. Aguillard (1987) ruled that teaching "creation science" in public schools violated the Establishment Clause; and Kitzmiller v. Dover Area School District (2005) held that intelligent design is not science and could not be taught as such in public school science classrooms.

Positions

Positions range along a spectrum. Young Earth creationists hold that the Earth is roughly 6,000–10,000 years old and that species were created in their present forms. Old Earth creationists accept an ancient Earth but reject common descent. Theistic evolutionists accept evolutionary science while believing God guided or initiated the process. The mainstream scientific position, reflected in statements by organisations such as the National Academy of Sciences, is that evolution is a well-supported scientific theory and that creationism, including intelligent design, is not science.

Public education

Much of the modern controversy concerns science curricula in publicly funded schools, particularly in the United States. Opponents of evolution have at times promoted strategies such as "teach the controversy" and "strengths and weaknesses" language in state standards. Scientific and educational organisations respond that there is no scientific controversy over the basic facts of evolution and that such language misrepresents the field.
