Bioinorganic chemistry

The haem group: an iron centre coordinated by a porphyrin ligand.

Bioinorganic chemistry is a field that examines the role of metals in biology. It encompasses the study of both natural phenomena — such as the behaviour of metalloproteins — and artificially introduced metals, including those in medicine and toxicology. Many biological processes, including respiration, photosynthesis and nitrogen fixation, depend on metal ions.

History

The field grew in the mid-twentieth century as X-ray crystallography revealed the structures of metalloproteins such as myoglobin and haemoglobin, and as model chemists synthesised analogues of active sites like the iron–sulfur clusters. Paul Ehrlich's earlier use of organoarsenic compounds to treat syphilis (Salvarsan) stands as an early application of inorganic chemistry to medicine.

Key topics

Major themes include the transport and storage of metal ions (e.g., ferritin for iron, metallothionein for zinc and copper), oxygen transport by haem proteins and haemocyanin, electron-transfer proteins such as cytochromes and ferredoxins, metalloenzymes catalysing difficult reactions — nitrogenase, photosystem II's oxygen-evolving complex, hydrogenases and methane monooxygenase — and metal-based drugs, notably the anticancer agent cisplatin.

Metals in biology

The essential metals include the bulk elements sodium, potassium, magnesium and calcium, and trace transition metals iron, zinc, copper, manganese, cobalt, molybdenum and nickel. Organisms regulate their uptake and distribution tightly (metal homeostasis), since both deficiency and overload are harmful: iron overload causes haemochromatosis, while copper mishandling underlies Wilson's and Menkes diseases.
