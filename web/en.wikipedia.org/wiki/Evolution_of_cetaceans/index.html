Evolution of cetaceans

The evolution of cetaceans — whales, dolphins and porpoises — is thought to have begun in the Indian subcontinent about 50 million years ago, during the Eocene epoch, from even-toed ungulates (artiodactyls). Molecular and fossil evidence shows that cetaceans are deeply nested within Artiodactyla, with hippopotamuses as their closest living relatives. Over roughly 10 million years, their ancestors made one of the most dramatic transitions in vertebrate evolution, from land-dwelling hoofed mammals to fully aquatic giants.

Early stages

The earliest known cetacean relative, the fox-sized Indohyus, was a small semi-aquatic herbivore of about 48 million years ago whose dense bones and ear structure link it to whales. The pakicetids, such as Pakicetus, were wolf-sized land mammals of Pakistan recognisable as early whales chiefly by the structure of the ear bone (the involucrum). Ambulocetus, the "walking whale", was a large amphibious predator that swam like an otter.

Fully aquatic whales

The remingtonocetids and protocetids spread through the shallow Tethys Sea, progressively adapting to life in water: the nostrils migrated back along the snout, the hind limbs shrank, and swimming shifted from paddling to oscillation of a fluked tail. By about 40 million years ago, the basilosaurids — including the enormous serpent-like Basilosaurus — were fully aquatic, with tiny hind limbs useless for walking.

Modern groups

Around 34 million years ago, in response to global cooling and changing oceans, the two modern suborders diverged: the Mysticeti (baleen whales), which evolved filter feeding, and the Odontoceti (toothed whales), which evolved echolocation. Subsequent radiations produced the roughly 90 living species, from river dolphins to the blue whale, the largest animal known ever to have lived.
