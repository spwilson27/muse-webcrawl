Frequency modulation (FM) is the encoding of information in a carrier wave by varying the instantaneous frequency of the wave. This contrasts with amplitude modulation (AM), in which the amplitude of the carrier varies while its frequency remains constant. FM is used most widely for high-fidelity radio broadcasting.

Principle

In FM, the frequency of the carrier signal is varied in proportion to the instantaneous amplitude of the modulating (message) signal, while the carrier's amplitude is held constant. The maximum shift from the carrier frequency is called the frequency deviation; in FM broadcasting it is ±75 kHz. The ratio of deviation to the maximum modulating frequency is the modulation index, which determines the bandwidth of the transmitted signal, approximated by Carson's rule.

History

Wideband FM was invented by Edwin Howard Armstrong, who demonstrated it in the 1930s and obtained a patent in 1933. Armstrong promoted FM as a way to overcome the static and interference that plagued AM radio. The FM broadcast band was moved to 88–108 MHz in 1945, rendering early receivers obsolete, and FM stereo broadcasting was approved in 1961. By the late 1970s FM had overtaken AM in music listening in many countries.

Advantages and disadvantages

FM is more resistant than AM to noise and interference, because most noise affects amplitude rather than frequency, and it exhibits a "capture effect" in which the stronger of two signals on the same frequency dominates. The trade-off is greater bandwidth consumption. FM is therefore used where fidelity matters more than spectrum economy, while narrower-band FM serves land mobile and maritime communications.
