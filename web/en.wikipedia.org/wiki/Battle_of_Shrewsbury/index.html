The Battle of Shrewsbury was fought on 21 July 1403 between an army led by the Lancastrian King Henry IV and a rebel army led by Henry "Hotspur" Percy of the House of Percy, at Battlefield, just north of Shrewsbury in Shropshire. It was the first time that English archers fought each other on English soil and proved the longbow's effectiveness in large numbers; for that reason it has been described as a precursor of the bloody battles of the Wars of the Roses.

Background

The Percys had grievances against Henry IV over unpaid sums for defending the Scottish border and the Welsh Marches, the king's refusal to allow them to ransom Scottish prisoners, and his support of their rivals. In July 1403 Hotspur raised an army in Cheshire and marched on Shrewsbury, hoping to join forces with the Welsh rebel Owain Glyndŵr. Henry IV marched swiftly to intercept him and reached Shrewsbury first.

The battle

After failed negotiations, the battle opened with a massive exchange of arrows, in which thousands were killed or wounded; the young Prince of Wales (the future Henry V) was struck in the face by an arrow but refused to leave the field. The king's right wing was driven back, but the royal centre held. Hotspur led a charge to kill the king, whose position was disguised by several decoys wearing his arms, but Hotspur himself was killed — struck in the face by an arrow, according to tradition — and the rebel army broke.

Aftermath

The Earl of Worcester was captured and beheaded. Hotspur's body was exhumed and quartered as a warning. The Percy threat was not finished — Hotspur's father, the Earl of Northumberland, rebelled again in 1405 and 1408 — but Henry IV's throne was secured for the moment. The battle features prominently in Shakespeare's Henry IV, Part 1.
