Go Tell It on the Mountain is a 1953 novel by American author James Baldwin. His first major work of fiction, it is a semi-autobiographical account of a fourteen-year-old boy's religious crisis in a storefront Pentecostal church in Harlem. The novel is widely regarded as one of the greatest American novels of the 20th century: Time magazine included it among the 100 best English-language novels since 1923, and the Modern Library ranked it 39th on its list of the 100 best English-language novels of the century.

Plot and structure

The novel unfolds over a single day and night — the fourteenth birthday of John Grimes, a thoughtful Harlem teenager who lives under the stern authority of his stepfather Gabriel, a deacon and itinerant preacher haunted by his own past. During a Saturday-night "tarry service" at the Temple of the Fire Baptized, John undergoes an intense conversion experience on the threshing floor before the altar. Framed by this present action, three long central chapters — "The Prayer of the Saints" — recount the backstories of John's aunt Florence, his stepfather Gabriel, and his mother Elizabeth, tracing their journeys from the rural Jim Crow South to New York in the Great Migration, and exposing the secrets beneath Gabriel's righteousness: an earlier marriage, an illegitimate son, Roy, whose violent death Gabriel interprets as God's punishment, and his coldness toward John, the child of Elizabeth's first love.

Themes

Drawing on Baldwin's own adolescence as a teenage preacher, the novel examines the Black church as both refuge and instrument of oppression, the legacies of slavery and racism within a single family, fathers and sons, sexuality, and the search for identity and grace. Its prose is saturated with the language and cadence of the King James Bible, from which the title — taken from the spiritual "Go Tell It on the Mountain" — also comes.

Reception and adaptations

Published when Baldwin was 28, the novel established his reputation, though comparisons with Richard Wright's protest fiction initially obscured its distinctiveness. It has since become a fixture of American literature syllabi. A television film adaptation aired on PBS's American Playhouse in 1985, starring Paul Winfield as Gabriel and James Bond III as John.
