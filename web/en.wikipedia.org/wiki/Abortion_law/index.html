Abortion law

Abortion law comprises the statutes, regulations, and court decisions governing induced abortion in each jurisdiction. Laws vary widely: many countries permit abortion on request within gestational limits, others allow it only on specified grounds such as risk to the pregnant person's life or health, fetal impairment, or socioeconomic reasons, and a few prohibit it in nearly all circumstances.

Historical development

Nineteenth-century statutes in Britain and the United States criminalised abortion at all stages, though common law had earlier distinguished abortion before "quickening". A liberalisation wave began in the Soviet Union in 1920 and spread through much of Europe after the Second World War; Britain's Abortion Act 1967 and the US Supreme Court's decision in Roe v. Wade (1973) were landmarks. The US position changed again with Dobbs v. Jackson (2022), which returned regulation to the states.

Legal frameworks

Common regulatory elements include gestational limits (frequently 12–24 weeks), mandatory counselling or waiting periods, parental involvement for minors, and conscientious objection provisions for medical staff. International human-rights bodies have increasingly framed access to safe abortion as an aspect of the rights to health and privacy, while other frameworks emphasise fetal rights.

By region

Most of Europe and much of Asia permit abortion on request in early pregnancy. Latin America has seen both liberalisation (Argentina, Colombia, Mexico) and strict prohibitions (El Salvador, Nicaragua). In Africa, the Maputo Protocol recognises abortion rights in defined circumstances, though implementation varies. Worldwide, an estimated 73 million abortions occur each year, and the safety of the procedure correlates strongly with its legal status and availability within health systems.
