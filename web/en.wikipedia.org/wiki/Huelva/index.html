Huelva (Spanish: [ˈwelβa]) is a city in southwestern Spain, the capital of the province of Huelva in the autonomous community of Andalusia. It stands at the confluence of the Odiel and Tinto rivers, near their joint estuary on the Gulf of Cádiz, close to the Portuguese border.

History

Huelva is among the oldest settlements in Iberia, with Phoenician and Tartessian roots; the Phoenicians knew it as Onoba, and it later became Roman Onuba. Its modern fame rests on two associations. First, the departure of Christopher Columbus: in August 1492 his ships sailed from nearby Palos de la Frontera, and the surrounding "Lugares colombinos" (Columbian places), including the monastery of La Rábida where Columbus planned his voyage, are major heritage sites. Second, mining: in 1873 the British Rio Tinto Company bought the ancient copper mines of the interior, built the port's mineral pier and a company quarter (the Reina Victoria district of English-style houses), and left a footballing legacy — Recreativo de Huelva, founded by British miners in 1889, is Spain's oldest football club.

Economy and environment

Today Huelva is an industrial port city with chemical and petrochemical complexes, fishing, and a surrounding agriculture famous for strawberries. The estuary's salt marshes (Marismas del Odiel) are a protected biosphere reserve, while the phosphogypsum stacks left by fertiliser production have been a long-running environmental controversy.
