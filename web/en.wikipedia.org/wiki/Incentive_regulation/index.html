Incentive regulation

Incentive regulation is an approach to the economic regulation of monopolies, especially public utilities, that relies on market-like incentives rather than detailed command-and-control oversight. Instead of guaranteeing a firm a rate of return on its costs — which can encourage over-investment and gives little reason to cut costs — incentive regulation rewards efficiency and performance.

Forms

The best-known form is price-cap regulation, often expressed as "RPI − X", in which prices may rise with inflation minus an expected efficiency factor X. Variants include revenue caps, sliding-scale regimes in which gains are shared between firm and consumers, yardstick competition comparing similar firms, and performance-based regulation tying returns to service quality and outcomes.

Rationale and criticism

Incentive regulation was widely adopted during the utility privatisations of the 1980s and 1990s, notably in the United Kingdom under the influence of economist Stephen Littlechild. Supporters argue it encourages cost reduction and innovation; critics warn it can lead firms to cut quality or investment, and that regulators still need extensive information to set caps fairly.
