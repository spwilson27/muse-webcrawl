Deontic logic

Deontic logic is the field of philosophical logic concerned with obligation, permission, prohibition, and related concepts. The term derives from the Greek déon, meaning "what is binding" or "what is proper". The field is generally traced to the work of Finnish philosopher Georg Henrik von Wright, whose 1951 paper "Deontic Logic" founded the modern discipline, building on earlier analogies between normative and modal concepts noticed by Ernst Mally and others.

Standard deontic logic

The basic system, known as standard deontic logic (SDL), extends propositional logic with an operator O read "it is obligatory that". Permission is defined as Pp = ¬O¬p ("it is permitted that p") and prohibition as Fp = O¬p. SDL is usually presented as a normal modal logic analogous to the system KD, with the key axiom D: Op → Pp — whatever is obligatory is permitted, ruling out irresolvable conflicts of obligation. Its semantics uses possible worlds restricted to "deontically perfect" alternatives.

Paradoxes and problems

SDL struggles with a family of puzzles, including Ross's paradox (from "you ought to mail the letter" SDL derives "you ought to mail the letter or burn it"), the Good Samaritan paradox, Chisholm's paradox about contrary-to-duty obligations ("ought"s that apply when a primary obligation has been violated), and dilemmas in which agents face conflicting duties. These problems have motivated dyadic (conditional) deontic logics, defeasible and input/output approaches, and applications in legal informatics and the specification of norms for artificial agents.
