Continued influence effect

The continued influence effect occurs when people continue to rely on information that they know has been retracted or corrected when making inferences and decisions. Even after a correction is issued and acknowledged, the original misinformation often continues to shape reasoning, particularly when no alternative explanation fills the gap left by the retracted information.

Research

The effect was demonstrated in classic experiments by Johnson and Seifert (1994), in which participants read reports about a warehouse fire. Some reports mentioned that volatile materials such as paint cans and gas cylinders were stored in a closet; this information was later retracted. Participants who received the retraction still cited the materials when explaining the fire, especially when answering questions requiring causal inferences. Subsequent research has replicated the effect across domains including news events, health information and science communication.

Explanations

The leading account is the mental model explanation: people build a model of an event, and a bare retraction leaves a gap in that model, so the discredited information is retained to preserve coherence. Corrections are more effective when they supply an alternative causal explanation. Other contributing factors include familiarity with the repeated misinformation and motivated reasoning.

Mitigation

Research on countering the effect suggests that effective corrections should be clear, repeat the correct information rather than the myth, provide a coherent alternative account, and ideally pre-empt misinformation through "prebunking" or inoculation. The Debunking Handbook (2020) summarises consensus recommendations from researchers in the field.
