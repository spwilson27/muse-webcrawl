The Xianbei (Chinese: 鮮卑) were an ancient nomadic people who lived in the eastern Eurasian steppes, in what is now Mongolia, Inner Mongolia, and northeastern China. Descended in part from the earlier Donghu, they rose to prominence after the decline of the Xiongnu in the 1st century AD and, under the confederation of Tanshihuai in the 2nd century, dominated the steppe from Manchuria to the borders of Han China.

From steppe confederation to Chinese dynasties

After Tanshihuai's death the confederation fragmented, and Xianbei groups moved south into the Chinese frontier zone. During the Sixteen Kingdoms period, Xianbei clans founded several states in northern China, including the Murong clan's Former Yan, Later Yan, and Southern Yan, the Qifu's Western Qin, and the Tufa's Southern Liang. The most consequential was the Tuoba clan's Northern Wei (386–534), which reunified northern China and, under Emperor Xiaowen in the 490s, moved the capital to Luoyang and pursued an extensive sinicisation programme — adopting Chinese surnames, dress, and language at court — before the dynasty split into Eastern and Western Wei. Xianbei elites intermarried with Han Chinese aristocracy and played a central role in the formation of the later Sui and Tang ruling houses.

Language and legacy

The Xianbei language is poorly attested; it is generally thought to have been Mongolic or para-Mongolic, though debate continues. Xianbei influence persisted in Chinese names, military institutions (such as the fubing militia system), and the ethnogenesis of several later peoples.
