Metaplasia

Histology showing metaplastic change in epithelium

Metaplasia (Greek: "change in form") is the transformation of one differentiated cell type to another differentiated cell type. It is generally an adaptive, reversible response to chronic irritation or inflammation, in which stem cells are reprogrammed—or new cells arise from other lineages—to produce a tissue type better able to withstand the adverse environment. Unlike dysplasia, metaplasia is not itself cancerous, though persistent metaplasia can predispose to malignant transformation.

Examples

Common examples include squamous metaplasia of the airways in smokers, where ciliated columnar epithelium is replaced by tougher stratified squamous epithelium; intestinal metaplasia of the stomach in chronic gastritis; and Barrett's esophagus, in which chronic gastroesophageal reflux causes the squamous epithelium of the lower esophagus to be replaced by intestinal-type columnar epithelium. Barrett's esophagus is a recognized precursor of esophageal adenocarcinoma. Metaplasia also occurs in connective tissues, as in myositis ossificans, where bone forms in soft tissue.

Mechanism

Metaplasia does not result from mature cells changing type directly; rather, it arises from the reprogramming of local stem or progenitor cells, under the influence of cytokines, growth factors, and chronic injurious stimuli in the tissue microenvironment. When the offending stimulus is removed, metaplastic tissue may revert to its original form.
