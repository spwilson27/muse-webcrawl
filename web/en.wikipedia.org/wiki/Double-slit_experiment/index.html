Double-slit experiment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Build-up of an interference pattern from individual electrons passing one at a time through a double slit]
Electron buildup over time: individual dots gradually form an interference pattern.

In modern physics, the double-slit experiment demonstrates that light and matter can satisfy the seemingly incongruous classical definitions for both waves and particles. This ambiguity is considered evidence for the fundamentally probabilistic nature of quantum mechanics.

The experiment was first performed with light by Thomas Young in 1801 as a demonstration of the wave behaviour of light. Versions with electrons were carried out in the twentieth century, most strikingly the single-electron build-up experiment of Akira Tonomura and colleagues (1989), in which the interference pattern emerges one detected electron at a time. Richard Feynman called the double-slit experiment 'the only mystery' of quantum mechanics, containing its 'heart'.

Overview

A beam of particles (or light) is directed at a barrier with two narrow slits; beyond it, a detector records where each particle arrives. When both slits are open, the accumulated detections form an interference pattern — alternating bands — as expected of waves passing through both slits and interfering. Yet each individual arrival is a single localised dot. Reducing the intensity so that particles pass one at a time does not remove the interference: each particle, in the quantum description, interferes with itself.

Which-path information

If a detector is placed to determine which slit each particle passed through, the interference pattern disappears and the distribution becomes the sum of two single-slit patterns. The loss of interference when path information is available — even in delayed-choice and quantum-eraser variants devised by John Archibald Wheeler and others — is captured by the trade-off between distinguishability and fringe visibility, and is understood in terms of entanglement with the detector and decoherence.

Interpretations

Different interpretations of quantum mechanics account for the experiment differently: in the Copenhagen interpretation the Wave function interferes and then 'collapses' on measurement; in the Many-worlds interpretation both paths occur in different branches; in pilot-wave (de Broglie–Bohm) theory each particle passes through one slit while its guiding wave passes through both. The experiment's results are the same in each; it is the canonical illustration that quantum behaviour resists classical pictures.
