A synagogue (from Ancient Greek συναγωγή, "assembly"; Hebrew: בית כנסת, beit knesset, "house of assembly") is a Jewish house of worship and assembly. Synagogues have a place for prayer (the main sanctuary), and may also have rooms for study, social halls, administrative and charitable offices, classrooms, and libraries. The synagogue is the central institution of Jewish communal life, although Jewish prayer can also take place anywhere a minyan (a quorum of ten Jewish adults, in traditional practice ten men) is gathered.

Origins and history

The origins of the synagogue are debated. Synagogues emerged during the Second Temple period, possibly developing during or after the Babylonian exile, and by the 1st century CE they were widespread in the Land of Israel and the Diaspora. After the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, the synagogue became the principal institution of Judaism, replacing the sacrificial cult with prayer, Torah reading, and study under the leadership of rabbis.

Architecture and interior

Synagogue architecture varies widely by era and country, from the mosaic-floored basilicas of late antiquity to Moorish Revival and modernist buildings. Interior features are comparatively constant:

  • Torah ark (aron ha-kodesh) – a cabinet, set into or against the wall facing Jerusalem, containing the Torah scrolls.
  • Eternal light (ner tamid) – a lamp kept perpetually burning before the ark.
  • Bimah – a raised platform from which the Torah is read.
  • Mechitza – in Orthodox synagogues, a partition separating men's and women's seating; liberal congregations sit mixed.

Services

Prayer services are held daily — morning (Shacharit), afternoon (Mincha), and evening (Ma'ariv) — with the fullest liturgy on Shabbat and festivals, including the public reading of the Torah. Services are led by a rabbi, a cantor (hazzan), or lay members of the congregation. Many synagogues also function as community centres, schools, and charities.

Notable synagogues

  • Dohány Street Synagogue, Budapest – the largest synagogue in Europe.
  • Dura-Europos synagogue, Syria – famous for its 3rd-century wall paintings.
  • Hurva Synagogue, Jerusalem – destroyed in 1948 and rebuilt in 2010.
  • Eldridge Street Synagogue, New York – a National Historic Landmark (1887).
  • Old New Synagogue, Prague – the oldest active synagogue in Europe (1270), associated with the legend of the Golem.
