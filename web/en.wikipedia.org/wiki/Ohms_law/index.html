Ohm's law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ohm's law
FormulaV = IR
Named afterGeorg Ohm
Published1827

Ohm's law states that the electric current through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points. Introducing the constant of proportionality, the resistance, one arrives at the three mathematical equations used to describe this relationship: V = IR, where V is the voltage, I is the current in amperes, and R is the resistance in ohms.

History

The law was established experimentally by the German physicist Georg Ohm and published in his treatise Die galvanische Kette, mathematisch bearbeitet (1827). Initially received with indifference, Ohm's work was later recognised as fundamental to electrical science, and the unit of electrical resistance was named the ohm in his honour.

Scope and limits

Ohm's law holds for many materials and devices — termed ohmic — over a wide range of conditions, but it is an empirical relationship rather than a universal law of nature. Non-ohmic devices such as diodes and transistors do not obey it, and resistance generally varies with temperature. The law also applies in modified form to AC circuits, where impedance replaces resistance. At microscopic level, a form of the law, J = σE, relates current density to electric field through the conductivity σ.
