Parvoviridae is a family of small animal viruses whose name derives from the Latin parvus, "small." Parvoviruses are among the smallest known viruses, with non-enveloped icosahedral capsids only about 18–26 nanometres across and a linear single-stranded DNA genome of roughly 4–6 kilobases. Because their genomes are so small, they encode few proteins and depend heavily on host-cell machinery — in particular on cells passing through S-phase — to replicate.

Structure and replication

The capsid is built from overlapping viral proteins (VP1, VP2, and in some species VP3) arranged with T=1 icosahedral symmetry. The genome carries hairpin sequences at both ends that serve as origins of replication; parvoviruses replicate through a "rolling hairpin" mechanism using their non-structural protein NS1/Rep, which has helicase and nickase activities. Viruses of the genus Dependoparvovirus — the adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) — cannot replicate productively without a helper virus such as adenovirus, a property that has made them valuable, largely non-pathogenic vectors for gene therapy.

Important members

Parvovirus B19 is the principal human pathogen in the family; it causes erythema infectiosum ("fifth disease" or "slapped-cheek syndrome") in children, can trigger aplastic crisis in people with haemolytic anaemias, and poses risks in pregnancy. Canine parvovirus emerged in the late 1970s as a devastating cause of enteritis and myocarditis in dogs and remains a major veterinary concern, closely related to feline panleukopenia virus. Other notable members include porcine parvovirus, minute virus of mice, and the invertebrate-infecting densoviruses.

Taxonomy

The family is divided into subfamilies including Parvovirinae (infecting vertebrates), Densovirinae (invertebrates), and Hamaparvovirinae (both). Classification within the family has been repeatedly revised as metagenomic surveys have uncovered many previously unknown parvoviruses in animal and environmental samples.
