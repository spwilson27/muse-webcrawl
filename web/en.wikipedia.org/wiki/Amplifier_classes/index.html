Power amplifier classes

In electronics, power amplifier classes are a system of letter symbols that classify amplifiers according to the fraction of the input signal cycle during which the active device (a valve or transistor) conducts current. The classification, running from class A through class H and beyond, expresses the trade-off between fidelity and efficiency in the amplifier's design.

Principal classes

In class A, the device conducts for the full 360° of the cycle; this gives excellent linearity but a maximum theoretical efficiency of only 25–50%, since power is dissipated even with no signal. In class B, each device conducts for half (180°) of the cycle, usually in a push–pull pair, raising efficiency to a theoretical 78.5% at the cost of crossover distortion. Class AB biases the devices slightly on (conduction somewhat more than 180°), largely eliminating crossover distortion while retaining good efficiency, and dominates analogue audio power amplifiers. In class C, conduction is for much less than half the cycle; the highly distorted output is filtered by a resonant load, making the class suitable for radio-frequency transmitters but not for audio.

Switching classes

Class D amplifiers switch the output devices rapidly on and off, encoding the signal in a pulse train (for example by pulse-width modulation) and recovering it with a low-pass filter. They reach practical efficiencies above 90%, run cool, and are used extensively in audio, motor drives and power supplies. Classes E and F are tuned switching designs for RF power amplification, while classes G and H improve efficiency by switching or modulating the supply voltage delivered to a conventional (usually AB) output stage.

Applications

The choice of class depends on the application: high-fidelity audio historically favoured A and AB, battery-powered and compact audio products now use D, and broadcast and communications transmitters use C, E or F for maximum RF efficiency.
