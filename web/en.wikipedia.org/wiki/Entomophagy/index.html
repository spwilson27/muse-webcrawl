Entomophagy

Entomophagy (from Greek ἔντομον éntomon, "insect", and φαγεῖν phagein, "to eat") is the practice of eating insects. Eggs, larvae, pupae and adults of certain insect species have been eaten by humans from prehistoric times to the present day, and the practice remains part of traditional diets in parts of Asia, Africa, Latin America and Oceania.

History and prevalence

An estimated 2 billion people worldwide regularly consume insects, and more than 2,000 insect species are recorded as edible. Commonly eaten groups include beetles, caterpillars, bees, wasps and ants, grasshoppers, locusts and crickets, cicadas, termites and dragonflies. In Mexico, chapulines (grasshoppers) are a popular snack; in Thailand, fried crickets and silkworm pupae are common street foods.

Nutrition

Edible insects are typically rich in protein, fat, fibre, and micronutrients such as iron and zinc, though nutritional profiles vary widely by species and preparation. Cricket flour and other insect-based ingredients have been marketed as sustainable protein sources.

Environmental impact

Advocates argue that insect farming requires substantially less land, water and feed than conventional livestock, and produces fewer greenhouse gas emissions per unit of protein, because insects are cold-blooded and convert feed efficiently. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations has promoted insects as food and feed in reports since 2013.

Food safety and regulation

Risks include allergenic cross-reactivity with shellfish and dust mites, microbial contamination and, for wild-collected insects, pesticide exposure. The European Union has authorised several insect species as novel foods, including house crickets and yellow mealworms.
