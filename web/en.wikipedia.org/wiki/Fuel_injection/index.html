Fuel injection

Fuel injection is the introduction of fuel into an internal combustion engine, most commonly automotive engines, by the means of a pump and injectors that atomize the fuel under pressure. It replaced carburetors in most applications during the 1980s and 1990s because it meters fuel more precisely, improving power, efficiency, emissions and cold starting.

Types

Systems are classified by injector location and timing. Throttle-body (single-point) injection uses one or two injectors above the throttle plate. Port (multi-point) injection places an injector at each cylinder's intake port. Direct injection sprays fuel directly into the combustion chamber at high pressure, enabling lean and stratified-charge operation in petrol engines and forming the basis of modern diesel common-rail systems, which can inject multiple times per cycle at pressures above 2,000 bar.

Control

An electronic control unit calculates the fuel quantity from sensors measuring airflow or manifold pressure, speed, temperature and exhaust oxygen, and adjusts injector pulse width accordingly. Feedback from oxygen sensors keeps the mixture near stoichiometric for catalytic converter operation. Mechanical systems, such as Bosch K-Jetronic, preceded fully electronic management.

History

Fuel injection appeared in early diesels in the 1920s–30s and in production petrol cars with the 1954 Mercedes-Benz 300SL's direct injection. Electronic systems spread after Bosch's D-Jetronic (1967), and emissions rules drove universal adoption.
