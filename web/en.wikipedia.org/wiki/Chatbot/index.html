Chatbot

A conversational user interface typical of a modern chatbot (image placeholder)

A chatbot (originally chatterbot) is a software application or web interface designed to have textual or spoken conversations with human users. Chatbots are used in dialog systems for various purposes, including customer service, information acquisition, entertainment, and, increasingly, general-purpose assistance powered by large language models.

Background

The term "chatterbot" was coined by Michael Mauldin in 1994 to describe conversational programs. The field's foundational figures include ELIZA (1966), created by Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT, which simulated a Rogerian psychotherapist using pattern matching, and PARRY (1972), written by psychiatrist Kenneth Colby at Stanford, which simulated a person with paranoid schizophrenia. The Turing test, proposed by Alan Turing in 1950, framed machine intelligence in terms of conversational indistinguishability.

Development

Early chatbots relied on keyword pattern matching and hand-written rules. Later systems such as A.L.I.C.E. used the AIML markup language. From the 2010s, chatbots increasingly used machine learning and natural language processing. The release of transformer-based large language models led to a new generation of general-purpose assistants, beginning prominently with ChatGPT in 2022, followed by systems such as Claude, Gemini, and others.

Applications

Chatbots are deployed for customer support, virtual assistants (such as Siri, Alexa, and Google Assistant), healthcare triage, education, e-commerce, and accessibility. They also raise concerns about misinformation, over-reliance, privacy, and the anthropomorphization of software.
