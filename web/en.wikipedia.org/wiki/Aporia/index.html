Aporia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Aporia (Ancient Greek: ἀπορία, aporía, literally "lacking a passage" or "impasse") is a term denoting a state of puzzlement or perplexity, and in philosophy a difficulty encountered in an inquiry or argument where plausible reasoning leads to contradictory or unacceptable conclusions. In rhetoric, aporia can also be a figure of speech in which the speaker expresses — or feigns — doubt about how to proceed.

In ancient Greek philosophy

The word derives from the privative a- and poros, a way or passage: to be in aporia is to find no way through a problem. Socrates, as portrayed in Plato's early dialogues, repeatedly brings his interlocutors to a state of aporia: their confident definitions of virtues such as courage, piety, or justice collapse under questioning, leaving them perplexed. Plato presents this condition not as a failure but as a productive beginning of philosophy, since awareness of one's ignorance is a precondition of genuine inquiry. In the Meno, Meno compares Socrates to a torpedo fish that numbs those who touch it; Socrates replies that he is himself numb and perplexed, and that the recognition of aporia motivates the search for knowledge.

Aristotle systematised the method: in the Topics and in Book Beta of the Metaphysics, he sets out the aporiai — the puzzles on both sides of a question — that must be surveyed before philosophical progress can be made. Working through the aporiai, reconciling reputable opinions (endoxa) where possible, became a standard procedure in later ancient and medieval philosophy.

In rhetoric

As a rhetorical figure (also called dubitatio), aporia is the expression of doubt or uncertainty, genuine or feigned, about what to say, which side to take, or how an audience will judge. Speakers use it to invite sympathy, to weigh alternatives publicly, or to insinuate a point while pretending not to know how to state it.

In modern philosophy

The term was given renewed prominence by Jacques Derrida, for whom an aporia is an irresolvable paradox or blind spot that haunts a text or concept — for example, the aporias of hospitality, forgiveness, justice, and death explored in works such as Aporias (1993). For Derrida, encountering an aporia is not merely a logical failure but the condition of any genuine decision, since a choice fully determined by rules would require no decision at all. The term is also used in literary criticism for moments of undecidability in a text.
