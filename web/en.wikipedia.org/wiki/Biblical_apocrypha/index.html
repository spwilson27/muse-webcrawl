Biblical apocrypha

Principal booksTobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees, additions to Esther and Daniel
SourceSeptuagint and Latin Vulgate
StatusCanonical in Catholic and Orthodox churches; "apocrypha" / deuterocanonical in Protestant usage

The biblical apocrypha (from the Greek apokrypha, "hidden things") denotes a set of ancient Jewish and early Christian writings that were included in the Greek Septuagint and the Latin Vulgate but are absent from the Hebrew Bible. Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches regard most of these books as deuterocanonical — part of the canon of Scripture — whereas Protestant traditions generally treat them as apocryphal: valuable for edification but not authoritative for doctrine.

Contents

The core books usually listed are Tobit; Judith; the Wisdom of Solomon; Sirach (Ecclesiasticus); Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah; 1 and 2 Maccabees; additions to the books of Esther and Daniel (including the Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Holy Children, Susanna, and Bel and the Dragon); and, in some lists, 1 and 2 Esdras and the Prayer of Manasseh. These works were composed roughly between the third century BCE and the first century CE and preserve history, wisdom teaching, and devotional literature from the Second Temple period.

Canon debates

The status of the books was discussed from antiquity: Jerome distinguished them from the Hebrew canon, while Augustine defended their ecclesiastical use. The Council of Trent (1546) formally affirmed the deuterocanon for the Roman Catholic Church. Protestant reformers, following the Hebrew canon for the Old Testament, relegated the books to a separate "Apocrypha" section — as in the 1611 King James Version — or omitted them, a practice that became common in the nineteenth century.
