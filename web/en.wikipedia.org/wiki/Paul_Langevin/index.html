Paul Langevin (23 January 1872 – 19 December 1946) was a French physicist who developed Langevin dynamics and the Langevin equation. He was a leading advocate of Einstein's theory of relativity in France, originated the "twin paradox" thought experiment's most famous form, and pioneered the use of ultrasound for submarine detection during the First World War.

Education and career

Langevin studied at the École Supérieure de Physique et de Chimie Industrielles (ESPCI) and the École Normale Supérieure, and spent a year at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge under J. J. Thomson. He obtained his doctorate in 1902 under Pierre Curie, whose close collaborator and friend he became. He succeeded Curie as professor at ESPCI and later held the chair of general and experimental physics at the Collège de France.

Scientific work

Langevin's doctoral work concerned ionised gases. In 1905 he published his theory of paramagnetism, applying statistical mechanics to magnetic moments — yielding the Langevin function — and an important theory of diamagnetism. In 1908 he introduced his equation of Brownian motion, giving a stochastic description of a particle subject to friction and random forces, a cornerstone of statistical physics. He was among the first French physicists to champion relativity and to clarify its paradoxes; his 1911 presentation of a travelling twin who returns younger drew wide attention. During the First World War he developed an ultrasonic echo-location system for detecting submarines, using quartz piezoelectric transmitters — the ancestor of sonar.

Political activity

Langevin was a committed anti-fascist and human rights activist. He helped found the Comité de vigilance des intellectuels antifascistes and supported the French Popular Front. His relationship with the married Marie Curie, revealed in 1911, caused a public scandal at the time of her second Nobel Prize. During the Second World War he was arrested by the Vichy regime and placed under house arrest. In 1944 he joined the French Communist Party. He died in Paris in 1946; his ashes were transferred to the Panthéon in 1948, alongside those of Jean Perrin.
