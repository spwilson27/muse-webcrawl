The Book of Leinster (Irish: Lebor Laignech), previously known as the Book of Noughaval, is a medieval Irish manuscript compiled around 1160 and now kept in the library of Trinity College Dublin. Written in Irish on vellum, it is one of the most important sources for medieval Irish literature, genealogy and mythology.

Compilation

The book was produced under the patronage of Diarmait Mac Murchada (Dermot MacMurrough), king of Leinster, apparently at Terryglass in County Tipperary. Its principal scribe and probable compiler was Áed Ua Crimthainn, abbot of Terryglass, who addressed a letter to the king's son-in-law bound into the manuscript. About 400 pages survive; the original was larger, and leaves are known to have been lost or dispersed.

Contents

The Book of Leinster is a great compendium of the learned tradition of Ireland. Among its contents are:

  • The fullest surviving version of the Lebor Gabála Érenn, the Book of Invasions central to the Mythological Cycle.
  • The most complete version of the Táin Bó Cúailnge, the central epic of the Ulster Cycle, though it breaks off before the end.
  • The Dindsenchas, the lore of place-names, explaining how Irish places got their names.
  • Extensive genealogies and regnal lists of the kings of Leinster and of Ireland.
  • Tales, poems, a list of Irish saints, and adaptations of classical material such as the destruction of Troy (Togail Troí).

Significance

Together with the older Lebor na hUidre (Book of the Dun Cow) and the later Yellow Book of Lecan, the Book of Leinster preserves much of what is known of early Irish narrative literature. Its Táin text, in particular, underlies most modern editions and translations of the epic.
