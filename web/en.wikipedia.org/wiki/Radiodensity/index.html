Radiodensity (or radiopacity) is the property of a material to impede the passage of electromagnetic radiation, particularly X-rays. Materials that block X-rays are termed radiopaque, while those that allow them to pass more readily are termed radiolucent.

On a conventional radiograph, radiopaque structures appear white or light grey, while radiolucent structures appear darker. Bone, which contains calcium, is strongly radiopaque; air in the lungs is highly radiolucent; soft tissues lie between these extremes. This contrast between densities is the basis of diagnostic radiography.

Hounsfield scale

In computed tomography (CT), radiodensity is quantified on the Hounsfield scale, in which water is defined as 0 HU and air as −1000 HU. Fat measures roughly −50 to −100 HU, soft tissue about +20 to +40 HU, and cortical bone +400 to +1000 HU or more. Metals and iodinated contrast media give very high values.

Contrast agents

Deliberately radiopaque substances are used as contrast agents: iodine-based compounds for blood vessels and soft tissues in CT and angiography, and barium sulfate suspensions for imaging the gastrointestinal tract. Medical implants such as pacemakers and orthopaedic hardware are radiopaque, which allows their position to be verified on X-ray images.
