Saros (astronomy)

Saros cycle

Length6,585.32 days (approx. 18 years, 11 days, 8 hours)

The saros is a period of exactly 223 synodic months — approximately 6,585.32 days, or 18 years, 11 days and 8 hours — after which the Sun, Earth and Moon return to almost the same relative geometry, so that a nearly identical eclipse can occur. The cycle was known to Babylonian astronomers and was used in the ancient world to predict the recurrence of solar and lunar eclipses.

After one saros, the Moon returns to the same node and phase, reproducing an earlier eclipse shifted about 120° westward.

How it works

An eclipse requires the Moon to be near a node of its orbit at new or full moon. The saros works because 223 synodic months (the phase cycle) almost exactly equal 239 anomalistic months (the cycle of the Moon's distance) and 242 draconic months (the cycle of returning to the same node). Because the period is not a whole number of days — it includes an extra third of a day — each successive eclipse in a series occurs about 8 hours later, and is visible roughly 120 degrees of longitude farther west.

Saros series

Eclipses are grouped into saros series, each lasting between about 1,226 and 1,550 years and producing 69 to 87 eclipses. A series begins with a succession of partial eclipses near one pole, progresses to central (total or annular) eclipses, and ends with partial eclipses at the opposite pole. At any one time roughly 40 saros series are active. The Antikythera mechanism, an ancient Greek astronomical calculator, included a dial for tracking the saros cycle.
