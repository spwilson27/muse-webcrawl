Texas oil boom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Wooden derricks at an early Texas boomtown oilfield.

The Texas oil boom, sometimes known as the gusher age, was a period of dramatic economic and social change in Texas in the early 20th century, beginning with the discovery of a large petroleum reserve near Beaumont. The boom transformed Texas from a largely rural, agricultural state into an industrialised and urbanised one, and made Texas a centre of the American oil industry.

Spindletop

On 10 January 1901, the Lucas Gusher at Spindletop, a salt dome south of Beaumont, blew out, producing an estimated 100,000 barrels of oil a day before it was capped nine days later. The field produced more than 3.5 million barrels in its first year — more than all other American fields combined — and tripled total United States oil production almost overnight. Beaumont's population swelled from about 10,000 to 50,000 within months.

Spread of the boom

Successive major discoveries followed across the state: at Sour Lake, Humble, Batson, and later in North and West Texas, including the giant East Texas Oil Field discovered in 1930, the largest in the contiguous United States. Boomtowns sprang up around each strike, with wooden derricks crowded so closely their legs overlapped, and wildcatters like H. L. Hunt made immense fortunes. The chaos of unregulated production in East Texas drove prices down to ten cents a barrel and led to the imposition of prorationing by the Railroad Commission of Texas.

Impact

Oil revenue funded the growth of cities such as Houston, Dallas, and Fort Worth, built refineries and pipelines along the Gulf Coast, and endowed Texas universities through the Permanent University Fund. Major companies founded or expanded in the era included Texaco, Gulf Oil, and Humble Oil (later part of ExxonMobil). The boom permanently reshaped the economy and culture of Texas.
