Traumatic brain injury

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A traumatic brain injury (TBI), also known as an intracranial injury, is an injury to the brain caused by an external force. TBI can be classified based on severity — ranging from mild (sometimes called concussion) to severe — mechanism (closed or penetrating head injury), or other features. Falls are the leading cause overall; other common causes include vehicle collisions, violence, sports injuries, and military blast exposure.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms depend on severity. Mild TBI may cause headache, confusion, lightheadedness, blurred vision, fatigue, and brief loss of consciousness or memory disturbance, usually resolving within weeks, though a minority have persistent post-concussive symptoms. Moderate and severe injuries can cause prolonged unconsciousness or coma, seizures, dilated pupils, clear fluid from the nose or ears, weakness, and profound confusion. In children, symptoms may appear as changes in eating or nursing, persistent crying, and drowsiness.

Mechanisms and pathology

Primary injury occurs at the moment of impact: contusions, lacerations, skull fractures, intracranial haemorrhages (epidural, subdural, subarachnoid, and intracerebral), and diffuse axonal injury from rotational acceleration that shears nerve fibres. Secondary injury unfolds over hours to days — cerebral oedema, raised intracranial pressure, ischemia, excitotoxicity, and inflammation — and is the main target of medical management. Focal injuries tend to follow local impact; diffuse injuries follow rapid acceleration–deceleration.

Diagnosis and management

Consciousness is graded with the Glasgow Coma Scale (3–15). CT scanning rapidly identifies bleeding and fractures; MRI is more sensitive for diffuse injury. Treatment of severe TBI aims to prevent secondary injury: maintaining oxygenation and blood pressure, monitoring and controlling intracranial pressure, treating seizures, and surgery where needed to evacuate haematomas or perform decompressive craniectomy. Rehabilitation — physical, occupational, speech, and neuropsychological — continues for months or years.

Epidemiology and prevention

Tens of millions of TBIs occur worldwide each year, and TBI is a major cause of death and long-term disability in young adults. It is associated with increased risks of depression, dementia, and neurodegenerative disease; repetitive head impacts in contact sports and the military are linked to chronic traumatic encephalopathy. Prevention includes helmets, seat belts, fall prevention in older adults, and rules changes in sport.
