Haskell Brooks Curry (12 September 1900 – 1 September 1982) was an American mathematician and logician. He is best known for his work in combinatory logic, for which he laid much of the foundation, and three concepts in computer science and logic bear his name: currying, Curry's paradox and the Curry–Howard correspondence. The programming languages Haskell, Brook and Curry are also named in his honour.

Life and work

Curry studied at Harvard and MIT, took his PhD at Göttingen in 1930 under David Hilbert with a dissertation on combinatory logic, and spent most of his career at Pennsylvania State University. Building on the earlier work of Moses Schönfinkel, he developed combinatory logic as a foundation for mathematics free of bound variables, working out its relation to Alonzo Church's lambda calculus, whose equivalence with combinatory logic he helped establish.

Legacy

Curry's paradox (1942) shows how naive logical systems with unrestricted self-reference collapse, and he anticipated the deep correspondence between proofs and programs later developed by William Howard. His two-volume Foundations of Mathematical Logic (1963, with Robert Feys) remained a standard reference in the field.
