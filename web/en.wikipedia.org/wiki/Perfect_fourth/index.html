Perfect fourth

In music, a perfect fourth is an interval spanning four letter names (a fourth) with five semitones. For example, the ascending interval from C to F is a perfect fourth, as F lies five semitones above C and there are four staff positions (C, D, E, F) between them. In just intonation the perfect fourth corresponds to the simple frequency ratio 4:3.

Character and use

The perfect fourth is one of the perfect intervals, along with the unison, octave, and perfect fifth. In medieval and Renaissance counterpoint it was treated as a consonance, but in later tonal practice a fourth formed against the bass note is usually treated as a dissonance requiring resolution — for instance in the cadential six-four chord. Above other voices, however, fourths remain consonant and are ubiquitous in melody and harmony.

The interval opens many familiar melodies: the first two notes of "Here Comes the Bride" and of "Amazing Grace" (in most renditions) form an ascending perfect fourth. Chords built in stacked fourths give quartal harmony, used prominently by composers such as Anton Bruckner and by jazz pianists including McCoy Tyner.

Variants

A fourth widened by one semitone is an augmented fourth (the tritone); a fourth narrowed by one semitone is a diminished fourth. The inversion of the perfect fourth is the perfect fifth.
