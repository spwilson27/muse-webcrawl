In geology, an igneous intrusion (or intrusive body, or simply intrusion) is a body of igneous rock that formed when magma crystallised beneath the Earth's surface. Because the surrounding rock insulates the magma, it cools slowly, allowing large crystals to grow: intrusions are thus composed of phaneritic (coarse-grained) rocks such as granite, diorite, and gabbro. The pre-existing rock displaced by the magma is called the country rock. Intrusions are exposed at the surface only after erosion strips away the overlying rock.

Classification

Intrusions are classified by their relationship to the layering (bedding or foliation) of the country rock and by their size and shape:

Discordant intrusions

Discordant intrusions cut across the layering of the country rock. Types include batholiths, the largest discordant bodies (covering more than about 100 km² at the surface, often forming the cores of mountain ranges such as the Sierra Nevada batholith); stocks, smaller batholith-like bodies; dikes (dykes), tabular sheets that cut across layering, often feeding volcanic eruptions; and necks (volcanic plugs), solidified conduits exposed after a volcano erodes, such as Shiprock in New Mexico or Devil's Tower.

Concordant intrusions

Concordant intrusions lie parallel to the layering. Sills are tabular horizontal sheets, often only metres thick but sometimes hundreds of kilometres wide — the Karoo and Ferrar dolerite sills, associated with the breakup of Gondwana, are among the largest known. Laccoliths are lens-shaped bodies that arch the overlying strata upward into a dome, and lopoliths are large concave-downward saucer-shaped intrusions, of which South Africa's Bushveld Complex (the world's largest known layered intrusion and the source of most of the world's platinum) is the type example.

Emplacement

How magma ascends and opens space for itself remains an active research question. Proposed mechanisms include stoping (breaking off and assimilating blocks of country rock), ballooning of the magma chamber, faulting and dyke injection, and ductile flow of the country rock at depth. Evidence from gravity and seismic surveys suggests large batholiths are assembled incrementally from many small intrusive pulses over millions of years rather than in a single event.
