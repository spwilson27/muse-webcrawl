Chaldea

Map of ancient Mesopotamia, with Chaldea in the far south near the Persian Gulf.

Chaldea (from Greek Khaldaía; Akkadian māt Kaldu) was a region in southern Babylonia, in the marshy lowlands between the lower Euphrates and the head of the Persian Gulf, in what is now southern Iraq. Its inhabitants, the Chaldeans, were a West Semitic people who migrated into the region during the early first millennium BC, during the decline that followed the collapse of the Late Bronze Age. The name later became synonymous with Babylonia as a whole, and "Chaldean" came to denote Babylonian astrologer-priests in Greek and biblical usage.

History

Chaldea
Region: Southern Mesopotamia
Period: c. 9th century BC – 539 BC
Peoples: Chaldean tribes (Kaldu)
Major tribes: Bit-Yakin, Bit-Dakkuri, Bit-Amukani
Principal centres: Ur, Borsippa, Babylon

The Chaldeans first appear in Assyrian records in the 9th century BC, organized into tribal territories called "houses" (bītu), the largest being Bit-Yakin in the far south. For two centuries they resisted Assyrian domination of Babylonia, and Chaldean chieftains repeatedly seized the Babylonian throne, most notably Marduk-apla-iddina II (the biblical Merodach-Baladan), who fought Sargon II and Sennacherib. Chaldean and Babylonian resistance helped provoke Sennacherib's destruction of Babylon in 689 BC.

The Neo-Babylonian Empire

In 626 BC the Chaldean general Nabopolassar took the throne of Babylon, allied with the Medes, and destroyed the Assyrian Empire, founding the Neo-Babylonian Empire — often called the "Chaldean Empire". His son Nebuchadnezzar II (reigned 605–562 BC) made Babylon the greatest city of the age, defeated Egypt at Carchemish, captured Jerusalem and deported its population in the Babylonian captivity described in the Bible. The empire ended in 539 BC when Cyrus the Great of Persia took Babylon.

"Chaldean" as astrologer

In the Hellenistic world, Babylonian priests were famed for astronomy and astrology, and in Greek literature and in the Book of Daniel "Chaldeans" simply means astrologers or wise men of Babylon, regardless of ethnic origin. This usage underlies references to Chaldean learning in authors down to late antiquity, and the name also survives in the modern Chaldean Catholic Church, an Eastern Catholic church whose members descend from Christians of northern Iraq.
