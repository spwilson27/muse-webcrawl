In physics (in particular in statistical mechanics), the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution is a particular probability distribution named after James Clerk Maxwell and Ludwig Boltzmann. It was first defined and used for describing particle speeds in idealized gases, where the particles move freely inside a stationary container without interacting with one another, except for very brief collisions in which they exchange energy and momentum.

The distribution is a probability distribution for the speed of a particle constituting the gas — the magnitude of its velocity vector — and depends only on the temperature of the system and the mass of the particle. For a gas of particles of mass m at temperature T, the probability density function of speed v is:

f(v) = (m / 2πkBT)3/2 · 4πv² · e−mv²/2kBT

Typical speeds

Three characteristic speeds can be derived from the distribution: the most probable speed (the peak of the curve), vp = √(2kBT/m); the mean speed, ⟨v⟩ = √(8kBT/πm); and the root-mean-square speed, vrms = √(3kBT/m). For nitrogen at room temperature these are roughly 422, 476 and 516 m/s respectively.

Derivation and assumptions

Maxwell derived the distribution in 1860 using symmetry arguments about the velocity components of gas molecules — the first statistical law in physics. Boltzmann later (1872–1877) placed it within his kinetic theory and the general framework of equilibrium statistical mechanics: the speed distribution follows from the Boltzmann factor e−E/kT applied to translational kinetic energy, combined with the density of states in velocity space. The result assumes a dilute, classical (non-quantum) gas in thermal equilibrium; for quantum gases the Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac distributions apply instead. More generally, in equilibrium the probability of any microstate is governed by the Boltzmann distribution, of which the Maxwell–Boltzmann speed law is the special case for particle speeds.

Applications

The distribution underlies the kinetic theory of gases, explaining diffusion, viscosity and thermal conductivity, and it determines the fraction of molecules able to escape a liquid (evaporation) or surmount an activation energy barrier, giving the temperature dependence of reaction rates in chemistry.
