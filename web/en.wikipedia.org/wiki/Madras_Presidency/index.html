The Madras Presidency, officially the Presidency of Fort St. George, was an administrative subdivision (province) of British India encompassing most of southern India, including much of the present-day states of Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh, and parts of Kerala, Karnataka, Odisha, and the union territory of Lakshadweep. It was established by the English East India Company and, after 1858, administered by the British Crown until Indian independence in 1947, when it became the Madras Province of the Dominion of India, and then Madras State in 1950.

History

The East India Company obtained a grant for a trading post at Madraspatnam in 1639 and built Fort St. George there in 1640. The settlement grew into a presidency in 1684. Through the eighteenth century the presidency expanded amid the Carnatic Wars with the French East India Company and conflicts with Mysore under Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan, and with the Marathas. By the early nineteenth century, conquests and subsidiary alliances had brought the whole of the Tamil, Telugu, and Malayalam coasts under Madras's administration, along with inland districts ceded by the Nizam of Hyderabad and annexed after the Anglo-Mysore Wars.

Under the Raj, Madras developed major educational and legal institutions — the University of Madras (1857), the Madras High Court (1862) — and pioneering social legislation. The presidency also suffered severely in the Great Famine of 1876–1878, which killed millions. Politically, the Justice Party, founded in 1916, championed non-Brahmin representation and won the first elections under dyarchy in 1920; the Indian National Congress and the Self-Respect Movement of Periyar E. V. Ramasamy also had strong roots in the presidency. Notable premiers and governors included C. Rajagopalachari, who led the Congress ministry of 1937–1939.

Geography and administration

The presidency was administered by a Governor based at Fort St. George, with a summer capital at Ootacamund in the Nilgiri Hills. It was divided into districts under collectors, and included princely states such as Travancore, Cochin, Pudukkottai, Banganapalle, and Sandur under British paramountcy. Its population spoke mainly Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, Kannada, and Odia, making it one of the most linguistically diverse provinces of British India — a fact that drove its reorganisation on linguistic lines after independence, beginning with the creation of Andhra State in 1953.

Legacy

After the States Reorganisation Act of 1956, the Malayalam-speaking districts went to Kerala, the Telugu districts had already formed Andhra Pradesh, and the remaining Tamil-majority state was renamed Tamil Nadu in 1969. The term "Madras Presidency" remains a reference point for the shared administrative and cultural history of South India.
