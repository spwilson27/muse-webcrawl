Rail transport in China is provided principally by the state-owned China State Railway Group Co., Ltd. (China Railway), operating the largest railway network in Asia and the second largest in the world. China's railways carry an enormous volume of passengers and freight; the spring festival travel season (chunyun) is the largest annual human migration in the world, with railways playing a central role.

History

The first railway in China was the short Woosung Road near Shanghai in 1876, dismantled by officials shortly afterwards. Railways expanded under foreign concessions and in Manchuria under Russian and Japanese control. After 1949 the People's Republic rebuilt and extended the network, including difficult lines such as the Qinghai–Tibet railway, which opened in 2006 and is the highest railway in the world, crossing the Tanggula Pass at 5,072 m.

High-speed rail

China has built by far the world's largest high-speed rail network since the mid-2000s, exceeding 45,000 km by the mid-2020s — more than the rest of the world combined. Lines such as Beijing–Shanghai (1,318 km, opened 2011) operate at up to 350 km/h. Indigenous Fuxing trainsets complement earlier Hexie trains derived from foreign technology. The network has transformed intercity travel, though its construction was accompanied by heavy debt and the 2011 Wenzhou collision, which killed 40 people and led to safety reforms and the fall of railway minister Liu Zhijun.

Freight and international links

Rail freight is dominated by coal and bulk traffic, including dedicated coal lines such as the Daqin railway. China–Europe freight trains, marketed under the China Railway Express brand, run through Kazakhstan, Russia, and Central Asia as part of the Belt and Road Initiative. International passenger connections include services to Mongolia, Russia, Vietnam, Laos, and (suspended at times) North Korea.
