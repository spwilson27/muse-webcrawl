Free group

Free group
FieldGroup theory, algebraic topology
Defining propertyNo relations among generators other than those forced by the group axioms
ExamplesThe integers (ℤ) are the free group on one generator

In mathematics, the free group FS over a given set S consists of all the words that can be built from members of S, considering two words to be different unless their equality follows from the group axioms (for example, st = suu−1t, but s ≠ t for distinct s, t ∈ S). The members of S are called the generators of FS, and the number of generators is the rank of the free group.

A group G is called free if there exists a subset S ⊆ G such that every element of G can be written in exactly one way as a product of finitely many elements of S and their inverses (aside from trivial variations such as st = sus−u).

Universal property

The free group on S is characterised by a universal property: any function from S to an arbitrary group G extends uniquely to a group homomorphism from FS to G. Consequently every group is a quotient of a free group — every group has a presentation by generators and relations.

Facts and examples

  • The free group on one generator is the infinite cyclic group ℤ.
  • The free group on two generators is non-abelian and contains a free subgroup of every countable rank.
  • Nielsen–Schreier theorem: every subgroup of a free group is itself free.
  • Free groups arise in algebraic topology as fundamental groups of graphs and of wedges of circles.
  • The existence of non-abelian free groups underlies the Banach–Tarski paradox.

History

Free groups first appeared implicitly in Walther von Dyck's work in 1882 on group presentations, and were studied by Jakob Nielsen in the 1920s, who proved that finitely generated subgroups of free groups are free; Otto Schreier proved the general subgroup theorem in 1927.
