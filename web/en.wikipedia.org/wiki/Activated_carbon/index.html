Activated carbon, also called activated charcoal, is a form of carbon processed to have small, low-volume pores that increase the surface area available for adsorption or chemical reactions. One gram of activated carbon can have a surface area in excess of 3,000 square metres, owing to its highly microporous structure.

[Image: Granular activated carbon]

Production

Activated carbon is manufactured from carbonaceous source materials such as bamboo, wood, coconut husk, peat, coal and petroleum pitch. Production involves carbonisation followed by activation, either physical (using steam or carbon dioxide at high temperature) or chemical (using agents such as phosphoric acid or potassium hydroxide), which develops the internal pore network.

Uses

Activated carbon is used in water purification, air filtration, gas masks, gold recovery, sugar refining, and the decolourisation of solutions. In medicine, activated charcoal is given orally to treat certain poisonings by binding toxins in the gastrointestinal tract. It is also used in respirators, solvent recovery, and as a catalyst support.

Reactivation

Spent granular carbon can often be regenerated by thermal reactivation, in which adsorbed organics are destroyed in a furnace, restoring most of the adsorptive capacity.
