Employee misclassification

Employee misclassification is the practice by employers of classifying workers as independent contractors (also known as freelancers, self-employed, or consultants) rather than as employees. Employers then do not have to pay payroll taxes for the workers, comply with wage and hour laws such as minimum wage and overtime rules, or provide benefits such as health insurance and workers' compensation. Misclassification may be deliberate, to reduce labour costs, or arise from genuinely ambiguous working arrangements.

Tests for classification

Legal systems use various tests to determine whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor. In the United States, the Internal Revenue Service applies a common-law test focusing on the degree of control exercised by the business over the worker's behaviour, the financial arrangements between the parties, and the type of relationship. The Department of Labor applies an "economic reality" test under the Fair Labor Standards Act, and several states apply the stricter "ABC test", under which a worker is presumed an employee unless the hiring entity satisfies all three prongs concerning control, the nature of the work relative to the business, and the worker's independent trade.

Consequences

Misclassified workers lose protections such as minimum wage, overtime pay, unemployment insurance, and employer-sponsored benefits, while governments lose tax revenue. Employers found to have misclassified workers may face back wages, penalties, and liability for unpaid taxes. The issue has become prominent in the gig economy, where platform companies classify drivers and couriers as independent contractors, prompting litigation and legislation such as California's Assembly Bill 5 (2019) and the ballot measure Proposition 22 (2020).
