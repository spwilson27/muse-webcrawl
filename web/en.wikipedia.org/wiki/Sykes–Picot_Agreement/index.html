The Sykes–Picot Agreement was a 1916 secret treaty between the United Kingdom and France, with the assent of the Russian Empire and the Kingdom of Italy, which defined their mutually agreed spheres of influence and control in the Middle East in the event of the partitioning of the Ottoman Empire. The agreement was based on the premise that the Triple Entente would defeat the Ottoman Empire in the First World War and formed part of a series of secret agreements sometimes thought of as a formal partition plan.

Terms

Under the agreement, negotiated by diplomats Mark Sykes and François Georges-Picot:

  • France would receive direct control of south-eastern Turkey, northern Iraq (around Mosul), Syria, and Lebanon (the "blue zone"), and a sphere of influence over an Arab state or confederation inland.
  • Britain would receive direct control of southern Mesopotamia (around Baghdad and Basra) and the ports of Haifa and Acre (the "red zone"), and a sphere of influence extending across the desert to Persia.
  • Palestine, containing the holy places, was to be placed under an international administration (the "brown zone").
  • Russia was to receive territory in north-eastern Anatolia and northern Kurdistan.

Contradictions and exposure

The agreement conflicted with promises made to Sharif Hussein of Mecca in the McMahon–Hussein Correspondence (1915–16), in which Britain had encouraged an Arab revolt against the Ottomans with the prospect of Arab independence, and with the Balfour Declaration of 1917, which favoured a "national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine. After the October Revolution, the Bolshevik government published the secret treaties in November 1917, embarrassing Britain and France and angering their Arab allies.

Legacy

After the war, the League of Nations mandate system broadly implemented the division: France received mandates for Syria and Lebanon, and Britain for Iraq, Palestine, and Transjordan, though boundaries were adjusted (notably at the San Remo conference of 1920 and in the transfer of Mosul to the British sphere). "Sykes–Picot" remains a widely invoked symbol in the Middle East of colonial interference and of artificial borders drawn by outside powers.
