Charon (mythology)

In Greek mythology, Charon or Kharon (Ancient Greek: Χάρων) is the ferryman of the dead who carries the souls of the newly deceased across the rivers Styx and Acheron that divide the world of the living from the world of the dead, in the realm of Hades.

Mythology

Charon was said to be the son of Erebus and Nyx (Night). He is depicted as a grim, filthy old man, or as a winged demon wielding a double hammer. A coin for Charon — the obol placed in or on the mouth of the dead — was the fare for passage; souls who could not pay, or whose bodies were left unburied, had to wander the shores of the river for a hundred years before being allowed to cross.

Charon appears in Aristophanes' comedy The Frogs, in Book VI of Virgil's Aeneid, where the Sibyl shows him the golden bough to gain passage for the living Aeneas, and in Dante's Inferno, where he ferries the damned across the Acheron. In Etruscan mythology the similar figure Charun was a psychopomp associated with the underworld.

In later culture

The largest moon of the dwarf planet Pluto, discovered in 1978, was named Charon after the ferryman. The figure of the ferryman of the dead has remained a common image in Western art and literature.
