Virulence

Virulence is a pathogen's or microorganism's ability to cause damage to a host. In the context of gene-for-gene systems, often in plants, virulence refers to a pathogen's ability to infect a resistant host. In most other contexts, especially in zoology, virulence is defined as the degree of damage a pathogen causes to its host; it is related to, but distinct from, pathogenicity (the qualitative ability to cause disease).

Virulence factors

The ability of a pathogen to cause disease is determined by its virulence factors, which include:

  • Adhesins – surface molecules that allow attachment to host cells.
  • Colonization factors – structures such as pili and fimbriae.
  • Invasins – proteins that facilitate entry into host cells.
  • Toxins – exotoxins and endotoxins that damage host tissues.
  • Capsules and immune evasion mechanisms – structures that resist phagocytosis and complement.

Evolution of virulence

Evolutionary theory predicts that virulence evolves as a trade-off between transmission and host exploitation. Pathogens that are transmitted vertically (from parent to offspring) tend to evolve lower virulence, while horizontally transmitted pathogens may evolve higher virulence if it increases transmission. The classic example is the myxoma virus introduced to control rabbits in Australia, which evolved intermediate levels of virulence over time.

Measurement

Virulence is often quantified by the median lethal dose (LD50), the number of organisms required to kill 50% of a test population, or by case fatality rates in epidemiological studies.
