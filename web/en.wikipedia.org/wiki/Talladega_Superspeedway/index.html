Talladega Superspeedway, nicknamed "Dega", is a motorsports complex in Lincoln, Alabama, north of Talladega. Built in 1969 by the International Speedway Corporation on the site of a former airfield, it is the longest oval used by NASCAR at 2.66 miles (4.28 km), a tri-oval with steep 33-degree banking in the turns. It is famous for extremely high speeds, close "pack racing" and multi-car accidents known as "The Big One".

History

Founder Bill France Sr. envisioned a track faster than his Daytona International Speedway. The first race in September 1969 was marred by a driver boycott over tyre failures at the track's unprecedented speeds; France pressed on with replacement drivers. The track was called the Alabama International Motor Speedway until 1989. In 1987 Bobby Allison's car became airborne and tore down catch fencing, injuring spectators; the incident led NASCAR to mandate restrictor plates at Talladega and Daytona to cap speeds, a regime later replaced by tapered spacers. Bill Elliott's 1987 qualifying lap of 212.809 mph remains the fastest in NASCAR history.

Racing characteristics

Because engine power is limited by the rules package, cars run in large, tightly bunched packs using the aerodynamic draft, producing frequent lead changes and three- and four-wide racing. The finish line is located unusually far past the tri-oval, near the entrance to turn one, which has produced controversial finishes. Dale Earnhardt is the track's most successful Cup Series driver with ten victories; his son Dale Earnhardt Jr. won four consecutive races there from 2001 to 2003.

Events

The speedway hosts two NASCAR Cup Series weekends annually, including the GEICO 500 in spring and the YellaWood 500 in the autumn playoffs, along with Xfinity Series, Truck Series and ARCA races. Its seating capacity is around 80,000, with thousands more camping in the vast infield.
