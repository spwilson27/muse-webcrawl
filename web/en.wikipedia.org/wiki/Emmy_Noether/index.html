Emmy Noether

Born23 March 1882, Erlangen, Germany
Died14 April 1935 (aged 53), Bryn Mawr, Pennsylvania
Known forNoether's theorem; abstract algebra; Noetherian rings
[Image: a black-and-white portrait photograph of Emmy Noether in the 1920s, smiling, in a dark dress]

Emmy Noether (23 March 1882 – 14 April 1935) was a German mathematician who made fundamental contributions to abstract algebra and theoretical physics. Her theorem connecting symmetries and conservation laws — Noether's theorem (1918) — is a cornerstone of modern physics, and her work on rings, ideals and noncommutative algebra reshaped algebra itself. Einstein called her the most important woman in the history of mathematics.

She achieved this despite systematic exclusion: barred as a woman from full academic status in Germany for much of her career, she lectured unpaid, sometimes under a male colleague's name, and only received a salaried position in her forties.

Work

At Göttingen, invited by Hilbert and Klein to help with the mathematics of general relativity, Noether proved that every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a conservation law — time symmetry to conservation of energy, spatial symmetry to momentum, rotational symmetry to angular momentum. The result, published in 1918, is used daily across physics.

Her 1921 paper 'Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen' founded the modern theory of ideals in commutative rings; concepts such as Noetherian rings and modules bear her name, as does the normalisation lemma and much of the vocabulary of modern algebra, transmitted by students she taught and by van der Waerden's famous textbook.

Exile and death

Dismissed by the Nazis in 1933 because she was Jewish, Noether emigrated to the United States, to a professorship at Bryn Mawr College and lectures at Princeton's Institute for Advanced Study. She died unexpectedly in 1935, aged 53, following surgery.

Recognition came largely posthumously: Noether is now routinely ranked among the greatest mathematicians of the twentieth century, and her name attaches to prizes, lectures, schools and a crater on the Moon.
