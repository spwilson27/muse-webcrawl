Absolute dating

Absolute dating is the process of determining an age on a specified chronology in archaeology and geology, usually expressed as a number of years before the present. It contrasts with relative dating, which only places events in order without measuring the time elapsed between them.

Radiometric methods

Most absolute dating relies on radiometric dating: the decay of radioactive isotopes at known rates. Important techniques include radiocarbon dating of organic material (up to about 50,000 years), potassium–argon and argon–argon dating of volcanic rocks, uranium–lead dating of minerals such as zircon, and uranium–thorium dating of carbonates.

Other methods

Other techniques include dendrochronology (tree rings), thermoluminescence and optically stimulated luminescence of buried sediments and ceramics, electron spin resonance, fission-track dating, amino acid racemisation, palaeomagnetic dating, and ice-core and varve layer counting.

Applications

Absolute dates underpin the geological time scale and allow archaeologists to date artefacts, sites and human migrations independently of historical records. Dates are conventionally reported with an uncertainty range, and radiocarbon ages must be calibrated against curves such as IntCal to convert them to calendar years.
