Prosecutor's fallacy

The prosecutor's fallacy is a statistical error commonly made in court, in which the probability of a defendant's innocence is confused with the probability of the evidence arising if the defendant were innocent. Formally it confuses P(evidence | innocence) with P(innocence | evidence) — an instance of the confusion of the inverse, related to neglect of the base rate.

Example

If a DNA profile matches one person in a million, a prosecutor may argue that the chance the defendant is innocent is one in a million. But in a population of tens of millions, several innocent people would be expected to match; the actual probability of innocence depends on how many people could match and on the other evidence — the reasoning formalised by Bayes' theorem.

Notable cases

The fallacy featured in analyses of the trial of Sally Clark, in which a statistic about double infant deaths was misused, and in discussions of cases involving DNA database searches. Its mirror image, the defence attorney's fallacy, dismisses strong evidence by noting that other people also match, ignoring that the defendant was identified by additional, independent means.
