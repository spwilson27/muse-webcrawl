Venus and Adonis (Shakespeare poem)

Venus and Adonis is a narrative poem by William Shakespeare published in 1593, during a plague closure of the London theatres, and probably his first published work. It retells the classical myth of Venus's unrequited love for the beautiful youth Adonis, drawing chiefly on Book 10 of Ovid's Metamorphoses. It was enormously popular, going through many editions in Shakespeare's lifetime.

Summary

Venus, goddess of love, falls passionately for the boy Adonis as he hunts. She pulls him from his horse and woos him with elaborate, witty rhetoric, but Adonis, interested only in the chase, resists her. A comic interlude describes his stallion breaking loose to pursue a mare. Venus warns Adonis against hunting the boar, dreaming of his death, but he slips away at dawn and is killed by the boar. Grieving, Venus curses love itself and prophesies its sorrows; from Adonis's blood springs the anemone, which she plucks and carries to her breast.

Context and dedication

Shakespeare dedicated the poem to Henry Wriothesley, 3rd Earl of Southampton, calling it "the first heir of my invention". Its sequel, The Rape of Lucrece (1594), was dedicated to the same patron. The erotic epyllion was a fashionable genre, following works such as Marlowe's Hero and Leander.

Reception

Contemporaries quoted and imitated the poem constantly; it was Shakespeare's bestseller in his lifetime, reprinted at least ten times before 1640. Modern critics praise its rhetorical brilliance and its dark, ambivalent treatment of desire, in which the goddess's wooing reverses conventional gender roles.
