Electrophoresis

Electrophoresis is the motion of dispersed particles relative to a fluid under the influence of a spatially uniform electric field. Electrophoresis of positively charged particles (cations) is sometimes called cataphoresis, and electrophoresis of negatively charged particles (anions) is sometimes called anaphoresis. The technique is fundamental to analytical chemistry and molecular biology for separating molecules by size and charge.

History

The phenomenon was first observed by Ferdinand Friedrich Reuss in 1807, who noted that clay particles migrated in water under an applied voltage. In the 1930s Arne Tiselius developed moving-boundary electrophoresis for separating proteins, work for which he received the 1948 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Zone electrophoresis on supporting media and, later, in gels greatly improved resolution and convenience.

Gel electrophoresis

In gel electrophoresis, molecules such as DNA, RNA, and proteins are driven through a porous gel—agarose for nucleic acids, polyacrylamide for proteins—which acts as a molecular sieve. Shorter molecules move faster, so mixtures resolve into bands by length. DNA is uniformly negatively charged, so its migration depends almost purely on size; proteins are often denatured and uniformly coated with the detergent SDS (SDS-PAGE) so that they too separate by mass. Resulting bands are visualized with stains or fluorescent dyes such as ethidium bromide.

Applications

Electrophoresis underlies DNA sequencing and fingerprinting, forensic analysis, clinical testing of serum proteins, and verification of PCR products. Capillary electrophoresis, performed in narrow tubes, offers high resolution and automation and is widely used in pharmaceutical analysis and genome sequencing.
