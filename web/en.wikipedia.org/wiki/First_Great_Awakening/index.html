First Great Awakening

The First Great Awakening (sometimes Great Awakening or the Evangelical Revival) was a series of religious revivals that swept Britain and its American colonies in the 1730s and 1740s. Part of a wider evangelical movement across the Protestant world, it emphasised personal conversion — the "new birth" — heartfelt preaching and a direct relationship with God, in reaction against formal, doctrinal religion. It permanently shaped American Protestantism and left a common religious experience across the colonies.

Course of the revival

Local revivals began in the 1730s — Jonathan Edwards's congregation at Northampton, Massachusetts, experienced revival in 1734–35, which Edwards described in A Faithful Narrative. The movement ignited across the colonies with the preaching tours of the English evangelist George Whitefield in 1739–41, whose powerful open-air sermons, publicised by the press, made him arguably the first transatlantic celebrity. In Britain, John and Charles Wesley and the Welsh revival of Howell Harris fed the parallel Evangelical Revival that gave rise to Methodism.

Beliefs and controversies

Awakening preachers stressed human sinfulness, justification by faith alone, the necessity of conversion and the work of the Holy Spirit. Edwards's sermon "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" (1741) became its most famous text. The revival split churches between enthusiastic "New Lights" and sceptical "Old Lights", encouraged itinerant preaching outside parish structures, and prompted criticism of emotional excess (and of Whitefield by, among others, Charles Chauncy).

Legacy

The Awakening boosted Baptist and Methodist growth, inspired new colleges (including Princeton, Brown, Rutgers and Dartmouth), encouraged missions to Native Americans and enslaved Africans, and — historians debate how directly — fostered habits of questioning authority that later fed revolutionary sentiment. A Second Great Awakening followed in the early nineteenth century.
