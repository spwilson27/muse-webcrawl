The Saab 900 is a front-engine, front-wheel-drive mid-size/compact executive car produced by the Swedish manufacturer Saab from 1978 to 1998, in two generations. It was developed from the earlier Saab 99 and became the company's best-known and best-selling model of its era, with 908,817 first-generation ("classic") cars built and 273,568 of the second generation, for a total of about 1.18 million.

First generation (1978–1993)

The "classic" 900 retained the 99's distinctive layout: a longitudinally mounted engine, tilted 45 degrees, driving the front wheels via a gearbox mounted beneath it. It was launched as three- and five-door hatchbacks; saloons and a popular convertible followed. Distinctive features included the wraparound windscreen, deep curved body sides, and an ignition switch mounted on the centre console between the front seats.

The 900 became strongly associated with turbocharging: the 900 Turbo, introduced in 1978, was among the first production cars to combine a turbocharger with everyday usability, and top models such as the Turbo 16 Aero (SPG in North America) produced up to 175 hp. In 1983 Saab introduced its APC (Automatic Performance Control) system, allowing the turbo engine to adapt to varying fuel quality.

Second generation (1994–1998)

The "new generation" (NG900), introduced for the 1994 model year, was developed under General Motors ownership on the GM2900 platform shared with the Opel Vectra. The engine was now mounted transversely. It retained the hatchback body style and ignition-between-the-seats layout, and added features such as the "Night Panel" instrument-dimming function. In 1998 it was facelifted and renamed the Saab 9-3.

Legacy

The 900 is remembered for its unconventional engineering, safety innovations (including early use of heated seats, cabin air filters, and robust roll-over structures), and a loyal enthusiast following. The classic 900 convertible and Turbo models are considered modern classics.
