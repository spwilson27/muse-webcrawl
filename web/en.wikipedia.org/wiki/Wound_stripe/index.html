Wound stripe

TypeMilitary uniform distinction
Introduced (UK)1916
Awarded forEach occasion of being wounded in action
FormVertical gold (or colored) braid stripe worn on the sleeve

A wound stripe is a distinction worn on the sleeve of a military uniform to denote that the wearer has been wounded in combat. Typically one stripe is awarded for each occasion on which a service member was wounded, so a veteran wounded three times would wear three stripes.

British and Commonwealth use

The British Army introduced wound stripes in 1916 during the First World War, following a suggestion that wounded soldiers should receive visible recognition. The stripe was a short length of gold Russian braid worn vertically on the lower left sleeve of the service dress jacket; metal versions were also produced. Stripes were also granted for the Second World War and, retrospectively in some cases, for earlier campaigns. The distinction fell out of use with post-war uniform changes, though it saw a limited revival in Commonwealth and ceremonial contexts.

United States

The United States Army authorized gold wound chevrons in 1918, worn point-up on the lower right sleeve — a precursor distinction to the Purple Heart, which was revived in 1932 and thereafter largely replaced wound chevrons. The modern U.S. Army instead uses overseas service bars and service stripes with different meanings.

Other countries

Several other armies, including those of France, Germany (with its Wound Badge, the Verwundetenabzeichen), and Russia, adopted wound badges or stripes in the 20th century; these vary between sleeve stripes and pin-on badges in grades according to the number or severity of wounds.
