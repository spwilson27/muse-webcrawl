Wire wrap

Wire-wrap connections on the back of a wire-wrap board

Wire wrapping is one of several methods of building electronic circuitry. It involves wrapping a bare wire around a metal post under tension, using a special tool, to form an electrical connection without solder. Wire-wrap construction is still used for prototyping and was extensively employed in early computers, telephone exchanges, and other equipment requiring many reliable interconnections.

Method

A wire-wrap connection is made by coiling the stripped end of a solid-core wire, usually 30 AWG, around a square or rectangular terminal post. A proper wrap forms several turns — typically seven turns of bare wire for a standard connection — in which the sharp corners of the post bite into the wire, creating many gas-tight contact points (as many as 28 for seven turns, four per turn) that resist oxidation. Because the joint is made under high pressure, it forms cold welds at the corners, producing a connection that is electrically and mechanically stable over decades of service.

Connections may be made with hand tools, hand-powered squeeze tools, or electric or pneumatic wire-wrap guns. Errors can be corrected by unwrapping with an unwrapping tool and re-wrapping, making the technique popular for prototyping, where changes are frequent. Wiring lists for large panels were often generated by computer, and automated wiring machines could produce complete backplanes.

History and applications

Wire wrapping was developed at Bell Telephone Laboratories in the early 1950s and became a mainstay of the telephone industry, where millions of connections in switching equipment proved extremely reliable. The technique was used extensively in early computers, including the Apollo Guidance Computer and many minicomputers; the backplanes of machines such as the DEC PDP series were wire-wrapped. Wire-wrap boards, with dual in-line package (DIP) sockets fitted with long square posts, were the standard prototyping method for digital circuits before solderless breadboards and inexpensive printed circuit boards became common.

Advantages and limitations

Wire wrapping avoids the heat and mess of soldering, produces connections that tolerate vibration, and allows circuits to be modified readily. Its disadvantages include the bulk of dense wiring, difficulty of use at radio frequencies, incompatibility with surface-mount components, and the cost and declining availability of special posts and tools. Nevertheless, wire-wrap remains in niche use for prototypes and for high-reliability assemblies, and vintage equipment is often maintained with the technique.
