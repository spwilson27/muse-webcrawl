Space telescope

PurposeAstronomical observation from space, avoiding atmospheric distortion and absorption
FirstOrbiting Astronomical Observatory 2 (1968)
NotableHubble (1990), Spitzer, Kepler, James Webb (2021)

A space telescope (or space observatory) is a telescope in outer space used to observe astronomical objects. Space telescopes avoid the filtering and distortion of electromagnetic radiation by Earth's atmosphere and can observe wavelengths — such as ultraviolet, X-rays, and much of the infrared — that are absorbed before reaching the ground.

History

Lyman Spitzer proposed a large space telescope in 1946. NASA's Orbiting Astronomical Observatories of the 1960s–70s were the first successful space telescopes, followed by ultraviolet and infrared missions such as IUE and IRAS. The Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990, became the best-known; its flawed mirror was repaired by a 1993 servicing mission. NASA's Great Observatories programme added the Compton Gamma Ray Observatory, Chandra X-ray Observatory, and Spitzer Space Telescope.

Modern missions

The Kepler mission (2009–2018) transformed exoplanet science by the transit method. The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in December 2021 and stationed at the Sun–Earth L2 point, observes primarily in the infrared with a 6.5 m segmented mirror. ESA's Gaia is mapping the Milky Way astrometrically, and future missions include the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope.

Advantages and drawbacks

Advantages include full-spectrum access and seeing limited only by diffraction. Drawbacks are high cost and the difficulty or impossibility of repair and upgrading, which make design margins critical.
