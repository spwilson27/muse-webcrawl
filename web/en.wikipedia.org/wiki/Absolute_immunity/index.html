Absolute immunity

In United States law, absolute immunity is a complete shield from civil liability — and in some applications from suit itself — granted to certain public officials for acts performed within the core functions of their office, regardless of malice, bad faith or knowledge that the act was unlawful. It is contrasted with qualified immunity, which protects officials only where they have not violated clearly established law.

Scope

Judges enjoy absolute immunity from damages for acts performed in their judicial capacity, even if done maliciously or in excess of jurisdiction (Stump v. Sparkman). Prosecutors are absolutely immune for acts intimately associated with the judicial phase of the criminal process, such as initiating a prosecution and presenting the state's case (Imbler v. Pachtman), though only qualified immunity applies to investigative or administrative functions. Legislators are protected for legislative acts, rooted in the Speech or Debate Clause. The President has absolute civil immunity for official acts (Nixon v. Fitzgerald), and in Trump v. United States (2024) the Supreme Court held that a former president has absolute immunity from criminal prosecution for acts within his exclusive constitutional authority, presumptive immunity for other official acts, and no immunity for unofficial acts.

Rationale and criticism

The doctrine is justified on the grounds that officials exercising discretionary judgment must be free from the fear that disgruntled litigants will harass them with suits, and that other checks — appellate review, professional discipline, elections, impeachment — provide remedies. Critics argue that absolute immunity can leave victims of serious official misconduct, such as prosecutorial fabrication of evidence, without any civil remedy.
