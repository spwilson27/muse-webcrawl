Appeal to fear

An appeal to fear (also called argumentum ad metum or argumentum in terrorem) is a fallacy in which a person attempts to create support for an idea by increasing fear towards an alternative. It is a type of appeal to emotion and is related to fearmongering and scare tactics.

Logic

The general pattern is: "If you do not accept P, then Q will occur" — where Q is a frightening consequence and is presented as if it followed from rejecting the proposal, without evidence that Q is likely or that accepting P would prevent it. The arguer tries to make the audience evaluate a claim by the fear it produces rather than by evidence. Fear appeals are common in politics, advertising, and public-health messaging; research in persuasion shows they can be effective when paired with a credible, achievable remedy, but they mislead when the threatened harm is exaggerated or irrelevant to the conclusion.

Examples

Classic examples include warnings that electing an opponent will bring economic ruin or war, advertisements implying social rejection without a product, and the rhetorical strategy sometimes called "fear, uncertainty and doubt" (FUD) in business and technology. The philosopher's stock example is the claim that one must accept a proposition or face eternal punishment, sometimes labelled an appeal to force when backed by a direct threat from the speaker (argumentum ad baculum).

Related concepts

The appeal to fear is closely related to the appeal to consequences, slippery slope arguments, and scare tactics. Not every warning of danger is fallacious: a warning supported by evidence and proportionate to the risk is a legitimate prudential argument.
