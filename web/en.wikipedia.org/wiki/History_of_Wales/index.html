History of Wales

RegionWales, western Great Britain
Key eventsRoman conquest, Anglo-Norman expansion, Edwardian conquest (1283), Acts of Union (1536–1543), devolution (1999)

The history of Wales spans the period from the earliest human habitation of the region, tens of thousands of years ago, to the present day. Wales emerged as a distinct cultural and political identity from the Celtic Britons after the Roman withdrawal from Britain in the 5th century, and modern Welsh national identity consolidated in the early Middle Ages around kingdoms such as Gwynedd, Powys, and Deheubarth.

Prehistory and Roman period

Humans have lived in what is now Wales since the Palaeolithic; the Red Lady of Paviland, a burial in the Gower Peninsula dating to around 33,000 years ago, is among the earliest known ceremonial burials in Western Europe. Neolithic communities built chambered tombs such as Pentre Ifan, and Bronze Age and Iron Age peoples raised hillforts across the country. Rome conquered the Welsh tribes — including the Silures and Ordovices, who resisted fiercely under leaders such as Caratacus — during the campaigns of AD 48–78, establishing forts at Isca (Caerleon) and Deva (Chester) and mining gold at Dolaucothi.

Medieval Wales

After Rome's withdrawal, independent Brythonic kingdoms formed. In the 9th and 10th centuries, Rhodri the Great and his grandson Hywel Dda, who codified Welsh law, ruled much of Wales. The Norman invasion after 1066 brought Marcher lords to the south and east, while native princes retained the north and west. Llywelyn the Great and his grandson Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, recognized as Prince of Wales in 1267, briefly united much of the country before Edward I's conquest of 1282–83, sealed by the Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) and a ring of great castles. Owain Glyndŵr's revolt (1400–1415) was the last major Welsh bid for independence.

Union and industrialization

The Laws in Wales Acts 1535 and 1542, passed under Henry VIII, whose dynasty had Welsh origins, incorporated Wales into the Kingdom of England and imposed English law and administration. The translation of the Bible into Welsh (1588) helped preserve the Welsh language. In the 19th century, south Wales became a powerhouse of the Industrial Revolution: Merthyr Tydfil was briefly the iron capital of the world, and coal from the Rhondda and other valleys fuelled global shipping. Industrial growth brought a strong Nonconformist chapel culture, the Chartist Newport Rising of 1839, and later the Rebecca Riots in the rural west.

Modern Wales

The 20th century saw the decline of coal and heavy industry, the founding of Plaid Cymru (1925), the Welsh language revival, and campaigns for self-government. A 1979 referendum rejected devolution, but a narrow vote in 1997 approved it, and the National Assembly for Wales (now the Senedd) opened in 1999, gaining primary law-making powers after 2011. Cardiff, made the capital in 1955, hosts the devolved Welsh Government.
