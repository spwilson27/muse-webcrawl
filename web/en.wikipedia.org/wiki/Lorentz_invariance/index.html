Lorentz invariance

Lorentz invariance
Also calledLorentz symmetry, Lorentz covariance
Named afterHendrik Lorentz
FrameworkSpecial relativity; Standard Model

Lorentz invariance (or Lorentz symmetry) is the property of physical laws whereby they take the same form in all inertial frames of reference — that is, the outcome of an experiment does not depend on the orientation or the boost velocity of the laboratory through space. It is a key feature of Einstein's theory of special relativity, in which the Lorentz transformations replace Galilean transformations as the correct relation between frames.

Background

Lorentz invariance arose from attempts to reconcile Maxwell's equations of electrodynamics with mechanics. The Michelson–Morley experiment (1887) failed to detect the expected motion of the Earth through the luminiferous aether, and Lorentz and FitzGerald proposed length contraction to explain the null result. Einstein's 1905 theory derived the Lorentz transformations from two postulates: the principle of relativity and the constancy of the speed of light in vacuum for all observers.

A quantity that transforms in a well-defined way under Lorentz transformations is called Lorentz covariant; a scalar quantity unchanged by them, such as the spacetime interval or a particle's rest mass, is Lorentz invariant.

Tests and possible violation

Lorentz invariance has been verified to extraordinary precision by experiments ranging from modern versions of the Michelson–Morley and Kennedy–Thorndike experiments to observations of high-energy cosmic rays and gamma-ray bursts. Nevertheless, many approaches to quantum gravity suggest that Lorentz symmetry might be violated at the Planck scale, motivating searches for violations using the Standard-Model Extension framework, time-of-flight measurements of distant astrophysical sources, and tests of the equivalence principle. No confirmed violation has been found.

In general relativity, Lorentz invariance holds locally: in a sufficiently small freely falling region, the laws of special relativity apply approximately. Global Lorentz invariance is not required in curved spacetime.
