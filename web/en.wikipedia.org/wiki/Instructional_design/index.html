Instructional design

Instructional design (ID), also known as instructional systems design, is the practice of systematically designing, developing, and delivering instructional materials and experiences, both digital and physical, in a consistent and reliable fashion toward promoting efficient, effective, appealing, and engaging learning. It draws on learning theory, psychology, and systems thinking, and is used in schools, universities, corporate training, the military, and online education.

History

The discipline grew out of World War II training research, when psychologists developed systematic methods to train large numbers of soldiers. B. F. Skinner's work on programmed instruction in the 1950s introduced material broken into small steps with immediate feedback. Robert Gagné's The Conditions of Learning (1965) described nine events of instruction and a hierarchy of learning outcomes, and Benjamin Bloom's taxonomy (1956) provided a framework for classifying educational objectives. Robert Mager popularized precisely written behavioural objectives.

Models

The best-known generic model is ADDIE: Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, and Evaluation. The Dick and Carey model treats instruction as a system of interlocking components from goal analysis to summative evaluation. Agile alternatives such as the Successive Approximation Model (SAM) favour rapid prototyping and iteration, while backward design (Understanding by Design) starts from desired outcomes and acceptable evidence before planning activities. Designers also apply principles of multimedia learning (Richard Mayer), cognitive load theory (John Sweller), and Merrill's First Principles of Instruction.

Practice

Instructional designers analyse learners and needs, write measurable objectives, select strategies and media, storyboard and build materials (including e-learning modules, simulations, and job aids), and evaluate results, often against Kirkpatrick's four levels of training evaluation: reaction, learning, behaviour, and results.
