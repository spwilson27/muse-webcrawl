George Willis Ritchey

Born 31 December 1864, Tuppers Plains, Ohio, U.S.
Died 4 November 1945 (aged 80), Azusa, California, U.S.
Occupation Astronomer, telescope maker, optician
Known for Ritchey–Chrétien telescope design

George Willis Ritchey (December 31, 1864 – November 4, 1945) was an American optician and telescope maker and astronomer. He is best known for inventing, with the French astronomer Henri Chrétien, the Ritchey–Chrétien telescope design, which is used in nearly all modern professional research telescopes, including the Hubble Space Telescope.

Career

Ritchey was born in Tuppers Plains, Ohio, and trained as a furniture maker before becoming interested in astronomy and optics. In 1890 he took charge of the optical shop at the new Yerkes Observatory, where he worked under George Ellery Hale. There he ground and figured the mirrors for the observatory's 24-inch reflecting telescope and developed important techniques of astronomical photography, producing celebrated photographs of nebulae and the Moon.

When Hale established Mount Wilson Observatory, Ritchey joined him in Pasadena and headed the optical and instrument shops. He figured the mirrors for the observatory's 60-inch (1.5 m) reflector — the largest telescope in the world when it was completed in 1908 — and designed and supervised construction of the 100-inch (2.5 m) Hooker telescope, completed in 1917. In 1919, after a falling-out with Hale, Ritchey left Mount Wilson.

Ritchey–Chrétien design and later work

The Ritchey–Chrétien configuration, devised around 1910, uses two hyperbolic mirrors to eliminate coma and give a wide, flat field of view free of optical aberration. Ritchey later received support from France and worked at the Paris Observatory, and a 40-inch Ritchey–Chrétien telescope he designed was installed at the United States Naval Observatory in Washington, D.C., later relocated to Flagstaff, Arizona. In his final years he promoted ambitious plans for giant telescopes and wrote on telescope design and cellular mirror construction.

Honors

The lunar crater Ritchey and the Mars crater Ritchey are named after him, as is the asteroid 4300? — in fact asteroid (5071) is not his; the asteroid 4300? Documentation: the crater names are the principal honors. Ritchey's photographs of the Moon and nebulae remained standards of astronomical photography for decades.
