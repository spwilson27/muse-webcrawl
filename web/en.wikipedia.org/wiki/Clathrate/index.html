Clathrate

A clathrate is a chemical substance consisting of a lattice that traps or contains molecules. The word clathrate is derived from the Latin clathratus, meaning "with bars" or "a lattice". Traditionally the term refers to clathrate hydrates, in which a framework of water molecules linked by hydrogen bonds forms cages around small guest molecules such as methane, carbon dioxide, or chlorine; more broadly it includes inclusion compounds in which other hosts — hydroquinone, urea, or certain metal-organic frameworks — trap guests. The trapped molecules are held by van der Waals forces rather than chemical bonds, and the compound decomposes when the lattice melts or the pressure falls.

Clathrate hydrates

Clathrate hydrates, or gas hydrates, form at low temperatures and elevated pressures when water freezes around dissolved gas. Humphry Davy first described the chlorine hydrate in 1810. The principal crystal structures are cubic structure I (sI), cubic structure II (sII), and hexagonal structure H (sH), built from polyhedral cages such as the pentagonal dodecahedron. Methane clathrate ("methane ice") occurs naturally in enormous quantities in permafrost and in marine sediments on continental margins, and constitutes a potential energy resource larger than all other fossil fuels combined, as well as a climate concern: destabilisation of hydrates could release methane, a potent greenhouse gas, in a feedback known as the clathrate gun hypothesis.

Occurrence and significance

Gas hydrates plug oil and gas pipelines, where preventing their formation is a major engineering concern (flow assurance), and they are studied as a means of gas storage, transport, desalination, and carbon sequestration — CO2 can in principle be stored in hydrate form beneath the seafloor. Hydrates are also believed to occur widely in the outer Solar System, in comets, and on icy moons, and clathrate trapping is invoked to explain the composition of some planetary atmospheres and cryovolcanism. In chemistry, clathrates such as the hydroquinone and Dianin's compound clathrates have been used to separate and store volatile compounds, and the concept extends to clathrate compounds of silicon and germanium studied as thermoelectrics.
