Ramesses II

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The four colossal seated statues of Ramesses II at the Great Temple of Abu Simbel]

Ramesses II (c. 1303–1213 BC), also called Ramesses the Great, was the third pharaoh of the Nineteenth Dynasty of Egypt and one of its most celebrated rulers. Reigning for sixty-six years (1279–1213 BC) — the second-longest reign in Egyptian history — he campaigned in Syria against the Hittites, fought the great chariot Battle of Kadesh, signed the world's earliest surviving peace treaty, and covered Egypt and Nubia with colossal statues, temples and inscriptions, including Abu Simbel and the Ramesseum. In later tradition he is often identified, without firm evidence, as the pharaoh of the Exodus.

Kadesh and the peace

In his fifth regnal year, 1274 BC, Ramesses led some 20,000 men against the Hittite king Muwatalli II at Kadesh on the Orontes. Ambushed by Hittite chariots, the Egyptians escaped disaster through Ramesses' personal stand and the timely arrival of reinforcements; both sides claimed victory, and Ramesses had his 'heroic' version carved on temple walls across Egypt. Fifteen years later (1258 BC) he and Hattusili III concluded a formal peace treaty — a copy hung at the United Nations headquarters — sealed by Ramesses' marriage to a Hittite princess.

Builder and legacy

Ramesses moved the capital to Pi-Ramesses in the Delta, raised colossi at Memphis, Luxor and Abu Simbel, completed the hypostyle hall at Karnak, and fathered over a hundred children by numerous wives, chief among them Nefertari, whose painted tomb is among the finest in the Valley of the Queens. Dying at about ninety, he was succeeded by his thirteenth son, Merneptah. Nineteenth-century rediscovery — Shelley's sonnet 'Ozymandias' (from his Greek name) — made him the emblem of vanished imperial grandeur.
