What Is It Like to Be a Bat?

"What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" is a 1974 paper by the American philosopher Thomas Nagel, published in The Philosophical Review. It is one of the most widely cited works in the philosophy of mind and is a classic statement of the problem of consciousness.

The argument

Nagel asks the reader to imagine what it is like to be a bat. Bats perceive the world primarily through echolocation: they emit high-frequency sounds and interpret the echoes to build a picture of their surroundings. A human can imagine having webbed wings, sleeping upside down, and hunting insects in the dark, but none of this imagining captures the subjective character of the bat's experience — what it is like, from the inside, to perceive the world by sonar.

Nagel's point is general. Every conscious organism has a point of view, and there are facts about what it is like to be that organism that are accessible only from that point of view. A Martian scientist who knew everything about the physiology of a bat's brain would still not know what it is like to be a bat, unless the scientist could somehow occupy the bat's point of view.

Implications

Nagel used the bat to argue that consciousness resists the standard methods of physicalist reduction. Physical theories describe objective facts, accessible from many points of view; but facts about experience are essentially tied to a particular point of view. He did not conclude that consciousness is non-physical, but that our present physical concepts are inadequate to it. The paper helped define the modern debate about qualia, the "hard problem" of consciousness, and the explanatory gap between brain processes and experience.

Reception

The paper has been enormously influential, generating replies from philosophers including Daniel Dennett, who argued that Nagel underestimated the power of imaginative and scientific reconstruction, and from others who took the argument as showing that a purely objective science of the mind is impossible. The phrase "what it is like" has become the standard way of picking out the subjective character of experience in philosophy of mind.
