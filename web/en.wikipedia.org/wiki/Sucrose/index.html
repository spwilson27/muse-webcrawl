Sucrose is a sugar composed of glucose and fructose subunits. It is a disaccharide, a molecule composed of two monosaccharides: glucose and fructose. Sucrose is produced naturally in plants, from which table sugar is refined. It has the molecular formula C12H22O11.

For human consumption, sucrose is extracted and refined from either sugarcane or sugar beet. Sugar mills—typically located in tropical regions near where sugarcane is grown—crush the cane, extract the juice and purify it into raw sugar; other factories refine raw sugar into white table sugar. Global production is around 180 million tonnes per year.

Structure and properties

In sucrose, glucose and fructose are linked by an α,β-glycosidic bond between carbon 1 of glucose and carbon 2 of fructose. Because both anomeric carbons participate in the bond, sucrose is a non-reducing sugar: it does not react with Benedict's or Fehling's reagents. On heating with dilute acid, or under the enzyme invertase (sucrase), sucrose hydrolyses to an equimolar mixture of glucose and fructose called invert sugar, which is sweeter and less prone to crystallise—an important property in confectionery.

Biosynthesis and role in plants

Plants synthesise sucrose in the cytosol of photosynthetic cells from UDP-glucose and fructose-6-phosphate, via sucrose-phosphate synthase. Sucrose is the principal form in which carbohydrates are transported through the phloem from leaves to the rest of the plant, and it serves as an energy store in sugarcane stems and sugar beet roots.

Digestion and health

Humans hydrolyse sucrose with the enzyme sucrase-isomaltase in the small intestine, absorbing the resulting glucose and fructose. Sucrose provides about 4 kcal/g. High intake is associated with dental caries, obesity and type 2 diabetes; the World Health Organization recommends limiting free sugars to less than 10% of total energy intake. Sucrose itself has a glycaemic index of about 65.

History

Sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea and its sugar refined in India by the first millennium BC; the word "sugar" derives through Arabic sukkar from Sanskrit śarkarā. Industrial beet-sugar production began in the early nineteenth century in Europe. The chemistry of sucrose was clarified in the 1920s–40s by Walter Haworth and others.
