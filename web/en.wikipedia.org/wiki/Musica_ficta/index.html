Musica ficta

Musica ficta ("false" or "feigned music") was a term used in European music theory from the late 12th century to about 1600 to describe pitches, whether notated or added by performers by way of improvisation, that lie outside the system of musica recta or musica vera — the pitches recognised by the Guidonian hand. In modern usage the term is most often applied to the unwritten accidentals that performers and editors add to medieval and Renaissance polyphony, such as raising a note by a semitone at a cadence to form a leading tone, or flattening a note to avoid the tritone ("mi contra fa").

Historical usage

In medieval theory, musica recta comprised the notes of the hexachord system attributed to Guido d'Arezzo. Notes outside that system — initially just B♭, which was part of the "soft" hexachord — were considered "feigned". As the hexachord system expanded, musica ficta came to include chromatic notes such as F♯, C♯ and E♭ that were routinely needed in polyphonic practice but rarely written down.

Modern editorial practice

Because the rules were conventions known to singers rather than fully notated instructions, modern editors must decide where to supply ficta in editions of early music, and their choices are often shown above the staff. The extent and rules of ficta application have been a major subject of musicological debate: theorists such as Karol Berger have reconstructed the historical rules, while performers disagree about cadential sharpenings, the avoidance of tritones and diminished intervals, and the treatment of cross-relations. The standard rules taught to editors are to provide a semitone below a cadential octave or unison, to avoid melodic and harmonic tritones, and to respect the principle una nota super la semper est canendum fa — a note above la is always sung as fa (i.e. flattened).
