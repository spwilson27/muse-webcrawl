Gastrolith

Gastroliths found with a sauropod fossil
TypeRock retained in the digestive tract
Found inBirds, reptiles, some fish and invertebrates; fossils of dinosaurs

A gastrolith, also called a stomach stone or gizzard stone, is a rock held inside a gastrointestinal tract. Gastroliths in some species are retained in the muscular gizzard and used to grind food in animals lacking suitable grinding teeth. In other species the rocks are ingested and pass through the digestive system and are replaced frequently. The grain size depends upon the size of the animal and the gastrolith's role in digestion.

Function

Many living animals use gastroliths: birds (especially granivorous and herbivorous species), crocodiles, alligators, seals, and toothed whales swallow stones that lodge in the gizzard or stomach. In birds, grit ground against food in the muscular gizzard substitutes for chewing. In aquatic animals, gastroliths have also been proposed to act as ballast, helping regulate buoyancy, although this function is debated. Some crustaceans, notably crayfish, form internal gastroliths of calcium carbonate in the stomach wall as a store of calcium used after moulting.

Paleontology

Among fossils, gastroliths are most commonly associated with plesiosaurs and sauropod dinosaurs. Plesiosaur skeletons are often found with stones in the stomach region, and clusters of highly polished, rounded stones found within sauropod skeletons have long been interpreted as gizzard stones used to grind tough plant material. However, some researchers have questioned whether sauropod gastroliths could have processed enough food to be the animals' primary means of mechanical digestion, suggesting instead that the stones may have been swallowed accidentally or for mineral supplementation.

Identification

Geologists identify fossil gastroliths by their high degree of surface polish and rounding, their composition (often differing from surrounding rock), and their association with skeletal remains. Isolated polished stones that cannot be linked to an animal are sometimes called exoliths. Historically, some supposed gastroliths from dinosaur-bearing strata have been reinterpreted as ordinary river-polished pebbles.
