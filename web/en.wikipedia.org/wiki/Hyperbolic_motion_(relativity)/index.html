Hyperbolic motion (relativity)

Hyperbolic motion is the motion of an object with constant proper acceleration in special relativity. It is called hyperbolic motion because the equation describing the path of the object through spacetime is a hyperbola, in the same way the trajectory of a constantly accelerating object in Newtonian mechanics is a parabola. The motion was described by Hermann Minkowski (1908) and worked out by Max Born (1909) and Arnold Sommerfeld (1910).

Description

An object undergoing constant proper acceleration α, starting from rest at position x = c²/α at time t = 0, follows the worldline

x(τ) = (c²/α) cosh(ατ/c),   ct(τ) = (c/α) sinh(ατ/c)

where τ is proper time. Its coordinate velocity approaches the speed of light asymptotically but never reaches it. Because the worldline asymptotes to a light cone, a uniformly accelerating observer possesses an event horizon (the Rindler horizon): events beyond it can never send signals that reach the observer.

Rindler coordinates

A family of observers undergoing hyperbolic motion, each maintaining constant distance from the others in their own rest frame (Born rigidity), defines the Rindler coordinate system for flat spacetime. Rindler coordinates are the flat-spacetime analogue of the near-horizon geometry of a black hole; the acceleration temperature associated with the Rindler horizon is the Unruh effect, in which an accelerating detector registers a thermal bath of particles.

Applications

Hyperbolic motion is the standard idealization for relativistic rockets: at a comfortable constant proper acceleration of 1 g, a spacecraft would approach the speed of light, and relativistic time dilation would allow crews to cross interstellar — in principle intergalactic — distances within a human lifetime of ship time. It is also central to discussions of the twin paradox and Bell's spaceship paradox, which concern the relativity of simultaneity among accelerating observers.
