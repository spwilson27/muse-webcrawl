European microstates

The European microstates or European ministates are a set of very small sovereign states in Europe which includes Andorra, Liechtenstein, Malta, Monaco, San Marino, and Vatican City. In total land area, Vatican City is the smallest country in the world, at about 0.49 square kilometres (0.19 sq mi), followed by Monaco at about 2 square kilometres.

Characteristics

The microstates are remnants of the patchwork of small political entities that characterised Europe before the consolidation of the modern nation-state. Several survive through special relationships with larger neighbours: Andorra's heads of state are the co-princes, the President of France and the Bishop of Urgell in Spain; Liechtenstein is in a customs and currency union with Switzerland and is represented diplomatically by Swiss missions in many countries; Monaco has a close relationship with France, which guarantees its independence; San Marino, which claims to be the world's oldest surviving republic, is entirely surrounded by Italy.

Despite their size, most of the microstates enjoy high standards of living, in several cases built on financial services, tourism, and advantageous tax regimes. Four of them — Andorra, Liechtenstein, Monaco, and San Marino — use the euro through monetary agreements with the European Union even though they are not EU members, and are permitted to mint limited quantities of their own euro coins. Malta, by contrast, is a full member of the European Union, having joined in 2004; it is sometimes excluded from narrower definitions of "microstate" because of its larger population.

Relations with the European Union

None of the continental microstates is an EU member. Liechtenstein participates in the European Economic Area, while Andorra, Monaco, and San Marino have customs agreements with the EU and have negotiated toward broader association agreements. Vatican City's relationship with the EU is centred on its use of the euro and the special position of the Holy See in international law.

Historical microstates

Many other small European states have disappeared over the centuries, absorbed into larger neighbours through war, dynastic inheritance, or plebiscite. Examples include the Republic of Ragusa on the Dalmatian coast, the Free City of Danzig, the Saar Protectorate, and numerous German and Italian principalities that vanished during the unifications of the nineteenth century.
