Cru is a French wine term traditionally meaning "growth," from the past participle of the verb croître. In practice it designates a vineyard or a group of vineyards, a wine-making estate, or the wine produced from it, where the name is taken as an indication of typicity and quality associated with a place. The term is closely bound to the concept of terroir — the idea that the site itself shapes the wine.

Usage is not uniform across France: in Bordeaux, cru usually refers to a château estate as ranked in classifications such as that of 1855; in Burgundy it refers to precisely delimited vineyards ranked as grand cru and premier cru; and in Champagne it describes villages graded by the historic échelle des crus.

Meaning and usage

"Un cru" is the product of a specific place; a "grand cru" is (depending on the region) a great growth, and "premier cru" a first growth. Because cru evolved region by region without a single national definition, EU and French wine law regulate each appellation's use of the term separately. The prestige attached to "grand cru" has led some producers outside the classic regions to use analogous quality tiers, which appellations police as protected terms.

Bordeaux

In Bordeaux, classified-growth terminology originates in the Bordeaux Wine Official Classification of 1855, drawn up for the Exposition Universelle in Paris at Napoleon III's request. Brokers ranked Médoc red-wine estates (plus one from Graves) into five tiers from premier grand cru classé (first growths: Lafite, Latour, Margaux, Haut-Brion, and — promoted in 1973 — Mouton Rothschild) down to fifth growths, while Sauternes and Barsac received their own ranking. The classification, formally unchanged since 1855 except for Mouton's promotion, still structures prices. Other Bordeaux classifications exist for Saint-Émilion (revised roughly every decade), Graves, and the Crus Bourgeois of the Médoc.

Burgundy

Burgundy's appellation system attaches rank to land rather than to a producer. At the apex stand 33 grand cru vineyards, each its own appellation (e.g., Chambertin, Montrachet, Romanée-Conti), followed by premier cru plots within village appellations, then village and regional wines. A single grand cru vineyard may have dozens of owners, each bottling under the same vineyard name — the opposite of the Bordeaux château model.

Champagne and Alsace

In Champagne, grand cru and premier cru are village designations: historically 17 villages were rated 100% on the échelle des crus scale and called grand cru, and 41 more rated 90–99% were premier cru; the ratings once governed grape prices. In Alsace, Alsace Grand Cru is an appellation for wines from 51 designated lieux-dits, restricted mainly to Riesling, Gewürztraminer, Pinot Gris, and Muscat.

Other uses

Writers about wine in other countries borrow "cru" loosely for esteemed single vineyards, and beer culture has adopted similar vocabulary for celebrated breweries. In French the word extends metaphorically — a person's own "cru" is their home vintage or year — but in wine law it remains a regulated, regionally defined term.
