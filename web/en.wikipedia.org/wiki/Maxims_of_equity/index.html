Maxims of equity

Maxims of equity are legal maxims that serve as a set of general principles or rules which are said to govern the way in which equity operates. They were developed by the English Court of Chancery and other courts that administer equity, and are illustrative of the broader doctrines of fairness and conscience that underpin equitable jurisdiction. Although the maxims do not have the binding force of specific rules, judges and commentators frequently invoke them as concise expressions of equitable doctrine.

Principal maxims

  • Equity will not suffer a wrong to be without a remedy. Where a legal right exists, equity will provide a remedy where the common law provides none; this idea underlies much of equity's concurrent and auxiliary jurisdiction.
  • Equity follows the law. Equity normally applies the rules of law and does not depart from them without reason (aequitas sequitur legem).
  • Where there is equal equity, the law shall prevail. When both parties have equal equitable claims, the court gives effect to the legal title.
  • Where the equities are equal, the first in time shall prevail. As between competing equitable interests, priority in time usually determines priority of claim (qui prior est tempore potior est jure).
  • He who seeks equity must do equity. A claimant who asks for equitable relief must act fairly towards the other party, for example by performing his own obligations.
  • He who comes into equity must come with clean hands. A claimant guilty of misconduct in the very transaction in suit may be denied relief (the clean hands doctrine).
  • Delay defeats equities. Equity aids the vigilant, not those who sleep on their rights (vigilantibus non dormientibus aequitas subvenit); unreasonable delay may bar a claim through laches.
  • Equality is equity. Equity favours the equal division of property and burdens among those equally entitled.
  • Equity looks to the intent rather than the form. Courts of equity attend to the substance of transactions rather than their outward form.
  • Equity looks on that as done which ought to be done. Where there is an obligation to do an act, equity may treat the parties as though it had already been done — the basis of doctrines such as equitable conversion.
  • Equity imputes an intention to fulfil an obligation. A person is presumed to intend to do what he is bound to do, supporting doctrines such as performance and satisfaction.
  • Equity acts in personam. Historically, decrees of Chancery bound the person (and conscience) of the defendant rather than operating directly on property.
  • Equity will not complete an imperfect gift. Equity will generally not assist a volunteer to perfect an incomplete transfer.
  • Equity will not allow a statute to be used as an instrument of fraud. Statutory formalities will not be permitted to defeat equitable obligations where doing so would facilitate fraud.
  • Equity will not permit a trust to fail for want of a trustee. The court will appoint or act as trustee rather than allow a valid trust to collapse.
  • Equity abhors a vacuum. Equity disfavours gaps in the vesting of ownership.

Role in modern law

The maxims continue to be cited in common law jurisdictions, including England and Wales, the United States, Canada and Australia, particularly in cases involving trusts, fiduciary duties, injunctions, specific performance and proprietary estoppel. Critics note that the maxims can conflict with one another and never decide cases by themselves; they operate as summaries of developed doctrine rather than substitutes for it.
