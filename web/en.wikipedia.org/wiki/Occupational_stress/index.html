Occupational stress

Occupational stress is stress related to one's job. It is often defined as arising when the demands of work exceed the worker's capacity or resources to meet them, or when there is a poor fit between the person and the work environment. Occupational stress is a major focus of occupational health psychology and organisational research because of its effects on employee health, job performance, absenteeism, and turnover.

Sources of stress

Common stressors include excessive workload and time pressure, lack of control over how work is done, role ambiguity and role conflict, poor relationships with colleagues and supervisors, job insecurity, shift work and long hours, organisational change, and exposure to traumatic events in occupations such as emergency services and healthcare. Workplace bullying and harassment are also significant contributors.

Theoretical models

Several influential models frame occupational stress research. The demand–control model (Karasek) proposes that strain is greatest when high psychological demands combine with low decision latitude. The effort–reward imbalance model (Siegrist) emphasises the harmful effects of high effort unrewarded by pay, esteem, or career prospects. The job demands–resources model broadens the picture, linking demands to burnout and resources to engagement. Lazarus and Folkman's transactional model treats stress as a function of the individual's appraisal of demands and their coping resources.

Health effects and management

Chronic occupational stress is associated with cardiovascular disease, musculoskeletal disorders, anxiety, depression, and burnout. Organisations address it through primary interventions (redesigning work to reduce stressors), secondary interventions (stress-management training and resilience programmes), and tertiary interventions (counselling and return-to-work support). Regulatory bodies such as the UK Health and Safety Executive publish management standards for work-related stress.
