Pheromone

A pheromone is a secreted or excreted chemical factor that triggers a social response in members of the same species. Pheromones are chemicals capable of acting like hormones beyond the body of the secreting individual, to affect the behaviour of the receiving individuals. The term, coined by Peter Karlson and Martin Lüscher in 1959, derives from the Greek pherein (to transport) and hormon (to excite).

Types

Alarm pheromones trigger flight or aggression in response to danger, as in many social insects and aphids. Trail pheromones guide ants and termites to food sources. Sex pheromones signal readiness to mate and can act over long distances in moths — bombykol, from the silkworm moth, was the first pheromone chemically identified (1959). Releaser pheromones cause immediate behavioural changes, while primer pheromones produce slower, long-term physiological effects, such as queen pheromones that suppress reproduction in worker honeybees. Other classes include aggregation, territorial, and epideictic pheromones.

Detection

In vertebrates, pheromones are often detected by the vomeronasal (Jacobson's) organ as well as the main olfactory system; in insects, specialized sensilla on the antennae are tuned to specific pheromone components with extraordinary sensitivity — a male moth can respond to very small numbers of molecules.

Human pheromones

Claims of human sex pheromones remain controversial. Compounds such as androstadienone and estratetraenol have been studied, but robust, replicable evidence of true human pheromones is lacking, and the adult human vomeronasal organ appears vestigial. Commercial "pheromone perfumes" are not supported by convincing evidence.

Applications

Pheromones are used in agriculture for pest monitoring and mating disruption — flooding orchards with synthetic sex pheromone confuses male moths and reduces mating, decreasing insecticide use. They are also used in traps for invasive species and stored-product pests.
