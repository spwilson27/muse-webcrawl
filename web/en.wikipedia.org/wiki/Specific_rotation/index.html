Specific rotation

The specific rotation of a chemical compound, denoted [α], is a standardized measure of the compound's optical activity — the angle through which it rotates the plane of plane-polarized light. It allows the optical rotation of different substances, measured under different conditions, to be compared directly.

Definition

For a solution, the specific rotation is defined by:

[α]Tλ = α / (l · c)

where α is the observed rotation in degrees, l is the path length of the sample cell in decimetres, and c is the concentration in grams per millilitre. The temperature T (commonly 20 °C) and wavelength λ are specified as superscript and subscript; λ is frequently the sodium D line at 589 nm, written [α]20D.

Conventions

A positive value indicates a dextrorotatory compound, which rotates light clockwise as viewed by the observer, and a negative value a levorotatory compound. Enantiomers have specific rotations of equal magnitude and opposite sign, while a racemic mixture has a specific rotation of zero. For neat liquids, concentration in the formula is replaced by density.

Applications

Specific rotation is a standard identifying property reported for chiral substances such as sugars, amino acids, and pharmaceuticals; it is used to assess enantiomeric purity and, in the sugar industry, to determine concentration in saccharimetry. The phenomenon was investigated by Biot in the early nineteenth century, and the underlying theory of circular birefringence was developed by Fresnel.
