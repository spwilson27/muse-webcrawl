Sarmatians

The Sarmatians were a group of Iranian nomadic peoples who dominated the Pontic steppe north of the Black Sea between roughly the 3rd century BC and the 4th century AD. Closely related to the Scythians, whom they succeeded, they spoke an Iranian language and are named in Greek and Roman sources as formidable cavalry warriors. Sarmatian groups included the Roxolani, Iazyges, Alans and Siraces.

History

The Sarmatians emerged in the steppes between the Don and the Urals and moved westward, displacing the Scythians in the centuries before the Common Era. Greek writers, struck by accounts of Sarmatian women fighting alongside men, connected them with the Amazons of myth. Sarmatian society was organised in tribal confederations practising pastoral nomadism, with wealth displayed in richly furnished burials (kurgans) containing weapons, horse gear and gold jewellery, often in the "animal style."

Warfare

Sarmatian armies were built around heavy cavalry — cataphracts armoured in scale armour, wielding the long two-handed lance called the kontos — supported by horse archers. Their tactics influenced the Roman army, which recruited Sarmatian cavalry: in AD 175 Marcus Aurelius settled 5,500 Sarmatian horsemen in Britain, an episode sometimes linked to later Arthurian legends. Sarmatians fought Rome in repeated Danubian wars yet also traded and served as allies.

Decline

Gothic expansion in the 3rd century and the Hunnic invasions of the 370s destroyed Sarmatian power. Surviving groups were absorbed into the Goths, Huns, early Slavs and others; the Alans survived longest, and their descendants include the modern Ossetians of the Caucasus.
