Yule goat

The Yule goat (Swedish: julbock, Norwegian: julebukk, Finnish: joulupukki in its older meaning, Danish: julebuk) is a Scandinavian and Northern European symbol and tradition of the Christmas season, centred on a goat made of straw or depicted in ornaments. It is one of the oldest surviving Yule symbols in the region.

Origins

The Yule goat's roots are commonly traced to pre-Christian Germanic and Norse traditions: the god Thor travelled in a chariot pulled by two goats, Tanngrisnir and Tanngnjóstr, and harvest customs across Northern Europe preserved the last sheaf of grain — believed to hold the spirit of the harvest — sometimes fashioned into a goat shape. Goethean and later folklore linked goats with the midwinter season and with gift-bringing and mischief.

Customs

In earlier centuries, men in some regions went "julebukking", visiting houses in goat costume to sing songs, play pranks, and receive food and drink. In Sweden and Finland, the Yule goat was originally the bringer of Christmas gifts — a role later transferred to Santa Claus figures (the Finnish word joulupukki, literally "Yule goat", is now the name of Santa Claus himself). Today the goat survives most visibly as a straw ornament, often bound with red ribbons, placed under or beside the Christmas tree.

The Gävle goat

The most famous Yule goat is the Gävle goat, a giant straw goat erected annually since 1966 in the Swedish city of Gävle. It has become internationally known in part because it has repeatedly been destroyed by arson and vandalism despite extensive security measures.
