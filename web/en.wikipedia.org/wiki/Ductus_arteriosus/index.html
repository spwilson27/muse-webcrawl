Ductus arteriosus

The ductus arteriosus is a blood vessel in the developing fetus that connects the pulmonary artery to the aorta, allowing most of the blood from the right ventricle to bypass the fetus's fluid-filled non-functioning lungs. Upon closure at birth, it becomes the ligamentum arteriosum.

Development and structure

The ductus arteriosus develops from the left sixth aortic arch. Its wall contains intimal cushions and a thick layer of smooth muscle whose contraction at birth closes the vessel. If the vessel fails to close after birth, it is known as a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), a congenital heart defect particularly common in premature infants.

Closure at birth

Immediately after birth, two main changes promote closure: the abrupt rise in oxygen tension in the blood (paO2) and the fall in circulating prostaglandins, whose source — the placenta — is removed at delivery. The smooth muscle constricts (bradykinin released from the newly inflated lungs also helps), producing functional closure typically within the first 24–48 hours of life in term infants, followed by permanent anatomical closure over the following weeks as the intimal cushions seal the lumen.

Clinical significance

Patent ductus arteriosus can cause left-to-right shunting, heart failure, and endarteritis; it is treated with prostaglandin synthesis inhibitors (indomethacin or ibuprofen), catheter-delivered closure devices, or surgical ligation. Conversely, in congenital heart lesions that depend on ductal flow (such as transposition of the great arteries or hypoplastic left heart syndrome), prostaglandin E1 (alprostadil) is infused deliberately to keep the ductus open until surgery.
