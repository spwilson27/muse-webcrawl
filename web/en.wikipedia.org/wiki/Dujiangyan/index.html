Dujiangyan

Dujiangyan (Chinese: 都江堰) is an ancient irrigation system in Dujiangyan City, Sichuan province, China. Originally constructed around 256 BC by the State of Qin as an irrigation and flood control project, it is still in use today. It is a component of the Qin dynasty water management works and has been designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

History

During the Warring States Period, the people of the Minjiang River valley suffered from annual flooding. Li Bing, governor of Shu for the Qin state, investigated the problem together with his son. Rather than damming the river — which would have blocked the passage of military and supply boats — Li Bing designed a system that channelled and divided the water. The project was completed in stages over about eight years, beginning around 277 BC and finishing near 256 BC.

Construction

The work used long, sausage-shaped baskets of woven bamboo filled with stones, called zhulong, laid along the river to form dividing dikes. The key components are the Fish Mouth Levee (Yuzui), which splits the river into inner and outer channels; the Flying Sand Weir (Feishayan), which discharges excess water and sediment; and the Bottle-Neck Channel (Baopingkou), a cut through Mount Yulei that regulates the flow into the Chengdu plain.

Legacy

Dujiangyan turned the Chengdu plain into a fertile agricultural region, helping earn Sichuan the nickname "land of abundance". The system is still operated today, irrigating several thousand square kilometres of farmland. A temple, Erwang Temple, commemorates Li Bing and his son.
