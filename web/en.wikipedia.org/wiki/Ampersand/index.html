The ampersand, also known as the and sign, is the logogram &, representing the conjunction "and". It originated as a ligature of the letters e and t — the Latin word et, meaning "and" — written together in cursive Roman handwriting. Examples appear as early as the 1st century AD, and the character took its modern forms in early medieval and Renaissance scripts.

Name

The character was once treated as the 27th letter of the English alphabet. Schoolchildren reciting the alphabet pronounced letters that could also stand as words (such as A, I, and &) with the phrase per se — "A per se A" — and the final "& per se and" was slurred into the single word "ampersand," a name attested from the early 19th century.

Usage

The ampersand is common in company names (e.g., "Johnson & Johnson"), abbreviations such as R&B and Q&A, and bibliographic citations of works with multiple authors. Formal prose generally prefers the spelled-out word "and." Stylized italic ampersands, in which the et ligature remains visible, are prized in typography. In computing, & appears in programming operators, HTML entities (&amp;), and shell syntax.
