The AIM-120 Advanced Medium-Range Air-to-Air Missile (AMRAAM; pronounced "am-ram") is an American beyond-visual-range air-to-air missile capable of all-weather day-and-night operations. The AIM-120 is in service with the United States Air Force (USAF) and the United States Navy (USN), and is the primary medium-range air-to-air missile for the air forces of many allied nations. It has been used in multiple combat engagements.

AMRAAM was developed starting in the late 1970s as a follow-on to the AIM-7 Sparrow, which required the launching aircraft to continuously illuminate the target with its radar. The AIM-120 instead uses active radar homing: after launch, it flies toward the target using inertial navigation with updates from the launching aircraft via datalink, then activates its own radar seeker for the terminal phase. This "fire-and-forget" capability allows the pilot to launch multiple missiles at multiple targets simultaneously and maneuver away after launch.

Development

The United States Air Force and Navy agreed in 1979 to jointly develop AMRAAM under a memorandum of agreement, with the Air Force as lead service. Hughes Aircraft won the development contract in 1981, and Raytheon was selected as a second source. Development proved protracted, and the missile did not enter service until September 1991 — just after the Gulf War — first with USAF F-16 units and later with the Navy's F/A-18 Hornet. It gradually replaced the Sparrow on most U.S. fighters, including the F-15, F-16, F/A-18, F-22, F-35 and the F-14 (late in that type's career).

Variants

Successive variants have improved range, guidance and resistance to electronic countermeasures. The AIM-120A was the initial production version. The AIM-120B added reprogrammable memory and improved electronics. The AIM-120C introduced clipped wings and fins to fit inside the internal weapons bays of the F-22 Raptor, along with an improved warhead and guidance; sub-variants such as the C-5, C-6 and C-7 progressively increased range and added GPS-aided navigation and better counter-countermeasures. The AIM-120D (originally designated C-8) is the most advanced version, with a two-way datalink, enhanced GPS/inertial navigation, an expanded no-escape envelope and substantially increased range — commonly estimated at over 160 km, though exact figures are classified. A surface-launched version forms the interceptor of the NASAMS ground-based air defense system, operated by numerous countries.

Combat use

The AMRAAM scored its first combat kill on 27 December 1992, when a USAF F-16 shot down an Iraqi MiG-25 that had violated the southern no-fly zone. It was used extensively during the 1999 NATO bombing of Yugoslavia, where U.S. and Dutch fighters used it to down several Yugoslav MiG-29s. The missile has since been employed in conflicts including operations over Iraq and Syria, amassing a record of air-to-air victories, and it was the weapon used to destroy the Chinese balloon that crossed North America in February 2023 (fired by an F-22). More than 20,000 AMRAAMs have been produced, and the missile has been exported to over 40 countries.
