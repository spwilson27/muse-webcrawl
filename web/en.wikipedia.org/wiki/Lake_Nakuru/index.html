Lake Nakuru

Lake Nakuru is one of the Rift Valley lakes of Kenya, lying just south of the city of Nakuru at an elevation of about 1,754 m above sea level. It is a shallow, alkaline soda lake famed for the vast flocks of flamingos that feed on its algae — at times numbering in the millions and turning its shores pink. The lake is protected by Lake Nakuru National Park, established in 1961, and was designated a Ramsar wetland site in 1990 and part of the Kenya Lake System UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2011.

Ecology

The lake's alkalinity supports dense growth of cyanobacteria such as Spirulina, the principal food of the lesser flamingo. Flamingo numbers fluctuate dramatically with water level and algal abundance: drought concentrates the soda and can crash the food supply, while flooding dilutes the lake and shifts flocks to other Rift Valley lakes such as Bogoria and Elementaita. The lake also supports greater flamingos, great white pelicans, and many other waterbirds. In the 1970s the fish-eating birds were boosted by the introduction of the alkaline-tolerant tilapia Oreochromis alcalicus grahami from Lake Magadi.

National park

Lake Nakuru National Park covers about 188 km² around the lake. Originally a bird sanctuary, it was later fenced and became a sanctuary for black and white rhinoceroses, with Rothschild's giraffes introduced from western Kenya. Lions, leopards, buffalo, waterbuck, and baboons are also resident; the Baboon Cliff is a well-known viewpoint. The park is one of Kenya's most visited.

Threats

The lake lies immediately beside the growing city of Nakuru, and has suffered pollution from urban and industrial effluent, deforestation and siltation in its catchment (notably via the Njoro River), and wide water-level swings. In recent years unusually high rainfall and rising water levels across the Rift Valley lakes flooded shorelines and displaced residents around Nakuru.
