Landes forest

The Landes forest (French: Forêt des Landes) in the Landes de Gascogne region of south-western France is the largest man-made woodland in Western Europe. Covering roughly 10,000 km² across the departments of Gironde, Landes and Lot-et-Garonne, it is composed overwhelmingly of maritime pine (Pinus pinaster) plantations.

History

Before the nineteenth century the area was a sparsely inhabited marshland used for sheep grazing, watched over by shepherds on stilts. From 1857, under a law promoted during the Second Empire, communes were required to drain and plant their moorlands with pine, a programme associated with engineers such as Jules Chambrelent. The forest transformed the regional economy around timber, resin tapping and later paper production.

Ecology and fires

The forest grows on poor, sandy podzol soils over an impermeable layer. It is managed in even-aged stands and harvested on rotations of a few decades. Its uniform pine cover is highly flammable; major fires, notably in 1949, 2022, have burned large areas, prompting extensive firebreak networks and organised prevention.
