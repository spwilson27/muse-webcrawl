Science in Australia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Science in Australia encompasses a research system that, relative to the country's population of about 26 million, has contributed disproportionately to global knowledge. Australian scientists and institutions have produced numerous notable inventions and discoveries, and Australians have won Nobel Prizes in physiology or medicine, chemistry, and physics, including Howard Florey (for the development of penicillin), William Lawrence Bragg (the youngest laureate in history, for X-ray crystallography), Peter Doherty, Barry Marshall and Robin Warren (for showing that stomach ulcers are caused by the bacterium Helicobacter pylori), Elizabeth Blackburn, and Brian Schmidt (for the accelerating expansion of the universe).

History

Indigenous Australians developed sophisticated bodies of astronomical, ecological, and medicinal knowledge over tens of thousands of years. European scientific work began with the voyages of exploration — Joseph Banks catalogued Australian flora on Cook's 1770 voyage. The nineteenth century saw the founding of universities (Sydney in 1850, Melbourne in 1853), museums, and royal societies in the colonies. The Australian Association for the Advancement of Science was founded in 1888.

In 1916 the federal government created an advisory council for science and industry, the forerunner of the Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation (CSIRO), founded in 1926 and expanded greatly after the Second World War under Ian Clunies Ross. The Australian Academy of Science was founded in 1954, with the Shine Dome in Canberra as its landmark headquarters.

Institutions

Major research institutions include CSIRO, the nation's largest government research agency, active in agriculture, mining, astronomy, oceans, and manufacturing; the Australian Research Council, which funds university research; the National Health and Medical Research Council; the Australian Nuclear Science and Technology Organisation (ANSTO), which operates the OPAL research reactor; the Australian Institute of Marine Science; and Geoscience Australia. Australia hosts part of the Square Kilometre Array radio telescope in Western Australia, and Antarctic research is coordinated from Hobart.

Notable contributions

Australian inventions and discoveries include the black box flight recorder (David Warren), the Cochlear bionic ear implant (Graeme Clark), Wi-Fi technology patented by a CSIRO team (John O'Sullivan and colleagues), spray-on skin for burns victims (Fiona Wood), the cervical cancer vaccine Gardasil (Ian Frazer), polymer banknotes, and the medical application of penicillin pioneered by Howard Florey. Australian researchers have also been leaders in ecology, radio astronomy, and immunology.
