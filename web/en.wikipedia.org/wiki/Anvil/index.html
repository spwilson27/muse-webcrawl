Anvil

An anvil is a metalworking tool consisting of a large block of metal — traditionally wrought iron or steel — with a hardened, flat top surface (the face) on which metal objects are struck, shaped, and cut. Most Western anvils have a projecting conical horn for bending and curving work, a flat heel at the opposite end, square and round holes (hardy and pritchel holes) for holding accessory tools, and a narrow waist above a broad base.

Structure

The classic London-pattern anvil comprises the face (the main striking surface, of hardened steel), the horn (a tapered cone used for drawing curves and rings), the step (a softer, lower cutting surface between face and horn), the heel, and the waist and foot that give the tool its mass distribution. The square hardy hole near the heel takes shaped bottom tools such as hardies for cutting and fullers for grooving; the small round pritchel hole holds punches and allows holes to be punched through hot work. An anvil's efficiency comes from its mass and hardness: a good anvil rebounds a hammer noticeably, returning energy to the work.

History

The earliest anvils were stones; metal anvils emerged with Bronze Age metalworking, and iron anvils became standard in the Iron Age. Roman and medieval smiths used simple block and stake anvils; the familiar horned form crystallised in the late medieval and early modern periods and was standardised in the 18th and 19th centuries, when firms in England and America produced wrought-iron, steel-faced anvils in standard sizes, priced and sold by weight (often marked in hundredweights). In the 20th century solid cast-steel anvils became common as wrought production declined.

Use

A smith heats metal in a forge until plastic, then shapes it on the anvil with hammer blows: drawing out on the face, bending over the horn, upsetting, punching through over the pritchel hole, and cutting with a hardy seated in the hardy hole. Farriers use lighter, often more elaborately horned anvils to shape horseshoes. Beyond the forge, anvils appear as improvised striking tools and, in folklore and cartoons, as a byword for heaviness; they also feature in heraldry and in the iconography of smith gods such as Hephaestus and Vulcan.
