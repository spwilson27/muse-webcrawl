Ghee

Ghee (from Sanskrit: घृत, ghṛta, "sprinkled") is a class of clarified butter that originated in ancient India. It is commonly used in Indian cuisine, traditional medicine (Ayurveda) and religious rituals. Ghee is typically prepared by simmering butter, which is churned from cream, skimming off impurities from the surface, and then pouring and retaining the clear liquid fat while discarding the solid residue that has settled to the bottom.

Preparation

Traditionally, ghee is made from cow's or water buffalo's milk curd that is churned into butter (makhan) and then slow-cooked until the water evaporates and the milk solids brown, imparting a nutty aroma. Commercial ghee is often made directly from cream or butter. Because most moisture and milk solids are removed, ghee keeps for months without refrigeration in warm climates, which made it indispensable before modern storage.

Culinary use

Ghee is used for frying, sautéing, tempering spices (tadka), drizzling over rice, dal and breads such as roti and paratha, and in sweets including halwa and Mysore pak. It has a high smoke point (about 250 °C), making it well suited to deep-frying. Related products include the spiced niter kibbeh of Ethiopia and the browned smen of North Africa.

Cultural and religious significance

In Hindu ritual, ghee is a sacred offering poured into the sacrificial fire (homa), used to light lamps and anoint images of deities, and is one of the five substances of the panchamrita. In Ayurveda it is regarded as a vehicle (yogavahi) that carries the properties of medicinal herbs, and is used both internally and externally.

Nutrition

Ghee is almost pure fat — roughly 60% saturated — with negligible lactose and casein, so it is tolerated by many people sensitive to those milk components. It contains fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K, and small amounts of butyrate and conjugated linoleic acid.
