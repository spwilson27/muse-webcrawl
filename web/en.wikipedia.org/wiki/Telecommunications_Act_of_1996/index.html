Telecommunications Act of 1996
EnactedSigned 8 February 1996 by President Bill Clinton
Public Law104–104
PurposeFirst major overhaul of US telecom law since 1934; promotes competition and deregulation

The Telecommunications Act of 1996 is a United States federal law, the first significant overhaul of American telecommunications regulation in more than sixty years, amending the Communications Act of 1934. Its stated goal was to let "anyone enter any communications business" and let any communications business compete in any market, replacing regulated monopolies with competition.

Provisions

The Act required incumbent local telephone companies (ILECs) to interconnect with competitors and unbundle network elements so that competitive local exchange carriers could enter local markets, while allowing the regional Bell companies into long-distance service once competition was established. It relaxed national and local caps on radio and television station ownership, extended broadcast licences, mandated the V-chip for televisions, and created the E-rate programme subsidising internet access for schools and libraries. Title V, the Communications Decency Act, criminalised indecent online content; its core speech provisions were struck down in Reno v. ACLU (1997), but Section 230 survived, shielding online platforms from liability for user content while permitting good-faith moderation.

Effects

The Act is widely credited — and criticised — for accelerating media consolidation: radio ownership concentrated dramatically in firms such as Clear Channel, and local telephone competition largely collapsed into a few large providers. A wave of telecom investment fed the late-1990s bubble and subsequent bankruptcies, while cable and telephone companies emerged as rival broadband platforms.
