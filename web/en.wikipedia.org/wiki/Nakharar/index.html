Nakharar

Nakharar (Armenian: նախարար, from Parthian naxvadār, "holder of primacy") was a hereditary title of the highest order granted to houses of the ancient and medieval Armenian nobility. The nakharar system structured the political life of the Kingdom of Armenia and persisted, in evolving forms, for over a millennium.

The system

Armenia was divided among dozens of nakharar houses, each ruling its own hereditary domain (a gavar or larger district), maintaining its own army, taxes and courts, and holding a defined rank at court recorded in precedence lists such as the Gahnamak. Great houses included the Mamikonian (hereditary commanders-in-chief, sparapets), Bagratuni (hereditary coronants of the king), Kamsarakan, Artsruni, Rshtuni and Siuni. The king of Armenia was in effect first among these magnates, and royal power depended on balancing them.

History

The origins of the nakharar order lie in the tribal organisation of pre-Christian Armenia, reinforced under Parthian and Arsacid rule. After the partition of Armenia between Rome and Persia (387) and the abolition of the monarchy in Persian Armenia (428), the nakharars became the main bearers of Armenian political identity, leading revolts such as Vardan Mamikonian's rebellion of 450–451 against Sasanian religious pressure. The system survived under Arab rule through nakharar princes, and was transformed — though its great families endured — under the Bagratid kingdom (9th–11th centuries) and later centuries of foreign domination.
