Modularity

Modularity is the degree to which a system's components may be separated and recombined, often with the benefit of flexibility and variety in use. The concept appears across many disciplines, including engineering, computer science, biology, mathematics, and design, where systems are understood as being built from interdependent parts that interact through well-defined interfaces.

A system decomposed into interchangeable modules

Overview

A modular system is one that can be divided into smaller parts called modules, which can be independently created, modified, replaced, or exchanged between different systems. Modularity generally offers benefits such as reduced complexity, easier maintenance, scalability, and the ability to reuse components across contexts. The opposite of modularity is an integrated or monolithic design, in which components are tightly coupled and difficult to separate.

In engineering and design

In engineering, modular design is an approach that subdivides a system into smaller parts that can be independently created and then used in different systems. Examples include modular buildings, constructed from prefabricated sections, and modular furniture. In product design, modularity allows manufacturers to vary products by combining standard components in different configurations.

In computer science

In software engineering, modular programming is a design technique that emphasizes separating the functionality of a program into independent, interchangeable modules, each containing the parts needed to execute one aspect of the desired functionality. A module interface expresses the elements that are provided and required by the module; other modules can access its elements by strictly adhering to that interface. Modularity in software supports encapsulation, information hiding, and separation of concerns.

In biology

In biology, modularity refers to the organization of organisms or networks into discrete, semi-autonomous units. Biological modules may be structural, such as body segments, or functional, such as groups of genes that interact more strongly with each other than with other groups. Modularity is thought to facilitate evolvability by allowing one module to change without disrupting others. In network biology, modularity is also a quantitative measure of the degree to which a network can be divided into communities.

In mathematics

In mathematics, the term arises in several contexts. The modularity theorem, formerly the Taniyama–Shimura conjecture, states that every elliptic curve over the rationals is modular, meaning it can be associated with a modular form; this result was central to the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. In graph theory and network science, modularity is a measure used to evaluate the strength of a division of a network into modules or communities.
