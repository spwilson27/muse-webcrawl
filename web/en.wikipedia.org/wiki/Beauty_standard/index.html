Beauty standard

Beauty standards are the socially constructed norms within a culture that define the traits considered physically attractive or desirable in a person, particularly regarding appearance, body shape, skin, hair, and facial features. Standards vary widely across cultures and historical periods, and they are transmitted through family, peers, media, fashion, cosmetics, and — increasingly — social media imagery and filters.

Historical variation

Ideals of beauty have shifted dramatically over time. In many pre-industrial societies, fuller figures signified wealth and fertility; in parts of West Africa, fattening practices were historically associated with beauty and marriageability. In imperial China, foot binding was practiced for centuries as a marker of status and attractiveness. During the Victorian era, a pale complexion and corseted waist were prized in Britain and America. The twentieth century saw rapid shifts in Western ideals, from the 1920s flapper silhouette to the curvaceous figures of 1950s film stars, the very thin "heroin chic" look of the 1990s, and the athletic or curvaceous ideals amplified by social media in the 2010s.

Cross-cultural standards

Standards differ by region: skin-lightening products remain widely sold in parts of Asia and Africa, reflecting a preference for lighter skin in some societies, while tanning is popular in many Western countries. Preferences for eye shape, hair texture, height, and body composition likewise vary. Globalization and international media have spread some standards — notably Eurocentric features — prompting criticism that they marginalize local ideals.

Effects and criticism

Research links narrow beauty standards to body dissatisfaction, eating disorders, depression, and low self-esteem, particularly among adolescents. Critics argue that commercial industries profit from insecurity, and that digitally altered images create unattainable expectations. Movements promoting body positivity and body neutrality, along with regulatory measures such as requiring disclosure of retouched advertising photographs (adopted in countries including France and Israel), have emerged in response.
