Primary myelofibrosis (PMF) is a rare chronic blood cancer in which scar tissue (fibrosis) builds up in the bone marrow, impairing its ability to produce normal blood cells. It belongs to the group of BCR-ABL1-negative myeloproliferative neoplasms, alongside polycythaemia vera and essential thrombocythaemia, and arises from a malignant clone of haematopoietic stem cells.

Signs and symptoms

Many patients are asymptomatic at diagnosis, which may follow a routine blood count. As the disease progresses, ineffective blood production leads to anaemia, fatigue, night sweats, fever, weight loss and bone pain. Blood formation shifts to the spleen and liver (extramedullary haematopoiesis), causing marked splenomegaly with abdominal discomfort and early satiety. Complications include infections, bleeding, gout, portal hypertension and transformation to acute myeloid leukaemia, which occurs in roughly 10–20 per cent of cases.

Cause and diagnosis

Most cases carry one of three "driver" mutations: JAK2 V617F (in about half of patients), CALR or MPL. Diagnosis, per WHO criteria, combines bone marrow biopsy showing megakaryocyte proliferation and reticulin or collagen fibrosis, mutation testing, blood counts and exclusion of other myeloid neoplasms. Prognosis is estimated with scoring systems such as the IPSS and DIPSS.

Treatment

Treatment is guided by risk and symptoms. The JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib reduces spleen size and symptoms; fedratinib and other JAK inhibitors are alternatives. Allogeneic stem cell transplantation is the only potentially curative option but carries substantial risk and is reserved for suitable higher-risk patients. Supportive care includes transfusion, androgens and splenectomy in selected cases.
