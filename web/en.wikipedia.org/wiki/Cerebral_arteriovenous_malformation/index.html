Cerebral arteriovenous malformation

A cerebral arteriovenous malformation (cerebral AVM, CAVM) is an abnormal connection between the arteries and veins in the brain, forming a tangled web of blood vessels called a nidus in which arterial blood flows directly into veins without passing through a capillary bed. AVMs are usually congenital, though the cause is not fully understood, and are estimated to occur in roughly 1 in 100,000 people per year as diagnosed cases.

Signs and symptoms

Many AVMs are asymptomatic and found incidentally on imaging. The most serious presentation is rupture causing intracerebral or subarachnoid haemorrhage, which may be fatal or disabling. Unruptured AVMs can cause seizures, headaches, a pulsatile bruit, or focal neurological deficits due to mass effect or "steal" of blood from surrounding brain. The estimated annual rupture risk for an unruptured AVM is commonly quoted at 2–4%, higher after a previous rupture and with deep location, deep venous drainage or associated aneurysms.

Grading and treatment

The Spetzler–Martin grading system (grades I–V, with grade VI inoperable) scores AVMs by size, eloquence of adjacent brain, and venous drainage to predict surgical risk. Management is individualised: small accessible AVMs may be surgically excised; deep or eloquent lesions may be treated with endovascular embolisation, stereotactic radiosurgery (e.g. Gamma Knife), or combinations; some are simply monitored. The ARUBA trial (2014) found that for unruptured AVMs, medical management alone was superior to intervention over the initial follow-up period, a result that remains debated.
