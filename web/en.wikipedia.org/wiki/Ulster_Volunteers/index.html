The Ulster Volunteers, formally the Ulster Volunteer Force (UVF), was a unionist militia founded in 1912–13 to block the implementation of Irish Home Rule for the province of Ulster. Supported by the Ulster Unionist Council and led politically by Edward Carson and James Craig, it enrolled up to 100,000 men who had signed the Ulster Covenant of 28 September 1912, in which nearly half a million men and women pledged to resist Home Rule.

Background

The Third Home Rule Bill, introduced in 1912, threatened to place Ireland under a Dublin parliament. Ulster Protestants, a majority in the northeast, mobilised against it: the Ulster Covenant was signed by 471,414 people, and in January 1913 the Ulster Volunteer Force was formally constituted under the command of Lieutenant-General Sir George Richardson, a retired Indian Army officer. Unionist leaders planned, if necessary, a provisional government for Ulster.

Larne gun-running

On the night of 24–25 April 1914, the UVF landed some 25,000 German, Austrian, and Italian rifles and three million rounds of ammunition at Larne, Bangor, and Donaghadee in the "Larne gun-running", a major unopposed operation that transformed the militia into a genuinely armed force. The Curragh incident of March 1914, in which British Army officers indicated they would resign rather than coerce Ulster, had already shown the government's difficulty in acting against the Volunteers.

First World War and legacy

With the outbreak of the First World War in August 1914, Home Rule was suspended and most Ulster Volunteers enlisted; some 30,000 of them formed the core of the 36th (Ulster) Division, which suffered heavy losses at the Battle of the Somme in July 1916. The UVF was revived briefly in 1920 during the Irish War of Independence and many members were absorbed into the Ulster Special Constabulary. The name was later adopted by the loyalist paramilitary Ulster Volunteer Force founded in 1966, a separate organisation.
