Balneotherapy

Balneotherapy
FieldPhysical medicine and rehabilitation
UsesMusculoskeletal, dermatological and chronic pain conditions
MediaMineral water, thermal water, mud, gases

Balneotherapy (from Latin balneum, "bath") is the therapeutic use of bathing in natural mineral or thermal waters for the treatment of disease. It is a traditional component of spa medicine practised in many countries, particularly in central and eastern Europe, Japan and the Middle East, and is often combined with other modalities such as mud therapy (peloid therapy), climatotherapy and physical exercise.

History

The medicinal use of mineral springs dates to antiquity. Roman baths were built at famous springs such as Bath in England and Aquincum in Hungary, and resorts including Baden-Baden, Karlovy Vary, Vichy and Spa (from which the word "spa" derives) became fashionable centres of treatment in the 18th and 19th centuries. In the 19th century balneology developed into a recognised medical discipline, with physicians prescribing specific waters for specific diseases.

Mechanisms

Proposed mechanisms of action include the thermal effects of warm water (muscle relaxation, increased circulation), the mechanical effects of buoyancy and hydrostatic pressure, and the chemical effects of dissolved minerals and gases such as sulfur, radon, carbon dioxide and salts absorbed through the skin or inhaled. Immersion also unloads joints, which can facilitate movement and exercise in patients with arthritis.

Indications and evidence

Balneotherapy is most commonly used for chronic musculoskeletal conditions including osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, fibromyalgia and chronic low back pain, and for skin conditions such as psoriasis and atopic dermatitis (notably at the Dead Sea). Clinical trials and systematic reviews suggest modest short-term benefits for pain and function in some conditions, though study quality varies and the independent contribution of the water itself, as opposed to rest, warmth and the spa environment, is debated.

Types of water

  • Sulfur waters – used for skin and joint disorders
  • Radon waters – low-dose radon springs used in parts of Europe for inflammatory joint disease
  • Carbon dioxide waters – claimed to improve peripheral circulation
  • Saline and brine waters – used for respiratory and skin conditions
  • Thermal waters – defined in many countries as spring water above a set temperature
