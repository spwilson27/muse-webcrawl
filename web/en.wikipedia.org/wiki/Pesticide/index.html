Pesticides are substances that are meant to control pests. In general, a pesticide is a chemical (such as carbamate) or biological agent (such as a virus, bacterium or fungus) that deters, incapacitates, kills or otherwise discourages pests. Target pests can include insects, plant pathogens, weeds, molluscs, birds, mammals, fish, nematodes and microbes that destroy property, cause nuisance, spread disease or act as disease vectors.

Although pesticides have benefits, some also have drawbacks, such as potential toxicity to humans and other species.

Definition and types

The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) defines a pesticide as any substance or mixture of substances intended for preventing, destroying or controlling any pest, including vectors of human or animal disease, unwanted species of plants or animals, and substances used as plant growth regulators, defoliants or desiccants.

Pesticides are often classified by the organism they target:

  • Insecticides — kill insects (e.g. organophosphates, carbamates, pyrethroids, neonicotinoids).
  • Herbicides — kill unwanted plants or "weeds" (e.g. glyphosate, atrazine, 2,4-D).
  • Fungicides — prevent or kill fungi and their spores.
  • Rodenticides — control mice and other rodents.
  • Bactericides, nematicides, molluscicides and others target bacteria, nematodes and molluscs respectively.

History

Humans have used pesticides since before 2000 BC; the Sumerians used sulfur compounds, and the Chinese used mercury and arsenic compounds to control pests. The modern era began in the 1940s with synthetic organic compounds such as DDT, whose insecticidal properties were discovered by Paul Hermann Müller in 1939. Rachel Carson's 1962 book Silent Spring drew public attention to the environmental harm of indiscriminate pesticide use, particularly DDT's effects on birds, and helped spur modern environmental regulation.

Uses

Pesticides are used predominantly in agriculture to protect crops from yield losses caused by pests, weeds and diseases, but also in public health programs to control disease vectors such as malaria-carrying mosquitoes, in forestry, and in homes and gardens. Global agricultural use has risen steadily and is on the order of several million tonnes of active ingredient per year; herbicides account for roughly half of total use.

Environmental effects

Pesticides can contaminate soil, water, turf and other vegetation. Effects include harm to non-target species such as bees and other pollinators, aquatic organisms and birds; development of pesticide resistance in target pests; and bioaccumulation of persistent compounds in food chains. Neonicotinoid insecticides have been restricted in the European Union because of their effects on pollinators.

Health effects

Exposure can cause acute poisoning, especially among agricultural workers in developing countries, and long-term exposure has been associated in epidemiological studies with various chronic conditions. Regulatory agencies set maximum residue limits for food and require safety testing before approval.

Alternatives

Integrated pest management (IPM) combines biological control, habitat manipulation, resistant crop varieties and judicious chemical use to reduce reliance on pesticides. Other approaches include organic farming, biopesticides, pheromone traps and genetic methods such as Bt crops.
