Treaty of Utrecht

The Treaty of Utrecht was a series of peace treaties signed in the Dutch city of Utrecht in March and April 1713, which — together with the Treaties of Rastatt and Baden of 1714 — ended the War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714). The war had been fought over the inheritance of the childless Charles II of Spain, whose will left his entire empire to Philip, Duke of Anjou, grandson of Louis XIV of France; the Grand Alliance of Britain, the Dutch Republic, Austria and others fought to prevent a Bourbon union of France and Spain and the upset of the European balance of power. The Utrecht settlement recognised Philip as king of Spain on condition that the French and Spanish crowns never be united, redrew the map of Europe and the colonial world in Britain's favour, and is often taken by historians as a landmark in the emergence of the balance of power as the organising principle of international relations.

Terms

Philip V kept the Spanish crown and the Spanish American empire, solemnly renouncing for himself and his descendants any claim to the throne of France — a renunciation the other powers accepted as the keystone of the settlement, though French jurists had doubted its validity under French fundamental law. Spain's European possessions were dismembered: the Spanish Netherlands went to Austria; Sicily went to Victor Amadeus II of Savoy (and with it a royal crown); Milan, Naples and Sardinia went to Austria; and Gibraltar and Minorca — captured during the war — were ceded to Britain "for ever", Gibraltar remaining a British territory despite Spanish claims to this day.

Britain was the treaty's great winner overseas as well. France recognised Queen Anne's title and the Hanoverian (Protestant) succession, undertook to demolish the fortifications of Dunkirk and to expel the Jacobite Pretender from France, and ceded in North America Hudson Bay, Newfoundland, Acadia (Nova Scotia) and St Kitts. Spain granted Britain the asiento — the contract to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America for thirty years — together with an annual permission ship to trade at Porto Bello, concessions whose abuse by smugglers helped cause the War of Jenkins' Ear a generation later. The Dutch received a barrier of fortresses in the Austrian Netherlands against France; Prussia gained recognition of its royal title and territory in Upper Guelders; Portugal recovered positions in Amazonia and the Banda Oriental from France and Spain.

Significance

Utrecht confirmed Britain's emergence as the leading maritime and commercial power and began the long eighteenth-century duel with France for world empire. For Spain it marked the loss of its European empire and its reduction to a second-rank power, though the Spanish monarchy survived intact in the Americas. The negotiations themselves — a congress of ambassadors settling the fate of continents by written instruments — and the treaty's preamble, invoking a "just balance of power" (the phrase appears in the Anglo-Spanish treaty) as the foundation of peace, made Utrecht a founding document of the modern European states system, reaffirmed in stages at Vienna in 1815 after the very different wars of the Napoleonic era.
