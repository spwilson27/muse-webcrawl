Shōen

Shōen (荘園) were private, tax-exempt landed estates in Japan that developed from the 8th century and proliferated through the Heian and Kamakura periods, gradually undermining the centralised public land system of the imperial state. By the 12th century a large proportion of Japan's cultivated land lay within shōen controlled by court nobles, great temples and shrines, and increasingly by local warrior managers.

Formation

Under the ritsuryō system, all land was in principle the emperor's, allotted to cultivators and taxed. Edicts of 723 and 743 allowed those who reclaimed new land to hold it permanently, encouraging the creation of private estates. Owners commonly "commended" their land to a powerful courtier, temple or shrine (kishin), which obtained exemptions from taxation and from entry by provincial officials (funyū rights) in return for a share of the income.

Structure

Rights over a shōen were divided among several layers of proprietors — the absentee patron (honke), the proprietor (ryōke), the estate manager (gesu or jitō) and the cultivators — each entitled to a share of the harvest under the shiki system of divisible rights. The Kamakura shogunate appointed its own stewards (jitō) to many estates, deepening the split between courtly ownership and warrior control.

Decline

Shōen decayed during the Muromachi period as local warriors absorbed estate rights, and they were effectively abolished by Toyotomi Hideyoshi's land surveys (taikō kenchi) in the late 16th century, which measured all land and taxed it directly.
