Osteoclast

An osteoclast is a type of bone cell that breaks down bone tissue. This function is critical in the maintenance, repair, and remodelling of bones of the vertebral skeleton. The osteoclast disassembles and digests the composite of hydrated protein and mineral at a molecular level by secreting acid and a collagenase, a process known as bone resorption. Osteoclasts and osteoblasts are instrumental in controlling the amount of bone tissue: osteoblasts form new bone, while osteoclasts resorb it.

Morphology

Osteoclasts are large multinucleate cells, typically containing 5 to 20 nuclei, with abundant acidophilic cytoplasm full of lysosomes and mitochondria. They are found on bone surfaces in shallow depressions called resorption bays, or Howship's lacunae. The side of the cell facing the bone forms a specialised "ruffled border" of deep folds that greatly increases surface area for secretion and uptake.

Origin and regulation

Osteoclasts derive from the monocyte/macrophage lineage of haematopoietic stem cells. Their formation (osteoclastogenesis) is driven principally by two factors produced by osteoblasts and stromal cells: macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and RANK ligand (RANKL), which binds the receptor RANK on osteoclast precursors. Osteoprotegerin (OPG), a decoy receptor for RANKL, inhibits the process. Hormones including parathyroid hormone (which promotes resorption indirectly) and calcitonin (which inhibits osteoclast activity directly) fine-tune the balance.

Mechanism of resorption

The osteoclast seals itself to the bone surface via integrin-mediated attachment, forming an isolated microenvironment. Proton pumps (vacuolar H+-ATPases) in the ruffled border, supplied by carbonic anhydrase II, acidify this compartment to about pH 4.5, dissolving the hydroxyapatite mineral; the enzyme cathepsin K then degrades the exposed type I collagen matrix. Breakdown products are endocytosed and released on the opposite side of the cell (transcytosis).

Clinical significance

Excessive osteoclast activity contributes to osteoporosis, Paget's disease of bone, rheumatoid arthritis and the bone destruction around metastatic tumours. Genetic failure of osteoclast function causes osteopetrosis ("marble bone disease"), in which bones are abnormally dense but brittle. Drugs that suppress osteoclasts — bisphosphonates and the anti-RANKL antibody denosumab — are mainstays of osteoporosis therapy.
