Markan priority

Markan priority is the hypothesis that the Gospel of Mark was the first of the three Synoptic Gospels to be written, and was used as a source by the authors of the Gospel of Matthew and the Gospel of Luke. It is a central component of the widely held two-source hypothesis for solving the Synoptic problem, and is accepted by a majority of modern biblical scholars.

Evidence

Several lines of argument support Markan priority. Mark is the shortest gospel, yet Matthew and Luke rarely agree with each other against Mark in wording or order where all three overlap; where their narrative order differs, one of them usually follows Mark. Mark's Greek style is rougher and more colloquial, and it is easier to explain Matthew and Luke smoothing or improving Mark's text than Mark degrading theirs. Mark also preserves details that the others soften, such as passages implying limitations on Jesus or harsh portrayals of the disciples — editorial tendencies scholars call lectio difficilior ("the harder reading"). Matthew and Luke additionally share non-Markan material conventionally attributed to the hypothetical sayings source Q.

History of the hypothesis

Although earlier writers such as Griesbach had proposed other orders, Markan priority was developed in the nineteenth century by scholars including Karl Lachmann, Christian Hermann Weisse and Heinrich Julius Holtzmann, whose 1863 work laid the foundation of the two-source theory. Rival solutions include the Augustinian hypothesis (Matthew first), the two-gospel hypothesis, and the Farrer hypothesis, which accepts Markan priority but dispenses with Q. Mark is commonly dated to around 65–75 CE, written around the time of the First Jewish–Roman War and the destruction of the Jerusalem Temple.
