Shawl

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A shawl (from Persian شال shāl) is a simple item of clothing, loosely worn over the shoulders, upper body and arms, sometimes also over the head. It is usually a rectangular or square piece of cloth, often folded into a triangle, but can also be triangular in shape to begin with. Shawls serve for warmth, as religious or ceremonial dress, and as fashion accessories, and historically fine examples — above all the shawls of Kashmir — were luxury goods traded across Asia and Europe.

History

The weaving of woollen shawls is an ancient craft of Kashmir, where the fine undercoat of the changthangi goat (pashmina) and the even finer shahtoosh were worked into cloth prized from Mughal courts to Europe. The Mughal emperor Akbar is credited with popularising and refining the Kashmir shawl industry in the sixteenth century. In the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, imported Kashmir shawls became so fashionable in Britain and France that European mills imitated them — most famously at Paisley, Scotland, whose name became attached to the teardrop boteh motif in English. Napoleon is said to have given many Kashmir shawls to Josephine, whose collection helped set the fashion.

Types

  • Kashmir shawls — hand-woven pashmina, including embroidered kani (twill-tapestry) shawls.
  • Prayer shawls — the Jewish tallit, with fringes (tzitzit) at the corners.
  • Manton de Manila — embroidered silk shawls of Spanish and Filipino tradition, ultimately derived from Chinese trade silks.
  • Rebozo — the long Mexican shawl, used also for carrying children and goods.
  • Ruanas and poncho-like wraps of the Andes.
  • Wedding and ring shawls — so fine they can pass through a finger ring.

Cultural significance

Shawls carry ritual meaning in many cultures: draped over the head or shoulders in prayer and worship, presented as honours in South Asian ceremonies, and worn as markers of mourning or celebration. The shawl survives in modern fashion as a versatile wrap and in traditional dress across South Asia, the Middle East, Latin America and Europe.
