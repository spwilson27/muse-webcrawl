Climate model

A climate model is a quantitative representation of the interactions between the atmosphere, oceans, land surface, and ice that drive Earth's climate. Global climate models (GCMs), also called general circulation models, divide the planet into a three-dimensional grid and solve the fundamental equations of fluid dynamics and radiation transfer on supercomputers.

Types

Models range from simple energy-balance models to fully coupled Earth system models incorporating the carbon cycle, atmospheric chemistry, and ice sheets. Atmospheric GCMs model the atmosphere and land surface; coupled atmosphere–ocean GCMs (AOGCMs) add dynamic oceans; regional climate models nest a high-resolution domain inside a global model.

Development

The first general circulation models were developed in the 1950s–1960s by Norman Phillips, Joseph Smagorinsky, and Syukuro Manabe. Manabe's work on radiative–convective equilibrium provided the first credible estimates of climate sensitivity to carbon dioxide. Model resolution and complexity have grown steadily with computing power.

Evaluation

Models are tested against instrumental records, satellite observations, and palaeoclimate data, and through intercomparison projects such as CMIP, whose outputs underpin the assessment reports of the IPCC.
