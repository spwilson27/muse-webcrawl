Agadir (Arabic: أكادير; Berber languages: ⴰⴳⴰⴷⵉⵔ) is a city in southwestern Morocco, on the shore of the Atlantic Ocean near the foothills of the Atlas Mountains, just north of the mouth of the Souss River. It is the capital of the Souss-Massa region and one of Morocco's principal tourist resorts and fishing ports.

History

The site, whose name means "fortified granary" or "wall" in Berber, was a Portuguese trading post in the early 16th century before passing to the Saadian sultans. The city was the scene of the 1911 Agadir Crisis, a confrontation between France and Germany. On 29 February 1960 a devastating earthquake destroyed much of the city and killed roughly 12,000–15,000 people; Agadir was rebuilt a short distance south of the original site in modernist concrete style.

Economy

Modern Agadir is known for its long sandy beach, mild climate, resort hotels, and marina, as well as for sardine and anchovy fishing, fish processing, and the export of citrus fruit and vegetables from the Souss plain. The hilltop kasbah ruins bear the inscription "God, Country, King".
