Charango

The charango is a small Andean stringed instrument of the lute family, originating in the Quechua and Aymara populations of the Altiplano in post-colonial times, after European stringed instruments were introduced by the Spanish. It has ten strings in five courses and a small body; traditionally the sound box was made from the shell of an armadillo, though modern instruments are carved from a single block of wood or built like a miniature guitar.

History

The charango developed in the mining regions of Potosí, Bolivia, during the colonial era, adapting European vihuela and mandolin-type instruments to local materials and music. It became central to Andean folk music (música criolla andina) and the nueva canción movement, played by artists such as Ernesto Cavour and groups including Los Kjarkas and Inti-Illimani.

Tuning and playing

The standard tuning is G–C–E–A–E (sol–do–mi–la–mi), with the middle course tuned in octaves, giving the instrument a bright, ringing sound despite its short scale (~37 cm). Larger and smaller relatives include the lower-pitched ronroco and the small hualaycho. Strumming with elaborate rhythmic patterns (repique) is a characteristic technique.
