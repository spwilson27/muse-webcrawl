Melanesians

Melanesians are the predominant indigenous inhabitants of Melanesia, the region of Oceania extending from New Guinea to the Fiji islands, including Papua New Guinea, the Solomon Islands, Vanuatu and New Caledonia. The name, coined by the French explorer Jules Dumont d'Urville in 1832, comes from the Greek for "black islands".

Culture and languages

Melanesia is one of the most linguistically diverse regions on Earth, with over a thousand languages, mostly Papuan and Austronesian. Traditional societies were organised around clan structures, big-man leadership, elaborate exchange systems such as the kula ring, and rich traditions of carving, dance and ritual.

Population

Melanesians number roughly ten million or more, the majority in Papua New Guinea. Genetic studies show Melanesians carry notable Denisovan ancestry inherited from archaic admixture in the region's deep settlement history, which stretches back some 40,000–50,000 years.
