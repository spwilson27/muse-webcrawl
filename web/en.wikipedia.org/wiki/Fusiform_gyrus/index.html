Fusiform gyrus

The fusiform gyrus, also known as the lateral occipitotemporal gyrus, is part of the temporal lobe and occipital lobe in Brodmann area 37. The fusiform gyrus is located between the inferior temporal gyrus and the parahippocampal gyrus. Its name derives from its spindle-like (fusiform) shape.

Function

The fusiform gyrus is involved in higher-level visual processing. It contains the fusiform face area (FFA), a region that responds strongly to faces and is considered critical for face recognition; damage to it can produce prosopagnosia, the inability to recognize familiar faces. Other specialized subregions include the visual word form area (VWFA), associated with reading and word recognition, and areas responsive to bodies (the fusiform body area) and to color.

Clinical significance

Abnormalities of the fusiform gyrus have been implicated in conditions such as prosopagnosia, synesthesia, and aspects of autism and schizophrenia. Stimulation of the fusiform gyrus in epilepsy patients can produce distortions of face perception.
