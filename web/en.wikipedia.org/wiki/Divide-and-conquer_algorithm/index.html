Divide-and-conquer algorithm

In computer science, divide and conquer is an algorithm design paradigm. A divide-and-conquer algorithm recursively breaks down a problem into two or more sub-problems of the same or related type, until these become simple enough to be solved directly. The solutions to the sub-problems are then combined to give a solution to the original problem.

The three steps

  1. Divide the problem into a number of sub-problems that are smaller instances of the same problem.
  2. Conquer the sub-problems by solving them recursively. If the sub-problem sizes are small enough, solve them directly.
  3. Combine the solutions of the sub-problems into the solution for the original problem.

Examples

Classic divide-and-conquer algorithms include merge sort and quicksort for sorting, binary search, the Karatsuba algorithm for fast multiplication, Strassen's algorithm for matrix multiplication, and the fast Fourier transform (Cooley–Tukey algorithm).

Analysis

The running time of a divide-and-conquer algorithm is commonly expressed by a recurrence relation of the form T(n) = aT(n/b) + f(n), solved by the master theorem or the Akra–Bazzi method. When the work done at each level decreases geometrically, the total cost is dominated by one level; this explains, for example, why merge sort runs in Θ(n log n).

Advantages and implementation issues

Divide and conquer is a powerful tool for solving conceptually difficult problems and often yields optimal algorithms. It lends itself naturally to parallel execution, since sub-problems can be solved independently (as in the MapReduce framework). Recursive implementations may incur overhead and stack usage; problems with overlapping sub-problems are better handled by dynamic programming or memoization.

History

The name "divide and conquer" echoes the Latin maxim divide et impera. The paradigm underlies algorithms known since antiquity (such as the Euclidean algorithm's recursive form) and was formalized in 20th-century algorithm analysis; John von Neumann developed merge sort in 1945.
