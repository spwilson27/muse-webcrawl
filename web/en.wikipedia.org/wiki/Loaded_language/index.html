Loaded language

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Word choice carries judgement

Loaded language (also called loaded terms, strong emotive language, or, when used as a fallacy, the loaded question's verbal cousin) is wording chosen not merely to describe but to provoke an emotional response or to smuggle an evaluation into an apparently neutral statement. Such words and phrases carry strong positive or negative connotations beyond their literal meaning — for example, describing the same group as "freedom fighters", "rebels", "insurgents" or "terrorists", or calling a policy a "reform", a "scheme" or a "regime measure".

Analysis

The philosopher Charles Stevenson analysed such terms as "persuasive definitions": expressions with a stable emotive charge but a shifting descriptive content, allowing a speaker to redirect attitudes without openly arguing. Loaded language is a staple of propaganda, advertising and political rhetoric, and media-literacy teaching often uses paired examples — "pro-life" versus "anti-abortion", "estate tax" versus "death tax" — to show how framing works. Related devices include euphemism and its opposite, dysphemism; weasel words; glittering generalities; and pejorative labels that beg the question at issue. Neutral reporting standards, such as Wikipedia's own neutral point of view policy, generally require replacing loaded expressions with descriptive ones ("said" rather than "claimed" or "admitted", for instance) and attributing evaluative language to its sources. Critics note that the accusation of using loaded language can itself be a rhetorical weapon, and that genuinely neutral vocabulary is not always available for contested subjects.
