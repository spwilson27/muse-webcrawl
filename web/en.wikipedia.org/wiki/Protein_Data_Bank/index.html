The Protein Data Bank (PDB) is a worldwide archive of three-dimensional structural data for large biological molecules, including proteins and nucleic acids. Founded in 1971, it is the primary repository for experimentally determined macromolecular structures obtained by X-ray crystallography, NMR spectroscopy and, increasingly, cryo-electron microscopy. The archive is managed by the Worldwide Protein Data Bank (wwPDB), an international collaboration whose members operate the regional data centres through which structures are deposited, validated, annotated and distributed free of charge. Nearly all scientific journals and funding agencies require researchers to deposit new structures in the PDB before publication, making it one of the central resources of structural biology and a foundation for the development of structure-prediction methods such as AlphaFold.

History

The PDB was established in 1971 as a collaboration between Brookhaven National Laboratory in the United States and the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre in the United Kingdom, following discussions among crystallographers about the need to share atomic coordinate data; at first it held just seven structures, exchanged on magnetic tape. For years deposit was voluntary and growth slow—the archive passed 100 structures only in the early 1980s—but the combination of faster detectors, synchrotron radiation and journal mandates in the 1990s caused exponential growth, to roughly 10,000 structures by 1999 and 100,000 by 2014. In 1999 management in the United States passed to the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB). In 2003 the wwPDB was formed by the RCSB PDB, the Macromolecular Structure Database at the European Bioinformatics Institute (now PDBe) and the Protein Data Bank Japan (PDBj), later joined by the Biological Magnetic Resonance Data Bank (BMRB), to guarantee that the archive remains a single, freely available global resource.

Contents and file formats

Each entry describes the atomic coordinates of a macromolecular structure together with extensive metadata: the sequence and source organism, experimental method and resolution, the authors and primary citation, ligands and cofactors, and validation reports assessing the structure's geometric quality. Entries are identified by a four-character PDB ID (such as 1CRN, the plant protein crambin, or 4HHB, haemoglobin). Data were historically distributed in the legacy "PDB format", whose fixed-width columns could not represent very large structures; the archive's standard is now the PDBx/mmCIF format, with the richer PDBML (XML) variant also available. A companion resource, the Electron Microscopy Data Bank (EMDB), archives the density maps from cryo-EM experiments, while computed structure models from prediction programs are distributed through partner model archives rather than the PDB itself.

Methods represented

X-ray crystallography supplied the great majority of entries for the PDB's first four decades. Since about 2013, the "resolution revolution" in cryo-electron microscopy—driven by direct electron detectors and improved image processing—has produced a surge of large and previously intractable structures, including viruses, ribosomes and membrane-protein complexes, and cryo-EM has overtaken crystallography in the size and prominence of new deposits. Solution NMR contributes a smaller share, chiefly of small proteins, and provides ensembles of models rather than a single structure.

Use and validation

PDB structures underpin research in enzymology, drug design, protein engineering and evolutionary biology; structure-based drug discovery routinely begins from a PDB entry, and landmark deposits include the first structures of the ribosome, of HIV protease (a target of antiretroviral drugs), and of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and main protease in 2020, which were downloaded millions of times during the COVID-19 pandemic. Since 2008, deposition has used a common wwPDB system with mandatory validation reports generated from software assessing bond geometry, clashes, fit to experimental data and other criteria; the reports accompany every entry so users can judge reliability. In 2021 the deep-learning system AlphaFold 2, trained on the PDB's 50 years of accumulated structures, largely solved the protein structure prediction problem, and the resulting AlphaFold Protein Structure Database of predicted models—cross-linked to PDB entries—was built with EMBL-EBI and DeepMind.
