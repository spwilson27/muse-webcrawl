Hafez (Persian: حافظ, Ḥāfeẓ, "the memorizer; the safe keeper"; 1315–1390), in full Khwāja Shams-ud-Dīn Muhammad Hāfez-e Shīrāzī, was a Persian lyric poet whose collected works — his Divān — are regarded as a pinnacle of Persian literature and are found in the homes of most Persian speakers, who learn some of his poems by heart and use them in everyday proverbs and sayings.

Life

Little is reliably known of Hafez's life. Born in Shiraz in south-western Persia, he memorised the Quran in his youth — hence his pen name, Hafez, "one who has memorised [the Quran]". He worked as a court poet under the Muzaffarid rulers of Shiraz, declined invitations from foreign sultans (including from the Deccan), and is said to have met the conqueror Timur late in life. He died in Shiraz in 1390 and was buried in the Musalla Gardens, where his tomb — the present pavilion dates from 1935 — remains a place of pilgrimage.

Poetry

Hafez perfected the ghazal, the short lyric form built from semantically independent couplets bound by a single rhyme and refrain. His poems move fluidly among the imagery of earthly love and wine and the language of Sufi mysticism and divine love, so that nearly every couplet sustains both readings; behind the beloved and the cupbearer stand recurring figures of hypocrisy and self-righteousness that the poet delights in mocking. His Divān contains some 500 ghazals along with qasidas, masnavis, quatrains and other pieces, and its verbal music — dense with wordplay and allusion — has made it famously resistant to translation.

Legacy

Iranians traditionally consult the Divān of Hafez for bibliomancy (the fāl-e Ḥāfeẓ), opening it at random for guidance, especially on the night of Yalda. Hafez profoundly influenced later Persian, Turkish and Urdu poetry, and in the West he inspired Goethe's West–Eastern Divan and was praised by Emerson and others. Annual commemorations are held at his tomb on 12 October, Hafez Day in Iran.
