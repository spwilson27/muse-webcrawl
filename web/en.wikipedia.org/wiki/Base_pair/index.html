Base pair

A base pair (bp) is a fundamental unit of double-stranded nucleic acids consisting of two nucleobases bound to each other by hydrogen bonds. Base pairs are the building blocks of the DNA double helix and also contribute to the folded structure of single-stranded RNA. In canonical Watson–Crick pairing, adenine (A) pairs with thymine (T) through two hydrogen bonds, and guanine (G) pairs with cytosine (C) through three.

Pairing rules

Complementarity of base pairing underlies the copying of genetic information: each strand of DNA serves as a template for the synthesis of its partner. In RNA, uracil (U) replaces thymine. Less common geometries include wobble pairs (such as G–U), Hoogsteen pairs and other non-canonical arrangements found in folded RNAs and in transient structures. Because G–C pairs have three hydrogen bonds, GC-rich DNA has a higher melting temperature than AT-rich DNA.

As a unit of length

The size of a gene or genome is conventionally measured in base pairs: kilobase pairs (kb), megabase pairs (Mb) and gigabase pairs (Gb). The human genome contains about 3.2 billion base pairs in its haploid set. Along the axis of B-DNA, consecutive base pairs are separated by about 0.34 nanometres, with roughly 10.5 pairs per turn of the helix.

Unnatural base pairs

Synthetic biologists have created unnatural base pairs — using hydrogen-bonded designs such as the Benner bases or hydrophobic pairs such as dNaM–dTPT3 — that can be replicated inside living cells, expanding the genetic alphabet beyond four letters and enabling organisms with semi-synthetic genomes.
