Mbira is an African musical instrument family of lamellophones, consisting of a wooden board (often fitted with a resonator) with attached staggered metal tines, played by holding the instrument in the hands and plucking the tines with the thumbs (and sometimes the fingers). Metal- and bamboo-tined lamellophones have existed in Africa for thousands of years; iron-tined instruments are documented in the Zambezi valley from around the 13th century.

Shona mbira

The best-known type is the mbira dzavadzimu ("mbira of the ancestors") of the Shona people of Zimbabwe, with 22–28 keys arranged in three manuals. It is played inside a large calabash resonator (deze) fitted with shells or bottle caps that buzz in sympathy. In the all-night bira ceremony, mbira music — interlocking cyclic patterns in 12/8-like metre, accompanied by hosho gourd rattles, handclapping and singing — calls ancestral spirits to possess spirit mediums.

History and influence

Missionaries and the Rhodesian government discouraged mbira playing, but the instrument became a symbol of Zimbabwean identity during the liberation struggle; songs by Thomas Mapfumo adapted mbira patterns to electric guitars in the chimurenga style. Players such as Ephat Mujuru, Dumisani Maraire (who popularised it in North America) and Stella Chiweshe brought the mbira to international audiences. The related kalimba was popularised worldwide by Hugh Tracey.
