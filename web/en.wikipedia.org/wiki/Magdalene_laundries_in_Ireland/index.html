Magdalene laundries in Ireland

The Magdalene laundries in Ireland, also known as Magdalene asylums, were institutions operated largely by Roman Catholic religious orders from the eighteenth century until the late twentieth century. They housed women deemed "fallen" — including unmarried mothers, women considered promiscuous, orphans, and girls sent by families, courts or priests — who worked without pay in commercial laundries under harsh regimes of confinement, prayer and silence.

History

The first Magdalene asylum in Ireland opened in Dublin in 1765. After independence, the laundries became embedded in the institutions of the Irish state: government departments and local authorities sent business to them and placed women and girls there. An estimated 30,000 women passed through the ten laundries that operated in the Republic in the twentieth century; the last, at Sean MacDermott Street in Dublin, closed in 1996.

Exposure and inquiry

The discovery in 1993 of a mass grave at the High Park convent in Drumcondra, where 155 women were exhumed, many without death certificates, drew public attention. In 2013 the McAleese Committee report documented state involvement in the laundries, and Taoiseach Enda Kenny delivered a formal state apology in the Dáil. A redress scheme was established for survivors.

Legacy

The laundries have become a central symbol of the abuses of institutional Ireland, commemorated in survivor testimony, memorials and works such as the film Philomena (2013).
