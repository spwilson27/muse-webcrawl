Diarrhetic shellfish poisoning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Bivalve shellfish can accumulate DSP toxins]

Diarrhetic shellfish poisoning (DSP) is one of the four recognised syndromes of shellfish poisoning, caused by eating bivalve shellfish — such as mussels, oysters, scallops, and clams — contaminated with toxins produced by certain dinoflagellate algae. The principal toxins are okadaic acid and the dinophysistoxins, produced by species of the genera Dinophysis and Prorocentrum.

Symptoms

Symptoms begin between thirty minutes and a few hours after consumption and are predominantly gastrointestinal: diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain and cramps. Recovery is usually complete within two to three days, and no deaths have been attributed to DSP. Because symptoms mimic bacterial food poisoning, the condition is probably under-reported.

Mechanism

Okadaic acid is a potent inhibitor of the protein phosphatases PP1 and PP2A. In the intestine it increases the phosphorylation of proteins controlling sodium secretion by intestinal cells, causing water to move into the gut lumen and producing profuse watery diarrhoea. The toxins are fat-soluble and heat-stable, so cooking does not reliably destroy them.

Monitoring

Because the toxins accumulate in shellfish during harmful algal blooms, harvest areas are monitored and closed when toxin levels exceed regulatory limits (in the European Union, 160 micrograms of okadaic-acid equivalents per kilogram of shellfish meat). Testing has shifted from mouse bioassays to chemical methods such as liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). DSP was first described after outbreaks in Japan in the late 1970s and is now reported worldwide wherever Dinophysis blooms occur.
