In music, a cent is a logarithmic unit of measure used for musical intervals. Twelve-tone equal temperament divides the octave into 12 semitones of 100 cents each, so the octave equals 1200 cents. The cent was proposed by Alexander John Ellis in 1885, in his annotated translation of Hermann von Helmholtz's On the Sensations of Tone, and has become the standard unit for measuring and comparing small intervals and tuning differences.

Definition

The size in cents of an interval with frequency ratio f2/f1 is 1200 × log2(f2/f1). One cent corresponds to a frequency ratio of 21/1200, about 1.0005778. Because the scale is logarithmic, cents add and subtract like the intervals themselves.

Use

Cents are used to describe temperament deviations — for instance, the equal-tempered perfect fifth is 700 cents, exactly 2 cents narrower than the just fifth (3:2, about 702 cents), while the equal-tempered major third (400 cents) is about 14 cents wider than the just major third (5:4, about 386 cents). Ethnomusicologists use cents to document non-Western pitch systems, and tuners and instrument makers use them to specify intonation accuracy. Under ideal conditions trained listeners can perceive differences of roughly 4–6 cents on sustained tones, so the cent is comfortably finer than the threshold of pitch discrimination.
