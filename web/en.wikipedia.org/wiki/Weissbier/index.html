Weissbier (German for "white beer"), also called Weizenbier ("wheat beer"), is a top-fermented beer brewed with a large proportion of malted wheat in place of the barley malt typical of other styles. It is most closely associated with Bavaria in southern Germany, where the best-known form is the cloudy, yeast-suspended Hefeweizen.

Brewing and character

By German convention, at least half of the grist must be wheat malt. A special strain of top-fermenting yeast produces the style's signature aromas: fruity esters reminiscent of banana and phenols suggesting clove and spice, arising from compounds such as isoamyl acetate and 4-vinyl guaiacol. Hefeweizen is served unfiltered and cloudy (hefetrüb), while the filtered, clear variant is called Kristallweizen. Weissbier is traditionally served in a tall, vase-shaped glass that supports a large creamy head, and is usually poured in one motion with the bottle rolled or swirled to rouse the yeast.

History

Wheat brewing in Bavaria was restricted for centuries: the Reinheitsgebot of 1516 permitted only barley, hops, and water, partly to reserve wheat for bakers. The ducal house of Degenberg held a wheat-beer privilege, which passed to the Wittelsbach dukes, who ran a lucrative monopoly from breweries such as the Hofbräuhaus. As lager styles displaced top-fermented beers in the 19th century, weissbier nearly vanished, but it was revived in the 1960s–70s — notably by the Schneider brewery — and became one of Germany's most popular styles.

Variants

Stronger Weizenbock, dark Dunkelweizen, and seasonal variants extend the family. Related wheat styles brewed elsewhere include Belgian witbier (flavoured with coriander and orange peel) and Berliner Weisse (a low-alcohol, sour wheat beer).
