Corporation tax in the United Kingdom

Corporation tax is a tax levied in the United Kingdom on the profits of UK resident companies and on the UK-source profits of non-resident companies with a permanent establishment in the UK. It was introduced in the Finance Act 1965, replacing the previous system under which companies paid income tax and profits tax.

Rates

The main rate of corporation tax fell from 52% in the early 1980s through successive reductions to 19% from April 2017, then the lowest in the G20. From 1 April 2023 the main rate rose to 25% for companies with profits over £250,000, while a small profits rate of 19% applies to profits of £50,000 or less, with marginal relief between the thresholds. Special rates apply to ring-fenced oil and gas profits and to banks, which pay an additional surcharge.

Computation

Taxable profits are computed from accounting profit with adjustments: capital allowances replace depreciation deductions, and certain expenses (such as client entertaining) are disallowed. Companies may surrender losses between group members through group relief. Research and development tax credits provide enhanced relief for qualifying R&D expenditure. Most companies pay tax nine months and one day after the end of their accounting period; very large companies pay by quarterly instalments.

History

Before 1965 companies were taxed under the schedular income tax system with an additional profits tax. The 1965 reform introduced a separate corporation tax and a classical system taxing dividends in shareholders' hands; an imputation system with advance corporation tax operated from 1973 until 1999. Since 2009 the system has been broadly territorial, exempting most foreign dividends and foreign branch profits.
