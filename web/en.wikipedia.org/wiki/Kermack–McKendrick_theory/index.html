Kermack–McKendrick theory

Placeholder image: classic SIR epidemic curves.

The Kermack–McKendrick theory is a mathematical framework for predicting the course of infectious disease epidemics, developed by Scottish scientists William Ogilvy Kermack and Anderson Gray McKendrick in a series of papers beginning in 1927. It underlies the family of compartmental models still used in epidemiology today.

The SIR model

The simplest form divides a population into three compartments: Susceptible, Infectious, and Recovered (or removed). Susceptibles become infected at a rate proportional to their contacts with infectious individuals; infectious individuals recover at a constant rate. The model yields a celebrated threshold theorem: an epidemic can take off only if the initial density of susceptibles exceeds a critical value determined by the infection and recovery rates.

Assumptions and extensions

The original model assumes a closed, homogeneously mixing population with no births, deaths from other causes, or latent period. Extensions add compartments such as Exposed (SEIR), account for waning immunity, age structure, vital dynamics, and spatial and network effects.

Influence

Kermack and McKendrick's 1927 paper, "A contribution to the mathematical theory of epidemics", is regarded as a founding document of mathematical epidemiology and has informed public-health responses to influenza, measles, and COVID-19.
