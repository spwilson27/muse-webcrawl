Accommodation (psychology)

Accommodation is a term developed by psychologist Jean Piaget to describe what occurs when new information or experiences cause you to alter your existing schemas. In other words, accommodation occurs when existing schemas (knowledge) do not work and need to be changed in order to deal with a new object or situation. It is one of two processes — the other being assimilation — through which children and adults adapt their mental structures to new experiences, a combined process Piaget called adaptation.

In Piaget's theory

Piaget proposed that infants and children build schemas — organized patterns of knowledge or behavior — through interaction with their environment. When a child encounters something new, they first try assimilation: incorporating the new experience into an existing schema. For example, a child who has a schema for "dog" might call a cow a dog on first seeing it. When the mismatch between the schema and reality becomes apparent, the child accommodates: the schema itself is modified or a new schema is created, so that cows and dogs are distinguished. The tension between the two processes, called disequilibrium, drives cognitive development in Piaget's account, and the balance between them is termed equilibration.

Examples

A classic classroom example involves a child who knows what a zebra is only as a kind of "striped horse." On learning that zebras are a distinct species, the child must accommodate by revising the schema. In adult life, learning a new language, adjusting one's driving habits after moving to a country that drives on the other side of the road, or restructuring one's professional knowledge when changing careers all involve accommodation.

Related concepts

Accommodation in psychology should not be confused with accommodation in vision (the focusing of the eye's lens), accommodation in communication theory (adjusting speech to an audience), or Piaget's related notions of assimilation and equilibration.
