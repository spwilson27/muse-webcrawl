Secular equilibrium

In nuclear physics, secular equilibrium is a situation in a radioactive decay chain in which the amount of a radioactive isotope remains constant because its production rate (due to decay of a parent isotope) exactly equals its own decay rate. It arises when the parent isotope has a much longer half-life than the daughter isotope.

In secular equilibrium the daughter's activity approaches and then tracks the parent's activity.

Conditions and mathematics

Consider a parent nuclide 1 decaying to a daughter nuclide 2 with decay constants λ₁ and λ₂, where λ₁ ≪ λ₂ (the parent is far longer-lived). After a time long compared with the daughter's half-life — on the order of several daughter half-lives — the daughter's population reaches a steady ratio:

N₂ / N₁ = λ₁ / λ₂, so that λ₂N₂ = λ₁N₁

That is, the activities (decay rates) of parent and daughter are equal. Every subsequent member of the chain likewise comes into equilibrium, so in an old, undisturbed sample of a decay series such as the uranium-238 series, all members have (nearly) equal activities.

Transient equilibrium

When the parent's half-life is longer than the daughter's but not overwhelmingly so, the system reaches transient equilibrium, in which the daughter's activity exceeds the parent's by a constant factor and both then decay together with the parent's effective half-life. A classic example is the molybdenum-99 / technetium-99m generator used in nuclear medicine.

Applications

Secular equilibrium underlies uranium-series dating methods, radon generation from radium sources, and the assumption, in ore assay and geochronology, that undisturbed ancient material has equal activities throughout the decay chain. Departures from equilibrium are themselves informative, recording processes such as leaching, weathering, or chemical fractionation within roughly the last million years.
