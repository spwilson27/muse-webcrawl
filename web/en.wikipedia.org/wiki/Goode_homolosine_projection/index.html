Goode homolosine projection

TypePseudocylindrical, equal-area, composite
Introduced1923 (atlas use from 1925)
Proposed byJohn Paul Goode
PropertiesEqual-area; interrupted

The Goode homolosine projection (or interrupted Goode homolosine projection) is a pseudocylindrical, equal-area, composite map projection used for world maps. It was developed by the American geographer and cartographer John Paul Goode in 1923 as an alternative to the Mercator projection for portraying global areal relationships.

Development

Goode created the projection by combining the sinusoidal projection and the Mollweide projection. The sinusoidal projection is used between latitudes 40°44′ north and south, where its local accuracy is greatest, and the Mollweide projection is used poleward of those latitudes. The name "homolosine" combines "homolographic" (an older name for the Mollweide projection) and "sine". Goode began experimenting with the composite in 1916, and the projection first appeared in print in 1923; it was used in Goode's School Atlas from 1925.

Interruptions

The projection is most often presented in an interrupted form, in which the oceans (or, in an alternative arrangement, the continents) are cut so that land masses can be shown with minimal distortion of shape and area. In its uninterrupted form it resembles a squashed orange peel laid flat.

Properties

Because it is equal-area, the homolosine correctly represents the relative sizes of regions, making it popular for thematic world maps showing distributions such as population, climate and agriculture. Distances and directions are not generally accurate, and shapes are distorted away from the central meridians of each lobe.
