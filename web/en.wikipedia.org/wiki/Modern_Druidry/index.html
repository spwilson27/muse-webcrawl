Modern Druidry

Modern Druidry, also called Druidism, is a modern spiritual or religious movement that promotes reverence for nature and draws inspiration from the druids — the priestly class of the ancient Celtic peoples described by classical authors such as Julius Caesar and Pliny the Elder. Unlike the Germanic-focused Heathenry, Druidry looks to Celtic sources and landscapes, celebrating seasonal festivals at sites such as Stonehenge and Avebury.

History

Modern Druidry began as a fraternal and cultural movement: the Ancient Order of Druids was founded in London in 1781, and Welsh cultural Druidry flourished through the eisteddfod tradition of Iolo Morganwg, who devised ceremonies he claimed (falsely) were ancient. In the 1960s the movement turned explicitly religious and nature-focused with Ross Nichols's Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids (OBOD, 1964), and Reformed Druids of North America appeared in the United States in 1963. Druidry was recognized as a religion for charity-law purposes in the United Kingdom in 2010.

Beliefs and practice

Druids typically celebrate eight seasonal festivals (the Wheel of the Year, shared with Wicca), honor ancestors and spirits of place, and value creativity under the concept of awen — flowing inspiration. Training in OBOD proceeds through the grades of Bard, Ovate, and Druid. Most modern Druids describe their path as a spirituality rather than a dogmatic religion; some are polytheists, others pantheists or animists. The movement grew alongside the 1960s counterculture of the hippies and remains strongly associated with environmentalism and the protection of sacred sites.
