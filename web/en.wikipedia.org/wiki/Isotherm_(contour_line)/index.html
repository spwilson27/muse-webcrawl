Isotherm (contour line)

[Image: Weather map with isotherms showing mean temperatures]
Isotherms on a map of mean annual temperature.

An isotherm (from Greek ἴσος isos, "equal", and θέρμη therme, "heat") is a line on a map or chart of the Earth's surface connecting points having the same temperature at a given time or on average over a given period. Isotherms are a type of contour line (isoline) widely used in meteorology and climatology to visualise temperature distribution.

History

The Prussian naturalist Alexander von Humboldt is credited with devising isotherms around 1817, using them to compare climates across the globe independently of latitude. Temperature maps using isotherms became a standard feature of weather charts and atlases during the nineteenth century.

Characteristics

The spacing of isotherms indicates the magnitude of the temperature gradient: closely spaced lines mark rapid change, while widely spaced lines mark gentle change. Isotherms bend toward the poles over warm ocean currents and toward the equator over cold currents, and they shift seasonally with the Sun. The frost line, commonly the 0 °C isotherm, is important in agriculture and building practice.

Related isolines

Other meteorological isolines include isobars (equal pressure), isohyets (equal rainfall) and isotachs (equal wind speed).
