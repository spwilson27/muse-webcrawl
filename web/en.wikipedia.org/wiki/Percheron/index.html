The Percheron is a breed of draft horse that originated in the Huisne river valley in western France, part of the former Perche province, from which the breed takes its name. Renowned for its intelligence and willingness to work, the Percheron is usually grey or black in colour and is noted among draft breeds for its refinement, clean legs with little feathering, and spirited action, traits traditionally attributed to Arabian blood introduced centuries ago.

History

The breed developed in the Perche region of Normandy. Legend attributes its Arabian influence to horses brought back after the Crusades or captured from Moorish forces after the Battle of Tours, and Arabian stallions were documented at the Le Pin stud in the 18th and 19th centuries. In 1823 a famous stallion named Jean le Blanc, from whose line nearly all modern Percherons descend, was foaled in the region.

Percherons were exported to the United States from the 1840s onward and became the most numerous draft breed in America by the early 20th century; the 1930 U.S. census recorded more registered Percherons than all other draft breeds combined. They pulled stagecoaches, freight wagons, and artillery, and worked farms across North America, Australia, and elsewhere. Numbers collapsed with mechanization after the Second World War, and the breed was later listed as threatened, but it has since recovered and is now used for driving competitions, logging, carriage work, shows, and under saddle.

Characteristics

French Percherons typically stand 155–185 cm at the withers; American-bred horses are often taller and heavier. The head is refined with a straight or slightly dished profile, wide forehead, and alert ears. The neck is arched and muscular, the back short, the croup broad and sloped, and the legs clean with strong joints and hard hooves. Foals are born dark and most turn grey with age, though black is also accepted by the French and American registries.

Uses

Today the Percheron is used as a carriage and parade horse, in combined driving and draft-horse showing, for skidding logs in sensitive forests, and as a riding horse; crosses with Thoroughbreds and other light breeds produce popular sport and police horses. In France the breed is also raised for horsemeat, and the heavier French type reflects that market.
