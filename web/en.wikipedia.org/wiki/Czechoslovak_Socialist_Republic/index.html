The Czechoslovak Socialist Republic (Czech and Slovak: Československá socialistická republika, ČSSR) was the formal name of Czechoslovakia from 1960 until 1990, during the one-party communist regime established after the February 1948 coup. The state was a member of the Warsaw Pact and the Eastern Bloc, aligned with the Soviet Union.

History

After the Communist Party of Czechoslovakia seized power in February 1948, the May 1948 constitution proclaimed a "people's democracy". The Constitution of 1960 formally declared that socialism had been achieved, changed the country's name to the Czechoslovak Socialist Republic, and entrenched the leading role of the Communist Party.

In 1968, reformist leader Alexander Dubček introduced liberalising reforms — the "Prague Spring" — seeking "socialism with a human face". In August 1968, Warsaw Pact forces invaded to crush the reforms. The ensuing period of "normalization" under Gustáv Husák reversed the reforms and imposed tighter orthodoxy. A constitutional act of October 1968 federalised the state into the Czech Socialist Republic and the Slovak Socialist Republic, effective 1 January 1969 — the only Prague Spring reform to survive.

End of the regime

Dissent grew through the Charter 77 movement of the late 1970s. In November–December 1989, the Velvet Revolution brought mass protests that ended communist rule peacefully. In March 1990 the country was renamed the Czech and Slovak Federative Republic, and on 1 January 1993 Czechoslovakia dissolved into the independent Czech Republic and Slovakia.
