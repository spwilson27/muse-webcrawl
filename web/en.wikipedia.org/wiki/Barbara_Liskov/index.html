Barbara Liskov

Barbara Liskov
Born: November 7, 1939, Los Angeles, California, U.S.
Alma mater: UC Berkeley; Stanford University (PhD 1968)
Awards: Turing Award (2008), John von Neumann Medal (2004)
Fields: Programming languages, distributed computing

Barbara Liskov (born November 7, 1939) is an American computer scientist who has made pioneering contributions to programming languages and distributed systems. She is an Institute Professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. In 2008 she received the ACM Turing Award for her work on data abstraction, fault tolerance, and distributed computing.

Early life and education

Liskov earned her bachelor's degree in mathematics from the University of California, Berkeley in 1961. She went on to Stanford University and in 1968 became one of the first women in the United States to receive a PhD in computer science, with a thesis on computer chess endgames supervised by John McCarthy.

Career and contributions

At MITRE Corporation she led the design of the Venus operating system. Joining MIT in 1972, she led the development of the CLU programming language (1974–1975), which introduced clusters—an early form of abstract data types—along with iterators and exception handling, concepts that influenced later languages such as Java and C#. She then led the Argus project, extending these ideas to distributed programs. Her name is attached to the Liskov substitution principle, a foundational rule of object-oriented design stating that objects of a subtype should be substitutable for objects of a base type without breaking program correctness. Later work addressed Byzantine fault tolerance, including the PBFT algorithm (with Miguel Castro), and distributed storage systems. Her textbook Program Development in Java (with John Guttag) is widely used.

Awards

Besides the Turing Award and the IEEE John von Neumann Medal, Liskov was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame and is a member of the National Academy of Engineering and the National Academy of Sciences.
