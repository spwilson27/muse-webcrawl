Uranium–lead dating

Uranium–lead (U–Pb) dating is one of the oldest and most precise radiometric dating methods. It exploits two independent decay chains — uranium-238 decaying to lead-206 and uranium-235 decaying to lead-207 — so that the two ages can be checked against each other. U–Pb dating of zircon crystals established the age of the Earth and remains the gold standard for dating ancient rocks.

Method

When a mineral such as zircon crystallises from magma, it readily incorporates uranium but strongly rejects lead, so nearly all lead found in the crystal later is radiogenic. Measuring the ratios of parent to daughter isotopes gives two ages; if the system has remained closed, the ages agree ("concordant"). Plotting the two ratios on a concordia diagram, developed by George Wetherill in 1956, lets analysts detect and correct for episodes of lead loss, whose lower intercept can itself date a metamorphic event.

Applications

Clair Cameron Patterson used U–Pb dating of meteorites in 1956 to calculate the age of the Earth as 4.55 billion years. The method has since dated the oldest known terrestrial materials — Jack Hills zircons from Australia at about 4.4 billion years — as well as lunar samples, the timing of mass extinctions via dated volcanic ash beds, and the calibration of the geological time scale.

Techniques

Modern U–Pb analysis uses thermal ionisation mass spectrometry (TIMS, including chemical-abrasion CA-ID-TIMS) for the highest precision, and ion microprobe (SHRIMP) or laser-ablation ICP-MS for rapid in-situ dating of individual crystal zones.
