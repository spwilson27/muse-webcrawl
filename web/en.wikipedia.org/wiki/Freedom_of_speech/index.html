Freedom of speech

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Freedom of speech is a principle that supports the freedom of an individual or a community to articulate their opinions and ideas without fear of retaliation, censorship or legal sanction. Recognised in Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, and constitutionally protected in most democracies, it is closely allied to freedom of expression, of the press, of thought and of information.

Justifications

Classic defences include Milton's and John Stuart Mill's "marketplace of ideas" argument (truth emerges from open contest; silencing an opinion robs humanity whether the opinion is true or false), the democratic argument (citizens cannot govern themselves without free political communication), and autonomy arguments about self-expression. Mill's harm principle permits restriction only to prevent harm to others.

Limits

No system treats speech as absolute. Common limits include defamation, incitement to imminent violence (the US Brandenburg standard), obscenity, fraud, true threats, national-security secrets and — in many European and other systems, though not US law — hate speech and Holocaust denial. Prior restraint (pre-publication censorship) is especially suspect in US doctrine. Modern controversies concern social-media moderation by private platforms, misinformation, harassment campaigns, and whether concentrated private power over speech requires new regulatory approaches — debates in which the principle's application, rather than its value, is the main dispute.
