Crosley was an American manufacturer associated with entrepreneur Powel Crosley Jr. of Cincinnati, Ohio. The Crosley companies became famous in the 1920s for inexpensive mass-produced radios, operated the powerful WLW radio station and the Cincinnati Reds baseball team, pioneered refrigerator door shelves (the "Shelvador"), and from 1939 to 1952 built some of America's first compact and sports cars.

Radios and broadcasting

After entering the radio business in 1921 with the affordable Harko set, Crosley became one of the largest American radio manufacturers. His station WLW gained a clear-channel licence and in 1934 began broadcasting at an extraordinary 500,000 watts — the most powerful commercial station in the world — before regulators ordered a return to 50,000 watts.

Automobiles

Crosley Motors introduced a tiny two-cylinder car in 1939, selling for under $350. Postwar models used the innovative CoBra (copper-brazed sheet-steel) four-cylinder engine, later replaced by the cast-iron CIBA engine. Crosley pioneered several American firsts: the first mass-produced sports car (the 1949 Hotshot), disc brakes on a production car, and a popular compact station wagon. Despite these innovations, sales dwindled as buyers preferred larger cars, and production ended in July 1952; the assets were sold and the last designs influenced other makers.

Legacy

Crosley's name survives in Crosley Field, the Reds' home until 1970, and in modern licensing of the brand for retro-styled electronics. The little cars are now prized by collectors of microcars.
