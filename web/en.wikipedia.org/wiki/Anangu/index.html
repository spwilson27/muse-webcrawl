Aṉangu is the term by which several Aboriginal Australian peoples of the Western Desert cultural bloc in Central Australia refer to themselves — in the Pitjantjatjara and Yankunytjatjara languages it simply means "people" or "person". The Aṉangu include the Pitjantjatjara, Yankunytjatjara, Ngaanyatjarra, Luritja, and Antakarinja peoples, whose traditional lands span the tri-state region where the Northern Territory, South Australia, and Western Australia meet.

Country and culture

Aṉangu have inhabited the central desert for tens of thousands of years. Their law and spirituality, known as Tjukurpa (often rendered "the Dreaming"), governs relationships between people, land, plants, and animals, and is embodied in the features of the landscape. The most famous of these places are Uluṟu and Kata Tjuṯa, which lie within the Aṉangu Pitjantjatjara Yankunytjatjara Lands region's cultural sphere and Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa National Park. Aṉangu artists were central to the Western Desert art movement, which began at Papunya in 1971–72 and brought acrylic "dot painting" to international attention.

Land rights

On 26 October 1985 the Australian government formally handed title to Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa back to its Aṉangu traditional owners, who immediately leased the land back to Parks Australia for joint management — a landmark in the Aboriginal land rights movement. In South Australia, the Pitjantjatjara Land Rights Act 1981 granted freehold title over some 103,000 square kilometres. Aṉangu requested that visitors not climb Uluṟu for decades, and the climb was permanently closed on 26 October 2019, the 34th anniversary of the handback.

Society today

Many Aṉangu live in communities such as Mutitjulu (at the base of Uluṟu), Amata, Pukatja (Ernabella), and Kalka on the APY Lands. Traditional languages remain widely spoken — Pitjantjatjara is one of the strongest surviving Aboriginal languages — and Aṉangu ranger and art-centre programs combine cultural obligations with employment.
