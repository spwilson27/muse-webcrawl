Battle of Ayacucho

The Battle of Ayacucho (Spanish: Batalla de Ayacucho) was the decisive military confrontation of the Peruvian War of Independence, fought on 9 December 1824 on the high plain of Quinua near Ayacucho in the Peruvian Andes. The United Liberating Army of Peru, commanded by General Antonio José de Sucre, defeated the Royal Army of Peru under Viceroy José de la Serna. The resulting capitulation effectively ended Spanish rule in mainland South America, securing the independence of Peru and making possible the creation of Bolivia the following year. "Ayacucho" derives from Quechua words commonly rendered as "corner of the dead".

Background

After the patriot victory at Junín (6 August 1824), Bolívar left the army to Sucre and returned to Lima. The royalist army, still the largest Spanish force in South America, retreated into the southern highlands, shadowed by Sucre through difficult terrain in which both armies suffered from hunger and desertion — the royalists especially, after a failed attempt to cut off the patriots. In December the royalists resolved to give battle on ground of their choosing above the Quinua plain.

Battle

On the morning of 9 December, the royalists descended from the Condorcunca heights in four divisions. Fighting was close and hard: the patriot left under La Mar was initially driven back, but the divisions of Córdova — advancing with the famous order "¡Adelante! ¡Paso de vencedores!" ("Forward! At the pace of victors!") — and Miller's cavalry broke the royalist centre and right. Viceroy La Serna was wounded and captured, effectively decapitating the royalist command. By early afternoon the royalist army was destroyed as a fighting force, and Canterac negotiated the Capitulation of Ayacucho, surrendering the remaining royalist forces in Peru. Sucre's magnanimous terms allowed royalist officers to return to Spain.

Consequences

Ayacucho ended the Spanish American wars of independence on the continent: the last royalist stronghold, the port of Callao, held out until January 1826, and Upper Peru became the independent republic of Bolivia in 1825, named for Bolívar, with Sucre as its first president. The battle is celebrated annually in Peru, and the battlefield is a national historic sanctuary.
