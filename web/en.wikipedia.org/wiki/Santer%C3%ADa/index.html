Santería

Santería, also known as Regla de Ocha or La Regla de Ifá, is an Afro-Cuban religion that developed in Cuba during the Spanish colonial period. It blends elements of Yoruba religion, brought by enslaved West Africans, with Roman Catholicism and some Spiritist influences.

Beliefs

Practitioners, called santeros, venerate a supreme creator, Olodumare, and a pantheon of deities known as orishas, each associated with forces of nature and aspects of human life. Under colonial rule, orishas were identified with Catholic saints — for example Changó with Saint Barbara and Yemayá with the Virgin of Regla — allowing worship to continue in concealed form.

Practice

Religious life centres on initiation, divination (including the Ifá system and diloggun cowrie-shell reading), offerings, drumming and dance ceremonies, and in some rites animal sacrifice intended to maintain reciprocal relations with the orishas. Knowledge is transmitted orally through priestly lineages, and ceremonies use the Lucumí ritual language derived from Yoruba.

Spread

Following the Cuban Revolution of 1959, emigration carried Santería to the United States, Puerto Rico, Venezuela, and elsewhere. In the 1993 case Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah, the U.S. Supreme Court protected its ritual animal sacrifice under the Free Exercise Clause.
