Thyme is the common name for several species of aromatic perennial herbs of the genus Thymus in the mint family, Lamiaceae, native chiefly to the Mediterranean region and neighbouring parts of Europe, North Africa and Asia. The best known is common or garden thyme, Thymus vulgaris, grown worldwide as a culinary herb and as the source of the essential oil rich in thymol. Thyme plants are small, wiry shrubs with tiny evergreen leaves and clusters of pink, lilac or white flowers beloved of bees.

History

Thyme has been used since antiquity. The ancient Egyptians used it in embalming; the Greeks burned it as incense in temples and added it to bathwater, believing it a source of courage — the name derives from the Greek thymos, spirit or courage. Roman soldiers exchanged sprigs of thyme as a sign of respect, and Roman cooking and medicine spread the herb across Europe. In the Middle Ages thyme was placed beneath pillows to aid sleep and ward off nightmares, and ladies embroidered sprigs of thyme on the scarves of knights.

Culinary use

With a subtle, dry, slightly minty flavour that holds up to long cooking, thyme is a staple of French cuisine — a component of the bouquet garni and of herbes de Provence — and of Mediterranean and Middle Eastern cooking, where wild thyme is gathered for the spice blend za'atar. It seasons soups, stews, roasted meats, beans and tomato sauces. Related cultivated kinds include lemon thyme (T. × citriodorus), prized for its citrus scent, and caraway thyme.

Thymol and medicine

The essential oil of thyme contains thymol, a powerful antiseptic that is the active ingredient in many mouthwashes and was used as a disinfectant long before synthetic alternatives. In herbal medicine, thyme preparations have traditionally been given for coughs and bronchitis; thyme extracts remain licensed in some countries as expectorants.
