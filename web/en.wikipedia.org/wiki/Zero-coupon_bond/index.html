Zero-coupon bond

A zero-coupon bond (also called a discount bond or deep discount bond) is a bond in which the face value is repaid at maturity and no periodic interest ("coupon") payments are made. Instead, the bond is issued at a price below its face value, and the investor's return is the difference between the purchase price and the redemption value.

Pricing

The price of a zero-coupon bond is the present value of its face value: P = F / (1 + r)T, where F is face value, r is the yield per period and T is the number of periods to maturity. Because all return is concentrated at maturity, zero-coupon bonds have the longest duration for a given maturity and are therefore especially sensitive to changes in interest rates.

Examples

United States Treasury bills are short-term zero-coupon instruments. Longer-term zero-coupon Treasuries are created when dealers "strip" the coupons from Treasury notes and bonds and sell principal and interest payments separately; the US Treasury's STRIPS programme (Separate Trading of Registered Interest and Principal of Securities), begun in 1985, formalised this market. Corporations and municipalities also issue zero-coupon bonds, and zero-coupon structures appear in some savings bonds.

Tax treatment

In the United States, holders of taxable zero-coupon bonds must generally pay income tax each year on the interest that accrues ("imputed" or "phantom" interest), even though no cash is received until maturity. This makes tax-exempt or tax-deferred accounts common homes for such bonds.

Uses

Because they guarantee a known sum at a known date with no reinvestment risk, zero-coupon bonds are popular for matching future liabilities, such as funding a child's education or a pension payment, and they play a central role in constructing the yield curve in fixed-income analysis.
