The Endangered Species Act of 1973 (ESA) is the primary law in the United States for protecting imperiled species. Signed by President Richard Nixon on 28 December 1973, it provides a framework to conserve and protect endangered and threatened species and their habitats, and passed Congress with overwhelming bipartisan support.

Provisions

Species may be listed as endangered (in danger of extinction) or threatened (likely to become endangered) based on the best available science, without regard to economic impact. Federal agencies must consult with the Fish and Wildlife Service or the National Marine Fisheries Service to ensure their actions do not jeopardise listed species or destroy designated critical habitat (Section 7). Section 9 prohibits the "take" — harming, harassing or killing — of endangered animals. The Supreme Court affirmed the Act's sweeping force in Tennessee Valley Authority v. Hill (1978), halting the nearly complete Tellico Dam over the snail darter.

Recoveries and debate

Species credited with recovery under the ESA include the bald eagle, the American alligator, the grey wolf, the peregrine falcon and the humpback whale; supporters note that the great majority of listed species have avoided extinction. Critics argue that recovery and delisting are slow, that the Act burdens landowners and development, and that its provisions invite litigation. Amendments in 1978, 1982 and 1988 added mechanisms such as incidental take permits and habitat conservation plans.
