Noether's theorem
FieldTheoretical physics, calculus of variations
Proved byEmmy Noether, 1915; published 1918
StatementEvery differentiable symmetry of the action corresponds to a conservation law

Noether's theorem (or Noether's first theorem) states that every differentiable symmetry of the action of a physical system with conservative forces has a corresponding conservation law. It was proved by the mathematician Emmy Noether in 1915 and published in 1918.

Statement and examples

The theorem connects symmetries and conserved quantities: invariance of the action under translations in time implies conservation of energy; invariance under translations in space implies conservation of linear momentum; invariance under rotations implies conservation of angular momentum; and gauge invariance in electromagnetism corresponds to conservation of electric charge.

Significance

Noether's theorem became a cornerstone of modern theoretical physics and the calculus of variations, underpinning the standard model of particle physics, where symmetries organise the fundamental interactions. It is widely regarded as one of the most important mathematical results guiding the development of physics, and a landmark achievement of Noether, who overcame significant institutional barriers facing women in academia.

Extensions

Noether's second theorem concerns infinite-dimensional (gauge) symmetries and yields identities among the equations of motion, playing a central role in general relativity and quantum field theory.
