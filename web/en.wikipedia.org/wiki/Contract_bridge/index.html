Contract bridge

Bridge is played by four players in two partnerships.

Contract bridge, or simply bridge, is a trick-taking card game using a standard 52-card deck. It is played by two competing partnerships of two players each, the partners sitting opposite one another around a table. Millions of people play bridge worldwide in clubs, tournaments, online and with friends, making it one of the world's most popular card games. The World Bridge Federation is the governing body for international competitive bridge.

Play

Each deal begins with an auction in which the four players bid to name the contract — the number of tricks their partnership undertakes to win, and the trump suit (or no trumps). The partnership winning the auction must then fulfil its contract in the play of the cards: thirteen tricks are played, with the declarer's partner becoming the dummy, whose cards are laid face up on the table and played by declarer. Points are scored for tricks bid and made, with bonuses for game and slam contracts; failure to make the contract gives penalty points to the defenders.

History

Bridge descends from whist and from earlier games such as biritch ("Russian whist"), documented in the 19th century. Auction bridge became popular in the early 1900s, and contract bridge was devised in the 1920s, principally by Harold Stirling Vanderbilt, whose revised scoring system was adopted with great success. In the 1930s Ely Culbertson popularised the game in the United States through books, broadcasts and celebrated challenge matches. Duplicate bridge, in which the same deals are played at many tables so that results can be compared, became the standard form of competitive play.

Bidding systems

Partnerships agree bidding systems and conventions — such as Standard American, Acol, and conventions like Stayman and Blackwood — through which bids convey information about hand strength and distribution as well as proposing contracts. Bridge is recognised as a mind sport, and its bidding and play involve elements of probability, inference, memory and partnership psychology.
