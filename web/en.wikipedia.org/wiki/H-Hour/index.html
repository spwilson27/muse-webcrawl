H-Hour

H-Hour

TypeMilitary designation of time
Related termD-Day

H-Hour is the military designation for the specific time at which an operation or exercise is to begin. It is used together with D-Day, which designates the day on which the operation begins. The letters are derived from the words "day" and "hour" themselves; the terms were deliberately chosen to be neutral so that actual dates and times could be substituted in plans without revealing them if a document fell into enemy hands.

Usage

In planning documents, times before and after the start of an operation are expressed relative to H-Hour. For example, "H−3" denotes three hours before the operation begins, and "H+90" denotes ninety minutes after it. The same convention is applied to D-Day: "D−1" is the day before the operation, and "D+7" is one week after. This relative notation allows schedules to be shifted as a whole when the start time changes.

History

The terms D-Day and H-Hour first came into widespread use during the First World War. The earliest recorded use of D-Day by the United States Army dates to September 1918, in orders for the Battle of Saint-Mihiel. The most famous H-Hour is that of Operation Overlord, the Allied invasion of Normandy on 6 June 1944, when the first seaborne troops landed on the Normandy beaches at 06:30 British Double Summer Time. In practice, H-Hour varied between beaches and between the seaborne and airborne assaults.

Other lettered times

Related designations used in Allied planning include M-Day, the day on which mobilization begins; N-Day, the day a unit is notified of deployment; and S-Day, the day a deployment begins. The generic forms "X-Day" and "Y-Day" have also been used as placeholders in exercises.
