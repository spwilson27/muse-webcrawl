Rime ice

Rime ice
FormationFreezing of supercooled water droplets on contact with a surface
TypesSoft rime, hard rime, clear ice (glaze)
AppearanceWhite or milky, granular, opaque
Typical conditionsFreezing fog, temperatures roughly −2 to −8 °C (soft rime)

Rime ice is a white ice that forms when supercooled water droplets in fog or cloud freeze upon impact with a surface whose temperature is below freezing. Because the tiny droplets freeze nearly instantly, air is trapped between them, giving rime its characteristic milky, granular, opaque appearance. It grows into the wind, coating trees, antennas, aircraft wings, and mountain structures with feathery or needle-like deposits.

Types

Soft rime forms in light winds at temperatures around −2 to −8 °C; it is fragile and feathery, resembling hoar frost but originating from fog droplets rather than water vapour. Hard rime forms at lower temperatures and stronger winds, producing a denser, comb-like deposit that adheres firmly and is difficult to remove. When droplets are larger and freeze more slowly — as in freezing rain — the result is instead clear, dense glaze ice. Rime differs from hoar frost, which deposits directly from vapour onto cold surfaces on clear, calm nights.

Effects

Heavy rime accretion can break tree branches and add destructive weight to power lines, towers, and wind turbines. On aircraft, rime ice roughens wing surfaces and degrades lift, though it is generally less dangerous and easier to shed than clear ice. On exposed summits such as Mount Washington in New Hampshire, rime coats buildings and instruments in thick white layers and is a celebrated feature of the winter landscape.
