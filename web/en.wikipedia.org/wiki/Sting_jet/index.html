Sting jet

A sting jet is a meteorological phenomenon, a mesoscale airstream within certain rapidly deepening extratropical cyclones that can produce some of the strongest and most damaging surface winds associated with such storms. The name derives from the hooked, descending cloud feature—the "sting at the end of the tail"—that wraps around the southern side of the cyclone centre in satellite imagery.

Mechanism

Sting jets form in the frontal-fracture region of a Shapiro–Keyser cyclone. Air parcels descending from the tip of the cloud head accelerate as they descend, aided by evaporative cooling and the release of conditional symmetric instability, reaching the top of the boundary layer with wind speeds that can exceed 40 m/s (around 90 mph) over a region only tens of kilometres wide.

Notable events

The concept was developed at the University of Reading following analysis of the Great Storm of 1987, which caused exceptional damage in southern England. Sting jets have since been implicated in the Burns' Day storm of 1990, Cyclone Gudrun (2005) and Storm St Jude (2013).
