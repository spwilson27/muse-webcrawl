Rail profile

The rail profile is the cross-sectional shape of a railway rail, perpendicular to its length. The profile determines how the rail carries wheel loads, how it is supported and fastened, and how it wears. Modern rails are rolled steel sections designed as I-beams optimised for the wheel–rail contact.

Main types

Flat-bottom rail (also called Vignoles rail) has a wide flat foot that is fastened directly to sleepers with spikes, screws or clips; it is the dominant profile worldwide. Bullhead rail, long used in Britain, has a roughly symmetrical double-headed section supported in cast-iron chairs; it was originally intended to be turned over when the top wore, though in practice corrosion of the lower surface defeated this. Grooved rail incorporates a groove beside the running head so that trams can run in paved streets flush with the road surface. Early railways also used bar rails, T-rails and bridge rails.

Dimensions and standards

Rails are specified by mass per unit length — for example UIC 60 (60 kg/m) in Europe or 115RE–132RE (pounds per yard) in North America. Heavier profiles carry higher axle loads and are ground or milled periodically to maintain the profile of the running head, controlling fatigue defects and noise.

Wheel–rail interface

The shape of the rail head works together with the coned profile of the wheel tread; small changes in profile strongly affect steering, wear and the risk of rolling contact fatigue.
