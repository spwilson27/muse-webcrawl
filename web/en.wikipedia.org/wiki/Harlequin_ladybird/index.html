The harlequin ladybird (Harmonia axyridis), also known as the Asian lady beetle or multicoloured Asian ladybird, is a large ladybird beetle native to eastern Asia. Introduced to North America and Europe as a biological control agent against aphids, it has become one of the world's most invasive insects, outcompeting native ladybirds throughout much of its introduced range.

Description

Adults are 5.5–8.5 mm long, domed and oval, and extraordinarily variable: colour forms range from yellow-orange with few or no spots to black with red spots, with up to 22 spots in common forms; a white or cream pronotum with a black M- or W-shaped central mark is characteristic. Larvae are spiny, black with orange markings. When threatened the beetles exude bitter, alkaloid-laden haemolymph ("reflex bleeding") that stains surfaces and taints wine when beetles are harvested with grapes.

Introduction and spread

The species was repeatedly released in the United States from 1916 onward; established populations appeared in Louisiana in 1988, and it spread across North America within a decade. It arrived in the United Kingdom in 2004 and spread faster than any other invading ladybird, reaching most of Europe. Harlequins overwinter in aggregations, often swarming into buildings in autumn, making them a household nuisance.

Ecological impact

Harmonia axyridis is a voracious generalist predator of aphids and other soft-bodied insects, but it also eats the eggs and larvae of other ladybirds and preys on butterfly eggs, and it competes with native species for food. Declines of native species such as the two-spot ladybird in Britain and several North American Coccinella species correlate closely with its arrival. It carries a microsporidian parasite to which it is tolerant but which can be lethal to native ladybirds that eat harlequin eggs — a mechanism termed "bioweapon" transmission.
