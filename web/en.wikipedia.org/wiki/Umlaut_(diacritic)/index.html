Umlaut (diacritic)

The umlaut is a diacritical mark consisting of two dots (¨) placed over a vowel. In German orthography it marks the fronted vowels ä, ö and ü, which arose through the sound change also called umlaut. The mark itself developed from a small superscript e written above the vowel, which over time was reduced to two strokes and then to two dots.

History

In medieval German manuscripts the sound change that fronted back vowels before a following i or j was at first unmarked; later scribes wrote a small e over the letter. In cursive hands this e became two vertical strokes, and in printed type two dots. Similar two-dot marks in other languages have different functions and origins: in French and English it may be a diaeresis (separating vowels in hiatus), and in languages such as Hungarian, Finnish, Estonian, Turkish and Swedish the dotted vowels are separate letters of the alphabet rather than modified forms of a, o and u.

Usage

In German, Ä/ä, Ö/ö and Ü/ü represent /ɛ/, /ø/ and /y/ (approximately). Alphabetisation treats them either as equal to the base letters or as the digraphs ae, oe, ue; the digraph spellings are also used in names and in contexts where the dots are unavailable, and in Swiss Standard German ß-related conventions differ. In transliteration of German names the umlauted vowels are commonly written with a following e (e.g. Goethe uses oe for ö historically).

In computing and popular culture

In Unicode the precomposed characters ä, ö and ü have their own code points, and the combining diaeresis U+0308 can follow any base letter. A stylistic use of umlaut dots on letters where they serve no linguistic purpose, popularised by heavy metal bands such as Motörhead and Mötley Crüe, is known as the "metal umlaut" or "röck döts".
