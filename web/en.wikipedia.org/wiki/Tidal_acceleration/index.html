Tidal acceleration

Tidal acceleration is an effect of the tidal forces between an orbiting natural satellite (such as the Moon) and the primary planet it orbits (such as Earth). The acceleration causes a gradual recession of the satellite from the primary and a corresponding slowdown of the primary's rotation. The process eventually leads to tidal locking, first of the smaller body and later, in principle, of the larger one.

Tidal bulges carried ahead of the Moon transfer angular momentum to its orbit

Mechanism

The Moon's gravity raises tidal bulges in Earth's oceans. Because Earth rotates faster than the Moon orbits, friction drags the bulges slightly ahead of the Earth–Moon line. The nearer bulge pulls the Moon forward along its orbit, increasing its orbital energy and angular momentum, so the Moon recedes. The reaction torque on Earth opposes its rotation, so the day lengthens. Lunar laser ranging from retroreflectors left by the Apollo missions measures the Moon's recession at about 3.82 centimetres per year.

Historical and geological evidence

Ancient eclipse records show that the length of day has increased over the past 2,700 years at an average rate slightly lower than the tidal prediction, the difference being attributed partly to post-glacial rebound changing Earth's oblateness. Fossil corals and tidal rhythmites record daily growth bands and tidal cycles: counts indicate that about 620 million years ago the day was roughly 22 hours long, and in the Devonian, about 400 million years ago, the year contained about 400 days.

Effects and future

The lengthening day affects timekeeping: leap seconds are inserted into Coordinated Universal Time partly because Earth's rotation is irregular and slowing. In the remote future, if the Earth–Moon system survived undisturbed, Earth would become tidally locked to the Moon, with a day and month of about 47 present days; however, the Sun's evolution into a red giant is expected to disrupt the system long before that state is reached. The same process operates elsewhere: most moons of the Solar System are already tidally locked to their planets, and Phobos is spiralling inward toward Mars under the corresponding tidal deceleration because it orbits faster than Mars rotates.
