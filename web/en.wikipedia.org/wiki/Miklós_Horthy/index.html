Miklós Horthy de Nagybánya (18 June 1868 – 9 February 1957) was a Hungarian admiral and statesman who served as the regent of the Kingdom of Hungary from 1920 until his deposition in 1944. An Austro-Hungarian naval officer who rose to become commander-in-chief of the fleet in the final year of the First World War, Horthy led the counter-revolutionary National Army against the Hungarian Soviet Republic in 1919 and was elected regent by the National Assembly in March 1920, ruling a kingdom without a king for nearly a quarter of a century.

Early life and naval career

Born into the landed gentry at Kenderes, Horthy entered the Austro-Hungarian naval academy at Fiume at fourteen and served as an aide-de-camp to Emperor Franz Joseph I. In the First World War he won distinction at the Battle of the Otranto Barrage (1917) and was appointed the fleet's last commander-in-chief in February 1918, being promoted to vice admiral.

Regency

Horthy's interwar regime was conservative and authoritarian, dominated by the goal of revising the Treaty of Trianon, which had stripped Hungary of two-thirds of its territory. Under prime ministers such as István Bethlen and Gyula Gömbös, Hungary aligned increasingly with Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany, regaining territory through the Vienna Awards of 1938 and 1940 and joining the Axis invasion of Yugoslavia and the Soviet Union in 1941.

Although Horthy's government passed anti-Jewish laws and Hungarian troops committed atrocities, Horthy resisted German demands for the deportation of Hungarian Jews for much of the war; after Germany occupied Hungary in March 1944, however, more than 400,000 Jews were deported, most to Auschwitz, with the cooperation of Hungarian authorities. In October 1944 Horthy announced an armistice with the Soviet Union; German forces responded with Operation Panzerfaust, forcing him to resign and install the Arrow Cross government of Ferenc Szálasi. Horthy spent the rest of the war in German custody.

Later life and legacy

After the war Horthy lived in exile in Estoril, Portugal, where he wrote his memoirs and died in 1957. He was never tried as a war criminal. His legacy remains deeply contested in Hungary: admirers see a patriot who preserved Hungarian sovereignty between two totalitarian powers, while critics stress his authoritarian rule, alliance with Hitler and responsibility for the Holocaust in Hungary. His ceremonial reburial at Kenderes in 1993 drew both official honours and protest.
