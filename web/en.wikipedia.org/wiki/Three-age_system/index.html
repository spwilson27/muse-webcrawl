Three-age system

The three-age system is the periodization of human prehistory into three consecutive ages based on the predominant tool-making material of each: the Stone Age, the Bronze Age and the Iron Age. The system was proposed by the Danish antiquarian Christian Jürgensen Thomsen in 1836 to organise the collections of the National Museum of Denmark, and it remains a basic framework of Old World archaeology.

Origins

Ideas of a sequence of ages of stone, bronze and iron appear in ancient authors such as Lucretius and in early antiquarian writing. Working as curator of the Danish National Museum, Thomsen arranged artefacts by material and showed, using closed finds and stratigraphy, that the sequence was chronological rather than merely typological. His guidebook Ledetraad til Nordisk Oldkyndighed (1836) set out the system, which his successor Jens Jacob Asmussen Worsaae confirmed through excavation.

Subdivisions

The Stone Age was later divided into Palaeolithic (Old Stone Age), Mesolithic and Neolithic; an intermediate Chalcolithic (Copper Age) is sometimes inserted between the Neolithic and the Bronze Age. John Lubbock popularised the Palaeolithic/Neolithic division in Pre-historic Times (1865).

Application and limits

The system works best in Europe, the Near East and parts of Asia, where the stone–bronze–iron sequence broadly holds, though the ages begin at very different dates in different regions. It fits sub-Saharan Africa, the Americas and Oceania poorly — many societies moved directly from stone to iron, or never used bronze — and modern archaeology generally prefers absolute dating by radiocarbon and other scientific methods, retaining the three ages as convenient labels rather than universal stages.
