Bay

A bay is a recessed, coastal body of water that directly connects to a larger main body of water, such as an ocean, a lake, or another bay. A large bay is usually called a gulf, sea, sound, or bight. Bays form in several ways: by the erosion of softer rock bands between harder headlands, by the drowning of river valleys as sea level rises, and by glacial action. Because their waters are partly sheltered from the open sea by surrounding land, bays have historically been favoured sites for ports, harbours and coastal settlements.

Formation

Where a coastline alternates between bands of hard and soft rock running at right angles to the shore, waves erode the soft rock faster, cutting inlets that widen into bays behind resistant headlands; the coast of Dorset in England, with Lulworth Cove and Swanage Bay, is a textbook example. Rising post-glacial sea levels drowned river mouths to form rias and broad shallow bays such as Chesapeake Bay in the United States, the largest estuary in that country. Glacial bays and fjords occur in formerly glaciated regions.

Definition in international law

The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (Article 10) gives "bay" a precise legal meaning: a well-marked indentation whose penetration is in such proportion to the width of its mouth as to contain land-locked waters, and whose area is at least that of a semicircle drawn on the mouth. Juridical bays meeting specified criteria (including a mouth no wider than 24 nautical miles) may be enclosed by a closing line, inside which the waters are internal waters of the coastal state. "Historic bays," such as Canada's Hudson Bay, are treated as exceptions.

Notable bays

Among the largest bays in the world is the Bay of Bengal, between India, Bangladesh, Myanmar and Sri Lanka; others of note include San Francisco Bay, the Bay of Fundy (famed for the world's greatest tidal range), Guanabara Bay at Rio de Janeiro, and Tokyo Bay.
