Slang
Slang expressions change rapidly and mark group identity

Slang is vocabulary (words and phrases) that is informal in register, specific to a particular in-group, and often fleeting, changing quickly within and between social subcultures. Slang terms are often metaphors or metonyms, and are primarily used in informal speech, though they also appear in informal writing. The origin of the word "slang" is uncertain; it was first attested in print around 1756 with the meaning "special vocabulary of tramps or thieves", and by the early 19th century had broadened to "language of a low and vulgar type".

Distinguishing slang

Linguists distinguish slang from neighbouring concepts. Unlike jargon, which is the technical vocabulary of a profession or activity, slang is chosen as much for social effect as for precision: it signals membership, youth, irreverence, or familiarity. Unlike a dialect, slang is principally a matter of vocabulary rather than grammar or pronunciation. Slang also differs from colloquialism in being more deliberately informal, playful, or transgressive.

Formation and spread

New slang arises through coinage, borrowing from other languages and subcultures, semantic shift (an old word gaining a new meaning), shortening, blending, and compounding. Historically, soldiers, sailors, criminals, jazz musicians, and teenagers were prolific sources. In the internet era, terms coined in online communities — for example in African-American Vernacular English or in gaming and social-media culture — can spread worldwide within weeks, and the lifespan of many expressions has shortened accordingly.

Social functions

Slang serves to establish and reinforce social identity, mark in-group belonging, exclude outsiders, express informality and intimacy, and inject novelty, humour, or mild shock into conversation. Because authorities and outsiders often view slang as low or improper, using it can also be an act of resistance or anti-establishment signalling.

Lexicography

The study of slang has a long lexicographical tradition, from Francis Grose's Classical Dictionary of the Vulgar Tongue (1785) through Eric Partridge's dictionaries to Jonathon Green's Green's Dictionary of Slang. Modern crowd-sourced resources such as Urban Dictionary document contemporary coinages, though without editorial verification.
