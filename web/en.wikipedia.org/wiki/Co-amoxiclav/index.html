Co-amoxiclav, also known as amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, is a combination antibiotic medication used to treat a number of bacterial infections. It consists of amoxicillin, a penicillin-family antibiotic, combined with potassium clavulanate, a β-lactamase inhibitor. It is sold under trade names including Augmentin.

Medical uses

Co-amoxiclav is used for infections including middle ear infections (otitis media), strep throat, pneumonia, cellulitis, urinary tract infections, sinusitis, and animal bites. The addition of clavulanic acid extends amoxicillin's spectrum to cover many bacteria that would otherwise destroy the amoxicillin molecule using β-lactamase enzymes. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Mechanism

Amoxicillin inhibits the synthesis of bacterial cell walls, causing bacterial death. Clavulanic acid has little antibacterial activity of its own but irreversibly binds β-lactamase enzymes secreted by resistant bacteria, protecting the amoxicillin from degradation.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, and candidiasis (thrush). More serious but less common reactions include allergic reactions and anaphylaxis in people with penicillin allergy, and cholestatic hepatitis — co-amoxiclav is one of the most common causes of drug-induced liver injury. It is generally considered acceptable for use during pregnancy.

History

The combination was developed by scientists at the British pharmaceutical company Beecham (later part of GlaxoSmithKline) in the 1970s, following the discovery of clavulanic acid from the bacterium Streptomyces clavuligerus. It was launched under the brand name Augmentin in 1981 and became one of the most widely prescribed antibiotics in the world.
