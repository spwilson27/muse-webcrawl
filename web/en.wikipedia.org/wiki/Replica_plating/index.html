Replica plating

Replica plating is a microbiological technique in which one or more secondary Petri plates containing different selective growth media are inoculated with the same pattern of colonies as a primary plate. Because the colonies on each plate grow in corresponding positions, researchers can compare the growth of each colony under different conditions and identify the original colony with a desired phenotype.

Method

A master plate bearing separated colonies is pressed onto a sterile velvet cloth stretched over a cylindrical block, transferring cells from every colony to the velvet in the same spatial arrangement. Fresh plates are then pressed in turn onto the velvet, picking up the cells. By using, for example, a complete medium and a medium lacking a particular nutrient, colonies that fail to grow on the second plate can be identified as auxotrophs and recovered from the master plate.

History

The technique was developed by Joshua Lederberg and Esther Lederberg in 1952. In a celebrated experiment, they used replica plating to show that antibiotic-resistant bacteria arise by spontaneous mutation before exposure to the antibiotic, rather than as an induced adaptation: resistant colonies could be recovered from the master plate at positions corresponding to growth on antibiotic plates, even though those original colonies had never encountered the drug.

Uses

Replica plating has been used to isolate nutritional mutants, to screen for antibiotic resistance, and in early work on bacterial conjugation and gene mapping. Modern high-throughput versions use robotic pinning tools and multi-well plates to replicate thousands of yeast or bacterial strains onto many test media.
