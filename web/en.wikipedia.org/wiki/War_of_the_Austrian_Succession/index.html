War of the Austrian Succession

The War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748) was a European conflict triggered by the death of Emperor Charles VI and the disputed accession of his daughter Maria Theresa to the Habsburg hereditary lands, despite the Pragmatic Sanction of 1713 by which European powers had agreed to accept her succession. Prussia under Frederick the Great seized Silesia, and the war widened into a global struggle involving France, Spain, Bavaria, and Saxony against Austria, Great Britain, the Dutch Republic, and (later) Russia, with fighting in Europe, North America (King George's War), the Caribbean, and India (the First Carnatic War). It ended with the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748), which confirmed Maria Theresa's succession but left Prussia in possession of Silesia.

Background

Charles VI spent his reign securing acceptance of the Pragmatic Sanction, which set aside Salic law to allow his daughter to inherit the Habsburg Monarchy. On his death in October 1740, however, Frederick II of Prussia demanded Silesia, invading in December without a declaration of war (First Silesian War). France, Bavaria, Spain, and Saxony joined to partition the Habsburg lands; the Bavarian elector Charles Albert was elected Emperor Charles VII in 1742 — the first non-Habsburg emperor in three centuries.

Course of the war

Britain, bound to Austria by alliance and fighting France and Spain in an overlapping war (the War of Jenkins' Ear), provided subsidies and an army in the Austrian Netherlands and Germany, where George II personally led troops at Dettingen (1743) — the last time a British monarch commanded in battle. France's Marshal Maurice de Saxe won notable victories at Fontenoy (1745), Rocoux (1746), and Lauffeld (1747) and overran much of the Austrian Netherlands. In Italy, India, and North America the colonial dimensions of the war produced hard fighting but few durable gains: British colonial forces captured Louisbourg in 1745, to French fury, while the French took Madras in 1746.

The Second Silesian War (1744–45) ended with the Treaty of Dresden confirming Prussia's hold on Silesia. With France financially exhausted, Britain eager to settle, and a Russian army marching toward the Rhine, the belligerents concluded the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle in October 1748.

Outcome

The treaty restored most conquests (Louisbourg was exchanged for Madras) but recognised Prussia's annexation of Silesia and Maria Theresa's succession. Resentments left by the settlement — Austria's determination to recover Silesia, France's colonial losses of trust in diplomacy — led directly to the Diplomatic Revolution of 1756 and the Seven Years' War.
