Chinese name

Chinese names are personal names used by ethnic Chinese and others in China and the Chinese-speaking world. A full name consists of a family name (姓, xìng) followed by a given name (名, míng) — the reverse of the Western order. Most names total two or three Chinese characters: a one-character family name plus a one- or two-character given name, as in 毛澤東 (Mao Zedong), where Mao is the family name.

Family names

Chinese family names descend from ancient clan names (xìng) and lineage names (shì), which merged by the Han dynasty. Although thousands of surnames have existed, a relatively small set dominates: the "hundred family surnames" (Bǎijiāxìng), a Song-dynasty rhyming list, includes most names in use, and the hundred most common surnames cover the great majority of the population. Most surnames are a single character, though compound two-character surnames such as Ouyang and Sima survive. Traditionally, women keep their birth surname after marriage.

Given names

Given names are chosen for meaning, auspiciousness, sound, and sometimes the number of character strokes; they may reflect hopes for the child or commemorations of events. Many families use a generation name — one character of the given name shared by all siblings and cousins of a generation, often following a poem recorded in the family genealogy. In the 20th century, trend-driven names surged after major events, and single-character given names became more common.

Other names

Historically, educated men adopted a courtesy name (zì) at adulthood and sometimes an art name (hào); emperors are known by temple names and era names, and monks and rulers could receive posthumous names. Milk names were traditionally given to infants. In diaspora and international contexts, many Chinese people also adopt a Western given name, placed in Western order.
