Leap of faith

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In philosophy and theology, a leap of faith is the act of believing in or accepting something not on the basis of reason or evidence. The phrase is commonly associated with the Danish philosopher Søren Kierkegaard, although he himself did not use the exact term; it derives from the Latin saltus fidei and from Kierkegaard's discussion of a qualitative transition (a "leap", Danish Springet) by which an individual moves into religious faith.

Kierkegaard

In works such as Fear and Trembling (1843) and the Concluding Unscientific Postscript (1846), Kierkegaard argued that faith is not the conclusion of rational deliberation but a passionate commitment made in the face of objective uncertainty—indeed, of paradox, as in the belief that the eternal God became a human being in time. His example is Abraham, the "knight of faith", who obeys God's command to sacrifice Isaac while believing, "by virtue of the absurd", that he will nevertheless keep his son.

Kierkegaard described human existence as progressing through aesthetic and ethical stages of life; the movement into the religious stage cannot be accomplished by gradual mediation but only by a leap—a decisive, subjective appropriation of faith.

Later usage

The phrase has passed into common usage for any decision to trust or commit to something without guarantee of success: investing in an unproven venture, entering a relationship, or accepting a hypothesis. It is also used in discussions of fideism, the view that faith is independent of, or even opposed to, reason.
