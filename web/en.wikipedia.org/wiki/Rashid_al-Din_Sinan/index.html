Rashid al-Din Sinan (Arabic: رشيد الدين سنان, c. 1131/1135 – 1193), also known to the Crusaders as the Old Man of the Mountain, was the leader of the Syrian branch of the Nizari Ismaili state during the time of the Crusades. From his mountain fortresses, above all Masyaf, he directed the network of devotees known in the West as the Assassins, and negotiated — and fought — with both the Crusader states and Saladin.

Early life

Sinan was born in southern Iraq, in a village near Basra, into a Twelver Shi'a family. In his youth he converted to Nizari Ismailism and travelled to the order's headquarters at Alamut in Persia, where he was educated alongside the future imam Hasan II. Sent to Syria in the 1160s, he consolidated the scattered Nizari strongholds in the Jabal Bahra (the Syrian coastal mountains) and made Masyaf the centre of an effectively autonomous principality.

Rule in Syria

Sinan rebuilt and fortified castles including al-Kahf, al-Qadmus and Masyaf, and organised the fidai — devotees prepared to carry out missions, including assassinations, at the cost of their own lives. Under his direction the Syrian Nizaris murdered prominent figures such as Conrad of Montferrat, king-elect of Jerusalem, in 1192. Sinan twice sent fidais against Saladin, who survived both attempts and eventually retaliated by laying siege to Masyaf in 1176 before withdrawing; thereafter an uneasy accommodation held between the two powers.

Legend

Crusader and later European writers draped Sinan in legend: the title "Old Man of the Mountain" (from Shaykh al-Jabal), tales of a paradisiacal garden in which drugged followers were given a foretaste of heaven, and Marco Polo's famous account of the Assassins' training all derive from this period. Sinan's contemporary reputation for uncanny power also made him a semi-divine figure to some of his own followers; after his death in 1193 his tomb at Masyaf became a site of veneration.
