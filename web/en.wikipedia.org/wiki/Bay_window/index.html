Bay window

A bay window is a window space projecting outward from the main walls of a building, forming a bay in a room. Typically it consists of three windows: a large central fixed or opening window flanked by two angled side windows, though the bay may be polygonal, rectangular (a "box bay") or curved (a "bow window").

Characteristics

Bay windows increase the floor area and admit more light than a flat window, and allow views in three directions. They may rise through several storeys or be confined to the ground floor with their own small roof. Internally the recess is often fitted with a window seat.

History

Projecting windows appear in medieval architecture in the form of the oriel window, often used in chapels and great halls. The true bay window became a standard feature of English domestic architecture in the later nineteenth century after the repeal of the window tax and changes to building regulations permitted projections over public streets. It is characteristic of Victorian and Edwardian terraced and detached houses, of Queen Anne Revival and Arts and Crafts designs, and of Chicago and San Francisco residential buildings, where the "San Francisco bay window" is a noted local feature.

Related forms

A bow window curves gently rather than forming angles; an oriel window is a projecting bay supported on corbels or brackets that does not reach the ground. Bay windows are also found on railway carriages, trams and the cabs of locomotives to improve visibility.
