Removal of herniated disc fragments that compress a nerve root.

A discectomy is the surgical removal of part or all of an intervertebral disc that has herniated and is pressing on a nerve root or the spinal cord. It is most commonly performed in the lumbar spine for disc herniation causing sciatica, and less often in the cervical spine, where it is frequently combined with fusion (anterior cervical discectomy and fusion).

Indications

Surgery is generally considered when leg pain from a herniated disc persists despite at least six weeks of conservative treatment, when there is significant or progressive muscle weakness, or — as an emergency — in cauda equina syndrome. Trials such as the Spine Patient Outcomes Research Trial (SPORT) suggest surgery provides faster relief of leg pain than continued conservative care, though differences often narrow over the long term.

Procedure

In a lumbar microdiscectomy, the surgeon works through a small incision using an operating microscope, removes a small amount of bone (laminotomy) to reach the spinal canal, retracts the nerve root, and excises the herniated fragment of nucleus pulposus. Only the loose, protruding material is normally removed rather than the whole disc. Endoscopic techniques perform the same decompression through a tube of about a centimetre's diameter.

Outcomes and risks

Most patients experience rapid relief of leg pain. Risks include infection, dural tear with cerebrospinal fluid leak, nerve injury, recurrent herniation at the same level (on the order of 5–15%), and the possibility that back pain persists. Rehabilitation emphasises early mobilisation and graded return to activity.
