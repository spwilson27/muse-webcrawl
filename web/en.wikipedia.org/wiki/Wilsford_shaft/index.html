The Wilsford shaft is a deep Bronze Age shaft excavated at Wilsford in Wiltshire, England, near Stonehenge. About 30 metres (roughly 100 feet) deep and around 2 metres across, it was sunk into the chalk in the Middle Bronze Age and contained an unusual assemblage of deliberately deposited objects, leading archaeologists to interpret it as a ritual shaft or "well".

Excavation and finds

The shaft was investigated in the 1960s. Its fills produced pottery, animal bone, worked wood, a shale ring, amber beads and a wooden bucket or tankard — a remarkable piece of Bronze Age carpentry preserved by the anaerobic conditions at depth. Waterlogged deposits at the bottom preserved organic materials rarely surviving from the period.

Interpretation

Because the shaft produced no evidence of practical use as a well alone and contained structured deposits of valued objects, it is generally interpreted as serving a ceremonial purpose, possibly a votive shaft associated with the ritual landscape around Stonehenge and Normanton Down. Comparable ritual shafts are known elsewhere in Bronze Age Britain and continental Europe.
