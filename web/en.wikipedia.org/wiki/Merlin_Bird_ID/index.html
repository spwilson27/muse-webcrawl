Merlin Bird ID is a free mobile application developed by the Cornell Lab of Ornithology that helps users identify birds. Originally released in 2014 as a step-by-step identification wizard for birds of North America, it later added photo identification and, in 2021, real-time sound identification. Merlin draws on the eBird database of bird observations and the Macaulay Library's collection of photographs and sound recordings.

Features

Bird ID wizard

The original identification tool asks five questions about an observed bird — where and when it was seen, its size, its main colours, and what it was doing — and returns a shortlist of likely species, ranked using eBird data on which species occur at that location and season.

Photo ID

Users can upload or take a photograph and receive suggested identifications generated by a computer vision model trained on millions of images from the Macaulay Library and eBird contributors.

Sound ID

Added in 2021, Sound ID listens through the device's microphone and identifies singing and calling birds in real time, displaying each species as it vocalises. The feature is powered by machine learning models trained on Macaulay Library recordings converted into spectrograms. Sound ID has been widely credited with drawing new audiences into birdwatching and with functioning offline after regional bird packs are downloaded.

Bird packs and coverage

Merlin's content is organised into downloadable regional "bird packs" containing species accounts, photographs, and sounds. Coverage has expanded from North America to most of the world, with identification models supporting thousands of species. The app is closely integrated with eBird: identifications can be saved to a user's life list and confirmed sightings contribute to the eBird database used by researchers and conservationists.

Reception

Merlin has been downloaded tens of millions of times and is frequently cited in the press as one of the most successful citizen-science applications. Its sound identification feature received particular attention during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, when interest in local birdwatching surged. Researchers have also used Merlin's growing dataset to study bird distribution and behaviour, while noting that automated identifications should be treated with appropriate caution.
