Remote sensing in archaeology

MethodsAerial photography, LiDAR, satellite imagery, geophysics
PurposeNon-invasive site detection and mapping

Remote sensing in archaeology is the use of aerial, satellite, and geophysical techniques to identify and map archaeological features without excavation. Buried walls, ditches, and roads alter soil moisture, crop growth, and surface microtopography, producing patterns — cropmarks, soil marks, and shadow marks — visible from above.

History

Aerial archaeology began after the First World War, when pilots such as O. G. S. Crawford in Britain observed that archaeological sites showed up clearly in aerial photographs. Declassified CORONA spy-satellite imagery from the 1960s later became a major resource for landscapes that have since changed.

Techniques

Modern methods include drone photography, multispectral and thermal satellite imaging, ground-penetrating radar, magnetometry, and LiDAR, which can strip away forest canopy digitally. LiDAR surveys have revealed extensive Maya settlement landscapes in Central America and the full extent of the medieval city of Angkor in Cambodia.
