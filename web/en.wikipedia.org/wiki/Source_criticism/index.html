Source criticism

Source criticism (or information evaluation) is the process of evaluating the qualities of an information source, such as its validity, reliability and relevance to the subject under investigation. It is a core method of historical scholarship and is also taught in library and information science, journalism and media literacy education.

Historical method

Historians distinguish external criticism, which tests a document's authenticity (authorship, date, provenance, whether it has been altered), from internal criticism, which assesses the credibility and meaning of its contents: the author's competence and eyewitness status, their biases and purposes, the time elapsed between event and record, and consistency with other sources. These procedures were systematized in the 19th century by historians such as Leopold von Ranke and in manuals like Langlois and Seignobos's Introduction to the Study of History (1898).

Principles

Common criteria include the source's proximity to the event, the author's authority and tendency (bias), corroboration by independent sources, and the circumstances of the source's production and preservation. Modern guides, especially in Scandinavian education, often summarise the tests as authenticity, time, dependence and tendency.

Biblical and textual criticism

In biblical studies, "source criticism" has a narrower meaning: the analysis of the Pentateuch and other texts to identify the underlying written sources from which they were composed, as in the Documentary Hypothesis distinguishing J, E, D and P strands. As a historical-critical method it is practised alongside form criticism, redaction criticism and textual criticism.

Digital age

The abundance of online information has renewed interest in source criticism for evaluating websites and social media, including lateral reading — checking what other sources say about a site — a practice promoted by fact-checkers and media-literacy curricula.
