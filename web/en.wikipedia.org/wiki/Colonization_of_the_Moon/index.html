Colonization of the Moon

Colonization of the Moon
Concepts: Permanent lunar bases, in-situ resource utilisation
Candidate sites: Lunar south pole, lava tubes
Programmes: Artemis, International Lunar Research Station

The colonization of the Moon refers to proposals for establishing permanent human settlements on Earth's Moon. Such colonies would serve scientific research, resource extraction, and as a proving ground for deeper space exploration. No permanent settlement yet exists, although government programmes in the twenty-first century have revived plans for long-duration lunar bases.

Advantages

The Moon is only about three days' travel from Earth, allows near-real-time communication, and possesses resources including water ice in permanently shadowed polar craters, regolith for construction and oxygen production, and near-constant sunlight at some polar peaks for solar power. Lunar materials could supply propellant and building material without the cost of launching them from Earth.

Challenges

Settlers would face radiation from cosmic rays and solar particle events, abrasive lunar dust, extreme temperature swings, micrometeorites, and the physiological effects of one-sixth gravity. Shielding with regolith, habitats in lava tubes, and closed-loop life support are among the proposed mitigations. The high cost of transport remains a central obstacle.

Plans

NASA's Artemis programme aims to establish a sustainable lunar presence, including the planned Artemis Base Camp near the south pole, while China and Russia have proposed an International Lunar Research Station. Commercial companies have also outlined private lunar facilities. Under the Outer Space Treaty, no state may claim sovereignty over the Moon, and the Artemis Accords set out principles for activity there.
