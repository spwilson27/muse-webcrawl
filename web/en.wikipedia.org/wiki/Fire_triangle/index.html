Fire triangle

ConceptCombustion model
ElementsHeat, fuel, oxygen
Extended modelFire tetrahedron (adds chemical chain reaction)
Used inFire safety, firefighting training

The fire triangle (also called the combustion triangle) is a simple model for understanding the ingredients necessary for most fires. It illustrates the three elements a fire needs to ignite and sustain itself: heat, fuel, and an oxidizing agent (usually oxygen). A fire naturally occurs when these elements are present and combined in the right mixture.

The three elements

Heat raises the temperature of the fuel to its ignition point. Sources of heat include flames, sparks, friction, electrical energy and focused sunlight. Fuel is any combustible material — wood, paper, petrol, natural gas, cloth and many other solids, liquids and gases. Oxygen supports the chemical processes of combustion; ambient air contains about 21% oxygen, and most fires need an atmosphere of at least about 16% oxygen to burn.

Fire suppression

The fire triangle explains the logic of firefighting: removing any one side of the triangle extinguishes the fire. Water primarily removes heat by cooling the fuel below its ignition temperature. Fire blankets and carbon dioxide extinguishers work by displacing or excluding oxygen, smothering the fire. Removing fuel — for example by creating a firebreak in a forest, or shutting off a gas supply — starves the fire. Dry chemical extinguishers act largely by interrupting the chemical chain reaction.

Fire tetrahedron

A more complete model, the fire tetrahedron, adds a fourth element: the self-sustaining chemical chain reaction of combustion. In this model, the heat produced by the exothermic reaction feeds back to sustain further burning. Halon and dry-powder extinguishers extinguish fires mainly by breaking this chain reaction, a mechanism the simple triangle does not capture.
