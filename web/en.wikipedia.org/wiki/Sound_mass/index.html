Sound mass

Sound mass (also sound-mass composition) is a musical texture in which many individual sounds are combined so that they are perceived as a single collective entity rather than as separate notes. In sound-mass music, the traditional elements of melody and harmony are subordinated to texture, timbre, dynamics, and the statistical distribution of pitches within a dense band or cluster.

Characteristics

Sound-mass works typically employ tone clusters, glissandi, microtones, and extended instrumental techniques. Individual instrumental parts may be extremely complex, yet the perceptual result is a slowly evolving mass of sound characterized by its density, register, and rate of internal change. Composers often specify overall statistical properties — for example, that all pitch classes in a certain band should be present — rather than controlling every melodic line.

History

Early precedents include Charles Ives's dense orchestral passages, Henry Cowell's tone clusters, and Edgard Varèse's conception of music as "organized sound" moving in masses, notably in Ionisation (1931). The technique came to prominence in the post-war avant-garde: Iannis Xenakis used stochastic methods to generate sound masses in works such as Metastaseis (1954) and Pithoprakta (1956); György Ligeti developed "micropolyphony" in Atmosphères (1961) and Lux Aeterna (1966); and Krzysztof Penderecki's Threnody to the Victims of Hiroshima (1960) for 52 strings became the emblematic sound-mass composition. Composers associated with the Polish "sonorism" school made extensive use of the idiom.

Related concepts

Sound mass is closely related to textural music, sonorism, and spectral music, and contrasts with pointillistic textures in which individual sounds remain distinct.
