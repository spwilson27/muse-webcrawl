Organolithium reagent

Organolithium reagent

Class: Organometallic compound

General form: R–Li

Properties: Strong base and nucleophile; air- and water-sensitive

An organolithium reagent is an organometallic compound containing a carbon–lithium bond. Organolithium reagents are among the most important reagents in organic synthesis: the highly polar carbon–lithium bond makes the carbon strongly nucleophilic and basic, so these compounds serve both as powerful bases and as carbon nucleophiles for forming new carbon–carbon bonds. They are also produced on an industrial scale as initiators for polymerisation.

Common reagents

  • Methyllithium (MeLi) — a fundamental carbanion equivalent.
  • n-Butyllithium (n-BuLi) — the most common organolithium, used for lithium–halogen exchange and deprotonations.
  • sec-Butyllithium and tert-butyllithium — progressively more reactive and more basic; t-BuLi is pyrophoric.
  • Phenyllithium and lithium diisopropylamide (LDA) — the latter a bulky, non-nucleophilic base central to enolate chemistry.

Structure and preparation

Organolithiums are typically prepared by reaction of an organic halide with lithium metal, or by lithium–halogen exchange with another organolithium. In solution and the solid state they are not simple monomers: they form aggregates — tetramers, hexamers and mixed clusters — whose structure depends on the solvent and additives; coordinating solvents such as THF and amines such as TMEDA break up aggregates and dramatically increase reactivity. Their Schlenk-type equilibria and aggregation are key to understanding their behaviour.

Reactions and handling

Organolithiums add to carbonyl compounds, undergo lithium–halogen exchange to generate aryl- and vinyllithiums, deprotonate weakly acidic C–H bonds (directed ortho metalation), and serve as precursors to other organometallics such as Gilman cuprates. Industrially, butyllithium initiates the anionic polymerisation of styrene and butadiene to make synthetic rubbers. They react violently with water and air — concentrated reagents can ignite spontaneously — so they are handled under inert atmosphere with syringe and cannula techniques, and supplied commercially as solutions in hydrocarbon or ether solvents.
