Deflation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In economics, deflation is a decrease in the general price level of goods and services. Deflation occurs when the inflation rate falls below 0% (a negative inflation rate). Inflation reduces the value of currency over time, but deflation increases it. This allows more goods and services to be bought than before with the same amount of currency. Deflation is distinct from disinflation, a slowdown in the inflation rate.

Causes

Deflation can arise from a contraction of the money supply or credit, a fall in aggregate demand (for example after an asset-price crash), or increases in productivity and supply that outpace demand growth. Under the quantity theory of money, deflation follows when the supply of goods grows faster than the supply of money in circulation.

Effects and the deflationary spiral

Economists generally believe that deflation is a problem in a modern economy because it increases the real value of debt, and may aggravate recessions and lead to a deflationary spiral: as prices fall, consumers delay purchases expecting lower prices later; production and wages are cut; demand falls further; and the real burden of debt rises, causing defaults that contract bank lending. Falling prices also constrain monetary policy, since nominal interest rates cannot fall far below zero (the zero lower bound), raising real interest rates even as the economy weakens. Irving Fisher's debt-deflation theory (1933) described this dynamic in the Great Depression, when US prices fell by roughly a quarter between 1929 and 1933.

Historical episodes

Notable episodes include the late 19th-century "good deflation" in several industrialising countries, associated with rapid productivity growth; the Great Depression; and Japan's prolonged mild deflation from the late 1990s through the 2010s, which motivated the policies known as Abenomics and affected the yen. Modern central banks typically target low positive inflation (around 2%) partly as insurance against deflation, deploying tools such as quantitative easing and automatic stabilizers on the fiscal side.
