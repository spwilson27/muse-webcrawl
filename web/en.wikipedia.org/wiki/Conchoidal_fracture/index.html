Conchoidal fracture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Conchoidal fracture describes the way brittle materials break when they do not follow any natural planes of separation. It produces smooth, curved surfaces resembling the interior of a seashell — the word conchoidal derives from the Greek konche, meaning shell. Materials that break this way include flint, obsidian, quartz, and glass.

Description

When a brittle, isotropic material with no cleavage is struck, the force spreads through it in a cone (a Hertzian cone), and the fracture propagates in concentric ripples from the point of impact. The resulting surface has a characteristic bulb of percussion and wavy undulations (Wallner lines) radiating from the striking point. This predictable fracture behaviour is what makes controlled flaking possible.

Lithic reduction

Conchoidal fracture is the basis of knapping, the shaping of stone tools by striking flakes from a core. Because fine-grained cryptocrystalline materials such as flint, chert, and obsidian fracture conchoidally, Stone Age toolmakers could control the size and shape of flakes with considerable precision, producing implements such as handaxes, arrowheads, and blades. Archaeologists study the features of conchoidal flakes — striking platforms, bulbs of percussion, and ripple marks — to reconstruct ancient technology and to distinguish human-made tools from naturally broken stone.

In mineral identification

Fracture type is a standard diagnostic property in mineralogy. Quartz, for instance, is characterised by conchoidal fracture and the absence of cleavage, which helps distinguish it from feldspars. Other fracture types include hackly (jagged), splintery, and uneven fractures.
