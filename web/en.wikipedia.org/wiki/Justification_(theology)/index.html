Justification (theology)

In Christian theology, justification is the act by which God moves a willing person from the state of sin (injustice) to the state of grace (justice), or — in the classical Protestant formulation — the forensic act in which God declares the sinner righteous on account of Christ. The doctrine was a central point of contention in the Protestant Reformation, where sola fide ("by faith alone") became one of the defining slogans of Protestantism.

Pauline origins

The doctrine is rooted in the letters of Paul the Apostle, above all the Epistle to the Romans and the Epistle to the Galatians, where Paul argues that a person is justified "by faith apart from works of the law". Abraham is presented as the model: "Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness." The Greek verb dikaioō and its cognates can mean both "to declare righteous" and "to make righteous", a semantic range that underlies much later disagreement.

Roman Catholic view

The Council of Trent (1545–1563) defined justification as "a translation from that state wherein man is born a child of the first Adam, to the state of grace", involving both the forgiveness of sins and the interior renewal of the person by grace. Catholic theology holds that justification is initiated by grace, received through faith, and expressed in works of love; it can be increased by good works and lost by mortal sin.

Protestant views

Martin Luther called justification by faith the article "by which the church stands or falls" (articulus stantis et cadentis ecclesiae). In Lutheran and Reformed theology, justification is distinct from sanctification: God declares the sinner righteous because the righteousness of Christ is imputed to them and received by faith alone; good works follow necessarily but do not contribute to the verdict. Arminian and Methodist traditions emphasise the role of free will cooperating with "prevenient grace".

Ecumenical developments

In 1999 the Roman Catholic Church and the Lutheran World Federation signed the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, affirming a consensus on basic truths of the doctrine and declaring that the mutual condemnations of the Reformation era do not apply to the partner's present teaching. The "New Perspective on Paul", associated with scholars such as E. P. Sanders and N. T. Wright, has reframed academic debate by stressing covenant membership in Paul's use of "works of the law".
