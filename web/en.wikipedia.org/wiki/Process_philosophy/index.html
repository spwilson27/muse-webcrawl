Process philosophy

Process philosophy is a longstanding philosophical tradition that emphasises becoming and change over static being. It takes process — change, flux, and temporal passage — as more fundamental than enduring substances. In the process philosophy of Alfred North Whitehead, processes are taken to have a quality of interrelatedness that substance philosophy lacks: every entity is constituted by its relations to events and occasions of experience.

History

Process themes are ancient: Heraclitus taught that all things are in flux ("you cannot step into the same river twice"). In the modern period, process thought was developed by philosophers such as Charles Sanders Peirce, William James, Henri Bergson, and John Dewey, reaching its most systematic form in Whitehead's Process and Reality (1929) and the work of Charles Hartshorne.

Whitehead's system

In Whitehead's metaphysics, the ultimate units of reality are "actual occasions" — momentary drops of experience that come into being by "prehending" (taking account of) prior occasions, and then perish, becoming data for new occasions. God plays a distinctive role in the system, ordering possibilities and being affected by the world in return — a view developed by Hartshorne into process theology, influential in liberal Christian thought.

Influence

Process philosophy has influenced theology, ecology, education, and the philosophy of science, and has been taken up by thinkers such as Gilles Deleuze. It is generally contrasted with the substance-based metaphysics dominant since Aristotle, though process thinkers regard the two as complementary standpoints.
