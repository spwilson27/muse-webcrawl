The White Revolution in India, also known as Operation Flood, was a dairy development programme launched in 1970 that transformed India from a milk-deficient nation into the world's largest milk producer, surpassing the United States in 1998. It is regarded as the world's largest dairy development programme and is credited with replicating the cooperative model of the Kaira District Co-operative Milk Producers' Union (Amul) across the country.

Background

Before the revolution, Indian milk production was stagnant, growing at around 1% per year through the 1950s and 1960s, and per-capita milk availability was declining. Dairy farming was dominated by smallholders keeping one or two animals, and middlemen captured much of the value of the milk sold in cities. In Anand, Gujarat, the Kaira cooperative — founded in 1946 under the guidance of Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and led by Tribhuvandas Patel and the young engineer Verghese Kurien — had shown that farmers could own the processing and marketing of their own milk through the Amul brand.

Operation Flood phases

The National Dairy Development Board (NDDB), established in 1965 with Kurien as its chairman, designed Operation Flood to replicate the "Anand pattern" nationally. It was financed partly by the sale of donated skimmed milk powder and butter oil from the European Economic Community, channelled through the World Food Programme.

  • Phase I (1970–1980) — linked 18 of India's premier milksheds to markets in the four metropolitan cities: Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata and Chennai.
  • Phase II (1981–1985) — expanded coverage to some 136 milksheds and more than 290 urban markets, and built a national milk grid.
  • Phase III (1985–1996) — consolidated the cooperative structure, expanded infrastructure, veterinary services and artificial insemination, and emphasised productivity and marketing.

Structure and features

The Anand pattern is a three-tier cooperative structure: a village dairy cooperative society collects milk from farmer members twice daily; a district union owns the processing plant; and a state federation handles marketing. Professional managers, accountable to farmer-elected boards, run the system. Key features included payment based on measured fat content, elimination of middlemen, crossbreeding programmes, compounded cattle feed, and first-aid veterinary services reaching remote villages.

Impact

India's milk production rose from about 17 million tonnes in 1950–51 to over 84 million tonnes by 2001–02 and continued climbing thereafter, making India the world's largest milk producer. Per-capita milk availability roughly doubled from around 124 grams per day in the early 1970s to over 220 grams per day by the early 2000s. Operation Flood created millions of rural jobs, a large share of the benefits flowing to small, marginal and landless farmers and to women, who perform much of the dairy work. Verghese Kurien came to be known as the "Father of the White Revolution" and received honours including the Padma Vibhushan.

Criticism

Critics have questioned the programme's emphasis on crossbred cattle over indigenous breeds, its dependence on imported dairy commodities in the early phases, and whether benefits were evenly spread across regions. Others note that the cooperative model weakened in some states where government control over federations increased.
