Ship of the line

A ship of the line was a type of naval warship constructed during the Age of Sail in the 17th through the mid-19th centuries, the designation taking its name from the line of battle tactics of naval warfare.

Ships of the line were built to take part, with other similar ships, in the line of battle, in which two opposing fleets formed themselves into a line so that each ship's broadside – guns mounted along its side – could fire on the enemy without hitting friendly ships.

History

The line-of-battle tactic was developed in the Anglo-Dutch Wars of the 1650s. Earlier warships such as the galleon and man-of-war evolved into larger, more heavily gunned vessels. By the 18th century ships were rated: first-rate ships carried 100 guns or more on three decks, second-rates 90–98, third-rates 64–80 (the most common, including 74-gun ships), while fourth-rates of 50–60 guns were by the Napoleonic era considered too weak for the line.

Famous examples include HMS Victory, flagship of Lord Horatio Nelson at the Battle of Trafalgar (1805), the French Bucentaure, and the Spanish Santísima Trinidad, at one time the largest warship in the world with four gun decks.

Construction and armament

Hulls were of oak, up to 60 cm thick at the waterline, able to withstand cannon fire at close range. Sails were carried on three masts. A single broadside from a first-rate could fire more than half a ton of iron shot. Later ships adopted copper sheathing to protect against shipworm and fouling.

Decline

Steam propulsion, explosive shell guns (notably at the Battle of Sinop, 1853), and iron armour made wooden ships of the line obsolete. France's Napoléon (1850) was the first purpose-built steam battleship, and HMS Warrior (1860) marked the ironclad era.
