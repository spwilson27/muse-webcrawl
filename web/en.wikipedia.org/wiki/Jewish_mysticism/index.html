Jewish mysticism is the body of mystical traditions within Judaism, centred on direct experience of and speculation about the divine. Academic study distinguishes early forms — biblical prophecy and apocalyptic literature, the Merkabah (chariot) and Heikhalot (palaces) mysticism of late antiquity — from the Kabbalah that emerged in medieval Provence, Spain, and Safed, and from Hasidism, the pietistic movement of 18th-century Eastern Europe that popularised Kabbalistic ideas.

The scholarly reconstruction of Jewish mysticism was pioneered by Gershom Scholem, founder of the academic field at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, whose Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism (1941) remains foundational, though later scholars (Moshe Idel and others) have revised many of his theses.

Ancient mysticism: Merkabah and Heikhalot

Biblical visions of Ezekiel's chariot (merkabah) and of the divine throne in Isaiah 6 inspired a tradition of visionary ascent literature. The Heikhalot texts (c. 3rd–8th centuries) describe adepts descending (or ascending) through seven heavenly palaces, protected by seals and hymns, to behold the divine throne; the Shi'ur Qomah speculates on the gigantic dimensions of God's body. Related writings include the apocalypses of 1 and 2 Enoch and the mystical hymns of Qumran. The compact Sefer Yetzirah (Book of Formation) taught creation through the ten sefirot ("numbers") and the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet, becoming a seed text for later Kabbalah.

Medieval Kabbalah

Kabbalah proper emerged in 12th-century Provence and Catalonia. The Sefer ha-Bahir (mid-12th c.) introduced the sefirot as divine emanations or attributes through which the infinite God (Ein Sof) interacts with creation, together with doctrines of reincarnation (gilgul) and the feminine divine presence (Shekhinah). Isaac the Blind of Posquières and the Gerona circle (Azriel, Nahmanides) systematised theosophical Kabbalah; Abraham Abulafia developed an ecstatic, meditative Kabbalah of letter combination and the Divine Name. The Zohar (Book of Splendour), published in Spain in the late 13th century and attributed traditionally to the 2nd-century sage Shimon bar Yochai but ascribed by scholars principally to Moses de León, became the canonical masterpiece of Kabbalah, a mystical midrash on the Torah written in literary Aramaic.

Lurianic Kabbalah

After the expulsion from Spain (1492), Safed in Ottoman Galilee became the centre of a new Kabbalistic synthesis taught by Isaac Luria (1534–1572), the "Ari," and recorded by Hayyim Vital. Lurianic cosmology taught tzimtzum (divine self-contraction to make space for creation), shevirat ha-kelim (the breaking of the vessels, scattering holy sparks into the material realm), and tikkun (restoration through human commandments and prayer). Its myth of cosmic catastrophe and repair resonated with the trauma of exile and influenced all later Jewish mysticism and messianic expectation.

Sabbateanism and Hasidism

Lurianic ideas fuelled the explosive messianic movement around Sabbatai Zevi (1665–66), whose conversion to Islam under Ottoman threat scandalised the Jewish world but left durable underground currents (the Dönmeh; later Frankism). In the 18th century, Israel ben Eliezer, the Baal Shem Tov, founded Hasidism in Podolia and Ukraine, teaching devekut (cleaving to God) in everyday life, joy in worship, and the charismatic leadership of the tzaddik (rebbe). Hasidism's dynastic courts (Chabad, Breslov, Satmar, Ger, Belz, and others) reshaped Eastern European Jewry and survive as major Haredi communities worldwide; its theology drew on Kabbalah while facing fierce opposition from the Mitnagdim led by the Vilna Gaon.

Modern study and revival

Scholem and his school made Kabbalah an academic discipline; in the later 20th and 21st centuries, popular and "New Age" Kabbalah movements (e.g., the Kabbalah Centre) spread simplified versions globally, drawing scholarly criticism. Academic approaches now span philology, history of religions, and comparative mysticism, and contemporary Jewish renewal movements draw selectively on Kabbalistic and Hasidic practice and meditation.
