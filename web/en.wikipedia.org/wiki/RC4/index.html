RC4

RC4 (also known as ARC4 or ARCFOUR, meaning Alleged RC4) is a stream cipher designed in 1987 by Ron Rivest of RSA Security. While remarkably simple and speedy in software, multiple vulnerabilities have been discovered in RC4, rendering it insecure. It is especially vulnerable when the beginning of the output keystream is not discarded, or when nonrandom or related keys are used.

History

RC4 was initially a trade secret, but in September 1994 a description of it was anonymously posted to the Cypherpunks mailing list and soon after to the sci.crypt Usenet group, from where it spread worldwide. Because the algorithm had never been patented and its name remained trademarked, the leaked version was dubbed "alleged RC4". It became part of widely used protocols including WEP and TLS.

Algorithm

RC4 generates a pseudorandom stream of bits (a keystream). A key-scheduling algorithm initializes a 256-byte permutation from a variable-length key; a pseudo-random generation algorithm then swaps elements and outputs bytes, which are combined with plaintext by XOR. Encryption and decryption are identical operations.

Security

Biases in the keystream were documented as early as 2001, when weaknesses exploited in the Fluhrer–Mantin–Shamir attack broke WEP encryption. Subsequent research found increasingly practical attacks against RC4 in TLS, leading the IETF to prohibit RC4 in TLS in RFC 7465 (2015), and browser vendors removed support. RC4 should not be used in new applications.
