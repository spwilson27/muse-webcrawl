Lactulose is a non-absorbable synthetic disaccharide sugar used in the treatment of constipation and hepatic encephalopathy. It is made up of the monosaccharides fructose and galactose. It is taken by mouth or administered rectally.

Lactulose was first described in 1929 and has been used medically since the 1960s. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines. It is available as a generic medication and is relatively inexpensive.

Medical uses

Constipation

Lactulose is used in the treatment of chronic constipation in patients of all ages as a long-term treatment. The dosage of lactulose for chronic idiopathic constipation is adjusted depending on the severity of the constipation and the desired effect, from a mild softener to causing diarrhoea. It is also used in the treatment of constipation in the elderly and in children, in whom it is generally considered safe when used at appropriate doses.

Hepatic encephalopathy

Lactulose is used in the treatment of hepatic encephalopathy, a complication of liver disease in which toxic substances such as ammonia accumulate in the blood and affect the brain. By acidifying the contents of the colon, lactulose traps ammonia in the form of ammonium ions, which are then expelled in the stool. It may be given orally or rectally, and in severe cases of hepatic encephalopathy it is a mainstay of therapy alongside treatment of the underlying cause.

Mechanism of action

Lactulose is not absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract because humans lack the enzymes to break it down. It reaches the colon essentially unchanged, where it is metabolised by colonic bacteria into lactic acid and small amounts of acetic and formic acid. These acids increase the osmotic pressure in the colon, drawing water into the bowel and softening the stool. The resulting acidification of the colonic contents also converts diffusible ammonia (NH3) into non-diffusible ammonium (NH4+), reducing its absorption into the blood.

Side effects

Common side effects include abdominal bloating, flatulence, cramping, nausea and diarrhoea, particularly at the start of treatment. Excessive doses may cause electrolyte disturbances such as hypokalaemia and hypernatraemia secondary to diarrhoea. Lactulose contains small amounts of free galactose and lactose, so it should be used with caution in people with galactosaemia or diabetes mellitus.

History

Lactulose was first described in 1929 by Montgomery and Hudson, and named because it was formed from lactose on heating. Its laxative properties were recognised in the 1950s, and it was introduced into clinical practice in the 1960s. Its benefit in hepatic encephalopathy was demonstrated by Bircher and colleagues in 1966.
