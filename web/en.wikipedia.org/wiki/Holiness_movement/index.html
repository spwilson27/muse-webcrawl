Holiness movement

The Holiness movement is a set of beliefs and practices within Christianity that emerged principally in the 19th century, rooted in the teachings of John Wesley and early Methodism. Its central doctrine is entire sanctification — also called Christian perfection — understood as a distinct second work of grace, subsequent to conversion (the "first work of grace" of justification and the new birth), in which the believer is cleansed from original sin and empowered to live a life of perfect love toward God and neighbor.

The movement spread through Methodist camp meetings, publications, and parachurch organizations in the United States, particularly after the founding of the National Camp Meeting Association for the Promotion of Holiness in 1867. Influential figures included Phoebe Palmer, whose "altar theology" and Tuesday Meetings popularized the doctrine, and later evangelists and writers across the Methodist and broader evangelical world.

Beliefs and practices

Holiness teaching emphasized a life separated from worldly practices. Historically, adherents often abstained from alcohol, tobacco, dancing, theater attendance, and immodest dress, and observed strict Sabbath observance. The movement stressed personal piety, the witness of the Holy Spirit, and evangelism, and it played a significant role in the temperance and abolitionist movements and in early leadership opportunities for women in ministry.

Denominations

As tensions grew with mainstream Methodism over doctrine and practice, many Holiness believers formed independent churches and denominations in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. These include the Church of the Nazarene (founded 1908), the Wesleyan Church, the Free Methodist Church, the Church of God (Anderson, Indiana), the Salvation Army (which arose from Holiness-influenced Methodism), and numerous smaller bodies. The Holiness movement is also a major tributary to Pentecostalism, which emerged in the early 20th century when some Holiness groups added the doctrine of baptism in the Holy Spirit evidenced by speaking in tongues — a teaching mainstream Holiness churches rejected.
