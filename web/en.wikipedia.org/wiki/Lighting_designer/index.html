Lighting designer

A lighting designer (or LD) in theatre, dance, opera, concerts and other live performances works with the director, choreographer, set designer, costume designer, and sound designer to create the lighting, atmosphere, and time of day for the production in response to the text, while keeping in mind issues of visibility, safety, and cost. The LD also works closely with the stage manager or show control programming, if show control systems are used. Outside stage lighting, the job of a lighting designer can be much more diverse, and they can be found working on rock and pop tours, corporate launches, art installations, or as architectural lighting designers.

Process

After reading the script and meeting the creative team, the lighting designer researches the period and mood of the piece and produces a light plot — a scaled plan showing the position, type, colour and focus of every instrument — along with channel hookups and cue sheets. During technical rehearsals the designer sets levels and timing for each cue at the lighting console, refining the design with the director until dress rehearsals.

History

The profession emerged as electric light replaced gas in the late 19th century. Jean Rosenthal, whose work on Orson Welles's Mercury Theatre productions in the 1930s and later on Broadway defined the craft, is often regarded as the first modern lighting designer. Other influential figures include Abe Feder, Tharon Musser — who computerized lighting control with A Chorus Line (1975) — and, in Britain, Richard Pilbrow.

Architectural lighting design

Architectural lighting designers work independently of the architectural team to shape how buildings and spaces are perceived, balancing daylight, electric light, energy use and human factors. Professional bodies include the International Association of Lighting Designers (IALD), founded in 1969.
