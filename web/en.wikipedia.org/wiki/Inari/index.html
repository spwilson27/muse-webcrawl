The thousand torii of Fushimi Inari

Inari (Japanese: 稲荷), formally Inari Ōkami, is the Shinto kami of rice, agriculture, fertility, foxes, industry and worldly prosperity — by some counts the most widely worshipped kami in Japan, with shrines numbered in the tens of thousands. Inari's messengers are foxes (kitsune), whose statues, wearing red bibs and holding keys, jewels or rice sheaves, guard every Inari shrine; the great head shrine, Fushimi Inari-taisha in Kyoto, climbs Mount Inari beneath tunnels of thousands of vermilion torii gates donated by devotees and businesses.

Character and worship

Inari is depicted variously — as an old man, a young woman, a bodhisattva-like figure, or in no fixed form — reflecting centuries of layering with Buddhist deities such as Dakiniten and with the food goddess Ukanomitama, with whom Inari is usually identified. Farmers prayed to Inari for the rice harvest; merchants, swordsmiths, geisha, fishermen and modern corporations pray for prosperity, and new businesses still dedicate torii at Fushimi Inari. The first horse day of February (Hatsuuma) is Inari's great festival. Offerings characteristically include fried tofu — inarizushi, tofu pouches stuffed with rice, bears the god's name — said to be the foxes' favourite food.

Foxes

The foxes of Inari are not the god but its servants and messengers, white and invisible to most eyes; they shade into the broader kitsune lore of shapeshifters, possessors and tricksters (the possessing spirit kitsune-tsuki was a folk diagnosis). Inari's foxes are overwhelmingly beneficent — guardians of shrine and storehouse — though folklore keeps the trickster edge: a fox's gratitude, like a fox's grudge, is never small.
