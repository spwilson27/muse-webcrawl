Pernambucan revolt

Date1645–1654
LocationCaptaincy of Pernambuco, Brazil
ResultPortuguese victory; end of Dutch Brazil

The Pernambucan revolt (Portuguese: Insurreição Pernambucana) was an uprising by Portuguese-Brazilian colonists in the Captaincy of Pernambuco against the Dutch occupation of northeastern Brazil, lasting from 1645 to 1654. The revolt ultimately expelled the Dutch West India Company from Brazil and restored Portuguese rule over the region.

Background

The Dutch had seized Recife and much of the sugar-producing Brazilian northeast in the 1630s, establishing the colony of New Holland. Under Governor Johan Maurits of Nassau-Siegen, the colony prospered, but his recall in 1644, combined with heavy taxation, debts owed by Portuguese planters to the Dutch West India Company, and religious tensions, fuelled discontent.

The uprising

In June 1645, planters led by André Vidal de Negreiros, João Fernandes Vieira, the Indigenous leader Filipe Camarão, and the Afro-Brazilian commander Henrique Dias rose in revolt. The insurgents won important victories at the two Battles of Guararapes (1648 and 1649), fought on the Jaboatão plain south of Recife, which became celebrated in Brazilian national memory as the birthplace of the Brazilian army and of a shared national identity among Europeans, Africans, and Indigenous peoples.

Outcome

Cut off from reinforcement after the First Anglo-Dutch War diverted Dutch resources, the remaining Dutch forces capitulated on 26 January 1654 under the Treaty of Taborda. The Dutch formally ceded their Brazilian claims in the Treaty of The Hague (1661), receiving financial compensation from Portugal.
