Critical regionalism

Critical regionalism is an approach to architecture that seeks to mediate between the universalising tendencies of modernism and the particular qualities of a place — its climate, light, topography, materials and craft traditions. The term was introduced by Alexander Tzonis and Liane Lefaivre in 1981 and developed most influentially by Kenneth Frampton, notably in his 1983 essay "Towards a Critical Regionalism: Six Points for an Architecture of Resistance".

Principles

Frampton, drawing on Paul Ricœur's essay "Universal Civilization and National Cultures", argued that architecture should resist both placeless international modernisation and sentimental, scenographic vernacular revival. Critical regionalism instead emphasises the tectonic — the honest expression of construction — and the tactile over the purely visual; it responds to local climate and light, uses regional materials in modern ways, and roots buildings in their topography.

Practitioners

Architects associated with the approach include Alvar Aalto (Finland), Jørn Utzon (Denmark), Luis Barragán (Mexico), Tadao Ando (Japan), Álvaro Siza (Portugal), Mario Botta (Switzerland), Balkrishna Doshi and Charles Correa (India), and Geoffrey Bawa (Sri Lanka).

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether critical regionalism can remain "critical" once adopted as a marketable style, and whether its categories impose an outside (often Western academic) reading on local practices.
