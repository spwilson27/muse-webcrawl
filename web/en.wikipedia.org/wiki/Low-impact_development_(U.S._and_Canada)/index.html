Low-impact development (U.S. and Canada)

Low-impact development (LID) is a term used in the United States and Canada to describe a land planning and engineering design approach to manage stormwater runoff as part of green infrastructure. LID emphasizes conservation and the use of on-site natural features to protect water quality. This approach implements engineered small-scale hydrologic controls to replicate the pre-development hydrology of a watershed through infiltrating, filtering, storing, evaporating, and detaining runoff close to its source. Green infrastructure investments can produce a wide range of other benefits beyond flood prevention and water management, including improved air quality, reduced energy costs, wildlife habitat creation and carbon sequestration.

History

The concept began in 1990 in Prince George's County, Maryland, where it was developed as an alternative to traditional stormwater best management practices (BMPs) such as detention ponds, which treated runoff at a single "end-of-pipe" location. Rather than conveying stormwater to large centralized facilities, LID manages it through distributed, small-scale practices at or near the source.

Practices

Common LID practices include:

  • Bioswales — vegetated channels that slow, filter, and infiltrate runoff.
  • Rain gardens (bioretention cells) — shallow planted depressions that collect and absorb runoff from roofs, driveways, and streets.
  • Green roofs — vegetated roof surfaces that retain precipitation.
  • Permeable pavement — paving materials that allow water to pass through into the soil below.
  • Rainwater harvesting — rain barrels and cisterns that capture roof runoff for later use.

Related approaches

LID is closely related to sustainable drainage systems (SuDS) in the United Kingdom, water-sensitive urban design (WSUD) in Australia, and "sponge city" programmes in China. In the United States, the Environmental Protection Agency promotes LID as a strategy to help communities meet Clean Water Act requirements, in particular by reducing combined sewer overflows and pollutant loads to streams, rivers, and estuaries.
