Pundit (explorer)


A pundit mapping Central Asia in disguise, 19th century.

The pundits were a group of Indian surveyors employed by the British in the 19th century to survey and explore regions of Central Asia, Tibet, and the Himalayas that were closed to Europeans. The term derives from their leader's description of them as trained native explorers; the most famous was Nain Singh Rawat. Their journeys formed part of the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India and the political contest known as the Great Game.

Training and methods

The scheme was organized by Captain Thomas George Montgomerie of the Survey of India in the 1860s. Recruits, often recruited from hill peoples of the Himalayan borderlands, were trained at Dehradun to walk with an exactly even pace of known length, so distances could be measured by counting steps. Their rosaries were modified to have 100 beads instead of the usual 108, so they could count paces unobtrusively, and survey notes were hidden in prayer wheels. Compass bearings, sextant observations of stars to fix latitude, and the temperature of boiling water to estimate altitude were recorded in secret, while the men travelled disguised as pilgrims or traders.

Notable pundits

Nain Singh reached Lhasa in 1866, fixed its position, and measured the route along the Yarlung Tsangpo (Brahmaputra). His cousin Kishen Singh (code-named A-K) completed a four-year journey through Tibet in 1878–1882. Kinthup, a Sikkimese pundit, traced the Tsangpo gorge and provided crucial evidence that the Tsangpo and Brahmaputra were the same river.

Legacy

The pundits' surveys filled in the map of Tibet and Central Asia with remarkable accuracy and were celebrated by the Royal Geographical Society, although the men themselves long remained anonymous in official reports, identified only by initials or code names.
