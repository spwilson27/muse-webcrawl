French Army mutinies (1917)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The French Army mutinies of 1917 were a wave of collective indiscipline in the French Army on the Western Front during the First World War. Following the costly failure of the Nivelle Offensive in April 1917, soldiers in roughly half of the French infantry divisions refused orders to attack, though in most cases they remained willing to hold defensive positions. The mutinies were driven by exhaustion, appalling living conditions, inadequate leave, and the perception that their lives were being wasted in futile assaults; they subsided after General Philippe Pétain, who replaced Robert Nivelle as commander-in-chief, combined limited repression with substantial reforms.

Background

By the spring of 1917 France had suffered over a million dead, and the poilus had endured Verdun and the Somme. Nivelle promised a decisive breakthrough on the Chemin des Dames with a massive artillery preparation; the Second Battle of the Aisne (16 April – 9 May 1917) instead gained little ground at a cost of roughly 187,000 French casualties in a few weeks. News of the Russian Revolution and a wave of strikes and pacifist agitation in the French rear added to the ferment.

The mutinies

Beginning in late April and peaking in late May and early June, incidents spread across the front: units refused to entrain for the line, marched on headquarters, sang the Internationale, and in a few cases elected "soviets". Guy Pedroncini's research, published after the archives opened in 1967, documented 250 collective acts of disobedience affecting 68 of the army's 112 infantry divisions, with incidents in 49 divisions serious enough to be counted as mutinies. Notably, the soldiers almost never attacked their officers or deserted to the enemy; their demand was essentially to defend the trenches but not to be sacrificed in hopeless offensives — a distinction Pétain himself recognised.

Response and aftermath

Pétain, appointed on 15 May, visited dozens of divisions, promising — and delivering — regular leave, better food and rest camps, and an end to grand offensives until the arrival of American troops and more tanks ("I am waiting for the tanks and the Americans"). Repression accompanied reform: courts-martial passed some 3,400 convictions and 554 death sentences, of which about 49 were carried out (the exact figure remains debated); others were sent to penal colonies or returned to duty. The German command, poorly informed through its intelligence, failed to exploit the crisis. The French Army recovered sufficiently to hold the line in 1917 and to play a central role in the Allied offensives of 1918, though historians still debate how fragile its recovery was. The mutinies were little discussed in France for decades — Pétain shielded the records, and the episode was officially hushed up until Pedroncini's 1967 study.
