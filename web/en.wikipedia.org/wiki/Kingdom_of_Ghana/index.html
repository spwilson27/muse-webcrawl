Kingdom of Ghana

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Ruins of Kumbi Saleh, stone foundations in the Mauritanian desert]

The Ghana Empire (also known as Wagadou, c. 300s–1200s AD) was a West African empire of the Soninke people, located in what is now south-eastern Mauritania and western Mali — far north of the modern nation of Ghana that borrowed its name. The first of the great Sudanese trading empires, it grew wealthy by taxing the trans-Saharan trade: gold from the southern fields of Bambuk and Bure exchanged for Saharan salt, carried by camel caravans to its twin capital at Kumbi Saleh, where Arab merchants described a fabulously rich king (the 'ghana' was his title) holding court amid gold-collared pages and horses caparisoned in gold.

Gold and salt

Ghana's kings controlled neither the goldfields nor the salt pans directly but sat astride the routes between them, taxing each load — a dinar of gold per donkey-load of salt entering, two leaving, by al-Bakri's 11th-century account. The king kept all gold nuggets for himself, leaving the dust in circulation (a policy credited in the sources with preventing inflation). Kumbi Saleh's two cities — the royal town with its sacred grove and the Muslim merchants' town with twelve mosques — embodied the empire's dual world of traditional religion and Islam.

Decline

From the 11th century the empire waned: the Almoravid invasion of tradition (1076, whose scale modern historians doubt — conversion pressure and disrupted trade mattered more than conquest), drying pasture and shifting routes, and the rise of vassals. By the early 13th century the Sosso under Sumanguru absorbed Ghana's remnants; Sundiata's Mali Empire then succeeded to its trade and legend. The Soninke diaspora carried its memory across West Africa.
