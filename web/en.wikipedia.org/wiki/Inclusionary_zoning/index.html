Inclusionary zoning

Inclusionary zoning (also called inclusionary housing) is a land-use policy, used mainly in the United States, that requires or incentivises developers of new housing to make a percentage of units affordable to low- and moderate-income households. In return, developers may receive benefits such as density bonuses, expedited approvals or fee waivers; some programmes allow payment of a fee in lieu of building the affordable units.

History and use

The practice began in Montgomery County, Maryland in 1974 with its Moderately Priced Dwelling Unit programme, and spread to hundreds of jurisdictions including New York City, Boston, San Francisco and Washington, D.C. Similar policies exist in the United Kingdom (through Section 106 agreements) and elsewhere.

Debate

Supporters say inclusionary zoning produces mixed-income neighbourhoods and affordable homes in high-opportunity areas without direct public spending. Critics argue that mandates can act as a tax on development, reducing overall housing supply or raising market-rate prices, and that fees-in-lieu may concentrate affordable units in cheaper areas.
