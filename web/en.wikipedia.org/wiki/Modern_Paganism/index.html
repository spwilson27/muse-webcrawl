Modern paganism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A stone circle at dawn with robed celebrants marking a solstice]
A stone circle at dawn with robed celebrants marking a solstice

Modern paganism, also known as neopaganism or contemporary paganism, is a religious movement influenced by the beliefs of pre-modern Europe, centred on nature worship and typically polytheistic or pantheistic. Its largest tradition is Wicca — founded in mid-twentieth-century England by Gerald Gardner — alongside Heathenry (Norse/Germanic), Druidry, Hellenic, Kemetic (Egyptian) and other reconstructions, and eclectic goddess spirituality.

Origins

Modern paganism grew from Romanticism, folklore studies and occultism, crystallising in the 1950s when Gardner published Witchcraft Today (1954) after Britain repealed its Witchcraft Act, claiming survival of an ancient witch-cult — a lineage historians dispute, while acknowledging the religion's genuine creative synthesis. In the US it fused with feminism (Starhawk, The Spiral Dance, 1979), environmentalism and the counterculture.

Beliefs and practice

Common threads are immanent divinity, reverence for nature, seasonal festivals (the Wheel of the Year: solstices, equinoxes, Samhain, Beltane and the rest), ritual in small groups (covens, kindreds, groves) and the absence of scripture or central authority. Adherents number from hundreds of thousands to over a million in the US and tens of thousands in the UK (censuses recorded some 75,000–90,000 'Pagans' in England and Wales recently, alongside separate Wiccan and Druid counts).

Recognition and issues

Pagans have won increasing legal recognition — military headstones in the US (2007), charity status for the Druid Network in the UK (2010) — and internal debates over cultural appropriation, racial exclusivity in parts of Heathenry (repudiated by most groups), and the line between reconstruction and invention.
