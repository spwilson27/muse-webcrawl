Tularemia, also known as rabbit fever or deer fly fever, is an infectious disease caused by the bacterium Francisella tularensis. It is a zoonosis of the Northern Hemisphere, maintained in wild animals — especially rabbits, hares, rodents and beavers — and transmitted to humans by tick and fly bites, direct contact with infected animals or carcasses, ingestion of contaminated water or meat, and inhalation of contaminated dust. It is not normally transmitted from person to person.

Signs and symptoms

After an incubation period of typically three to five days, illness begins with abrupt fever, chills, headache and malaise. The syndrome depends on the route of infection:

  • Ulceroglandular (the commonest form): a skin ulcer at the inoculation site with swollen, painful regional lymph nodes.
  • Glandular: lymphadenopathy without an ulcer.
  • Oculoglandular: conjunctivitis with preauricular lymph node swelling, from inoculation of the eye.
  • Oropharyngeal: sore throat, mouth ulcers and cervical lymphadenopathy, from contaminated food or water.
  • Pneumonic: cough, chest pain and breathlessness, either from inhalation or spread through the blood; this is the most serious form.
  • Typhoidal: systemic febrile illness without localising signs.

The organism

Francisella tularensis is a small, aerobic, Gram-negative coccobacillus and a facultative intracellular pathogen that survives inside macrophages. It is extraordinarily infectious by inhalation — as few as 10–50 organisms may cause disease — which led to its development as a biological weapon in the twentieth century and its listing as a Tier 1 select agent in the United States. The more virulent subspecies tularensis (type A) occurs in North America; subspecies holarctica (type B), found across the Northern Hemisphere, causes milder disease.

History

The disease was first described in 1911 by George Walter McCoy among ground squirrels in Tulare County, California, from which it takes its name; Edward Francis characterised the human illness and the organism, later honoured in the genus name Francisella. Outbreaks have occurred among hunters, landscapers (notably a 2000 outbreak on Martha's Vineyard linked to lawn mowing), laboratory workers, and in Scandinavia and Russia from mosquito bites and contaminated water. Soviet sources reported large wartime outbreaks in the USSR.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually serological, since culture is hazardous to laboratory staff and requires special media. Aminoglycosides (streptomycin or gentamicin) are the traditional treatment of choice; doxycycline and ciprofloxacin are alternatives, though relapses are more frequent with bacteriostatic agents. With treatment, mortality is low (around 1–2%); untreated pneumonic or typhoidal disease can be fatal in a substantial fraction of cases. There is no licensed vaccine in general use; a live vaccine strain (LVS) has been used for laboratory personnel.
