Antenna (electronics)

Antenna
TypeElectrical device; transducer
FunctionConverts electric power to radio waves and vice versa

In radio engineering, an antenna (American English) or aerial (British English) is the interface between radio waves propagating through space and electric currents moving in metal conductors, used with a radio transmitter or receiver. In transmission, currents supplied by the transmitter oscillate in the antenna and radiate energy as electromagnetic waves; in reception, passing radio waves induce a small voltage in the antenna that is amplified by the receiver.

Antennas are essential components of all radio equipment, from broadcast transmitters and two-way radios to wireless networks, mobile phones, radar and satellite communications. The first antennas were built by Heinrich Hertz in 1888 to prove the existence of electromagnetic waves predicted by Maxwell's equations.

Terminology and characteristics

An antenna's performance is described by several quantities:

  • Radiation pattern — the directional dependence of the strength of the waves radiated or received.
  • Gain — the ratio of intensity in a given direction to that of an ideal isotropic radiator.
  • Bandwidth — the range of frequencies over which the antenna operates satisfactorily.
  • Polarisation — the orientation of the electric field of the radiated wave.
  • Input impedance — which must be matched to the feed line for efficient power transfer.

By the reciprocity theorem, an antenna's properties — pattern, gain and impedance — are the same in transmission and reception.

Types

Common antenna types include the dipole, the monopole over a ground plane, the loop antenna, the Yagi–Uda array long used for television reception, the horn antenna, the parabolic reflector or "dish", the patch (microstrip) antenna used in phones and GPS receivers, and phased arrays whose beam can be steered electronically. Electrically small antennas are used where space is limited, at the cost of efficiency and bandwidth.

Resonance

Many antennas are resonant structures: a dipole, for example, is most effective when its length is approximately half the wavelength of the signal. Resonance yields a purely resistive feedpoint impedance and efficient radiation, but narrows the usable bandwidth, a trade-off quantified by fundamental limits such as the Chu–Harrington limit.
