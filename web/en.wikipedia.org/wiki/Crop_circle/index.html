Crop circle

A crop circle, crop formation, or corn circle is a pattern created by flattening a crop, usually a cereal. The term was first coined in the early 1980s by Colin Andrews. Crop circles have been described as a form of landscape art, and since the late 1970s they have attracted media attention, tourism and speculation about extraterrestrial or paranormal origins, although they are demonstrably made by people.

History

Reports of flattened-crop patterns predate the modern phenomenon — the 1678 English pamphlet of the "Mowing-Devil" is sometimes cited — but the modern era began in southern England around 1978, when simple circles appeared in Wiltshire and Hampshire. In 1991, Doug Bower and Dave Chorley announced that they had been making circles in southern England since 1978 using planks of wood, rope and a sighting device, inspired by the 1966 Tully "saucer nest" report in Australia. Their demonstration, and later videos of circle-makers at work, showed that even intricate formations could be produced overnight without leaving obvious signs of entry.

Circle makers

Following Bower and Chorley's disclosure, groups of anonymous "circlemakers" and land artists began producing ever more elaborate geometric designs — some hundreds of metres across, incorporating fractals, astronomical diagrams, and mathematical constants. Researchers such as John Lundberg and Rob Irving documented the practice as a form of guerrilla landscape art. Farmers sometimes suffer crop losses, while other landowners charge visitors admission to view formations.

Explanations

Scientific consensus holds that crop circles are man-made. Proposed natural causes, such as whirlwinds and ball lightning, and paranormal hypotheses persist among enthusiasts ("croppies"), who point to claimed anomalies in plant stems and electromagnetic readings, but no formation has withstood investigation as non-human in origin. The phenomenon has been widely studied as a modern legend and an example of how media coverage feeds imitation.
