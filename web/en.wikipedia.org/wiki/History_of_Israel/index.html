The history of Israel covers the region of the southern Levant, also known historically as Canaan, Palestine, or the Holy Land. The area was home to ancient Israelite kingdoms, and in the modern era became the site of the State of Israel, established in 1948. The earliest recorded mention of "Israel" appears on the Egyptian Merneptah Stele, dated to about 1208 BCE.

Ancient period

According to the Hebrew Bible, the Israelites emerged in the hill country of Canaan during the late Bronze Age and early Iron Age. The kingdoms of Israel and Judah are traditionally associated with the reigns of Saul, David, and Solomon in the 10th century BCE. The northern Kingdom of Israel was destroyed by the Neo-Assyrian Empire in about 722 BCE, and the southern Kingdom of Judah fell to the Neo-Babylonian Empire in 586 BCE, when the First Temple in Jerusalem was destroyed and much of the population was exiled to Babylon.

Following the conquest of Babylon by Cyrus the Great of Persia, Jews were permitted to return and rebuild the Temple, completed around 515 BCE. The region subsequently came under Hellenistic rule after the conquests of Alexander the Great, and an independent Jewish kingdom was briefly re-established under the Hasmonean dynasty following the Maccabean Revolt. Roman forces under Pompey intervened in 63 BCE, and the province of Judaea was incorporated into the Roman Empire. Jewish revolts in 66–73 CE and 132–135 CE (the Bar Kokhba revolt) were crushed, the Second Temple was destroyed in 70 CE, and Jerusalem was rebuilt as the Roman colony Aelia Capitolina.

Medieval and Ottoman periods

The region was ruled successively by the Byzantine Empire, the Rashidun and later caliphates, Crusader states, the Ayyubid and Mamluk sultanates, and, from 1517, the Ottoman Empire. Small Jewish communities persisted in Jerusalem, Safed, Tiberias, and Hebron, the four holy cities of Judaism. In the late 19th century, the Zionist movement, founded by Theodor Herzl, promoted Jewish immigration (aliyah) to Ottoman Palestine.

British Mandate

Britain captured Palestine from the Ottomans in World War I and governed it under a League of Nations Mandate from 1920. The 1917 Balfour Declaration expressed British support for a "national home for the Jewish people," while Palestinian Arabs opposed mass Jewish immigration, leading to periodic violence, including the 1929 riots and the 1936–1939 Arab revolt. Jewish immigration rose sharply in the 1930s as Jews fled persecution in Nazi Germany. After World War II and the Holocaust, the United Nations adopted the Partition Plan (Resolution 181) in November 1947, proposing separate Jewish and Arab states.

Establishment and early decades

On 14 May 1948, David Ben-Gurion proclaimed the establishment of the State of Israel. The following day, neighboring Arab states invaded, beginning the 1948 Arab–Israeli War. Israel survived and expanded beyond the partition boundaries; hundreds of thousands of Palestinian Arabs fled or were expelled in what Palestinians call the Nakba ("catastrophe"). Israel fought further wars with its neighbors in 1956 (the Suez Crisis), 1967 (the Six-Day War, in which Israel captured the West Bank, Gaza Strip, Sinai Peninsula, and Golan Heights), 1973 (the Yom Kippur War), and 1982 (the Lebanon War).

Peace process and recent history

Israel signed peace treaties with Egypt in 1979 and with Jordan in 1994. The Oslo Accords of 1993–1995, between Israel and the Palestine Liberation Organization, created the Palestinian Authority but did not produce a final settlement. Israel unilaterally withdrew from Gaza in 2005; the territory came under the control of Hamas in 2007. The Abraham Accords of 2020 normalized relations with the United Arab Emirates, Bahrain, Morocco, and Sudan. The October 7, 2023 Hamas-led attack on Israel and the ensuing war in Gaza marked the most severe escalation of the conflict in decades.
