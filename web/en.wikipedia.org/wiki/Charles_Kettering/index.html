Charles Kettering

Charles Franklin Kettering (August 29, 1876 – November 25, 1958) was an American inventor, engineer and businessman, and the holder of 186 patents. He was a founder of Delco and head of research at General Motors from 1920 to 1947. Among his most widely used inventions were the electric starting motor for automobiles and leaded gasoline — the latter now recognised as a major environmental and public-health disaster.

Early career and Delco

Kettering graduated from Ohio State University in 1904 and joined the National Cash Register Company (NCR), where he invented an electric cash register and an accounting machine system. In 1909 he co-founded the Dayton Engineering Laboratories Company (Delco). There he developed the first practical electric ignition, lighting and self-starter system, introduced on the 1912 Cadillac, which eliminated the dangerous hand crank and made driving accessible to far more people.

General Motors research chief

After Delco was sold to General Motors, Kettering directed GM's research laboratories for nearly three decades. His teams produced quick-drying automotive paints, high-compression engines, the refrigerant Freon (with Thomas Midgley Jr.), and — most controversially — tetraethyllead as a petrol additive ("Ethyl"), which suppressed engine knock but dispersed lead across the planet until its phase-out in the late twentieth century. He also worked on diesel engines that powered locomotives and on early solar energy conversion.

Philanthropy and legacy

With GM president Alfred P. Sloan, Kettering founded the Sloan-Kettering Institute for cancer research in New York (1945). Kettering University in Flint, Michigan, is named for him. His practical philosophy is summed up in his aphorisms, including "A problem well stated is a problem half-solved" and his dictum that the world hates change, yet it is the only thing that has brought progress.
