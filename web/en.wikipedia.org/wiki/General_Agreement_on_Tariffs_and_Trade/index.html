General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) was a legal agreement between many countries whose purpose was to promote international trade by reducing tariffs, quotas and other barriers and by eliminating discriminatory treatment in international commerce. Signed in Geneva in 1947 by 23 countries and applied provisionally from 1 January 1948, it served as the rulebook of world trade for nearly five decades, through eight rounds of multilateral negotiations that cut average industrial tariffs from around 40 per cent to under 4 per cent.

The GATT rested on two great principles: most-favoured-nation treatment (any advantage given to one member's goods applies to all) and national treatment (imported goods, once duties are paid, are treated no worse than domestic goods), with exceptions for customs unions and free-trade areas, safeguards, and special treatment for developing countries. In 1995 the GATT was absorbed into the World Trade Organization as GATT 1994, updated and supplemented by agreements on services (GATS) and intellectual property (TRIPS), and given a binding dispute-settlement system.

History

The GATT was a fallback: the planned International Trade Organization (Havana Charter, 1948) failed in the US Congress, leaving the provisional GATT as the de facto trade organisation. The Kennedy (1960s), Tokyo (1970s) and Uruguay (1986–94) rounds successively widened its scope from tariffs to non-tariff barriers, agriculture, textiles and finally services and intellectual property — the Uruguay Round creating the WTO.
