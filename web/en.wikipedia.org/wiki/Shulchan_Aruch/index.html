Shulchan Aruch

Author: Joseph Karo
Language: Hebrew
Published: 1565 (Venice)
Subject: Halakha (Jewish law)

The Shulchan Aruch (Hebrew: שֻׁלְחָן עָרוּךְ, literally "Set Table"), sometimes romanized Shulhan Arukh, is the most widely consulted code of Jewish law (halakha) in Orthodox Judaism. It was compiled by Rabbi Joseph ben Ephraim Karo in the 16th century and first published in Venice in 1565. The work condenses Karo's monumental commentary Beit Yosef into practical rulings organized for everyday use.

Structure

The Shulchan Aruch follows the four-part structure of Jacob ben Asher's earlier code, the Arba'ah Turim:

  • Orach Chayim ("Path of Life") — laws of prayer, synagogue, the Sabbath, and festivals.
  • Yoreh De'ah ("Teaches Knowledge") — dietary laws (kashrut), charity, mourning, and other subjects.
  • Even Ha'ezer ("Stone of Help") — marriage, divorce, and family law.
  • Choshen Mishpat ("Breastplate of Judgement") — civil and criminal law, courts, and finance.

The Rema's glosses

Karo, a Sephardic scholar working in Safed in Ottoman Palestine, largely followed Sephardic authorities. Rabbi Moses Isserles of Kraków, known as the Rema, added glosses (the Mapah, "Tablecloth") recording Ashkenazic custom and rulings where they differed. The combined text — Karo's rulings in the body, Isserles's glosses interwoven — became the standard printed Shulchan Aruch and the practical basis of Jewish law in both Sephardic and Ashkenazic communities, though acceptance was gradual and not universal; some authorities, including the Vilna Gaon, wrote critical commentaries, and the Hasidic movement produced adaptations such as the Shulchan Aruch HaRav.

Legacy

The work attracted hundreds of commentaries, among them the Magen Avraham, the Turei Zahav (Taz), and the Siftei Kohen (Shach) in the 17th century, and later summaries such as the 19th-century Kitzur Shulchan Aruch of Shlomo Ganzfried. Together with its commentaries, the Shulchan Aruch remains the reference code for Orthodox Jewish practice.
