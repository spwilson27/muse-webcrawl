Fertilisation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fertilisation is the fusion of gametes — in animals, a sperm and an egg — to form a zygote and initiate a new organism. It restores the diploid chromosome number halved by meiosis, combines two parents' genes (the point of sexual reproduction), and triggers the egg to begin development. In humans, fertilisation normally occurs in the fallopian tube within a day of ovulation; the resulting embryo implants in the uterus about a week later.

The process is heavily filtered: of the hundreds of millions of sperm ejaculated, a few hundred reach the egg, whose surface receptors and post-entry blocks (the cortical reaction) admit exactly one — polyspermy being lethal. In flowering plants, fertilisation is double: one fusion forms the embryo, the other the nutritive endosperm of the seed.

Assisted reproduction

In vitro fertilisation (IVF) — fertilisation in the laboratory, pioneered to the first 'test-tube baby', Louise Brown, in 1978 (Edwards and Steptoe; Edwards received the 2010 Nobel Prize) — has produced well over ten million births and spawned embryo screening, freezing and donation, with their attendant ethics.

In nature

External fertilisation (most fish, amphibians, corals spawning in synchrony) trades control for numbers; internal fertilisation enables land life and parental investment. Some species bypass fertilisation entirely (parthenogenesis), and a few fish and reptiles switch strategies — natural experiments in why sex persists.
