Marcher Lord

A Marcher Lord (Welsh: Barwn y Mers) was a noble appointed by the King of England to guard the border (the Welsh Marches) between England and Wales. Marcher Lords were a significant political institution in medieval Wales, acquiring their positions by inheritance, marriage or purchase, and wielding quasi-regal powers within their lordships.

Powers

Unlike ordinary English lords, the Marcher Lords exercised the royal rights (jura regalia) inside their territories: they could build castles without licence, hold their own courts with jurisdiction over all pleas except high treason, wage private war, raise armies, levy tolls and found boroughs and churches. Their lordships — around 140 at their greatest extent — lay outside the English common-law system and were governed by the custom of the March, a blend of Welsh and Norman law. The maxim "the king's writ does not run in the Marches" summed up their independence.

History

The system began with the Norman earls placed at Chester, Shrewsbury and Hereford by William the Conqueror, and expanded as Norman and Flemish adventurers carved out lordships in south and east Wales over the following two centuries. Families such as the Clares, Marshals, Mortimers and de Braoses became great Marcher dynasties. Even after Edward I's conquest of Wales (1282–83), the Marcher lordships retained their privileges, and Marcher rivalries helped fuel the baronial conflicts of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, including Glyndŵr's revolt and the Wars of the Roses, in which the Mortimer claim passed to the House of York.

The Laws in Wales Acts of 1535 and 1542 abolished the lordships, annexed their territories to England and Wales in shires, and extinguished the Marcher Lords' special powers.
