The dinoflagellates are a large group of single-celled flagellate eukaryotes, most of them marine plankton, characterised by two flagella: one wrapped around the cell's equator in a groove (the cingulum), causing a spinning motion — hence their name, from Greek dinos, "whirling" — and one trailing behind. About half of the roughly 2,000 living species are photosynthetic; others are predators, parasites or mixotrophs.

Ecology

Dinoflagellates are major primary producers in the oceans and important members of the phytoplankton. Some species live symbiotically inside reef-building corals — the zooxanthellae — supplying them with energy from photosynthesis; their expulsion under stress causes coral bleaching. Other species produce spectacular bioluminescence, making disturbed water glow at night, and some form harmful algal blooms known as red tides, producing toxins such as saxitoxin that can accumulate in shellfish and cause paralytic shellfish poisoning in humans.

Cell structure and life cycle

Many dinoflagellates have a cell covering of cellulose plates (the theca). Their nucleus, the dinokaryon, is unusual in lacking histones and keeping its chromosomes permanently condensed. Under adverse conditions many species form resistant resting cysts that sink to the sediment and can persist for years; fossil dinoflagellate cysts are used to date marine sediments.
