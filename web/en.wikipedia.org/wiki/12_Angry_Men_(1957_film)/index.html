12 Angry Men is a 1957 American courtroom drama film directed by Sidney Lumet and adapted by Reginald Rose from his own 1954 teleplay. The film tells the story of a jury of twelve men as they deliberate the conviction or acquittal of a teenager charged with murder on the basis of reasonable doubt. It stars Henry Fonda (who also produced), Lee J. Cobb, Ed Begley, E. G. Marshall, and Jack Warden.

Plot

In a New York City courthouse, a jury is instructed in the case of an 18-year-old boy accused of stabbing his father to death; a guilty verdict requires unanimity and carries a mandatory death sentence. In the jury room, an initial vote finds eleven jurors voting guilty and one, Juror 8 (Fonda), voting not guilty — not from certainty of innocence, but from a belief that the evidence deserves discussion. Over the course of the deliberations, Juror 8 casts doubt on the uniqueness of the murder weapon, the reliability of the witnesses, and the defendant's alleged shout of "I'm going to kill you." One by one, through argument, demonstration, and confrontation of personal prejudices, the other jurors change their votes, until the verdict is unanimously not guilty.

Production

The film was Lumet's feature directorial debut. It was shot almost entirely in a single jury-room set in just 21 days. Cinematographer Boris Kaufman progressively used longer lenses and lower camera angles as the film proceeds, visually intensifying the room's claustrophobia. With the exception of brief opening and closing scenes, the entire film takes place in the jury room on the hottest day of the year.

Reception and legacy

Although a modest box-office performer on release, the film was acclaimed by critics and nominated for three Academy Awards: Best Picture, Best Director, and Best Adapted Screenplay. It is now regarded as one of the greatest films ever made, frequently appearing on lists by the American Film Institute and the Sight and Sound polls. In 2007 it was selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry as being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant." A television remake directed by William Friedkin was released in 1997.
