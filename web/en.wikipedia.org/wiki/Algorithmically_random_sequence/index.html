Algorithmically random sequence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Visualisation of binary sequences of increasing Kolmogorov complexity

An algorithmically random sequence (or algorithmic random sequence) is a sequence of binary digits that appears random according to algorithmic information theory, even though it is generated by a deterministic process. The central notion, developed by Per Martin-Löf in 1966, is that of a Martin-Löf random sequence: an infinite binary sequence that passes all effective statistical tests for randomness, formalised as avoiding every effectively null set.

Definitions

Three equivalent characterisations are standard: (1) Martin-Löf randomness, defined via uniformly computably enumerable sequences of open sets of vanishing measure; (2) incompressibility — the prefix-free Kolmogorov complexity of the sequence's initial segments grows at least linearly, a criterion due to Leonid Levin and Claus Schnorr; and (3) unpredictability — no computable martingale can succeed unboundedly by betting on the sequence's bits.

Properties

Algorithmically random sequences have measure one among all binary sequences, yet no computable sequence is random. Chaitin's constant Ω is a famous example of a random real number. Random sequences satisfy the law of large numbers and every other effective statistical law, and the concept underpins the theory of algorithmic information and the foundations of probability.
