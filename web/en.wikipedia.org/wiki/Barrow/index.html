Barrow

A barrow is a mound of earth and stones raised over a grave or graves, characteristic of prehistoric burial customs in many parts of the world. Barrows are among the most common surviving monuments of Neolithic and Bronze Age Europe, particularly in Britain, where thousands remain. The term derives from the Old English beorg, meaning hill or mound.

Types

British barrows are conventionally divided into long barrows — elongated earthen mounds of the Neolithic period, often covering timber or stone burial chambers, such as West Kennet Long Barrow — and round barrows of the Bronze Age, which include bowl, bell, disc, saucer, and pond barrows. Round barrows often cover cremation burials accompanied by grave goods such as pottery, bronze weapons, and gold ornaments, as in the rich Wessex culture cemeteries around Stonehenge. Related monument types include the chambered cairns of Scotland, passage graves, and the kurgans of the Eurasian steppe.

Excavation and study

Antiquarians of the 17th to 19th centuries, including John Aubrey, William Stukeley, and Richard Colt Hoare, opened many barrows, sometimes crudely. Modern archaeology excavates them stratigraphically and uses radiocarbon dating to establish chronology. Barrows were often sited prominently on ridges and hilltops and reused for secondary burials over centuries; in the early medieval period, some were reused by Anglo-Saxon communities, and others were associated in folklore with treasure and supernatural guardians.
