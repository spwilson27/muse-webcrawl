Gallium is a chemical element with the symbol Ga and atomic number 31. It is a soft, silvery metal that is solid at room temperature but melts at 29.76 °C — just above it, liquefying in a warm hand. It is famous in the history of chemistry as a triumph of prediction: Dmitri Mendeleev forecast its existence and properties in 1871 as "eka-aluminium", and when Lecoq de Boisbaudran isolated it in Paris in 1875 (naming it for Gallia, France — with a pun, it was said, on his own name, le coq, the rooster, gallus in Latin), its measured density initially disagreed with Mendeleev's figure — whereupon Mendeleev insisted the measurement must be wrong, and re-measurement proved him right.

Properties and uses

Gallium wets glass and most metals, expands on freezing (like water), and has one of the longest liquid ranges of any metal (its boiling point is about 2,400 °C), making it useful in high-temperature thermometers. It does not occur free in nature and is obtained as a by-product of aluminium and zinc processing. Its compounds dominate its importance: gallium arsenide rivals silicon in high-frequency electronics and optoelectronics, and gallium nitride — the breakthrough material behind blue LEDs (Nobel Prize in Physics 2014) — powers modern lighting, Blu-ray lasers and fast chargers. Supply is concentrated in China, whose 2023 export controls made gallium a geopolitical material — a modern echo of the element-race politics of the periodic table era.
