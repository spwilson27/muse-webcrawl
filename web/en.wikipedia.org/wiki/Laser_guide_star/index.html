Laser guide star

A laser guide star is an artificial star image created for use in astronomical adaptive optics systems, which are used in large telescopes to correct atmospheric distortion of light (astronomical seeing). Because natural guide stars bright enough for wavefront sensing are not available over most of the sky, a laser is used to create an artificial reference source high in the atmosphere.

Types

Rayleigh beacons

A Rayleigh beacon is created by focusing a laser beam, often in the ultraviolet or green, into the lower atmosphere at an altitude of roughly 10–30 km. The backscattered light from air molecules (Rayleigh scattering) forms the artificial star. Rayleigh beacons are relatively inexpensive but sample only the lower part of the atmosphere and suffer from focal anisoplanatism (the cone effect), because the light cone from a low-altitude beacon does not fully overlap the cylindrical volume of light arriving from an astronomical object.

Sodium beacons

A sodium beacon uses a laser tuned to the D2 line of atomic sodium at 589 nm to excite sodium atoms in a layer of the mesosphere at about 90 km altitude, deposited there by ablating meteors. The atoms fluoresce, producing a much higher — and thus more representative — artificial star. Sodium laser guide stars are used at major observatories including Keck, the Very Large Telescope, Gemini, and Subaru.

Limitations

Even a sodium guide star cannot be used to measure overall tip and tilt (image motion), because the laser's upward path is disturbed by the same atmospheric turbulence, so the apparent position of the artificial star does not reveal the tilt of the incoming wavefront. A (much fainter) natural guide star is therefore still needed for tip–tilt correction. Other issues include laser fratricide between neighbouring telescopes, interference with satellites and aircraft — requiring coordination and, in the United States, clearance related to laser illuminations of space objects — and the elongated appearance of the sodium beacon when viewed from the edge of a large telescope aperture.

History

The concept was classified by the United States military during the Cold War era and was declassified in the early 1990s, after which it was rapidly adopted by civilian astronomy. A famous 1985 paper by Antoine Labeyrie had earlier suggested related ideas. The first sodium laser guide star systems on major telescopes came into routine science operation in the 2000s.
