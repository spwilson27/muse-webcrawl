Mockingbird

Mockingbirds are a group of New World passerine birds in the family Mimidae. They are best known for the habit of some species of mimicking the songs of other birds and the sounds of insects and amphibians, often loudly and in rapid succession.

Taxonomy

The mockingbirds are usually divided among three genera: Melanotis, Mimus and Toxostoma (the thrashers are close relatives in the same family). The largest genus, Mimus, includes the familiar northern mockingbird (Mimus polyglottos). The scientific name polyglottos means "many-tongued", a reference to the bird's imitative song.

Description and behaviour

Mockingbirds are medium-sized songbirds, typically grey and brown above with paler underparts, long tails and slender bills. They are highly vocal: a singing male may repeat phrases two to six times before moving to a new one, and its repertoire can include more than 200 distinct song types, many learned from other species and even from mechanical sounds.

They are strongly territorial and will aggressively defend nests and feeding areas against other birds, mammals and even humans. Their diet consists of insects, earthworms, berries and other fruit, varying seasonally.

Distribution

Mockingbirds are found throughout the Americas, from Canada to southern South America, including the Caribbean and the Galápagos Islands, where the mockingbirds observed by Charles Darwin played a part in his thinking about evolution by natural selection.

In culture

The northern mockingbird is the state bird of Arkansas, Florida, Mississippi, Tennessee and Texas. Mockingbirds feature prominently in literature, most famously in Harper Lee's 1960 novel To Kill a Mockingbird, where the bird symbolises innocence.
