Tanenbaum–Torvalds debate

The famous 1992 Usenet exchange on operating system kernel design

The Tanenbaum–Torvalds debate was a debate on the Usenet newsgroup comp.os.minix in January and February 1992 between Andrew S. Tanenbaum, creator of the MINIX operating system, and Linus Torvalds, creator of the Linux kernel, regarding kernel design: specifically, Tanenbaum's advocacy of microkernel architecture versus Torvalds' monolithic kernel design for Linux.

Background

Tanenbaum was a computer science professor at the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam and author of the textbook Operating Systems: Design and Implementation, which included MINIX, a small Unix-like system written for teaching purposes. In 1991 Torvalds, then a student at the University of Helsinki, released the first versions of Linux, a free operating system kernel with a monolithic design.

The debate

The exchange began on 29 January 1992, when Tanenbaum posted a message titled "LINUX is obsolete", arguing that the monolithic kernel was a regressive step back to the 1970s:

"I still maintain the point that designing a monolithic kernel in 1991 is a fundamental error. Be thankful you are not my student. You would not get a high grade for such a design."

Torvalds replied defending his design on practical grounds, noting that MINIX had significant limitations and that Linux was written as a hobby project for the Intel 386. Other participants, including Ken Thompson by some accounts of related discussions, joined the thread. The debate covered portability, Tanenbaum's prediction that the x86 architecture would soon be displaced, and the relative merits of microkernels and monolithic kernels.

Aftermath and analysis

The debate is frequently cited in computing literature and was reprinted in the book Open Sources: Voices from the Open Source Revolution (1999). In later years, both men played down any personal animosity; Tanenbaum stated that the disagreements were technical, not personal. The exchange remains a well-known case study in operating system design philosophy, even as modern systems have adopted hybrid approaches and Linux's modular design has blurred the original distinction.
