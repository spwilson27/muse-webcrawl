Antifreeze

An antifreeze is an additive which lowers the freezing point of a water-based liquid. An antifreeze mixture is used in internal combustion engines and other heat-transfer applications, such as HVAC chillers and solar water heaters, both to depress the freezing point in cold conditions and to raise the boiling point, allowing higher coolant temperatures.

Automotive use

The most common automotive antifreeze is based on ethylene glycol, mixed with water — typically at a 50/50 ratio — and blended with corrosion inhibitors (silicates, phosphates, or organic acids depending on the formulation, such as OAT and HOAT technologies). Ethylene glycol is toxic to humans and animals, which has prompted use of less toxic propylene glycol in some products. Engine coolant must be replaced periodically as inhibitors are depleted; neglect can lead to corrosion, overheating, or freeze damage to the engine block.

Other uses

Antifreezes are used in aircraft de-icing fluids, in cryobiology for preserving tissues (alongside other cryoprotectants), and as heat-transfer fluids in geothermal and industrial systems. Organisms that survive sub-zero environments produce natural antifreezes — see antifreeze proteins.
