History of education in Ireland

The history of education in Ireland extends from the monastic schools of early medieval Ireland, renowned across Europe for scholarship, through the bardic schools of Gaelic society, the hedge schools of the penal era, the establishment of a national system of primary education in 1831, and the expansion of free secondary education after 1967. The system has been shaped by the island's division in 1921, the dominant role of the Catholic Church as patron of most schools, and the revival of Irish-language education.

Early Irish monasteries such as Clonmacnoise were centres of learning.

Early and medieval Ireland

Monastic schools at Armagh, Bangor, Clonard and Clonmacnoise taught Latin, scripture and computistics, attracting students from Britain and the continent. Alongside them, secular bardic schools trained poets and brehons (jurists) in the native learned tradition. After the Anglo-Norman invasion, monastic education declined, and Tudor policy sought to supplant Gaelic learning with English schooling; Trinity College Dublin was founded in 1592.

Penal era and hedge schools

The Penal Laws barred Catholics from teaching or running schools. In response, itinerant teachers ran "hedge schools" — informal fee-paying schools, often literally held outdoors or in barns — teaching reading, writing, arithmetic and sometimes Latin and Greek classics to Catholic and dissenting children. By the 1820s an estimated 400,000 pupils attended some 9,000 hedge schools.

The national system

The Stanley letter of 1831 established a Board of National Education and a state-funded, nominally mixed system of primary schools, which in practice became largely denominational. The Intermediate Education Act 1878 and the founding of the Christian Brothers and other teaching orders reshaped secondary schooling. The universities question was addressed by the Royal University (1879) and the National University of Ireland (1908).

After partition

In the Irish Free State, the Irish language was made central to the curriculum and compulsory for civil-service entry. Free secondary education, announced by Donogh O'Malley in 1966 and introduced in 1967, together with the raising of the school-leaving age, transformed participation. In Northern Ireland, education remained largely segregated by community, with an integrated sector growing from 1981. Recent decades have seen curriculum reform, growth of multi-denominational Educate Together schools (from 1978), and debates over church patronage.
