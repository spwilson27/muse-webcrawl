The Liliaceae, or the lily family, are a family of perennial flowering plants, mostly bulbous or rhizomatous geophytes, in the order Liliales. The family occurs worldwide, mainly in temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere, with greatest diversity in the Himalayas, East Asia and North America. Important members include the true lilies (Lilium), tulips (Tulipa), fritillaries (Fritillaria), and trout lilies (Erythronium).

Description

Liliaceae are typically herbaceous perennials arising from bulbs or rhizomes, with parallel-veined leaves. The flowers are usually large and showy, with six undifferentiated perianth segments (tepals) in two whorls, six stamens, and a superior ovary of three fused carpels. The fruit is a capsule or, rarely, a berry.

Taxonomy

The family was once circumscribed very broadly, containing most petaloid monocots with superior ovaries. Molecular phylogenetic work by the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group successively moved many former members to other families: onions and amaryllis relatives to Amaryllidaceae, asparagus to Asparagaceae, hyacinths to Asparagaceae, and daylilies to Asphodelaceae. In the APG IV system the family comprises about 15 genera and some 610 species in subfamilies including Lilioideae and Calochortoideae.

Uses

Many species are grown as ornamentals — lilies and tulips are among the most valuable cut flowers and garden plants in the world. Lily bulbs are eaten in parts of East Asia, and some genera have traditional medicinal uses, though many are toxic to humans and pets.
