Emergency core cooling system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Simplified ECCS arrangement for a pressurized water reactor

Emergency core cooling systems (ECCS) are a set of backup systems in a nuclear power reactor designed to safely shut down and cool the reactor core during a loss-of-coolant accident (LOCA) or other emergency conditions. Because decay heat continues to be generated after a reactor trip, the core must be cooled even after shutdown; the ECCS supplies water to the reactor pressure vessel to prevent the fuel cladding from overheating and failing.

Components

A typical light-water reactor ECCS comprises several subsystems designed to operate over different pressure ranges and accident phases:

  • High-pressure injection – pumps borated water into the reactor coolant system while it remains at high pressure.
  • Accumulators – large tanks of borated water pressurized with nitrogen that discharge passively when reactor pressure falls below the accumulator pressure.
  • Low-pressure injection / residual heat removal – high-volume pumps that inject water once the system has depressurized, and which in the long term recirculate water from the containment sump through heat exchangers.
  • Core spray systems (in boiling water reactors) – spray water directly onto the core.
  • Containment spray – limits containment pressure and scrubs airborne fission products.

Function

ECCS actuation is typically automatic, triggered by low reactor coolant level or pressure signals. The systems are designed with redundancy and diversity so that no single failure prevents core cooling, and they are regulated under criteria such as 10 CFR 50.46 in the United States, which limits calculated peak cladding temperature during a design-basis LOCA to 2,200 °F (1,204 °C).
