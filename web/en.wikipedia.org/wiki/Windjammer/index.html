Windjammer

A windjammer is a large commercial sailing vessel with multiple masts, typically iron- or steel-hulled, that carried bulk cargo on long ocean routes in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The term was originally used somewhat contemptuously by steamship sailors but later became a romantic label for the last generation of great square-rigged cargo ships.

Characteristics

Windjammers were usually rigged as four- or five-masted barques, with square sails on all but the aftermost mast. They were economical to operate on routes where coal was expensive or unavailable, hauling cargoes such as grain, nitrate, wool, and timber. Famous examples include the Peking, the Pamir, the Passat, and the five-masted Preußen, one of the largest sailing ships ever built.

Decline

Steamships, the opening of the Suez and Panama canals, and the disruptions of the First World War steadily eroded the economics of sail. The grain races from Australia to Europe kept a handful of windjammers working into the 1930s under owners such as Gustaf Erikson of Åland. The loss of the Pamir in a hurricane in 1957 marked the effective end of commercial cargo sail, though many windjammers survive as museum ships, sail-training vessels, and cruise ships.
