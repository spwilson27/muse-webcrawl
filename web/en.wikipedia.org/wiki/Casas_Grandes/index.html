Casas Grandes

The adobe ruins of Paquimé, the principal settlement of Casas Grandes.

Casas Grandes (Spanish for “Great Houses”), also known by the name of its principal settlement Paquimé, is a prehistoric archaeological culture and site in the modern Mexican state of Chihuahua. Flourishing roughly between 1130 and 1450 CE, Paquimé became one of the largest and most complex settlements in the Greater Southwest and northern Mexico, noted for its multistory adobe great houses.

Paquimé

The site contains the remains of large adobe apartment compounds, some several stories high, along with ceremonial mounds, ballcourts in the Mesoamerican I-shaped form, an extensive water-control system of reservoirs and canals, and macaw breeding pens. Its T-shaped doorways recall those of the Ancestral Puebloan world to the north.

Economy and connections

Residents farmed maize, beans, squash, and cotton in the valley of the Casas Grandes River. Paquimé participated in long-distance exchange: scarlet macaws were raised for their feathers, and turquoise, copper bells, shell, and pottery moved through the region, linking the settlement to both Mesoamerica and the American Southwest. Its distinctive polychrome pottery, including Ramos Polychrome, is highly regarded.

Decline and legacy

Paquimé was abandoned in the 15th century; evidence of burning has prompted debate about whether the end involved conflict, drought, or social reorganization. Casas Grandes was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1998, and the nearby modern town and museum interpret the site.
