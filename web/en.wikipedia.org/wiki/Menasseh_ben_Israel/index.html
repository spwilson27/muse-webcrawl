Menasseh ben Israel

Menasseh ben Israel (c. 1604 – November 20, 1657), also known as Menashe Ben Israel, was a Portuguese-born Dutch rabbi, scholar, printer, and diplomat. He is best known for his mission to England in 1655–56, which helped pave the way for the readmission of Jews to England after their expulsion in 1290, and for founding the first Hebrew printing press in Amsterdam.

Life

Born Manoel Dias Soeiro on Madeira (or in Lisbon) to a family of New Christians (conversos) who fled the Portuguese Inquisition, he was raised in Amsterdam, where the family could practise Judaism openly. A prodigy in languages and learning, he became rabbi of the Neveh Shalom congregation at eighteen. In 1626 he established the first Hebrew press in the Netherlands, printing prayer books and scholarly works. Among his most famous portraits is an etching by Rembrandt, his Amsterdam neighbour.

The Hope of Israel and the English mission

Menasseh's writings attracted the attention of Christian millenarians in England, who saw Jewish settlement in England as a precondition for the Messiah's coming. In his influential work The Hope of Israel (1650), he argued that the Ten Lost Tribes had been found among the Native Americans, and that the dispersion of the Jews needed to encompass England for redemption to be complete. In 1655 he travelled to London to petition Oliver Cromwell's government for the formal readmission of the Jews. Although the Whitehall Conference of December 1655 reached no formal verdict, Jews were thereafter permitted to reside and worship in England in practice, marking the de facto resettlement.

Works and death

His major works include Conciliador, reconciling apparent contradictions in Scripture, and Nishmat Hayim, on the immortality of the soul. Saddened by the death of his son Samuel, who had accompanied him, Menasseh died in Middelburg in 1657 while returning to the Netherlands.
