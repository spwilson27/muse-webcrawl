The Carduchii (also spelled Karduchii; Greek: Καρδοῦχοι, Kardoûkhoi) were an ancient people inhabiting the mountainous country of Corduene, in the upper Tigris region south of Lake Van, roughly corresponding to parts of modern south-eastern Turkey and northern Iraq. They are best known from the account of the Greek historian and soldier Xenophon, whose Anabasis describes the harrowing seven-day passage of the Ten Thousand Greek mercenaries through Carduchian territory in 401–400 BC, during which the fiercely independent mountaineers harassed the Greeks with stones and arrows from the heights.

History and description

Classical authors described the Carduchii as formidable warriors living in villages among the defiles of the Zagros foothills, nominally within the Persian Achaemenid Empire but in practice autonomous and often in revolt against the Great King. Xenophon portrayed them as lacking a central king but capable of uniting rapidly against invaders; attempts at negotiation failed, and the Greeks fought their way through, burning villages as they went. Strabo and later geographers mention the region (as Gordyene or Corduene) and its people in accounts of the wars between Rome, Parthia and Armenia.

Relation to the Kurds

The Carduchii are frequently proposed as ancestors of the modern Kurds: the names "Karduchii" and the later classical "Kurd" (Cyrtii/Kyrtii) inhabit the same geography, and the region of Corduene corresponds closely to the Kurdish heartland. However, scholars remain divided — some regard the resemblance of names as suggestive rather than conclusive, and ethnic identification across two millennia of linguistic and population change is contested. The term "Kurd" in early medieval sources denoted a social and pastoral-nomadic category as much as an ethnic one.
