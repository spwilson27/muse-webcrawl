Benchmark (finance)

In finance, a benchmark is a standard against which the performance of a security, mutual fund, exchange-traded fund or investment manager can be measured. Generally, broad market and market-segment stock and bond indexes are used for this purpose. A benchmark allows an investor to judge whether a manager has added value (alpha) relative to simply holding the market, and forms the basis for performance fees and risk measures such as beta, tracking error and information ratio.

Types

Common equity benchmarks include the S&P 500, the MSCI World Index, the FTSE 100 and the Nikkei 225. Bond benchmarks include the Bloomberg Barclays Aggregate Bond Index. Funds are typically assigned a benchmark matching their asset class, region and style (for example, large-cap value or emerging-market debt). "Benchmark drift" occurs when a portfolio's actual exposures diverge from the benchmark over time.

Benchmarks as rates

The term also denotes reference interest rates such as SOFR (which replaced LIBOR), EURIBOR and SONIA, used to price loans, derivatives and floating-rate securities. Following the LIBOR manipulation scandal, regulators reformed benchmark governance under frameworks such as the EU Benchmarks Regulation and the IOSCO Principles for Financial Benchmarks.

Criticism

Critics note that cap-weighted benchmarks concentrate in the largest companies, that managers may "closet index" while charging active fees, and that an inappropriate benchmark can flatter performance. Benchmark selection is therefore a key governance decision for pension funds and other asset owners.
