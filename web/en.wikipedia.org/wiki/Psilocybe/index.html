Psilocybe

Psilocybe is a genus of gilled mushrooms in the family Hymenogastraceae, growing worldwide. Many species in the genus contain the psychedelic compounds psilocybin and psilocin, and are commonly known as "magic mushrooms." The genus name derives from the Greek words psilos (ψιλός), meaning "bare" or "smooth," and kubos (κύβος), meaning "cube," referring to the smooth cap of many species.

Taxonomy

The genus was first circumscribed by German mycologist Paul Kummer in 1871. Modern molecular phylogenetic studies have shown that Psilocybe as traditionally defined was polyphyletic, and the non-bluing, non-psychoactive species were moved to the sister genus Deconica in 2009. The bluing, psilocybin-containing species retained the name Psilocybe.

Description

Psilocybe species are small to medium-sized mushrooms with a hygrophanous cap that changes color as it absorbs or loses moisture. A characteristic feature of the psychoactive species is a blue bruising reaction when the fruit body is handled or damaged, caused by oxidation of psilocybin. The spore print is typically dark purplish-brown.

Distribution and habitat

Species of Psilocybe are found on every continent. They grow in a variety of habitats, including dung, compost, wood chips, mulch, and decaying plant material in grasslands and forests. Well-known species include Psilocybe cubensis, Psilocybe semilanceata (the liberty cap), Psilocybe cyanescens, and Psilocybe azurescens.

Psychoactive species

More than 100 species are known to contain psilocybin. These mushrooms have a long history of ritual use among indigenous peoples of Mesoamerica, where they were known in Nahuatl as teonanácatl ("divine mushroom"). Their legal status varies widely by country; possession is prohibited in most jurisdictions, though some have decriminalized them.
