Rood screen

A rood screen (also called a chancel screen, choir screen or jubé) is a partition, usually richly carved in wood or stone, that separated the chancel of a medieval church from the nave where the congregation worshipped. The word rood is the Old English word for a cross or crucifix: the screen was surmounted by a large crucifix — the rood — flanked by figures of the Virgin Mary and St John, often reached by a loft used for readings, singing or an organ.

History

Screens developed from earlier chancel barriers and templon screens, becoming common from the thirteenth century onward. In England they were frequently painted with rows of saints on their lower panels (the dado). During the English Reformation, roods were almost universally destroyed as idolatrous and many screens removed or whitewashed; the Council of Trent era saw similar removals in Catholic lands, though notable screens survive in Brittany, Germany and Scandinavia. The nineteenth-century Gothic Revival restored screens to many churches, notably through the work of Augustus Pugin.

Surviving examples

Outstanding surviving screens include those at St Laurence, Ludlow; Ranworth, Norfolk (famous for its painted panels); and the stone screen of York Minster. In Eastern Orthodox churches the iconostasis performs a comparable function.
