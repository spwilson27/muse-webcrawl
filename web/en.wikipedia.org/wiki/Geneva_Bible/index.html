The Geneva Bible is one of the most historically significant translations of the Bible into English, published in Geneva in 1560 by Protestant scholars who had fled Marian persecution in England. It was the first English Bible to adopt verse numbers, the first printed in roman type with a mass-produced, affordable format, and the first to include extensive marginal study notes.

Title page of the Geneva Bible, first edition, 1560.

Features

The Geneva Bible's Calvinist marginal notes explained the text for lay readers and sometimes criticised earthly rulers, which later made the version unwelcome to James I of England and helped prompt the King James Version of 1611. Despite that, the Geneva Bible remained the household Bible of English Protestantism for generations: it was the Bible of Shakespeare, of John Bunyan, and of the Pilgrims who carried it to New England on the Mayflower. It is sometimes called the "Breeches Bible" because Genesis 3:7 in early editions says Adam and Eve "sewed fig leaves together, and made themselves breeches".

Text and influence

The New Testament (1557), translated by William Whittingham with others, drew on the Greek text and earlier English versions; the complete Bible of 1560 added the Old Testament translated from Hebrew. Its phrasing influenced the King James translators, who consulted it, and some 140 editions appeared between 1560 and 1644 — long after the Authorized Version nominally superseded it.
