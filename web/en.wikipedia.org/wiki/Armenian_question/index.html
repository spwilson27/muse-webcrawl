The Armenian question was the debate following the Congress of Berlin in 1878 concerning how Armenians in the Ottoman Empire should be treated. The term became commonplace among diplomatic circles and in the popular press for the political problem posed by the condition of the Ottoman Armenians, comparable to the broader "Eastern Question" about the empire's decline. It encompassed demands for reforms, security of life and property, and — in the eyes of European powers and Armenian political organizations — guarantees protected by international oversight.

Background

Armenians formed a substantial Christian minority, concentrated in six eastern vilayets (Van, Erzurum, Mamuretülaziz, Bitlis, Diyarbakır and Sivas) as well as in Cilicia, Istanbul and other cities. Grievances over taxation, Kurdish tribal raids, land seizures and lack of judicial recourse grew during the 19th century. Article 61 of the Treaty of Berlin (1878) committed the Sublime Porte to implement reforms in the Armenian-inhabited provinces and guarantee security against Circassians and Kurds, with the Great Powers to supervise.

Escalation

Reforms were not implemented. Under Sultan Abdul Hamid II, the Hamidian massacres of 1894–1896 killed tens of thousands of Armenians. The Adana massacre followed in 1909. During the First World War, the Committee of Union and Progress government carried out the Armenian genocide of 1915–1917, in which hundreds of thousands to over a million Armenians were killed or deported.

Aftermath

The Treaty of Sèvres (1920) envisioned an independent Armenian state, but it was never ratified; the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) omitted the Armenian question entirely. The issue lives on in debates over international recognition of the Armenian genocide and in Armenian diaspora politics.
