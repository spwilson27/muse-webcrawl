African trypanosomiasis

African trypanosomiasis, also known as African sleeping sickness, is an insect-borne parasitic infection of humans and other animals. It is caused by the species Trypanosoma brucei, transmitted by the bite of an infected tsetse fly (Glossina species). Two subspecies infect humans: T. b. gambiense, responsible for most cases in West and Central Africa, and T. b. rhodesiense, found in East and Southern Africa.

Signs and symptoms

In the first stage, the parasites multiply in subcutaneous tissues, blood, and lymph; symptoms include bouts of fever, headaches, joint pains, and itching, sometimes with a chancre at the bite site and swollen lymph nodes (Winterbottom's sign in gambiense infection). In the second stage, the parasites cross the blood–brain barrier and infect the central nervous system, causing confusion, sensory disturbances, poor coordination, and the characteristic disruption of the sleep cycle that gives the disease its name. Without treatment, the disease is fatal.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by finding the parasite in blood smears, lymph node aspirate, or cerebrospinal fluid; serological tests such as the card agglutination test (CATT) are used for screening gambiense cases. Treatment depends on the stage and subspecies: pentamidine for first-stage gambiense disease, suramin for first-stage rhodesiense disease, and for second-stage disease combinations including eflornithine with nifurtimox, or melarsoprol. The oral drug fexinidazole, approved in 2018, simplified treatment of gambiense disease.

Epidemiology

The disease occurs regularly in parts of sub-Saharan Africa in populations with limited access to healthcare. Reported cases have fallen dramatically, from tens of thousands annually in the late 1990s to fewer than 1,000 per year in recent years, following sustained control efforts coordinated by the World Health Organization; gambiense disease has been targeted for elimination of transmission.
