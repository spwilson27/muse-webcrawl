Self-fulfilling prophecy

Self-fulfilling prophecy
FieldSociology, social psychology
Coined byRobert K. Merton (1948)
Related conceptsPygmalion effect, placebo effect, Thomas theorem

A self-fulfilling prophecy is a prediction that comes true at least in part as a result of a person's belief or expectation that it will come true. Defined by sociologist Robert K. Merton in 1948, the concept describes how a false definition of a situation evokes new behaviour that makes the originally false conception come true.

Merton's formulation

Merton illustrated the idea with a bank run: depositors falsely believe a sound bank is insolvent, withdraw their funds, and thereby actually ruin it. He drew on the Thomas theorem — "if men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences" — formulated by sociologists W. I. Thomas and Dorothy Swaine Thomas in 1928. Older precedents range from the Greek myth of Oedipus to Keynesian ideas of confidence in markets.

The Pygmalion effect

In education, the best-known form is the Pygmalion effect: teachers' expectations shape pupil performance. In the 1968 study by Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson, pupils randomly labelled as "about to bloom" gained more IQ than classmates, a result much discussed and debated in follow-up research. The negative counterpart — lowered expectations depressing outcomes — is called the Golem effect.

Other applications

Self-fulfilling prophecies are invoked in medicine (placebo and nocebo effects), economics (inflation expectations, speculative bubbles), criminology (labelling theory), and stereotyping, where expectations channel the behaviour both of the perceiver and the perceived.
