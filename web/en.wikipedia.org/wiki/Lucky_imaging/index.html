Lucky imaging

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lucky imaging (also called lucky exposures) is one form of speckle imaging used for astronomical photography. Speckle imaging techniques use a high-speed camera with exposure times short enough (100 ms or less) so that the changes in the Earth's atmosphere during the exposure are minimal.

With lucky imaging, those optimum exposures least affected by the atmosphere (typically around 10%) are chosen and combined into a single image by the shift-and-add method, yielding much higher angular resolution than would be possible with a single, longer exposure, which includes all the frames.

Explanation

Images taken with ground-based telescopes are subject to blurring by the motion of air in the atmosphere. For many astronomical imaging programs, a resolution of approximately 0.5–1.0 arcseconds is required. However, because of atmospheric seeing, only about 10% of images taken at high speed happen by chance to catch a moment when the atmospheric distortion over the telescope aperture is minimal. By selecting only these sharpest frames and aligning them on a bright reference star or object, much of the atmospheric blurring can be removed.

The technique was first developed by amateur astronomers using webcams and later adopted with scientific electron-multiplying CCD (EMCCD) cameras. In 2007, astronomers at Cambridge and Caltech used a lucky imaging camera on the 5-metre Hale Telescope at Palomar Observatory to obtain visible-light images with resolution rivalling that of the Hubble Space Telescope, around 35 milliarcseconds.

Advantages and limitations

Lucky imaging requires the target or a nearby reference to be bright enough to measure shifts in very short exposures, which limits the technique to relatively bright objects. It is most effective on telescopes of moderate aperture (roughly 2–4 metres without adaptive optics), where the probability of a "lucky" moment of good seeing is highest. It is widely used by amateur astronomers for high-resolution imaging of the Moon and planets.
