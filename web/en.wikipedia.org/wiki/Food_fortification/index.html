Food fortification

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Food fortification is the practice of adding one or more essential micronutrients — vitamins and minerals — to a food during processing, usually to prevent or correct a demonstrated deficiency in a population. When nutrients lost in processing are replaced, the process is often called enrichment.

Rationale and vehicles

Fortification works best with staple foods eaten regularly by the whole population, so the added nutrient reaches people without requiring behaviour change. Classic examples include iodine added to salt (which largely eliminated goitre and cretinism in many countries), vitamin D in milk, B vitamins and iron in wheat flour, vitamin A in sugar and cooking oil, fluoride in water, and folic acid in flour and grain products — mandatory in many countries since the 1990s to reduce neural tube defects in newborns.

History

Large-scale fortification began in the 1920s with iodised salt in Switzerland and the United States. During and after the Second World War, governments mandated fortified flour and margarine to combat deficiency diseases. International agencies such as UNICEF, the WHO and the Global Alliance for Improved Nutrition continue to promote fortification against "hidden hunger" — micronutrient malnutrition affecting an estimated two billion people.

Debate

Fortification is generally inexpensive and cost-effective, but it is debated: nutrients may be poorly absorbed, fortification can in principle deliver excess to some groups while missing others, and critics argue it medicalises food or favours processed staples over dietary diversity. Biofortification — breeding crops with higher nutrient content, such as vitamin-A-rich Golden Rice and orange sweet potato — is a complementary strategy.
