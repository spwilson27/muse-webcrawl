Fisher's exact test

Fisher's exact test is a statistical significance test used in the analysis of contingency tables. Named after its inventor, Ronald Fisher, it is one of a class of exact tests, so called because the significance of the deviation from a null hypothesis can be calculated exactly, rather than relying on an approximation that becomes exact only for large samples.

Use

The test is useful for categorical data that result from classifying objects in two different ways; it tests the null hypothesis that the classifications are independent. It is especially valuable when sample sizes are small, where the chi-squared test may be unreliable because expected cell counts are low.

Computation

For a 2×2 table with cell counts a, b, c and d and total n, and with row and column margins treated as fixed, the probability of the observed table under the null hypothesis follows the hypergeometric distribution:

p = [(a+b)! (c+d)! (a+c)! (b+d)!] / [a! b! c! d! n!]

The p-value is obtained by summing the probabilities of all tables with the same margins that are as extreme as, or more extreme than, the table observed. Extensions to larger r×c tables exist, commonly computed with the Freeman–Halton extension, though the enumeration of tables grows rapidly and Monte Carlo methods are often used.

Example

A famous example is Fisher's "lady tasting tea" experiment, in which a lady claimed she could tell whether milk or tea was poured first into a cup. Fisher designed an experiment with eight cups, four of each type, and used the exact test to assess whether her correct classifications could plausibly have arisen by chance.
