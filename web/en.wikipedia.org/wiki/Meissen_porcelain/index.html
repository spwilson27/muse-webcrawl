Meissen porcelain

Meissen porcelain or Meissen china was the first European hard-paste porcelain, produced experimentally in 1708 by Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus and developed after his death by his assistant Johann Friedrich Böttger, under the patronage of Augustus the Strong of Saxony. Production began in 1710 at the royal factory in the Albrechtsburg castle in Meissen, Germany, and Meissen remained Europe's dominant porcelain factory until the mid-18th century. Its trademark is the crossed swords in underglaze blue, in use in varying forms since the 1720s.

History

Chinese porcelain imported by the Dutch East India Company was enormously prized in Europe, and Augustus the Strong sought its secret. After experiments in Dresden, production moved to Meissen in 1710, where the process was a closely guarded state secret (the closely held formula being an "arcanum"). Arcanists leaked the secret across Europe, enabling factories at Vienna, Venice, and later Höchst, Sèvres, and Berlin.

Under Johann Joachim Kändler, modeller from 1731, Meissen produced celebrated tableware and figurines, including the Swan Service for Count Brühl and the famous monkey band and commedia dell'arte figures. Famous decorations include the "Blue Onion" pattern (from c. 1739) and chinoiserie scenes. The factory (today Staatliche Porzellan-Manufaktur Meissen) still produces porcelain by hand.

Marks

Early pieces bear incised or painted marks such as "AR" (Augustus Rex) and "KPM"; the crossed swords, adopted c. 1724, became one of the oldest trademarks in continuous use, with variations allowing dating by period.
