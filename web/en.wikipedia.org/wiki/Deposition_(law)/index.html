Deposition (law)

A deposition in law is sworn out-of-court testimony of a witness, recorded by a court reporter (and often videotaped), for later use in litigation. In the United States it is a core part of discovery under Rules 30 and 31 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. The witness (deponent) is placed under oath, questioned by opposing counsel, and subject to cross-examination, much as at trial.

Procedure

Parties are deposed on notice; non-parties by subpoena under Rule 45. A presumptive limit is one day of seven hours for a deposition under Rule 30. Objections are noted on the record; instructions not to answer are limited. Transcripts may be used at trial to impeach, for unavailable witnesses, or (for parties and experts) as substantive evidence in specified circumstances.

Purposes

Depositions discover facts, lock in testimony, assess credibility, preserve testimony, and support motions such as summary judgment (see Judgment). Related tools include interrogatories, requests for production and requests for admission.

In other jurisdictions

Most common-law systems outside the U.S. make far less use of oral depositions, relying more on witness statements and disclosure of documents.
