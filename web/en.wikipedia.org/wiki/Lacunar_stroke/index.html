Lacunar stroke or lacunar cerebral infarct (LACI) is the most common type of ischaemic stroke, resulting from the occlusion of small penetrating arteries that provide blood to the brain's deep structures. The word lacunar comes from the Latin lacuna, meaning an empty space or cavity, referring to the small fluid-filled cavities that remain after the infarcted tissue is resorbed.

Lacunar infarcts account for roughly a quarter of all ischaemic strokes. Patients who suffer them tend to have a better short-term prognosis than those with larger cortical strokes, although multiple lacunes accumulating over time can cause vascular dementia and gait disturbance.

Signs and symptoms

Five classic lacunar syndromes have been described, each associated with a lesion in a particular location:

  • Pure motor stroke/hemiparesis — weakness of the face, arm and leg on one side without sensory or visual involvement; usually the posterior limb of the internal capsule or the pons.
  • Pure sensory stroke — numbness or paraesthesia of the face, arm, leg and trunk on one side; usually the thalamus.
  • Ataxic hemiparesis — weakness and clumsiness on one side, with ataxia out of proportion to the weakness.
  • Dysarthria–clumsy hand syndrome — slurred speech and clumsiness of one hand.
  • Mixed sensorimotor stroke — combined weakness and sensory loss.

Cause

Lacunar infarcts are caused by disease of a single penetrating artery, most commonly lipohyalinosis — a degenerative thickening of the vessel wall associated with chronic hypertension — and by microatheroma at the origin of the penetrating vessel. They are categorised in the TOAST classification of stroke as "small-vessel occlusion".

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the clinical syndrome together with brain imaging. CT may show a small hypodensity in a deep structure, but MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging is more sensitive, showing a small bright lesion typically less than 15–20 mm in diameter in the territory of a penetrating artery. Investigation also aims to exclude other stroke mechanisms such as carotid stenosis and cardioembolism.

Treatment

Acute treatment follows the general principles of ischaemic stroke care, including thrombolysis within the treatment window where eligible. Long-term management concentrates on secondary prevention: blood-pressure control, antiplatelet therapy, statins, smoking cessation and glycaemic control in diabetes.

History

The term was coined in the 19th century by Amédée Dechambre and popularised by the pathologist Maxime Durand-Fardel; C. Miller Fisher's clinicopathological studies in the 1960s and 1970s established the modern concept of lacunar syndromes and their small-vessel pathology.
