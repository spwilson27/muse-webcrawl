Jalapeño

Species: Capsicum annuum

Origin: Mexico

Heat: Medium

Scoville scale: 4,000–8,500 SHU

The jalapeño is a medium-sized chili pepper pod type cultivar of the species Capsicum annuum. A mature jalapeño chili is 5–10 cm (2–4 in) long and hangs from the plant in a rounded, firm, smooth flesh pod. The name comes from Xalapa (Jalapa), the capital of Veracruz, Mexico, where the pepper was traditionally grown.

Cultivation

Jalapeños are grown widely in Mexico and the United States, especially in New Mexico and Texas. The plants thrive in warm climates and the peppers are usually picked while still green, though they ripen to red if left on the plant. The "corking" — light-colored striations on the skin — is often taken as a sign of maturity and flavor.

Culinary use

Jalapeños are used fresh in salsas, pico de gallo, and guacamole, pickled as a condiment, and stuffed in dishes such as jalapeño poppers. When dried and smoked, the ripe red pepper becomes the chipotle. Their heat, rated between roughly 4,000 and 8,500 Scoville heat units, comes from the compound capsaicin.

History

The pepper was cultivated in Mexico long before European contact. In 1999, a jalapeño traveled into space aboard a NASA shuttle, and the pepper has been celebrated in eating contests and festivals across the American Southwest.
