Hindenburg Line

The Hindenburg Line (German: Siegfriedstellung, the "Siegfried Position") was a German defensive line on the Western Front during the First World War, built during the winter of 1916–1917 across northern France. In the spring of 1917 the German armies withdrew to it in Operation Alberich, shortening their front by some 40 km (25 miles), freeing divisions for other duties, and devastating the evacuated territory. The line ran roughly from near Arras south-east to the Aisne near Laffaux, comprising deep belts of barbed wire, concrete pillboxes, dugouts, and trench systems arranged for defence in depth.

Construction and the 1917 withdrawal

Named by the British after Field Marshal Paul von Hindenburg (the Germans called it the Siegfriedstellung), the position was built by German workers and Russian prisoners of war behind the existing front. The withdrawal to it in February–March 1917 caught the Allies off guard and frustrated plans for a spring offensive on the old front line; the abandoned zone was systematically destroyed — roads cratered, wells poisoned, buildings demolished, and booby traps left — and some 125,000 French civilians were deported or evacuated.

1918: breaking the line

During the Hundred Days Offensive of 1918 the Allies breached the system in a series of set-piece attacks. The Battle of the St Quentin Canal (29 September 1918), in which British, Australian, and American troops crossed the canal — a formidable obstacle within the line — supported by tanks and a massive artillery preparation, is generally regarded as the decisive rupture of the Hindenburg Line. German resistance collapsed along the front in the following weeks, contributing to the request for an armistice.
