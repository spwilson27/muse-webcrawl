Bloody April refers to April 1917, a period during the First World War in which the British Royal Flying Corps (RFC) suffered especially heavy casualties supporting the Battle of Arras. The German Luftstreitkräfte, equipped with superior fighter aircraft such as the Albatros D.III and organised in elite Jagdstaffeln, inflicted losses of 245 aircraft on the RFC; the life expectancy of a new British pilot at the front fell to around eleven days.

Background

To support General Allenby's offensive at Arras, the RFC was ordered to maintain an aggressive offensive patrol policy deep over German lines, carrying out reconnaissance, artillery spotting and photography essential to the ground battle. Despite obsolete equipment such as the B.E.2 and F.E.2b, RFC commander Hugh Trenchard persisted with offensive operations.

The fighting

German fighter units, concentrated for the battle under the tactical system developed by Oswald Boelcke, included Manfred von Richthofen's Jasta 11. Richthofen alone claimed 21 victories in April 1917, his most successful month. German pilots normally fought over their own territory, with the wind advantage and the chance to survive being shot down; British crews lost over the lines were killed or captured.

Aftermath

Despite the losses, the RFC fulfilled its missions and the German air service failed to prevent British reconnaissance. New types such as the S.E.5, Sopwith Camel and Bristol F.2 Fighter reached the front in the following months, gradually redressing the balance.
