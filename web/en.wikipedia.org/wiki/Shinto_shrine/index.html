A Shinto shrine (Japanese: 神社, jinja) is a structure whose purpose is to house ("enshrine") one or more kami, the deities and spirits of the Shinto religion, and to provide a place for their worship. Unlike a Buddhist temple, a shrine is defined by the presence of a honden, the sanctuary in which the kami's sacred object (shintai) is kept. There are estimated to be some 80,000 Shinto shrines in Japan, from great shrines such as Ise Grand Shrine and Fushimi Inari-taisha to small wayside shrines.

Structure

A typical shrine precinct (keidai) is entered through one or more torii gates and includes:

  • Sandō — the approach path, often lined with stone lanterns
  • Temizuya — a purification pavilion where visitors rinse hands and mouth
  • Haiden — the hall of worship, where visitors pray before the deity
  • Honden — the main sanctuary, normally closed to the public, housing the shintai (a mirror, sword, jewel or other object in which the kami resides)
  • Kagura-den and shamusho — halls for sacred dance and the shrine office, where amulets (omamori) and fortune slips (omikuji) are sold
  • Shimenawa — sacred straw ropes marking pure space, also hung on sacred trees and rocks

Worship and festivals

The customary form of prayer is "two bows, two claps, one bow" before the haiden, after tossing a coin into the offering box. Shrines hold festivals (matsuri) honouring their kami, often featuring processions of portable shrines (mikoshi). New Year visits (hatsumōde), weddings, and the children's rite shichi-go-san are major occasions of shrine visitation. Priests (kannushi) and shrine maidens (miko) serve the kami with ritual, music and dance.

History

Early Shinto worship took place at sacred natural sites — mountains, waterfalls, rocks (iwakura) and groves (himorogi) — without permanent buildings. Permanent shrine architecture developed from the early centuries CE under the influence of continental building forms; the oldest styles, such as the shinmei-zukuri of Ise and taisha-zukuri of Izumo, preserve prehistoric raised-storehouse forms. Ise Grand Shrine is famously rebuilt every twenty years in the shikinen sengū ceremony. During the Meiji era the state reorganised shrines into a national hierarchy (State Shinto); after 1945 shrines became independent religious corporations, most joining the Association of Shinto Shrines.

Shrine ranks and names

Shrine names reflect their deity and status: taisha ("grand shrine"), jingū (shrines closely tied to the Imperial House, such as Meiji Jingū), gū (shrines for imperial princes or great deities), and hachiman-gū, tenjin and inari shrines dedicated to particular kami. Fushimi Inari-taisha heads a network of some 30,000 Inari shrines.
