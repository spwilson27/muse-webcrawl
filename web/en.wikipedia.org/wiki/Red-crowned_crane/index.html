The red-crowned crane (Antigone japonensis, also classified as Grus japonensis), also called the Manchurian crane or Japanese crane, is a large East Asian crane and among the rarest cranes in the world. It is named for the patch of bare red skin on its crown. Its snow-white plumage, black secondary feathers and graceful courtship dances make it a potent cultural symbol of longevity, fidelity and good fortune in China, Japan and Korea.

Description

Adults stand about 150–158 cm tall with a wingspan of up to 2.5 metres and weigh 8–10.5 kg, making them among the heaviest cranes. They are white with black wing feathers that, when the wings are folded, can make the tail appear black. Males are slightly larger than females. The birds are known for their elaborate synchronised dancing displays, performed year-round by bonded pairs.

Distribution and habitat

Two main populations exist: a migratory mainland population breeding in the wetlands of the Amur River basin in Russia, China and Mongolia and wintering in Korea and east-central China, and a non-migratory island population on Hokkaido, Japan. They inhabit reed marshes, wet grasslands and river floodplains, feeding on small animals, fish, amphibians and plant matter.

Conservation

The species is listed as Vulnerable, with a total population estimated at only a few thousand mature individuals. Threats include wetland drainage and conversion to agriculture, disturbance and collisions with power lines. The Hokkaido population, reduced to a few dozen birds in the early 20th century, recovered to over a thousand through winter feeding programmes and legal protection.

In culture

The crane is a beloved motif in East Asian art, appearing on Japanese kimono, folding screens and the former ¥1000 note. Korean and Chinese folklore associate it with immortals and long life; Japan Airlines used a red-crowned crane as its logo. It is unofficially considered a national bird candidate in China.
