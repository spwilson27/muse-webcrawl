Hyperalgesia

Hyperalgesia is an abnormally increased sensitivity to pain, in which a stimulus that normally causes pain is perceived as much more painful than usual. It results from damage to nociceptors or the peripheral or central nervous system, and is a hallmark of inflammation and neuropathic pain. It is distinct from allodynia, in which pain is caused by a stimulus that does not normally provoke pain.

Types and mechanisms

Primary hyperalgesia occurs at the site of tissue injury, where inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins, bradykinin, and nerve growth factor sensitize nociceptor endings, lowering their thresholds. Secondary hyperalgesia occurs in surrounding uninjured tissue and is attributed to central sensitization in the spinal cord, mediated largely by NMDA-receptor-dependent amplification of incoming signals. A clinically important form is opioid-induced hyperalgesia, a paradoxical state in which patients receiving opioids — especially at high doses — become more sensitive to pain, complicating pain management and differing from tolerance in that increasing the dose worsens it.

Clinical significance

Hyperalgesia accompanies sunburn, wounds, arthritis, fibromyalgia, and nerve injuries, and helps protect injured tissue during healing. Treatments target the underlying cause and may include NSAIDs (which reduce prostaglandin-mediated sensitization), gabapentinoids, and, for opioid-induced cases, dose reduction or NMDA antagonists such as ketamine.
