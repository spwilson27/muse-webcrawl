Municipality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A municipality is usually a single administrative division having corporate status and powers of self-government or jurisdiction as granted by national or regional laws. It is typically the smallest administrative unit with its own elected government: a city, town, village or (in some countries) a rural commune. Municipalities provide local services — water, waste, local roads, zoning, primary education in some systems, policing in others — and raise revenue through property taxes, fees and transfers.

Municipal organisation varies widely: strong-mayor and council-manager systems in the United States; the German Gemeinde under Land supervision; French communes (some 35,000, the most in Europe) grouped increasingly into intercommunal bodies; Swiss communes with direct-democratic assemblies (see Landsgemeinde). Municipal law derives from state constitutions or charters (Dillon's Rule in much of the US confines municipalities to powers expressly granted; home-rule provisions reverse the presumption). The municipality is the layer of government closest to residents and the historical seedbed of democracy, from the Greek polis to the New England Town meeting.
