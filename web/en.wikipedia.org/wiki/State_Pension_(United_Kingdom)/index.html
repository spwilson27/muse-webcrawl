State Pension (United Kingdom)

The State Pension is a state benefit in the United Kingdom that is part of the social security system. It is paid by the government to people who have reached the State Pension age and who have made sufficient National Insurance contributions. Since 6 April 2016, new claimants receive the "new State Pension", a single-tier flat-rate payment replacing the previous two-tier basic State Pension and State Second Pension.

History

Old-age pensions were introduced by the Old Age Pensions Act 1908, championed by Chancellor of the Exchequer David Lloyd George, providing a means-tested pension for people aged 70 and over. The modern contributory system dates from the National Insurance Act 1946, implementing the Beveridge Report. Subsequent reforms included the earnings-related State Earnings-Related Pension Scheme (SERPS) in 1978, replaced by the State Second Pension in 2002, and the single-tier reform of 2016.

Eligibility and amounts

Entitlement depends on the claimant's National Insurance record: under the new State Pension, 35 qualifying years are needed for the full amount and at least 10 years for any pension. The State Pension age, once 60 for women and 65 for men, has been equalized and is rising to 67, with a further rise to 68 planned. Payments are uprated annually under the "triple lock" by the highest of earnings growth, price inflation, or 2.5%.
