The lunar maria (singular: mare) are large, dark, basaltic plains on Earth's Moon, formed by lava flowing into ancient impact basins. They are visible to the naked eye as the dark patches of the "Man in the Moon". Early astronomers mistook them for actual seas, hence their Latin names — mare means "sea" — such as Mare Imbrium (Sea of Showers) and Mare Tranquillitatis (Sea of Tranquillity), the landing site of Apollo 11.

The dark maria cover much of the Moon's near side

Formation

During the Late Heavy Bombardment, about 4 billion years ago, giant impacts excavated huge basins in the lunar crust. Between roughly 3 and 3.5 billion years ago, basaltic lava from the Moon's interior flooded these low-lying basins, solidifying into smooth plains. Because mare basalt is rich in iron and magnesium and darker than the surrounding anorthositic highlands, the maria appear dark. The near side has far more maria than the far side, probably because the near-side crust is thinner.

Distribution and names

Major maria include Oceanus Procellarum (the largest, treated as an "ocean"), Mare Imbrium, Mare Serenitatis, Mare Crisium, Mare Nubium, Mare Humorum and Mare Fecunditatis. Wrinkle ridges, rilles and ghost craters partly buried by lava are common within them. Most Apollo and Luna landing sites were on or near maria, whose flat terrain and sampled basalts allowed scientists to date the plains and calibrate the Moon's cratering history.
