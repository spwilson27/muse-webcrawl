Ernest B. Schoedsack

Ernest Beaumont Schoedsack (8 June 1893 – 23 December 1979) was an American film director and cinematographer, co-director of King Kong (1933).

Ernest Beaumont Schoedsack (8 June 1893 – 23 December 1979) was an American motion picture cinematographer, producer, and director. Together with Merian C. Cooper he made pioneering travel-adventure documentaries and co-directed the landmark film King Kong (1933). Tall, adventurous and technically inventive, Schoedsack characteristically handled the camera and production logistics while Cooper drove story and promotion.

Early life

Schoedsack was born in Council Bluffs, Iowa. He ran away from home as a teenager, worked as a surveyor on the Pan-American Highway, and drifted into film as a cameraman for Mack Sennett's Keystone comedies. In the First World War he served with the US Signal Corps in France, filming combat; after the war he shot newsreel and relief footage across war-torn Europe and the Middle East, where he met Cooper.

Documentary expeditions

With Cooper and journalist Marguerite Harrison, Schoedsack travelled to Persia to film Grass (1925), documenting the Bakhtiari tribe's gruelling migration across the Karun River and the Zard-Kuh mountains. The pair then went to Siam to make Chang (1927), a dramatised story of a jungle farming family, famous for its footage of stampeding elephants; it was nominated at the first Academy Awards. Their fiction feature The Four Feathers (1929) combined studio work with location shooting in Sudan.

King Kong and after

At RKO, Schoedsack co-directed The Most Dangerous Game (1932) and then, with Cooper, King Kong (1933), sharing sets, crew and star Fay Wray between the two productions. Schoedsack directed the Kong–New York sequences and much of the live action around Willis O'Brien's stop-motion animation; the film became one of the most influential in cinema history. The team followed with Son of Kong (1933), which Schoedsack directed alone. His later career included The Last Days of Pompeii (1935) and, after wartime service testing military photographic equipment, Mighty Joe Young (1949), reuniting the Kong team. Wartime work damaged his eyesight, curtailing his career. He died in Los Angeles in 1979.
