Penal Laws (Ireland)

The Penal Laws (Irish: Na Péindlíthe) were a series of laws imposed in Ireland during the 17th and 18th centuries, following the Williamite War, designed to entrench the Protestant Ascendancy by restricting the civil, religious, educational, and economic rights of Roman Catholics — the majority of the population — and, to a lesser extent, Protestant dissenters such as Presbyterians.

Principal measures

Key statutes were enacted mainly between 1695 and 1728. The Education Act 1695 forbade Catholics from sending children abroad to be educated and banned Catholic schoolteachers. The Banishment Act 1697 ordered bishops and regular clergy out of Ireland. Legislation of 1703–1704 (the "Gavelkind" and Popery Acts) required Catholic land to be divided equally among all sons on death — fragmenting estates — while allowing the whole estate to pass intact if the eldest son converted to the Church of Ireland; Catholics were also barred from buying land, inheriting from Protestants, holding leases longer than 31 years, and owning a horse worth more than £5.

Further laws disarmed Catholics, excluded them from Parliament, the legal profession, the military, corporations and universities, and suppressed Catholic worship; the Disenfranchising Act 1728 removed the parliamentary vote from Catholics altogether. Presbyterians were affected chiefly by the sacramental test imposed by the Popery Act of 1704, which excluded them from crown and municipal office.

Effects and repeal

Enforcement varied over time and by region, but the cumulative effect was to transfer land overwhelmingly into Protestant hands — Catholic landownership fell from about 22 per cent in 1688 to about 5 per cent by 1776 — and to drive Catholic worship and education into informal settings, including hedge schools and Mass rocks.

Relief began with the Catholic Relief Acts of 1778, 1782, and 1793, which progressively restored rights to buy land, practise law, and (in 1793) vote. Most remaining disabilities were finally removed by the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829, following Daniel O'Connell's campaign for Catholic emancipation; the sacramental test for dissenters had been repealed in 1780.
