Juniperus virginiana, known as the eastern red cedar or eastern redcedar, is a species of juniper native to eastern North America, from southeastern Canada to the Gulf of Mexico and east of the Great Plains. Despite its common name it is a juniper, not a true cedar (Cedrus).

Description

It is a slow-growing evergreen coniferous tree, typically 5–20 m tall with a dense conical crown, though old specimens can reach 500 years of age — some of the oldest trees east of the Rockies include this species. The bark is reddish-brown, fibrous and peeling. The mature female cones are dark blue, berry-like, and coated with a white waxy bloom that gives them a pale blue appearance; they are an important winter food for birds, notably the cedar waxwing.

Ecology

Eastern red cedar is a pioneer species that colonises old fields, roadsides and limestone glades. In the Great Plains its spread into grasslands, encouraged by fire suppression, is regarded as a major ecological problem, altering habitat for grassland birds and increasing wildfire risk. It is also the alternate host of the cedar-apple rust fungus, a pest of apple orchards.

Uses

The fragrant, fine-grained heartwood — pinkish to deep red — is naturally rot-resistant and used for fence posts, furniture, and "cedar" chests and closet linings whose aroma repels moths. It was the principal wood for pencils in the United States until supplies declined. Cedarwood oil is distilled from the wood. Native American peoples used the tree medicinally and ceremonially, and it remains a popular ornamental and windbreak planting, with many cultivars selected.
