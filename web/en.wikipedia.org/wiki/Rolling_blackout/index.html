Rolling blackout


Also called: Rotational load shedding
Purpose: Prevent total grid failure
Typical duration: 30 minutes to several hours per block

A rolling blackout, also known as rotational load shedding, is an intentionally engineered electrical power shutdown in which electricity delivery is stopped for non-overlapping periods of time over different parts of a distribution network. Rolling blackouts are a last-resort measure used by an electric utility to avoid a total blackout of the power system when demand threatens to exceed supply.

How it works

Utilities divide their service territory into blocks of customers. When a shortfall is forecast, the system operator instructs the utility to shed a specified amount of load; the utility disconnects one block at a time, rotating the outage so that no single area bears the entire burden. Critical loads such as hospitals, emergency services, and water treatment plants are typically exempted or placed on protected circuits.

Notable instances

  • California, 2000–2001 — during the California electricity crisis, the state grid operator ordered rolling blackouts affecting millions amid market manipulation and supply shortages.
  • Japan, 2011 — after the Tōhoku earthquake and Fukushima disaster disabled generating capacity, Tokyo Electric Power Company implemented planned rotating outages across the Kantō region.
  • South Africa — Eskom's "load shedding," implemented in stages since 2007, is a long-running programme of rotational outages caused by generation shortfalls.
  • Texas, 2021 — during Winter Storm Uri, controlled outages ordered by ERCOT degenerated into days-long blackouts for millions when generating units failed in the cold.

Alternatives and criticism

Demand response programmes, time-of-use pricing, and interruptible industrial contracts can reduce peak demand without cutting power outright. Critics note that rolling blackouts impose disproportionate costs on small businesses and medically vulnerable customers, and that frequent recourse to them signals chronic underinvestment in generation or transmission capacity.
