Admissible decision rule

In statistical decision theory, an admissible decision rule is a rule for making decisions (an estimator, test, or other procedure) that is never strictly worse than any competing rule: there is no other rule that always has a risk less than or equal to it, and strictly smaller risk for at least one value of the parameter. A rule that is dominated in this way is called inadmissible.

Definition

Given a loss function and the resulting risk function R(θ, δ) for a decision rule δ, the rule δ is inadmissible if there exists another rule δ′ with R(θ, δ′) ≤ R(θ, δ) for all θ and strict inequality for some θ. Otherwise δ is admissible. The classical example of an inadmissible estimator is the usual sample mean of a multivariate normal distribution in dimensions three or higher, which is dominated by James–Stein estimators — the Stein's paradox result.

Significance

Admissibility is a minimal requirement of rationality for decision procedures: an inadmissible rule should never be used, since a uniformly better alternative exists. Bayes rules with proper priors are generally admissible, and complete class theorems show that, under regularity conditions, all admissible rules are Bayes rules or limits of them.
