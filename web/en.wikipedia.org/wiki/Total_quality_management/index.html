Total quality management


The PDCA (plan–do–check–act) cycle, a core tool of continuous improvement in TQM.

Total quality management (TQM) is a management approach to long-term success through customer satisfaction. In a TQM effort, all members of an organization participate in improving processes, products, services, and the culture in which they work. Total quality management uses strategy, data, and effective communications to integrate the quality discipline into the culture and activities of the organization.

Principles

Although formulations vary, TQM is generally described by a set of core principles:

  • Customer focus — quality is defined by the customer, and success depends on meeting and exceeding customer requirements.
  • Total employee involvement — all employees participate in working toward common goals; empowerment and teamwork are essential.
  • Process-centred — fundamental thinking about processes, with inputs transformed into outputs through monitored steps.
  • Integrated system — functional departments are connected horizontally within an overall organisational system.
  • Strategic and systematic approach — quality improvement is aligned with organisational strategy.
  • Continual improvement — the organisation constantly seeks incremental improvements in processes and products, often summarised by the Japanese concept of kaizen.
  • Fact-based decision making — decisions are based on data and analysis of performance measures.
  • Communications — effective communication maintains morale and aligns the organisation during change.

History

TQM grew out of twentieth-century quality practice: Walter A. Shewhart's statistical process control in the 1920s–30s; the teaching of W. Edwards Deming and Joseph M. Juran, whose influence was taken up enthusiastically in post-war Japan; the quality circles promoted by Kaoru Ishikawa; and the company-wide quality control movement in Japanese industry. The term "total quality management" is often credited to the United States Naval Air Systems Command in the mid-1980s, which adapted Japanese practices. TQM spread widely through Western industry in the 1980s and 1990s, aided by quality awards such as the Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award (established 1987), Japan's Deming Prize, and the EFQM Excellence Award in Europe.

Tools and methods

Practitioners employ the PDCA cycle (plan–do–check–act), Ishikawa (fishbone) diagrams, Pareto charts, control charts, benchmarking, quality function deployment ("house of quality"), and the seven basic tools of quality. Related and successor approaches include Six Sigma, Lean manufacturing, ISO 9000 quality management standards, and business process re-engineering; TQM ideas are frequently combined with these.

Criticism

Critics have argued that TQM was sometimes implemented as a fashionable programme rather than a genuine cultural change, that its results are difficult to attribute and measure, and that heavy formalisation can stifle innovation. Surveys of failed TQM programmes commonly cite lack of leadership commitment and treating the system as a short-term initiative. Despite this, its core ideas — customer focus, process thinking, and continuous improvement — remain foundational to modern quality management.
