Ottoman Caliphate

The Ottoman Caliphate was the claim of the Ottoman dynasty to the caliphate of Islam, held by Ottoman sultans from the early 16th century until the abolition of the caliphate by the Grand National Assembly of Turkey on 3 March 1924. The last Ottoman caliph was Abdülmecid II, who held the title as a purely religious and ceremonial office from 1922, after the sultanate itself had been abolished.

Origins

According to later tradition, the caliphal title passed to Selim I after his conquest of Mamluk Egypt in 1517, when the last Abbasid shadow-caliph of Cairo, al-Mutawakkil III, is said to have formally surrendered the title and the holy relics of the Prophet Muhammad. Modern historians debate the historicity of a formal transfer, noting that Ottoman use of the title "caliph" became prominent only gradually, and politically significant especially in the 18th and 19th centuries.

Political use

The Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca (1774) asserted the sultan's religious authority over Muslims in Russian-ruled Crimea, an early diplomatic use of the caliphate. In the late 19th century Sultan Abdülhamid II promoted pan-Islamic appeals to the caliphate to rally Muslims within and beyond the empire. During the First World War the Ottoman government proclaimed a jihad in the caliph's name in November 1914, with limited effect.

Abolition

After the Turkish War of Independence, the Grand National Assembly abolished the sultanate on 1 November 1922, leaving Abdülmecid II as caliph only. On 3 March 1924 the Assembly abolished the caliphate itself and exiled the Ottoman family, ending more than four centuries of Ottoman caliphal claims. Subsequent attempts to convene a caliphate congress elsewhere came to nothing.
