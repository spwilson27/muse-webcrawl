Alfred James Lotka (March 2, 1880 – December 5, 1949) was an American mathematician, physical chemist, and statistician famous for his work in population dynamics and energetics. He is best known for the predator–prey model published in his book Elements of Physical Biology (1925), now usually called the Lotka–Volterra equations after his independent rediscovery of work by Vito Volterra.

Biography

Lotka was born in Lwów, then in Austria-Hungary, to American parents, and was educated in England, earning a BSc from the University of Birmingham in 1901 and a doctorate in 1912. He emigrated to the United States, working as a chemist, patent examiner, and editor before joining Johns Hopkins University and later the statistical department of the Metropolitan Life Insurance Company, where he spent most of his career alongside Louis I. Dublin.

Scientific work

In addition to the predator–prey equations, Lotka developed the theory of the stable population — showing that a closed population subject to constant fertility and mortality schedules converges to a fixed age distribution growing at a constant rate — foundational to modern demography. His 1926 paper on the frequency distribution of scientific productivity produced Lotka's law, describing how the number of authors publishing a given number of papers falls off as an inverse square. He also wrote on the energetics of evolution and human longevity and served as president of the Population Association of America and the American Statistical Association.

Selected works

  • Length of Life (with L. I. Dublin, 1936)
