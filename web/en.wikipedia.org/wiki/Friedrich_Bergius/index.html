Friedrich Bergius

Friedrich Karl Rudolf Bergius (11 October 1884 – 30 March 1949) was a German chemist. He is known for the Bergius process, a method of producing liquid hydrocarbons from coal by high-pressure hydrogenation, and shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1931 with Carl Bosch "in recognition of their contributions to the invention and development of chemical high pressure methods".

Life

Bergius was born in Goldschmieden, near Breslau (now Wrocław, Poland), the son of a chemical factory owner. He studied chemistry at the University of Breslau and the University of Leipzig, receiving his doctorate in 1907. He worked as an assistant to Fritz Haber in Karlsruhe and to Max Bodenstein in Hanover, where he habilitated in 1914.

Work

From about 1913 Bergius developed the hydrogenation of coal under high pressure and temperature in the presence of catalysts, converting solid coal into liquid fuels and oils. The Bergius process was commercialised by IG Farben and became a strategically important source of synthetic fuel in Germany, particularly during the Second World War, when coal liquefaction plants supplied a substantial share of aviation fuel. Bergius also worked on the conversion of wood into sugar by hydrolysis with hydrochloric acid (the Bergius–Rheinau process), intended for food, fodder and industrial alcohol.

After the war Bergius emigrated to Argentina, where he worked as an adviser to the government; he died in Buenos Aires in 1949.
