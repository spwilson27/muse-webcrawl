Winnipeg Declaration

The CCF convention in Winnipeg, 1956

The Winnipeg Declaration of 1956 was a revision of the Regina Manifesto, the program of the Co-operative Commonwealth Federation (CCF), adopted at the party's national convention in Winnipeg, Manitoba, in August 1956. The declaration moderated the party's earlier socialist program, replacing the Regina Manifesto's call for the eradication of capitalism with a broader acceptance of a mixed economy and a focus on social welfare and Keynesian economic management.

Background

The Regina Manifesto, adopted in 1933 at the CCF's founding convention in Regina, Saskatchewan, had declared that "no CCF Government will rest content until it has eradicated capitalism and put into operation the full programme of socialized planning." Through the 1930s and 1940s the CCF achieved notable success, forming the government of Saskatchewan under Tommy Douglas in 1944 and becoming a significant force in federal politics.

By the mid-1950s, however, the political climate had changed. Postwar prosperity, the Cold War, and the CCF's disappointing results in the federal elections of 1949 and 1953 convinced many in the party that the Regina Manifesto's language was an electoral liability. David Lewis and other party strategists pressed for a modernization of the party's program.

Contents

The Winnipeg Declaration described the CCF's goal as a society in which economic activity would serve public rather than merely private purposes, but it explicitly accepted private enterprise in much of the economy. It advocated public ownership where private monopoly or the public interest required it, endorsing a mixed economy, full employment policies, expanded social security, and international cooperation. The slogan of the declaration was that the party sought "a society which plans the economy in the interests of all the people."

Significance

The Winnipeg Declaration marked the CCF's transition from a doctrinaire socialist party to a social-democratic one. It formed part of the groundwork for the creation of the New Democratic Party (NDP) in 1961, formed by an alliance of the CCF and the Canadian Labour Congress. The shift paralleled similar movements in European social-democratic parties, such as the German SPD's Bad Godesberg Program of 1959.
