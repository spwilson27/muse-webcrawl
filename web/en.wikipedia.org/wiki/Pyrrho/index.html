Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360 – c. 270 BC) was a Greek philosopher of classical antiquity and the founder of Pyrrhonism, the first school of philosophical scepticism. He taught that nothing can be known for certain about the nature of things, and that the wise person should therefore suspend judgement (epoché), achieving a state of tranquillity (ataraxia).

Life

Pyrrho came from Elis. Originally a painter, he turned to philosophy under the influence of the atomist tradition of Democritus. He accompanied Alexander the Great on his campaign to India, where he is said to have encountered the "gymnosophists" — naked philosophers — and Magi, an experience often credited with shaping his indifference toward externals. He wrote nothing; his doctrines are known through his pupil Timon of Phlius and later sources, above all Diogenes Laërtius and Sextus Empiricus. Anecdotes portrayed him as so indifferent to appearances that friends had to stop him walking off cliffs — stories likely embellished to caricature his teaching.

Philosophy

According to the summary preserved by Aristocles, Pyrrho held that things are equally indifferent, unmeasurable and undecidable; our perceptions and opinions neither tell the truth nor lie. One should therefore be without opinions, inclinations or disturbance, saying of each thing that it "no more is than is not". Living by appearances and custom, the sceptic follows nature and law without committing to beliefs about reality, and so attains quietude. Pyrrhonism influenced later sceptics of the Academy and was revived by Aenesidemus; in the modern era it shaped thinkers such as Montaigne.
