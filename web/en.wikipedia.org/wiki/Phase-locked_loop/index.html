Phase-locked loop

A phase-locked loop (PLL) is a control system that generates an output signal whose phase is related to the phase of an input ("reference") signal. It consists of a phase detector, a loop filter and a variable oscillator (usually a voltage-controlled oscillator, VCO) arranged in a feedback loop: the detector compares the phases of the reference and the oscillator output, and the resulting error signal steers the oscillator until the two signals are synchronised — "locked" — in frequency and phase.

Operation

The phase detector produces a signal proportional to the phase difference between its two inputs. The loop filter smooths this signal into a control voltage that adjusts the VCO frequency. If a frequency divider is inserted in the feedback path, the output locks at a multiple of the reference frequency, which is the basis of the frequency synthesizer. Key performance concepts include the lock range, capture range, loop bandwidth and phase noise (jitter).

History

The principle was described in the 1930s by the French engineer Henri de Bellescize and was used in early television receivers to synchronise horizontal and vertical scanning. Integrated PLLs became widely available in the 1970s, notably the Signetics NE565, and digital and all-digital PLLs followed.

Applications

  • Frequency synthesis in radios, televisions and mobile phones, generating stable tunable local-oscillator frequencies from a single crystal reference.
  • Clock generation and multiplication in microprocessors and digital systems.
  • Clock recovery in data communications, extracting timing from a received bit stream.
  • Demodulation of FM and phase-modulated signals, and motor-speed control.
