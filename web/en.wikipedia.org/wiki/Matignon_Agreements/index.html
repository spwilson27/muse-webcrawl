Matignon Agreements

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The agreements were negotiated at the Hôtel Matignon in Paris in June 1936.

The Matignon Agreements (Accords de Matignon) were agreements signed in the Hôtel Matignon by representatives of the French employers' association, the Confédération générale de la production française (CGPF), and the trade union federation Confédération générale du travail (CGT) on 7 June 1936, in the presence of Prime Minister Léon Blum. They were concluded during the great wave of strikes and factory occupations that followed the electoral victory of the Popular Front in May 1936.

Background

The Popular Front, a coalition of Socialists, Radicals and Communists, won the legislative elections of May 1936. Even before Blum's government took office, a spontaneous strike movement spread across French industry; by early June some two million workers were on strike and hundreds of factories were occupied. Workers demanded wage increases, the 40-hour week, paid holidays and recognition of union rights.

Terms

The agreements provided for:

  • General wage increases of between 7 and 15 percent, with the largest rises for the lowest paid;
  • Recognition of trade union rights within firms, including elected workers' delegates (délégués du personnel);
  • The establishment of collective bargaining agreements across industries;
  • No sanctions against strikers, and payment guarantees as work resumed.

Legislative follow-up

The agreements were rapidly supplemented by landmark social legislation: laws of June 1936 introduced the 40-hour working week and two weeks' paid annual holiday (congés payés), transforming French social law. The Matignon Agreements are regarded as a foundational moment in the history of French labour relations and of the modern French welfare state.
