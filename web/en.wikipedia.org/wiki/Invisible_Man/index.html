Invisible Man is a novel by American writer Ralph Ellison, published by Random House in 1952. It addresses many of the social and intellectual issues faced by African Americans in the early 20th century, including Black nationalism, the relationship between Black identity and Marxism, and the reformist racial policies of Booker T. Washington, as well as issues of individuality and personal identity.

Plot

The unnamed narrator begins by declaring himself "an invisible man" — invisible because people refuse to see him — living in a coal cellar in Harlem lit by 1,369 stolen-electricity light bulbs. His retrospective narrative follows him from the segregated South, where he is humiliated in a "battle royal" before white townsmen and then expelled from a Black college by its president, Dr. Bledsoe, to Harlem, where he works in a paint factory making "Optic White" paint, is radicalised, and becomes a charismatic speaker for the Brotherhood, a thinly veiled political organisation. Betrayed by every institution he serves and caught in the Harlem riot of 1943, he retreats underground, concluding that "even an invisible man has a socially responsible role to play".

Reception and legacy

Invisible Man won the U.S. National Book Award for Fiction in 1953. Its opening chapter, "Battle Royal", is among the most anthologised stories in American literature. Time magazine included the novel in its list of the 100 best English-language novels of the 20th century, and the Modern Library ranked it among the century's top twenty. Ellison's fusion of high modernism, blues and folklore moved beyond the protest fiction associated with Richard Wright and decisively shaped later American writing; echoes of the novel run through contemporary literature, including the work of Ta-Nehisi Coates.
