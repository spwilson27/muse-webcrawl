Comedy film

A comedy film is a category of film that emphasises humour, designed to amuse audiences and provoke laughter. Along with drama, it is one of the oldest and broadest genres in cinema, dating to the medium's earliest years. Comedy films typically end happily, and the genre encompasses many subgenres, from slapstick and screwball to satire, parody, black comedy, and romantic comedy.

Silent comedians such as Charlie Chaplin defined the early comedy film.

History

Comedy was present from cinema's infancy: the Lumière brothers' L'Arroseur arrosé (1895) is often cited as the first comedy film. The silent era produced legendary performers — Charlie Chaplin, Buster Keaton, and Harold Lloyd — whose physical (slapstick) comedy crossed language barriers. With the coming of sound in the late 1920s, verbal wit flourished in screwball comedies such as It Happened One Night (1934) and in the anarchic films of the Marx Brothers. Later decades brought the satires of Billy Wilder, the visual gags of Jacques Tati and Peter Sellers, ensemble sketch-derived comedies, and the gross-out and improvisation-driven styles of the 2000s.

Subgenres

  • Slapstick — exaggerated physical humour.
  • Screwball comedy — fast-talking battle-of-the-sexes farces of the 1930s and 1940s.
  • Romantic comedy — humour centred on courtship and relationships.
  • Parody/spoof — mocking other genres or specific films, e.g. Airplane! and Blazing Saddles.
  • Black comedy — humour from taboo or morbid subjects, e.g. Dr. Strangelove.
  • Satire — comic critique of society and politics.
  • Comedy-drama (dramedy) — a balance of humour and serious themes.

Commercial performance

Comedies are perennially popular because they are relatively inexpensive to produce and translate broadly, though dialogue-heavy humour sometimes travels less well internationally than action. Several comedy films, including Some Like It Hot, Annie Hall, and Groundhog Day, are regularly ranked among the greatest films ever made.
