Cuneus


The cuneus (highlighted) on the medial surface of the occipital lobe

The cuneus (Latin for "wedge"; plural cunei) is a smaller, wedge-shaped lobe of the occipital lobe of the brain. It is bounded anteriorly by the parieto-occipital sulcus and inferiorly by the calcarine sulcus, which separates it from the lingual gyrus below. Like the rest of the occipital lobe, it is part of the visual cortex.

Function

The cuneus contains part of the primary visual cortex (striate cortex, Brodmann area 17) along the upper bank of the calcarine sulcus, as well as extrastriate visual areas (Brodmann areas 18 and 19). The portion of the primary visual cortex in the cuneus receives information from the superior part of the contralateral retina, corresponding to the inferior visual field. It is involved in basic visual processing and has been implicated in visual attention and, in some studies, in processes related to consciousness and dreaming.

Clinical significance

Lesions of the cuneus, for example from posterior cerebral artery stroke, can cause a contralateral inferior quadrantanopia — loss of vision in the lower quarter of the visual field on the opposite side — because of its retinotopic representation of the lower visual field. The cuneus may also be affected in conditions such as posterior cortical atrophy.
