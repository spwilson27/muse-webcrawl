Allopurinol is a medication used to decrease high blood uric acid levels. It is specifically used to prevent gout, to prevent uric-acid kidney stones, and for the high uric acid levels that can occur during cancer chemotherapy (tumour lysis syndrome). It is taken by mouth or injected into a vein.

Mechanism of action

Allopurinol is a structural analogue of the natural purine base hypoxanthine. It and its active metabolite oxypurinol inhibit xanthine oxidase, the enzyme that converts hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to uric acid. Blocking this step reduces the production of uric acid and also lowers its precursors' conversion, so purines are instead excreted as the more soluble xanthine and hypoxanthine.

History

The drug was developed in the 1950s by Gertrude Elion and George Hitchings at Burroughs Wellcome, originally while seeking antileukaemia agents; it was first marketed for gout in 1966. Elion and Hitchings shared the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the rational drug-design work of which allopurinol was a product.

Side effects

Common side effects include rash and upset stomach. Rarely, allopurinol causes a severe hypersensitivity syndrome (with Stevens–Johnson syndrome, hepatitis and kidney failure), which is strongly associated with the HLA-B*58:01 allele — particularly common in Han Chinese, Korean and Thai populations, for whom screening is recommended. Starting allopurinol can precipitate an acute gout flare, so it is usually begun after a flare settles, often with anti-inflammatory cover. Dose adjustment is required in kidney impairment.
