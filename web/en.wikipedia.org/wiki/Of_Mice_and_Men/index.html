Of Mice and Men

Of Mice and Men is a novella by American author John Steinbeck, published in 1937. Set in California during the Great Depression, it tells the story of two migrant ranch workers — the sharp, small George Milton and the huge, intellectually disabled Lennie Small — who drift from job to job nursing a shared dream of owning a small farm of their own. The title is taken from Robert Burns's poem "To a Mouse": "The best laid schemes o' mice an' men / Gang aft agley" (often go awry).

Plot summary

George and Lennie flee the town of Weed after Lennie, who loves stroking soft things, frightens a girl by clutching her dress, and take work on a ranch near Soledad. There they meet the one-handed old swamper Candy, who offers his savings to join their farm dream; the boss's violent son Curley; and Curley's lonely, flirtatious wife. When Curley attacks Lennie, Lennie crushes his hand. Later, alone in the barn, Lennie accidentally kills Curley's wife while stroking her hair and flees to a riverside hiding place agreed with George. As a lynch party led by Curley closes in, George finds Lennie, retells the dream of the farm one last time, and shoots him mercifully rather than leave him to Curley.

Themes and reception

The novella explores loneliness, friendship, the fragility of dreams, and the treatment of the vulnerable — the disabled, the elderly, and the Black stable-hand Crooks — in Depression-era America. Steinbeck, who had worked as a ranch hand, structured the book like a play, with a small cast and scenes confined to a few sets; he adapted it for the stage the year of publication. Frequently taught in schools, the book is also among the most frequently challenged in the United States for its language and themes. Film adaptations include the 1939 version starring Burgess Meredith and Lon Chaney Jr. and a 1992 film with Gary Sinise (who also directed) and John Malkovich.
