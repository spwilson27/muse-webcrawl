Higher education accreditation in the United States

Higher education accreditation in the United States is a peer review process by which the validity of degrees and credits awarded by institutions of higher learning is assured. Accreditation is carried out by private, non-profit accrediting organisations rather than by the federal government, although the United States Department of Education recognises accreditors and accreditation status is a condition of access to federal student financial aid under Title IV of the Higher Education Act.

Types of accreditation

Institutional accreditors review entire colleges and universities. Historically these were regional accreditors — such as the Higher Learning Commission, the Southern Association of Colleges and Schools Commission on Colleges (SACSCOC), the New England Commission of Higher Education, and the WASC Senior College and University Commission — but since 2020 federal rules no longer restrict them geographically. National institutional accreditors often oversee career, technical, or faith-related schools. Programmatic (specialised) accreditors evaluate individual programmes, as the ABET does for engineering and the ABA for law schools.

Process

Institutions undergo periodic review, typically every seven to ten years, involving a self-study, a site visit by a team of peer evaluators, and a decision by the accreditor's commission. Possible outcomes range from reaffirmation to probation or withdrawal of accreditation, the loss of which generally means loss of federal aid eligibility and often closure.

Criticism

The system has been criticised for conflicts of interest, high compliance costs, and for accrediting institutions with poor student outcomes. The collapse of large for-profit chains such as Corinthian Colleges and ITT Technical Institute in the 2010s prompted reforms, including the federal closure of the accreditor ACICS in 2022.
