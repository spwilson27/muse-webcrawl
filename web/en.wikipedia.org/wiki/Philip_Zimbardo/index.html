Philip Zimbardo


Philip George Zimbardo (23 March 1933 – 14 October 2024) was an American psychologist, professor emeritus at Stanford University, best known for the 1971 Stanford prison experiment.

Philip George Zimbardo (March 23, 1933 – October 14, 2024) was an American psychologist and a professor at Stanford University. He became known for his 1971 Stanford prison experiment, a study of the psychological effects of perceived power, in which student volunteers were randomly assigned to play prisoners and guards in a mock prison.

Stanford prison experiment

In August 1971, Zimbardo set up a simulated prison in the basement of Stanford's psychology building. Twenty-four male students were selected and assigned to roles; the guards were given uniforms and symbols of authority, while prisoners were dressed in smocks and referred to by numbers. The study, funded by the U.S. Office of Naval Research, was scheduled for two weeks but was terminated after six days amid escalating abuse and emotional distress. The experiment became famous in debates about situational versus dispositional explanations of behaviour, though it has also been criticised on methodological and ethical grounds, including questions about demand characteristics and the extent of Zimbardo's own role as prison superintendent.

Later work

Zimbardo authored widely used textbooks and the popular book The Lucifer Effect (2007), examining how situational forces can lead ordinary people to commit evil acts — a theme he connected to the abuses at Abu Ghraib prison, where he testified as an expert witness for the defence of a military policeman. He also researched shyness, founding a clinic, and later worked on the psychology of time perspective (The Time Paradox, 2008) and on promoting everyday heroism through the Heroic Imagination Project. He served as president of the American Psychological Association in 2002.

He died at his home in San Francisco in October 2024, aged 91.
