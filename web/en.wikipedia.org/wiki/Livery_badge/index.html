Livery badge

A livery badge was a badge worn by household retainers, servants, and followers of a lord or magnate in late medieval England and elsewhere in Europe to display their allegiance. Typically made of base metal or silver, and sometimes enamelled, livery badges bore the heraldic device, crest, or emblem of the wearer's lord.

History

Livery badges became prominent in England during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, when powerful nobles maintained large affinities of retainers. Well-known examples include the white hart of Richard II, the white boar of Richard III, the swan badge of the de Bohun family, and the Stafford knot. Richard II's distribution of his white hart badge is recorded in chronicles and depicted in the Wilton Diptych.

Regulation

Because badges symbolised potentially dangerous private retinues, English parliaments repeatedly legislated to restrict who could give or wear livery, notably under Henry IV and Henry VII. The practice declined as retaining was curtailed by the Tudor monarchy.

Survival

Many livery badges survive as metal-detector finds, particularly along the Thames foreshore in London, and are recorded by schemes such as the Portable Antiquities Scheme. They provide evidence for heraldry, dress, and political allegiance in medieval society.
