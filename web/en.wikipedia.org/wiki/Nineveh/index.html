Nineveh

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A lamassu (winged human-headed bull) from Sennacherib's palace at Nineveh]

Nineveh was an ancient Assyrian city on the eastern bank of the Tigris, opposite modern Mosul in Iraq — the last and greatest capital of the Neo-Assyrian Empire. Under Sennacherib (r. 705–681 BC) it became perhaps the largest city in the world, girdled by 12 km of walls, watered by aqueducts, and adorned with the 'Palace Without Rival'; his grandson Ashurbanipal assembled there the famous royal library of cuneiform tablets. Destroyed by a Babylonian-Median coalition in 612 BC, its fall was celebrated in the biblical Book of Nahum; its excavated palaces and reliefs in the 19th century astonished Europe — and suffered grievous damage from looting and iconoclasm in the 2010s.

Imperial capital

Occupied since Neolithic times and sacred to Ishtar, Nineveh rose when Sennacherib moved the court from Dur-Sharrukin. He rebuilt the city wholesale: double walls and fifteen gates, canals and the Jerwan aqueduct bringing water 50 km, parks and orchards, and a palace whose carved reliefs narrated his campaigns (including the siege of Lachish, room by room). Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal added palaces and temples; the city's population may have approached 100,000–150,000.

Fall and rediscovery

After Ashurbanipal's death (631/627 BC) civil war and the Babylonian revolt under Nabopolassar doomed Assyria: Assur fell in 614 BC, and in 612 BC Babylonians, Medes and Scythians stormed Nineveh after a three-month siege, in fighting that left the palaces burned — the baked clay of Ashurbanipal's library preserving it for posterity. Austen Henry Layard's excavations (1840s) shipped the great winged bulls (lamassu) and reliefs to the British Museum. In 2014–2016 ISIS bulldozed and blew up major remains at the site, a loss partly documented by prior records.
