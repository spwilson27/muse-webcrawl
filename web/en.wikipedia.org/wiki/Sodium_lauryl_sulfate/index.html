Sodium lauryl sulfate

Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS)
Also called: Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)
Chemical formula: CH3(CH2)11OSO3Na
Molar mass: 288.38 g/mol
Appearance: White or cream powder
CAS number: 151-21-3

Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), also known as sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), is an organic compound with the formula CH3(CH2)11OSO3Na. It is an anionic surfactant used in many cleaning and hygiene products. The molecule consists of a 12-carbon hydrophobic tail attached to a hydrophilic sulfate group, giving it amphiphilic properties that make it an effective detergent and foaming agent. It is distinct from the related surfactant sodium alginate, which is a polysaccharide thickener rather than a detergent.

Production

SLS is produced by sulfating lauryl alcohol (dodecanol), typically derived from coconut oil or palm kernel oil, followed by neutralization with sodium carbonate or sodium hydroxide. The product is a mixture of sodium alkyl sulfates, predominantly the dodecyl (C12) homologue.

Applications

SLS is a common ingredient in toothpastes, shampoos, shaving foams, dishwashing liquids, laundry detergents, and floor cleaners, valued for its grease-removing and foaming properties and its low cost. Higher-purity grades (as SDS) are ubiquitous in biochemistry laboratories, where the detergent is used to denature proteins and impart a uniform negative charge in SDS-PAGE (polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis), allowing proteins to be separated by molecular weight.

Safety

SLS is a known skin irritant at high concentrations or with prolonged contact, and it is used as a standard irritant in dermatological patch testing. Regulatory assessments, including by the U.S. FDA and the EU, consider it safe for use in consumer products at typical concentrations when formulated to minimize irritation and rinsed off after brief contact. Claims linking SLS to cancer have been reviewed and are not supported by evidence. It should not be confused with sodium laureth sulfate (SLES), a milder, ethoxylated relative.
