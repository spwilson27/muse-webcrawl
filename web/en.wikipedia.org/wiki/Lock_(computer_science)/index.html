Lock (computer science)

In computer science, a lock or mutex (from mutual exclusion) is a synchronization primitive: a mechanism that enforces limits on access to a resource when there are many threads of execution. A lock enforces a mutual exclusion concurrency control policy: only one thread may hold the lock at a time, and other threads attempting to acquire it must wait until it is released.

Threads queue to acquire a lock protecting a critical section.

Types of locks

Spinlocks cause a waiting thread to busy-wait (loop repeatedly checking the lock), which is efficient for very short waits on multiprocessors but wasteful otherwise. Sleeping locks (often just called mutexes) put a waiting thread to sleep and wake it on release. Reader–writer locks allow many concurrent readers or a single writer. Recursive locks can be acquired multiple times by the same thread. Futexes (fast user-space mutexes) in Linux combine a user-space fast path with a kernel wait queue.

Problems

Locks can introduce deadlock, where threads each hold resources the other needs; livelock; priority inversion, in which a low-priority thread holding a lock blocks a high-priority one (famously affecting the Mars Pathfinder mission in 1997); and convoying, where performance collapses because threads queue behind a slow lock holder. Holding locks longer than necessary limits scalability, which motivates fine-grained locking, lock-free and wait-free algorithms, and transactional memory.

Granularity

Designers choose a locking granularity — from a single coarse lock guarding a whole data structure to fine locks per object or per bucket. Coarse locks are simpler and less error-prone; fine locks allow more concurrency but risk subtle races and deadlocks.
