Takeda Sōkaku (武田 惣角, October 10, 1859 – April 25, 1943) was a Japanese martial artist known as the founder of Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu, a fighting style that strongly influenced the later development of aikido. A member of the samurai class of the Aizu domain, he spent much of his life travelling throughout Japan teaching his art to police officers, military men, and other students.

Early life

Takeda was born in Aizu, in what is now Fukushima Prefecture, the second son of Takeda Sōkichi, a samurai and sumo wrestler. He studied kenjutsu, including Ono-ha Ittō-ryū swordsmanship, and is said to have learned the internal martial arts of the Aizu clan from figures such as Saigō Tanomo, a former chief retainer of the domain. In his youth he travelled widely, engaging in challenge matches and pilgrimages to shrines and temples.

Daitō-ryū

Takeda named his art Daitō-ryū, after the Daitō mansion where his ancestors were said to have studied. He taught a system of joint locks, throws, pins, and striking techniques intended for close combat. Among his most famous students were Morihei Ueshiba, the founder of aikido; Choi Yong-sool, who carried related teachings to Korea; and members of the Japanese military and police establishment.

Later life and legacy

Takeda remained active as a travelling instructor into his eighties, with tens of thousands of students recorded in his enrolment books (eimeiroku). He died in 1943. His son Takeda Tokimune succeeded him as head of Daitō-ryū, and the art continues to be practised worldwide.
