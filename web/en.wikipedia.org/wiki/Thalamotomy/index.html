Thalamotomy

Thalamotomy (from Greek thalamos, "chamber", + tomē, "cut") is a surgical procedure in which a small, precise lesion is made in the thalamus, a deep structure in the brain, in order to treat movement disorders and certain other neurological conditions. It is a form of ablative brain surgery, typically performed with stereotactic guidance.

The thalamus, the target of thalamotomy, lies deep in the centre of the brain.

TypeStereotactic ablative neurosurgery
TargetThalamus (especially the ventral intermediate nucleus, Vim)
UsesEssential tremor, Parkinsonian tremor, dystonia, chronic pain (historical)

Procedure

In a conventional radiofrequency thalamotomy, the patient is awake under local anaesthesia so that the surgical team can test the effect of stimulation and check for side effects. A stereotactic frame fixed to the skull provides coordinates; imaging (MRI or CT) localises the target nucleus, most often the ventral intermediate nucleus (Vim) for tremor. A thin electrode is advanced to the target, test stimulation is performed, and a radiofrequency current then heats the electrode tip to destroy a small volume of tissue, typically a few millimetres across.

Uses

Thalamotomy is used chiefly for medication-resistant tremor, particularly essential tremor and the tremor of Parkinson's disease, and historically for dystonia and intractable pain. Lesioning is normally performed on one side only, because bilateral thalamotomy carries a significant risk of speech and swallowing difficulties.

Alternatives and developments

Deep brain stimulation (DBS), in which an implanted electrode delivers adjustable electrical pulses to the same targets without destroying tissue, largely replaced thalamotomy in centres where it is available, because it is reversible and can be performed bilaterally. However, lesioning has seen renewed interest through MR-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) thalamotomy, an incisionless technique that creates the lesion with concentrated ultrasound beams under real-time MRI thermometry; it was approved in the United States in 2016 for essential tremor. Gamma Knife radiosurgery is another non-invasive ablative option, though its effects are delayed by months.

Risks

Possible complications include weakness, numbness, speech disturbance (dysarthria), balance problems, cognitive effects, and intracerebral haemorrhage. Bilateral procedures increase the risk of persistent speech and swallowing impairment.
