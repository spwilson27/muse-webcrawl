Gush Emunim

Gush Emunim (Hebrew: גוש אמונים, "Bloc of the Faithful") was an Israeli Orthodox Jewish activist movement committed to establishing Jewish settlements in the West Bank, the Gaza Strip, and the Golan Heights — territories captured by Israel in the 1967 Six-Day War. Founded in 1974 in the aftermath of the Yom Kippur War, it became the driving force of the settlement movement.

Ideology

Gush Emunim drew on the religious Zionist theology of Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook and especially his son Zvi Yehuda Kook, head of the Mercaz HaRav yeshiva in Jerusalem. It taught that the founding of the State of Israel was the beginning of messianic redemption (atchalta de-geulah), that settlement of the whole Land of Israel was a divine commandment, and that returning territory to Arab control was religiously forbidden.

History

The movement's defining early campaigns included repeated attempts to settle at Sebastia near Nablus in 1975, which ended in a compromise establishing the settlement of Elon Moreh. In 1977 the Likud government of Menachem Begin, sympathetic to its aims, greatly expanded settlement building. Gush Emunim opposed the Camp David Accords and the evacuation of Sinai settlements, and its more radical fringe produced the Jewish Underground, implicated in attacks on Palestinian mayors in 1980 and a foiled plot against the Temple Mount mosques. After the 1982 Lebanon War and the settlement of the political mainstream, the movement as an organisation declined, its functions absorbed by the Yesha Council, though its ideology remained influential in religious Zionism.

Legacy

Gush Emunim is widely credited with transforming Israeli politics: it made settlement of the occupied territories a central national project and shifted religious Zionism toward territorial maximalism.
