The Exodus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Artistic depiction of the parting of the Red Sea during the Exodus]

The Exodus (Hebrew: יציאת מצרים, "Departure from Egypt") is the founding myth of the Israelites, recounting their enslavement in ancient Egypt, their liberation under the leadership of Moses, the Ten Plagues, the crossing of the Red Sea, the revelation at Mount Sinai, and their forty years of wandering in the wilderness before reaching Canaan. The story is told mainly in the Book of Exodus, with elements in Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy.

Narrative

According to the biblical account, the descendants of Jacob prospered in Egypt but were enslaved by a pharaoh who feared their numbers. Moses, called by God at the burning bush, demanded their release; after ten plagues culminated in the death of Egypt's firstborn — from which the Israelites were spared, an event commemorated in Passover — Pharaoh relented, then pursued them to the sea, where his army was drowned. At Sinai, Moses received the Ten Commandments and the Law.

Historicity

Most scholars hold that the Exodus as described did not occur as a single large-scale historical event: there is no direct archaeological evidence for a mass migration of the biblical scale, and the consensus view is that the story crystallised over centuries, possibly preserving memories of smaller groups' experiences, including Semitic peoples in Egypt and the Hyksos expulsion. The narrative remains central to Jewish identity, commemorated annually at Passover, and has deeply influenced Christian and Islamic tradition and later liberation movements.
