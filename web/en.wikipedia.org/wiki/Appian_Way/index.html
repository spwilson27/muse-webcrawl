Appian Way

Appian Way (Via Appia)
LocationItaly: Rome to Brindisi
BuiltBegun 312 BC (to Capua); extended to Brundisium by c. 244 BC
BuilderAppius Claudius Caecus, censor
LengthAbout 500 km (312 miles) to Brindisi

The Appian Way (Latin and Italian: Via Appia) is one of the earliest and strategically most important Roman roads of the ancient republic. It connected Rome to Capua and was later extended to Benevento, Taranto, and the port of Brundisium (modern Brindisi) on the Adriatic coast of Apulia in southeast Italy, a distance of about 500 kilometres. The road was named after Appius Claudius Caecus, the Roman censor who began and completed the first section as a military road to the south in 312 BC during the Samnite Wars. The Romans called it the regina viarum, "queen of roads".

The original basalt paving of the Via Appia outside Rome, lined with stone pines and tombs.

History

The first section, from the Porta Capena in Rome to Capua, was built across the Pontine Marshes by corvée and engineering works, allowing rapid movement of troops and supplies. During the Second Punic War the road carried armies against Hannibal. In 71 BC, following the defeat of Spartacus's slave revolt, some 6,000 captured slaves were crucified along the Appian Way from Capua to Rome. The road was repeatedly restored, notably by Augustus and Trajan; Trajan also built a branch, the Via Traiana, from Benevento to Brundisium. St Paul is traditionally held to have travelled the Appian Way into Rome, and early Christian catacombs — San Callisto and San Sebastiano among them — line its first miles outside the city, alongside pagan tombs and villas such as the Villa of the Quintilii.

Construction

Typical of Roman engineering, the road was built with a deep foundation of stones and mortar, topped by tightly fitted polygonal basalt paving blocks slightly cambered for drainage, flanked by sidewalks, drainage ditches, and milestones. Its standard width of about 4.1 metres allowed two carts to pass. Way stations (mansiones) with stabling and lodging stood at intervals, supporting the imperial courier system (cursus publicus).

Today

The first 16 kilometres from Rome form the Parco Regionale dell'Appia Antica, a protected archaeological park popular with walkers and cyclists, where long stretches of original paving survive among pines, aqueduct ruins, and tombs. Much of the later route is followed by the modern SS7 road. In July 2024, "Via Appia. Regina Viarum" was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
