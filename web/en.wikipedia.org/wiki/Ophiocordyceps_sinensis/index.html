Ophiocordyceps sinensis (formerly known as Cordyceps sinensis) is an entomopathogenic fungus (a fungus that grows on insects) in the family Ophiocordycipitaceae. It is mainly found in the meadows above 3,500 metres (11,500 ft) on the Tibetan Plateau in south-west China and the Himalayan regions of Bhutan and Nepal. It parasitises the larvae of ghost moths, producing a fruiting body valued as an herbal remedy in traditional Chinese and Tibetan medicine.

Life cycle

Fungal spores infect the underground larvae of ghost moths (Thitarodes species). The fungus grows within the living caterpillar, eventually killing and mummifying it; in spring, a dark, blade-like fruiting body (stroma) grows out of the dead larva's head and emerges above the soil. The combination of mummified caterpillar and fungal stalk gives the organism its Tibetan name yartsa gunbu and its Chinese name dōng chóng xià cǎo — both meaning "winter worm, summer grass".

Harvest and trade

Harvested by hand each spring, caterpillar fungus has become one of the most valuable biological commodities in the world, at times fetching prices higher per gram than gold. The harvest provides a major share of cash income for many rural households on the Tibetan Plateau, and high demand has raised concerns about over-harvesting, sustainability and the ecological condition of the alpine meadows. Cultivation of the complete fungus–host system at scale has proved extremely difficult, though fermented mycelium products are sold as substitutes.

Medicinal claims

In traditional medicine the fungus is used to treat fatigue, respiratory and kidney ailments and as a general tonic and aphrodisiac. Modern studies have identified compounds such as cordycepin, but rigorous clinical evidence for most claimed benefits is lacking, and regulators in China have warned about arsenic contamination in some products.
