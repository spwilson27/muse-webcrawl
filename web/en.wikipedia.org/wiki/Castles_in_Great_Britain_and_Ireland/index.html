Castles in Great Britain and Ireland

Castles in Great Britain and Ireland number in the thousands, built between the Norman Conquest of 1066 and the end of the Middle Ages, with further fortified houses added into the 17th century. A castle was a fortified residence of a lord or noble, combining military defence with domestic and administrative functions.

History

The Normans introduced the motte-and-bailey castle, rapidly built in earth and timber, and later replaced in stone with keeps such as the White Tower of the Tower of London. The 12th and 13th centuries saw great enclosure castles with curtain walls and mural towers, culminating in Edward I's Welsh castles — Caernarfon, Conwy, Harlech and Beaumaris — among the finest military architecture in Europe. In Scotland, tower houses predominated; in Ireland, castles spread with the Anglo-Norman invasion and later Gaelic and planter building.

Decline and legacy

Gunpowder artillery, political centralisation, and changing domestic tastes ended serious castle building by the 17th century; many were slighted in the Civil War. The Gothic Revival produced romantic sham castles, and today the historic castles are protected as scheduled monuments and listed buildings, cared for by bodies such as English Heritage, Historic Environment Scotland, Cadw, and the National Trust.
