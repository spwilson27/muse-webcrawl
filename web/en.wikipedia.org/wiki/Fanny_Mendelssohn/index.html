Fanny Mendelssohn (14 November 1805 – 14 May 1847), later Fanny Hensel, was a German composer and pianist of the early Romantic era. The elder sister of Felix Mendelssohn, she composed more than 460 pieces of music, including a piano trio, a string quartet, an orchestral overture, choral works, and hundreds of songs and piano pieces. Although the conventions of her time and class confined her largely to private performance, her works are now widely published, performed, and recorded.

Early life and education

Fanny Mendelssohn was born in Hamburg, the eldest of four children of the banker Abraham Mendelssohn and Lea Salomon, and granddaughter of the philosopher Moses Mendelssohn. The family moved to Berlin in 1811. Fanny and Felix received the same exceptional musical education — piano with Ludwig Berger and Marie Bigot's tradition, composition with Carl Friedrich Zelter — and Fanny's talent was recognised as equal to her brother's: by thirteen she could play Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier from memory. Zelter wrote to Goethe praising both children.

But where Felix was groomed for a public career, Fanny was told by her father that music would be "only an ornament" in her life, and both her father and (ambivalently) Felix opposed her publishing under her own name. A handful of her songs appeared under Felix's name in his Opp. 8 and 9 collections — a fact revealed publicly when Felix, performing for Queen Victoria, had to confess that the Queen's chosen favourite, "Italien", was by his sister.

Marriage and the Sunday concerts

In 1829 she married the artist Wilhelm Hensel, who, unlike her family, strongly encouraged her composing and publication. From 1831 she directed the family's celebrated Sonntagsmusiken (Sunday musicales) in Berlin — semi-public concerts with orchestra and choir that became a fixture of the city's musical life and that gave her a platform as pianist, conductor, and composer, including the premiere of her Overture in C major, her only orchestral work. A year in Italy in 1839–40, where she was admired by Gounod and other musicians, strengthened her confidence; in 1846, without consulting Felix, she decided to publish, issuing collections of songs and piano pieces as her Opp. 1–7.

Death and legacy

On 14 May 1847, while rehearsing one of the Sunday concerts, she suffered a stroke and died the same day, aged 41. Felix, devastated, died six months later of a similar stroke. Her death and the family's ambivalence consigned most of her music to manuscript for over a century. Since the 1980s, scholarship and recordings — including her monumental piano cycle Das Jahr ("The Year", 1841), a month-by-month sequence rediscovered and published in the late twentieth century — have restored her to prominence as one of the most significant women composers of the nineteenth century, and modern research continues to identify works once attributed to her brother.
