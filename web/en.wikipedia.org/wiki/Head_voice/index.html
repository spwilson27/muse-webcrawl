Head voice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Head voice is a term used in vocal music to describe a singing register and the resonance felt in the head when singing in the upper part of the voice. Singers and voice teachers contrast it with chest voice, the register of normal speech in which resonance is felt in the chest. Terminology varies between schools of singing, and head voice is related to — but distinguished by many teachers from — falsetto: head voice generally retains a connected, speech-like quality and can be blended with chest voice, whereas falsetto is breathier and disconnected, particularly in male voices.

Physiology

In modern voice science, registers correspond to different modes of vibration of the vocal folds. Chest voice (modal register, M1) uses the full mass of the folds, dominated by the thyroarytenoid muscle. Head voice (M2 in much of the literature, when including falsetto) involves stretched, thinned folds with reduced vibrating mass, dominated by the cricothyroid muscle, producing a higher fundamental frequency with different harmonic content. Skilled singers learn to negotiate the passaggio — the transition zone between registers — so the change is inaudible.

Use in singing

Head voice is essential for the upper range of all voice types. Female classical singers perform most of their range in head-dominant production; male operatic high notes use a "covered" head-voice mix rather than pure falsetto. In popular music, head voice and falsetto underpin styles from doo-wop and soul to contemporary pop and R&B. Countertenors sing primarily in a developed falsetto/head register.

Historical terminology

The terms "chest" and "head" registers appear in treatises as early as the Renaissance and Baroque periods, reflecting singers' bodily sensations of resonance. Nineteenth-century pedagogues such as Manuel García II systematised register teaching after the invention of the laryngoscope allowed observation of the folds; twentieth-century voice science then reframed the registers in physiological terms while teachers continued to use the traditional imagery.
