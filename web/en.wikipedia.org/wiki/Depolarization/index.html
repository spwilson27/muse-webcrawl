Depolarization

In biology, depolarization or hypopolarization is the change within a cell, during which the cell undergoes a shift in electric charge distribution, resulting in less negative charge inside the cell compared to the outside. Depolarization is essential to the function of many cells, communication between cells, and the overall physiology of an organism.

Mechanism

Most cells maintain a negative resting membrane potential (around −70 mV in neurons). When a stimulus opens voltage-gated sodium channels, Na+ ions rush into the cell and the membrane potential rises toward zero and may overshoot to positive values. If depolarization reaches threshold, an action potential fires. Subsequent opening of potassium channels and closing of sodium channels repolarizes the membrane, and hyperpolarization may briefly follow.

Examples

Depolarization underlies nerve impulses, the initiation of muscle contraction (including the heartbeat, where pacemaker cells in the sinoatrial node depolarize spontaneously), and many receptor potentials. In the heart, abnormal depolarization patterns produce arrhythmias visible on an electrocardiogram. Depolarization block — sustained depolarization that inactivates sodium channels — is the mechanism of some neuromuscular blocking drugs such as succinylcholine.
