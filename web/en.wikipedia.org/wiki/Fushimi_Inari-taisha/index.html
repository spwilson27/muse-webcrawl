Fushimi Inari-taisha

Fushimi Inari-taisha (伏見稲荷大社) is the head shrine of the kami Inari, located in Fushimi-ku, Kyoto, Japan. Inari was originally the kami of rice and agriculture, and over time also became a patron of business and prosperity; the shrine is accordingly famous for its thousands of vermilion torii gates donated by worshippers and companies, which line the paths up Mount Inari. Fushimi Inari is the head of an estimated 30,000 Inari shrines across Japan.

History

Tradition dates the shrine's founding to 711, when the Hata clan enshrined Inari on the summit of Mount Inari. The shrine received imperial patronage early in the Heian period, and in 942 it was elevated to the highest rank for a shrine. The main structures were rebuilt in the 15th century after fire during the Ōnin War. In 1871 it was placed among the top-ranked government-supported shrines.

The torii gates

The shrine's most celebrated feature is the Senbon Torii ("thousands of torii gates"): two dense parallel rows of vermilion gates behind the main shrine, leading into the mountain trails. In fact the mountain paths hold around 10,000 gates of all sizes, each inscribed with the name of the donating individual or business and the date of donation. The custom of donating gates to have a wish come true, or in gratitude for one granted, spread during the Edo period. The full circuit of mountain trails is about 4 kilometres and takes 2–3 hours to walk.

Foxes

Foxes (kitsune) are regarded as Inari's messengers, and statues of foxes — often holding a key, a scroll, or a sheaf of rice in their mouths — stand throughout the grounds. Fried tofu, said to be a fox favourite, gives its name to the local dishes inarizushi and kitsune udon.

Festivals and popularity

Millions of worshippers visit during the New Year period (hatsumōde), the busiest time for the shrine. Fushimi Inari has repeatedly been ranked Japan's most popular attraction among international visitors, and it appeared prominently in the 2005 film Memoirs of a Geisha.
