Swing state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In a United States presidential election, a swing state (or battleground state, purple state) is a state that can reasonably be won by either the Democratic or Republican candidate, so that it becomes the focus of campaign resources under the Electoral College's winner-take-all allocation (in 48 states). Because most states lean reliably to one party ("red" and "blue" states), presidential campaigns concentrate advertising, visits and organisation in a handful of competitive states — in recent cycles, notably Pennsylvania, Michigan, Wisconsin, Arizona, Georgia, Nevada and North Carolina.

Analysis and criticism

Swing status is assessed from past margins, polling and demographics, and can shift — Ohio and Florida moved from bellwether to Republican-leaning, Virginia and Colorado the other way. The system is criticised for making most Americans' presidential votes effectively uncompetitive, skewing policy attention to battleground industries and regions, and (with the Senate and Electoral College) compounding small-state bias; defenders cite federalism, recount containment and the need for geographically broad coalitions. Analogous "marginal seats" drive campaigning in parliamentary systems under plurality rule.
