Australian republicanism is the political movement advocating the replacement of the Australian monarchy — currently embodied in the King of Australia, who is also the monarch of the United Kingdom — with a republican system of government, in which an Australian citizen would serve as head of state in place of the monarch and the Governor-General.

History

Republican sentiment in Australia dates to the colonial era, influenced by the Eureka Stockade of 1854, Irish nationalism, and a distinctly egalitarian "bush" ethos. Republican ideas circulated in publications such as The Bulletin in the late nineteenth century, though federation in 1901 was achieved as a constitutional monarchy.

The movement gained mainstream prominence in the early 1990s under Prime Minister Paul Keating, who championed an Australian republic as part of a broader national identity agenda. Opposition Leader John Howard, a constitutional monarchist, nevertheless committed to a constitutional convention, held in 1998, which recommended a republic model with a president chosen by a two-thirds majority of a joint sitting of Parliament.

1999 referendum

On 6 November 1999 Australians voted on whether to alter the Constitution to establish the Commonwealth of Australia as a republic. The proposal was defeated nationally (about 55% voting No) and in every state, although it succeeded in the Australian Capital Territory. Analysts attributed the defeat partly to division among republicans between supporters of the parliamentary-appointment model and "direct electionists" who wanted a popularly elected president and campaigned for a No vote in protest.

Later developments

Support for a republic has fluctuated in polling since 1999, generally declining from its 1990s peak. The issue revived periodically — including the appointment of an Assistant Minister for the Republic in 2022 — but no second referendum has been held, and debate continues over which model of president selection should be put to voters.
