Wood preservation

Wood preservation refers to the treatment of wood with chemicals or processes that increase its resistance to decay fungi, insects (such as termites and borers), marine organisms, and weathering, thereby extending its service life. Untreated wood in ground contact may fail within a few years; properly preserved wood can last for decades.

Why wood decays

Wood is degraded by fungi when it contains sufficient moisture (generally above 20%), oxygen, and moderate temperatures. Insects attack dry or damp wood depending on the species. Preservatives work by making the wood toxic or unpalatable to these organisms.

Preservative types

  • Creosote — a coal-tar distillate, long used for railway sleepers and utility poles.
  • Chromated copper arsenate (CCA) — a waterborne preservative widely used from the 1970s; withdrawn from most residential uses in North America and Europe in 2003–2004 over arsenic concerns.
  • Alkaline copper quaternary (ACQ) and copper azole — modern waterborne alternatives.
  • Borates — low-toxicity preservatives used mainly indoors, since they leach in wet conditions.
  • Oilborne preservatives — pentachlorophenol (now heavily restricted) and copper naphthenate.

Processes

Industrial treatment is usually done in pressure cylinders. In the full-cell (Bethell) process, a vacuum draws air from the wood, preservative is flooded in, and pressure forces deep penetration. Empty-cell processes (Lowry and Rueping) achieve good penetration with less chemical retained. Non-pressure methods include brushing, dipping, and diffusion.

Non-chemical methods

Thermal modification (heat treatment) and chemical modification such as acetylation improve durability without biocides. Good design — keeping wood dry and ventilated — remains a fundamental means of preservation.
