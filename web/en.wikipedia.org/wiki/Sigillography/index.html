Sigillography

Sigillography, also known under the older term sphragistics, is the scholarly study of seals: the small objects, usually in wax, lead, or clay, used to authenticate documents, close letters, and mark ownership. It is one of the auxiliary sciences of history, closely related to diplomatics, heraldry, and numismatics.

History of the discipline

The study of seals emerged in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries alongside diplomatics; Jean Mabillon's De re diplomatica (1681) treated seals as evidence for the authenticity of charters. In the nineteenth century large national corpora of seal impressions began to be published, and sigillography became an established specialism in archives and museums.

Materials and forms

Seals were impressed using a matrix (die) into softened beeswax, lead (as in papal bullae), clay, or later sealing wax and wafers. Medieval seals took many shapes — round, oval, vesica (pointed oval, common for women and clergy), and shield-shaped — and bore legends, usually in Latin, around a central device such as a portrait, coat of arms, building, or religious scene.

Uses

Because seals survive in vast numbers and are precisely datable, they are a major source for medieval social history, art, costume, architecture, and the development of heraldry. Great seals of monarchs, equestrian seals of the nobility, and the personal signets and merchants' marks of ordinary people each illuminate different strata of society.
