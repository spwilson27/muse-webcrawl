Epiphyseal plate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The epiphyseal plate, also called the growth plate or physis, is a layer of hyaline cartilage found in the metaphysis at each end of a growing long bone in children and adolescents. It is the site of longitudinal bone growth: new cartilage is produced on the epiphyseal side of the plate while cartilage on the diaphyseal side is calcified and replaced by bone, so the shaft lengthens while the plate maintains its thickness. When growth is complete, the plate ossifies entirely, leaving only a thin epiphyseal line.

The epiphyseal plate lies between the epiphysis and metaphysis of a growing long bone.

Structure and zones

Histologically, the growth plate is organized into zones reflecting successive stages of chondrocyte activity:

  • Zone of resting (reserve) cartilage — small, scattered chondrocytes that anchor the plate to the epiphysis and act as a stem-cell-like reservoir.
  • Zone of proliferation — chondrocytes divide rapidly and arrange themselves in longitudinal columns, like stacks of coins.
  • Zone of hypertrophy — the cells stop dividing and swell greatly, accumulating glycogen and preparing the matrix for calcification.
  • Zone of calcification — the hypertrophic cells die, the surrounding matrix calcifies, and blood vessels invade, bringing osteoblasts that deposit new bone on the calcified cartilage scaffold.

Regulation and closure

Growth at the plate is controlled by a feedback loop involving the Indian hedgehog and parathyroid hormone-related protein signaling pathways within the plate, and systemically by growth hormone, thyroid hormone, and — at puberty — the sex steroids. Estrogen (in both sexes) accelerates the exhaustion of the proliferative pool, causing the plates to fuse: typically around ages 14–16 in girls and 16–19 in boys, though timing varies by bone. Radiographs of the hand and wrist, compared with atlases such as Greulich and Pyle, are used clinically to estimate skeletal ("bone") age.

Clinical significance

Because the growth plate is weaker than surrounding ligaments and bone, it is a common site of injury in young athletes. Fractures through the plate are classified by the Salter–Harris system (types I–V) according to their path through the plate, metaphysis, and epiphysis; some types risk growth arrest and limb-length discrepancy. Slipped capital femoral epiphysis is a disorder of the proximal femoral plate in adolescents. Inherited disorders of the plate underlie many skeletal dysplasias, most famously achondroplasia, in which a gain-of-function mutation in the FGFR3 receptor suppresses chondrocyte proliferation. Deficiency of growth hormone or thyroid hormone slows plate activity, while precocious puberty causes premature fusion and short adult stature.
