Hub-and-spoke model

A hub-and-spoke network topology.

The hub-and-spoke model, also known as the hub and spoke distribution paradigm, is a system of connections arranged like the spokes of a wheel radiating from a central hub. Rather than travelling directly between two points, traffic (passengers, freight, data) moves from a spoke to the hub and then out another spoke to its destination. The model contrasts with point-to-point networks, in which each origin connects directly to each destination.

Applications

Aviation

The model transformed commercial aviation after the United States deregulated its airline industry in 1978. Airlines consolidated operations at fortress hubs — such as Delta at Atlanta, United at Chicago O'Hare and American at Dallas/Fort Worth — where banks of arriving flights feed banks of departing flights, allowing carriers to serve many more city pairs with a given fleet. FedEx pioneered the analogous system in air freight from its Memphis "SuperHub", a model adopted in parcel logistics worldwide.

Other fields

Hub-and-spoke organisation also appears in telecommunications and computer networking (star topology), container shipping and trucking, healthcare delivery (regional trauma centres), and retail distribution, where goods flow through central distribution centres to stores.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include economies of scale at the hub, higher load factors, more frequent service and a larger number of destinations reachable with one stop. Disadvantages include vulnerability to disruption at the hub (weather or congestion there propagates across the network), longer travel times and extra handling for traffic that must backtrack through the hub, and the capital cost of hub facilities. Some carriers, notably low-cost airlines, have therefore favoured point-to-point networks.
