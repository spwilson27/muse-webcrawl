Penn effect

Penn effect
FieldInternational economics
Named fromUniversity of Pennsylvania studies (Kravis, Heston, Summers)
Related conceptBalassa–Samuelson effect

The Penn effect is the stylised fact in economics that consumer price levels are systematically higher in richer countries: measured in a common currency at market exchange rates, goods and services cost more in high-income economies than in low-income ones. As a consequence, converting incomes at purchasing power parity (PPP) rather than market exchange rates raises the estimated real incomes of poorer countries relative to richer ones.

History

The effect takes its name from a series of empirical studies by economists at the University of Pennsylvania — Irving Kravis, Alan Heston, and Robert Summers — whose International Comparison Project (begun in the 1960s–70s) produced the Penn World Table, a standard dataset of PPP-adjusted national accounts. Comparing prices across countries, they found a strong positive correlation between a country's price level and its real per-capita income.

Explanation

The standard theoretical explanation is the Balassa–Samuelson effect: productivity grows faster in traded goods (manufacturing, technology) than in non-traded services (haircuts, childcare). Competition bids up wages economy-wide, so prices of non-traded services rise in rich countries even though tradable goods sell at roughly world prices. A basket of consumption — containing many services — therefore costs more where incomes are high. Other explanations involve factor endowments, demand patterns, and trade costs.

Implications

The Penn effect means market-exchange-rate comparisons systematically exaggerate international income inequality. It also underlies the popular "Big Mac index" of The Economist, which uses the price of a largely non-traded product to gauge currency under- and overvaluation.
