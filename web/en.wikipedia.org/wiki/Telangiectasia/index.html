Telangiectasias (spider veins) on the leg

Telangiectasias, from Greek telos ("end"), angeion ("vessel") and ektasis ("dilation"), also known as spider veins, are small dilated blood vessels near the surface of the skin or mucous membranes, measuring between 0.5 and 1 millimetre in diameter. They are distinct from the larger varicose veins, which arise from deeper venous insufficiency.

Causes

Telangiectasias may be acquired or hereditary. Acquired forms are common and are associated with cumulative sun exposure, ageing, prolonged standing, pregnancy and other hormonal influences, topical corticosteroid use, and the skin condition rosacea, in which facial vessels are prominent. Alcohol use and liver disease may contribute.

Several systemic and genetic diseases feature telangiectasias as a diagnostic clue: hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (Osler–Weber–Rendu syndrome), in which mucocutaneous telangiectasias accompany recurrent nosebleeds and internal arteriovenous malformations; ataxia–telangiectasia, a neurodegenerative immunodeficiency; and connective tissue disorders such as limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis (the "T" in the older CREST acronym), where they appear on the face, lips and hands alongside calcinosis and sclerodactyly.

Treatment

Isolated spider veins are usually a cosmetic issue. Treatment options include sclerotherapy, in which a solution injected into the vessels causes them to collapse and fade; surface lasers (such as pulsed dye and Nd:YAG) and intense pulsed light for fine facial vessels; and electrocautery for individual lesions. Underlying venous reflux, if present, may be treated to reduce recurrence. Telangiectasias caused by systemic disease prompt evaluation of the underlying condition.
