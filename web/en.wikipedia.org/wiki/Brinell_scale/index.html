Brinell scale

The Brinell scale is a method of measuring the hardness of materials, proposed by the Swedish engineer Johan August Brinell in 1900. It was the first widely used and standardised hardness test in engineering and metallurgy, and remains common for metals with coarse or inhomogeneous grain structures, such as castings and forgings.

Method

In the Brinell test, a hardened steel or tungsten carbide ball — commonly 10 mm in diameter — is pressed into the flat surface of the test piece under a specified load for a set time. The diameter of the resulting indentation is measured optically, and the Brinell hardness number (BHN, or HB) is calculated as the load divided by the curved surface area of the indentation:

HB = 2P / (πD(D − √(D² − d²)))

where P is the applied load, D the ball diameter and d the indentation diameter. Because the indentation is relatively large, the test averages out local structural variations, but it leaves a significant mark and is unsuitable for very thin or very hard materials.

Comparison

For most steels an approximate relationship exists between Brinell hardness and tensile strength, making the test a convenient non-destructive check. Related indentation methods include the Rockwell and Vickers tests, which use different indenters and are better suited to small or hard specimens.
