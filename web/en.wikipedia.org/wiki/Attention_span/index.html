Attention span

Attention span
FieldPsychology
DefinitionThe length of time a person can concentrate on a task without becoming distracted
Depends onAge, interest, fatigue, task type, environment

Attention span is the amount of time spent concentrating on a task before becoming distracted. It is not a single fixed quantity: it varies greatly with age, motivation, the nature of the task and the environment. Distraction occurs when attention is drawn away uncontrollably to another activity or sensation, and sustained focus typically requires periodically renewing interest in the task.

Development and typical values

Attention span increases markedly through childhood. Common developmental guidelines suggest young children can typically sustain focused attention on a single engaging task for roughly two to three minutes per year of age, though figures vary by source and circumstance; adults may sustain attention on demanding tasks for twenty minutes or much longer before needing a break or change, especially when the material is interesting. Choice and intrinsic motivation greatly extend apparent spans — the same person may drift during a dull lecture yet remain absorbed in a hobby for hours.

Measurement

Psychologists assess sustained attention with vigilance tasks such as continuous performance tests, in which participants respond to rare target stimuli over an extended period; performance decrements over time (the "vigilance decrement") index the limits of sustained concentration. Self-report measures and classroom observation are also used, particularly in developmental and clinical contexts such as the assessment of ADHD.

Popular claims and the media

It is often claimed that modern media and the internet have drastically shortened human attention spans — for example the widely repeated assertion that the average span fell from 12 seconds in 2000 to 8 seconds, "shorter than a goldfish". Researchers note that this statistic lacks a solid scientific source and that attention is better understood as task- and context-dependent rather than a single shrinking number. Nevertheless, heavy media multitasking correlates in some studies with poorer performance on laboratory attention tasks, and educators and designers actively study how to hold attention responsibly.
