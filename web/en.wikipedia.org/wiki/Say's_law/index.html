FieldClassical economics, macroeconomics

Say's law of markets is a principle of classical economics associated with the French businessman and economist Jean-Baptiste Say (1767–1832). It is commonly summarised in the phrase "supply creates its own demand": the production of goods generates income sufficient to purchase the goods produced, so that a general glut — aggregate supply exceeding aggregate demand across the whole economy — cannot persist.

Formulation

Say argued that production, not consumption, is the source of demand: in order to buy, an agent must first have produced something of value and received income from it. Money is merely a veil through which real goods exchange for other real goods. Particular goods can be overproduced relative to others, but this reflects misallocation between sectors, not a deficiency of demand in general.

Classical development

Versions of the doctrine were adopted by David Ricardo, James Mill, and John Stuart Mill, and it underpinned classical conclusions that unemployment would be self-correcting through wage and price flexibility, and that saving is automatically channelled into investment.

Criticism

The law was disputed by Malthus and Sismondi in Say's own time, and most famously attacked by John Maynard Keynes in The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), who treated Say's law as the core error of classical theory and argued that aggregate demand could fall short of aggregate supply, producing sustained involuntary unemployment. Debates over the precise meaning and validity of Say's law remain a staple of the history of economic thought.
