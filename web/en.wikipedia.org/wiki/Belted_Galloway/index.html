The Belted Galloway is a traditional Scottish breed of beef cattle. It derives from the Galloway cattle of the south-west of Scotland, and is distinguished by its broad white belt, or sheet, that encircles the midsection between the front legs and the hind legs. Affectionately called "Belties" — and in North America sometimes "Oreo cows" for their black-white-black resemblance to the biscuit — the breed is hardy, long-lived and well suited to rough grazing.

Characteristics

Belted Galloways are naturally polled (hornless) and have a thick double coat: a long, coarse outer layer that sheds rain over a soft, dense undercoat, an adaptation to the wet, cold climate of Galloway. The recognised colours are black, red and dun, each with the characteristic white belt; mismarked animals lacking a complete belt occur but cannot be registered as pedigree stock. Cows typically weigh 450–600 kg and bulls 800–1,000 kg. The breed is noted for maternal ability, easy calving, longevity and the production of well-marbled beef on forage alone.

History

Belted cattle have been described in Galloway for several centuries, though the origin of the belt pattern — possibly introduced by Dutch Lakenvelder blood — is uncertain. A separate herd book for Belted Galloways was established in 1921, distinct from the Galloway herd book dating to 1878, and the Belted Galloway Society was founded in 1921 in Scotland (with a separate society in the United States). The breed has been exported worldwide, including to North America, Australia, New Zealand and continental Europe, where it is valued for conservation grazing as well as beef production.
