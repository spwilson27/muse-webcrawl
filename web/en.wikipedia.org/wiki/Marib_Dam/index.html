The Marib Dam (Arabic: سَدّ مَأْرِب, Sadd Maʾrib) was an ancient dam across the Wadi Dhana near Marib in present-day Yemen, considered one of the greatest engineering achievements of the ancient world. It irrigated the oasis of Marib, capital of the kingdom of Saba (Sheba), and sustained the agricultural wealth that underpinned South Arabia's incense trade for over a thousand years.

History

Irrigation works at Marib date back to at least the second millennium BC in rudimentary form; the great dam was begun around the eighth century BC and repeatedly enlarged and repaired, notably under the Sabaean king Karib'il Watar and later under Himyarite rulers such as Sharhabil Ya'fur (who repaired it in AD 449–50) and the Ethiopian viceroy Abraha, whose inscription records a major repair in the 540s. The dam was an earthen embankment some 580 metres long between rocky outcrops, with stone sluice structures at each end feeding north and south canal systems, allowing the seasonal floodwaters (sayl) of the wadi to be spread over the fields.

Collapse and legend

The dam breached several times — notably in AD 145 and during the sixth century — and was abandoned after a final catastrophic breach, conventionally dated to about AD 570, following political and economic decline and, tradition holds, earthquake damage. The failure of the dam and the consequent emigration of South Arabian tribes became a famous episode in Arab memory, referenced in the Quran (Surah Saba, 34:15–17), which describes the gardens of Sheba replaced by bitter fruit after the flood of the dam (sayl al-ʿarim).

Modern dam

A new Marib Dam, a modern earth-fill dam 38 m high storing some 400 million cubic metres of water, was built in 1984–86 with financing from Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan of the United Arab Emirates — whose ancestors were said to have emigrated from Marib after the ancient dam's collapse — slightly upstream of the ancient site, and today supports groundwater recharge and irrigation in the region.
