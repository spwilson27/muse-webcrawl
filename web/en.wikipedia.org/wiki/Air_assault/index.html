Air assault

Air assault is the movement of ground-based military forces by vertical take-off and landing (VTOL) aircraft — typically helicopters — to seize and hold terrain, insert forces behind enemy lines, and engage enemy forces in terrain that is otherwise difficult to reach. Unlike airborne operations, in which troops are delivered by parachute from fixed-wing aircraft and cannot easily be extracted, air assault forces are landed and can be repositioned, reinforced, or withdrawn by the same aircraft that delivered them. Air assault should be distinguished from airlift, since air assault involves delivering forces directly into combat, often under fire.

Troops insert by helicopter during an air assault operation.

History

The concept emerged with the military helicopter in the early 1950s. During the Korean War, helicopters were used mainly for medical evacuation and liaison, but experiments with troop lift pointed toward a larger role. The French employed helicopters extensively in the Algerian War, and the concept was institutionalised by the United States Army following the 1962 Howze Board, which recommended the creation of "airmobile" divisions. The 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) deployed to Vietnam in 1965 and validated large-scale helicopter-borne operations at the Battle of Ia Drang. Air assault was subsequently adopted by armed forces worldwide, and the U.S. Army's 101st Airborne Division was reorganised as an air assault division, conducting one of the largest such operations in history during the 1991 Gulf War.

Organization and technique

Air assault operations are usually conducted by combined-arms teams built around helicopter lift units (assault helicopter battalions flying aircraft such as the UH-60 Black Hawk), heavy lift (CH-47 Chinook) for artillery and vehicles, and attack helicopter escort (AH-64 Apache). Insertion techniques include landing in cleared zones, fast-roping, rappelling, and SPIE (Special Patrol Insertion/Extraction) rigging; vehicles and guns may be carried as sling loads beneath the aircraft. A typical operation proceeds in phases: reconnaissance and preparation of the landing zone, the air movement itself in timed serials, the ground tactical plan to seize objectives, and subsequent sustainment or extraction by air.

Advantages and vulnerabilities

Air assault gives a commander the ability to mass forces rapidly over obstacles — rivers, mountains, jungle, urban areas — and to strike an enemy's flanks and rear. Its weaknesses are equally pronounced: helicopters are vulnerable to anti-aircraft artillery, man-portable air-defense systems, and small-arms fire at the landing zone; operations are sensitive to weather and darkness; and once inserted, troops depend on continued air lines of communication for supply and evacuation.
