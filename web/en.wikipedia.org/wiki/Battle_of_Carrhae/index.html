Battle of Carrhae

The Battle of Carrhae was fought in 53 BC near the town of Carrhae (modern Harran, Turkey) between the Roman Republic and the Parthian Empire. The Roman force, commanded by the triumvir Marcus Licinius Crassus, was destroyed by a much smaller Parthian army of horse archers and cataphracts led by the general Surena. Crassus was killed, some 20,000 Romans died and 10,000 were captured, and the legionary eagles (aquilae) were lost — one of the worst defeats in Roman history, which ended Rome's first great push into Mesopotamia and destabilized the late Republic.

Background

Crassus, the richest man in Rome and a member of the First Triumvirate with Pompey and Caesar, sought military glory to match his partners and invaded Parthia in 54 BC without senatorial authorization for war. Rejecting the Armenian king's offer of a mountain route and reinforcements, he marched directly across the open Mesopotamian plain toward Seleucia, guided — treacherously, it was said — by an Arab chieftain in Parthian pay.

Battle

Near Carrhae the Romans encountered Surena's all-cavalry army. Crassus formed his infantry in a hollow square. The Parthian horse archers, supplied with arrows by a camel train, shot the legions to pieces from beyond effective reply range — the origin of the "Parthian shot", fired backward while retreating. A detachment led by Crassus's son Publius was drawn away by a feigned retreat and annihilated; Publius's head was displayed to the Roman army on a spear. The survivors withdrew to Carrhae and abandoned their wounded; the next day Crassus was killed during a parley that turned violent. Legend later claimed molten gold was poured down his throat in mockery of his greed.

Aftermath

The quaestor Gaius Cassius Longinus led some 10,000 survivors back to Syria and defended the province against Parthian counter-invasion. The captured eagles remained in Parthian hands until Augustus recovered them by diplomacy in 20 BC. Crassus's death removed a balancing figure from Roman politics, hastening the collapse of the Triumvirate, the civil war between Caesar and Pompey, and the fall of the Republic.
