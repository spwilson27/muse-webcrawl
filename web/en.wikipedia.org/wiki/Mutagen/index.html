Mutagen

In genetics, a mutagen is a physical or chemical agent that permanently changes genetic material, usually DNA, in an organism and thus increases the frequency of mutations above the natural background level. As many mutations can cause cancer in animals, mutagens are therefore also likely to be carcinogens, although not necessarily in every case.

History

The mutagenic effect of X-rays was demonstrated in 1927 by Hermann Joseph Muller, who showed that radiation dramatically increased mutation rates in the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster. Charlotte Auerbach and colleagues later showed during the Second World War that mustard gas is also mutagenic. These discoveries founded the field of mutation research and raised early concerns about radiation safety.

Types of mutagens

Physical mutagens

Ionizing radiation such as X-rays and gamma rays breaks chemical bonds in DNA, causing single- and double-strand breaks. Ultraviolet light, though non-ionizing, induces the formation of pyrimidine dimers that distort the double helix and block replication unless repaired.

Chemical mutagens

Chemical mutagens act by several mechanisms. Base analogues such as 5-bromouracil resemble normal bases and are mistakenly incorporated into DNA. Intercalating agents including ethidium bromide slip between base pairs and cause insertions or deletions. Alkylating agents such as ethyl methanesulfonate add alkyl groups to bases, altering their pairing properties. Reactive oxygen species generated in metabolism also damage DNA continuously.

Biological mutagens

Some viruses integrate into host genomes, and transposons — mobile DNA sequences — can insert into genes and disrupt them, acting as endogenous mutagens.

Effects

Mutations range from single-nucleotide changes to large chromosomal rearrangements. Mutations in somatic cells may contribute to cancer; mutations in germ cells can be inherited. Organisms possess DNA repair systems — including nucleotide excision repair and mismatch repair — that counteract mutagenic damage.

Testing

The Ames test, developed by Bruce Ames in the 1970s, uses histidine-dependent strains of Salmonella bacteria to detect chemicals that restore growth by mutation. It remains a standard first screen for mutagenicity in drugs, food additives, and industrial chemicals.
