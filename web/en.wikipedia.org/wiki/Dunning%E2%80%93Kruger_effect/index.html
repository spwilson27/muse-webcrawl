Dunning–Kruger effect

The Dunning–Kruger effect is a cognitive bias in which people with limited competence in a particular domain overestimate their abilities in that domain. It was identified by psychologists David Dunning and Justin Kruger, whose 1999 study found that participants who scored in the lowest quartile on tests of humour, grammar and logic grossly overestimated their test performance.

Original study

In their paper "Unskilled and Unaware of It", Kruger and Dunning proposed that incompetence robs people of the metacognitive ability to recognise their own incompetence: the skills needed to produce a correct response are the same skills needed to evaluate one. High performers, by contrast, tended to slightly underestimate their relative standing.

Explanation and debate

The effect is often summarised as "a little knowledge is a dangerous thing". Later commentators have raised methodological concerns, arguing that some of the pattern can arise from statistical artefacts such as regression to the mean combined with a general tendency to rate oneself above average. Others have found the effect robust in certain settings but weaker or more domain-specific than popular accounts suggest. In popular culture the term is widely used for overconfidence among novices.
