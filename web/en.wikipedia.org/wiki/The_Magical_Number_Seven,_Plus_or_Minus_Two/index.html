"The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two: Some Limits on Our Capacity for Processing Information" is a 1956 paper by cognitive psychologist George A. Miller of Harvard University, published in The Psychological Review. It is one of the most frequently cited papers in psychology and a foundational document of the cognitive revolution.

Content

Miller reviewed experiments on two kinds of limitation. First, in absolute judgment tasks — such as identifying tones differing only in pitch, or tastes differing in saltiness — people can reliably distinguish only about seven categories (roughly 2.6 bits of information). Second, the span of immediate (short-term) memory is likewise about seven items, plus or minus two. Miller showed that this limit is measured not in bits but in "chunks": by recoding information into meaningful units (for example, grouping digits into familiar numbers), people can greatly increase the amount they hold, while the number of chunks stays near seven.

Influence and critique

The paper's playful title and elegant synthesis made "Miller's law" famous beyond psychology, influencing design, linguistics and human–computer interaction. Later research, notably by Nelson Cowan (2001), argued that the true capacity of short-term memory, when rehearsal and chunking are controlled, is closer to four items plus or minus one.
