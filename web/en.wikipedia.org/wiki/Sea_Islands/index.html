Sea Islands

Marsh and tidal creeks among the Sea Islands of the Lowcountry.

The Sea Islands are a chain of more than a hundred tidal and barrier islands on the Atlantic coast of the southeastern United States, stretching from the mouth of the St. Johns River in Florida through Georgia to the Santee River in South Carolina. They are separated from the mainland by tidal marshes, sounds and the Intracoastal Waterway.

Location: Atlantic coast of Florida, Georgia and South Carolina, United States

Major islands: Amelia Island, Cumberland Island, Sapelo Island, St. Simons Island, Hilton Head Island, Daufuskie Island, St. Helena Island, Edisto Island

Known for: Gullah culture, Sea Island cotton, historic plantations and resorts

History

The islands were inhabited by Indigenous peoples such as the Guale and Yamasee for millennia before Spanish missions were established in the 16th and 17th centuries. Under British and then American rule, plantation agriculture — first indigo and rice, then the exceptionally long-stapled Sea Island cotton — was worked by enslaved Africans, whose relative isolation on the islands fostered the distinctive Gullah (in South Carolina) and Geechee (in Georgia) culture, language and traditions.

Civil War and after

Union forces occupied the islands early in the Civil War following the Battle of Port Royal (1861), and the Port Royal Experiment made them a centre of education and land reform for freedpeople. Many island communities remained majority African American into the 20th century, preserving African-derived language, crafts such as sweetgrass basketry, and foodways.

Modern era

Some islands, such as Hilton Head Island, have become major resort and golf destinations, while others — Sapelo and Cumberland — remain largely protected or sparsely settled, with ferry-only access. The islands are low-lying and vulnerable to hurricanes and sea-level rise.
