Amiens Cathedral (French: Cathédrale Notre-Dame d'Amiens) is a Roman Catholic church in Amiens, France, and the seat of the Bishop of Amiens. It is the largest Gothic cathedral in France by interior volume — about 260,000 cubic metres — and one of the finest examples of the Rayonnant phase of French Gothic architecture. Construction began in 1220 under master mason Robert de Luzarches, and the nave was completed by about 1270, a remarkably rapid pace made possible by generous funding, including profits from the woad trade.

Architecture

The cathedral's nave vault rises 42.3 metres, among the tallest in France. Its west façade features three deeply recessed portals with elaborate sculpture, including the famous Beau Dieu Christ on the central trumeau, and a large rose window (rebuilt in the 16th century) between towers of unequal design. The labyrinth in the nave floor, laid in 1288, and the 16th-century Flamboyant choir stalls — over 4,000 carved figures — are notable features. A revered relic, said to be the head of John the Baptist, brought from Constantinople in 1206, made the cathedral a pilgrimage site.

History

The cathedral survived the French Revolution with relatively light damage and was restored in the 19th century under Eugène Viollet-le-Duc. It escaped serious harm in both World Wars. It was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1981.
