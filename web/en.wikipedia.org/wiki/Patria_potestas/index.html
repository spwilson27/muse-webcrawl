Patria potestas

Patria potestas was the power held in ancient Roman law by the male head of a family — the pater familias — over his children and other descendants in the male line. It was one of the defining institutions of Roman family law and lasted, in modified form, into late antiquity.

Scope of the power

Under patria potestas, adult sons as well as children remained legally subordinate to their father: property they acquired generally belonged to him, and they could not own assets independently except for the peculium, a fund the father allowed them to manage, and later military earnings (peculium castrense). Early tradition attributed to the pater familias even a power of life and death (ius vitae necisque) and the right to sell children, though by the classical period these were heavily restricted or obsolete.

Acquisition and ending

Patria potestas arose by birth in lawful marriage, by adoption, or by adrogation. It ended with the death of the pater familias, by emancipation of the child, or by certain changes of status. Daughters passed from their father's power into marriage (in the older cum manu form) or remained under it in later sine manu marriage.

Legacy

The concept influenced European civil law ideas of parental authority, though modern parental rights differ fundamentally, being framed as duties toward the child rather than powers over them.
