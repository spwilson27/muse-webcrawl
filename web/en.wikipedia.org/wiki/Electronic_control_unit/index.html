Electronic control unit


An automotive electronic control unit (ECU)

An electronic control unit (ECU), also known as an electronic control module (ECM), is an embedded system in automotive electronics that controls one or more of the electrical systems or subsystems in a car or other motor vehicle.

Modern vehicles have many ECUs, and these can include some or all of the following: engine control module (ECM), powertrain control module (PCM), transmission control module (TCM), brake control module (BCM or EBCM), central control module (CCM), central timing module (CTM), general electronic module (GEM), body control module (BCM), and suspension control module (SCM). These ECUs together are sometimes referred to collectively as the car's computer (technically, there is no single computer but multiple ECUs). Sometimes one assembly incorporates several individual control modules (for example, the PCM incorporates both engine and transmission control modules).

Key elements

The core of the ECU is one or more microcontrollers or digital signal processors that execute software stored in read-only memory or flash memory. Inputs come from sensors placed throughout the vehicle, and outputs drive actuators such as fuel injectors, ignition coils, solenoid valves, and electric motors. ECUs communicate with one another over vehicle networks, most commonly the CAN bus, as well as LIN, FlexRay, and automotive Ethernet.

Types

Engine control unit

An engine control unit controls a series of actuators on an internal combustion engine to ensure optimal engine performance. It does this by reading values from a multitude of sensors within the engine bay, interpreting the data using multidimensional performance maps (called lookup tables), and adjusting the engine actuators accordingly.

Body control module

The body control module (BCM) manages comfort and convenience functions such as electric windows, mirrors, air conditioning, immobilizer functions, and central locking.

Design and testing

ECU software is developed under functional-safety standards such as ISO 26262, and the hardware must survive harsh environments: wide temperature ranges, vibration, humidity, and voltage transients. Before production, ECUs are validated with hardware-in-the-loop simulation.

Modern trends

As the number of ECUs in vehicles grew past one hundred in some luxury models, manufacturers began consolidating functions into fewer, more powerful domain controllers and zonal computers, an architecture sometimes called a zone-oriented or software-defined vehicle design.
