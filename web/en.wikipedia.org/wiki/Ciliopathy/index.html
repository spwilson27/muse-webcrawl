Ciliopathy

A ciliopathy is any genetic disorder caused by defects in the structure or function of cilia — the hair-like organelles projecting from the surface of most cells of the body — or of the basal bodies and centrosomes from which cilia arise. Because cilia are nearly ubiquitous and serve key roles in development and sensory signalling, ciliopathies produce a wide and overlapping range of symptoms.

Background

For much of the 20th century cilia were largely studied for their role in motility, as in the airways and in sperm. The recognition in the early 2000s that the non-motile primary cilium acts as a cellular "antenna" coordinating signalling pathways such as Hedgehog, Wnt and platelet-derived growth factor led researchers to link many previously unrelated syndromes to ciliary dysfunction.

Signs and symptoms

Features that recur across the ciliopathies include:

  • Retinal degeneration and blindness (as in retinitis pigmentosa and Leber congenital amaurosis)
  • Cystic disease of the kidneys (nephronophthisis, polycystic kidney disease)
  • Polydactyly and other skeletal abnormalities
  • Brain malformations, including the molar tooth sign of Joubert syndrome
  • Obesity, as in Bardet–Biedl syndrome and Alström syndrome
  • Situs inversus and laterality defects, and chronic respiratory disease, as in primary ciliary dyskinesia
  • Liver fibrosis and anosmia (loss of smell)

Known ciliopathies

Syndromes classified as ciliopathies include Bardet–Biedl syndrome, Joubert syndrome, Meckel–Gruber syndrome, nephronophthisis, Senior–Løken syndrome, orofaciodigital syndrome, Ellis–van Creveld syndrome, Jeune syndrome (asphyxiating thoracic dystrophy), Usher syndrome, Alström syndrome and primary ciliary dyskinesia. The list continues to grow as new disease genes are mapped to ciliary proteins.

Genetics

Ciliopathies are typically inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern and show extensive genetic heterogeneity: mutations in many different genes encoding ciliary, basal-body or intraflagellar transport proteins can produce similar phenotypes, and mutations in the same gene can produce different syndromes depending on their severity.
