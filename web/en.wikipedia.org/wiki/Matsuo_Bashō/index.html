Matsuo Bashō

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A stone monument inscribed with Bashō's famous frog haiku beside a still pond]
A stone monument inscribed with Bashō's famous frog haiku beside a still pond

Matsuo Bashō (1644 – 28 November 1694) was the most famous poet of Japan's Edo period and the master who elevated the Haiku (then called hokku) from comic wordplay into a major art. His travel diaries, above all Oku no Hosomichi (The Narrow Road to the Deep North), blend haiku with prose (a form called Haibun) in pursuit of an aesthetic of impermanence, lightness (karumi) and sabi — the beauty of loneliness and age.

Life

Born Matsuo Kinsaku near Ueno in Iga Province to a low-ranking samurai family, Bashō served the young lord Todo Yoshitada as a poetry companion; after Yoshitada's death he moved to Edo (Tokyo), taught poetry, and took the name Bashō ('banana plant') from a tree given by a disciple, whose hut it sheltered. From 1684 he made the great journeys — on foot, in deliberate poverty — that define his legend, visiting the poetic places (utamakura) of the classical canon.

Poetry and journeys

Bashō's frog poem — 'old pond / a frog jumps in / the sound of water' (1686) — is the most famous haiku ever written, a revolution of stillness against wit. His travel records Nozarashi Kikō, Oi no Kobumi and Oku no Hosomichi (journey of 1689, published 1702) trace a pilgrim-poet through the war-haunted north, finding eternity in cicadas' voices and summer grasses 'the dreams of warriors' bones'.

Influence

Bashō's school (the Shofu style) spread haikai poetry nationwide; his disciples included Kikaku and Joso. After his death on a journey to Osaka, his reputation grew into something like sainthood ('haisei', the haiku saint). In the West, translations by R. H. Blyth and others made Bashō the gateway to Japanese poetry and a tutelary figure for the Beats and imagists.
