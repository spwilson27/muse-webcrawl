Stereotactic surgery

Stereotactic surgery is a minimally invasive form of surgical intervention that uses a three-dimensional coordinate system to locate small targets inside the body and perform precise actions on them, such as biopsy, ablation, injection or implantation of electrodes. It is used chiefly in neurosurgery, where it allows deep structures of the brain to be reached along carefully planned trajectories that avoid critical tissue.

Technique

In frame-based stereotaxy, a rigid frame is fixed to the patient's skull under local anaesthesia. Imaging (CT or MRI) is performed with the frame in place, and fiducial markers in the images define a coordinate space in which any target can be located with sub-millimetre accuracy. An arc-and-carriage system attached to the frame then guides instruments to the computed coordinates. Frameless (neuronavigation) systems instead register optical or electromagnetic tracking of the patient's head to pre-operative images.

History

The first stereotactic apparatus, built by Victor Horsley and Robert Clarke in 1908, was used in animal experiments. Human stereotaxy began with Ernest Spiegel and Henry Wycis in 1947, and Lars Leksell's elegant arc-centred frame (1949) became the model for later systems. Leksell went on to pioneer stereotactic radiosurgery, in which precisely focused beams of radiation — as in the Gamma Knife — treat a target without any incision.

Applications

Stereotactic methods are used for brain tumour biopsy and resection guidance, drainage of abscesses and cysts, movement-disorder surgery including deep brain stimulation for Parkinson's disease and essential tremor, epilepsy evaluation with depth electrodes, and radiosurgical treatment of tumours and arteriovenous malformations. Accuracy, small incisions and reduced operating time make the approach standard in modern neurosurgical practice.
