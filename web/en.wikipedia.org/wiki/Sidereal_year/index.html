Sidereal year

Length: 365.25636 days (J2000)
Equals: 365 d 6 h 9 min 9.76 s
Compared: c. 20 min longer than the tropical year

A sidereal year (from Latin sidus, "star") is the time taken for the Earth to complete one orbit of the Sun with respect to the background of distant stars — that is, the time for the Sun to return to the same position relative to the fixed stars as seen from Earth. Its modern value is approximately 365.25636 days (365 days, 6 hours, 9 minutes, and 9.76 seconds) for the epoch J2000.0.

Difference from the tropical year

The sidereal year is about 20 minutes and 24.5 seconds longer than the tropical year (the year of the seasons, approximately 365.24219 days), which is measured relative to the March equinox. The difference arises from the precession of the equinoxes: Earth's axis wobbles over a cycle of roughly 25,800 years, so the equinoxes shift westward along the ecliptic by about 50.3 arcseconds per year, and the Sun returns to the equinox slightly before completing a full circuit against the stars. Because calendars are designed to keep the seasons in step, civil timekeeping follows the tropical year, not the sidereal year.

Uses

Astronomers use the sidereal year and its subdivisions in celestial mechanics, orbital calculations, and the definition of the Gaussian year (365.2568983 days), once used to define the astronomical unit. The slow divergence between sidereal and tropical reckonings also matters in astrology debates: Western astrology uses the tropical zodiac, while Hindu astrology uses a sidereal zodiac, the two now separated by roughly 24 degrees of arc.
