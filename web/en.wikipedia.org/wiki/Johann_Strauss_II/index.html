Johann Baptist Strauss II (25 October 1825 – 3 June 1899) was an Austrian composer of light music, principally dance music and operettas, and a violinist who directed his own dance orchestra. Known as the Waltz King, he composed over 500 works — waltzes, polkas, marches, quadrilles and stage works — and is chiefly remembered for An der schönen blauen Donau ("The Blue Danube"), Op. 314, and the operetta Die Fledermaus.

Early career

The eldest son of Johann Strauss I, he studied music in secret with his mother's encouragement, since his father intended him for a banking career. He formed his own orchestra and debuted at Dommayer's Casino in 1844, becoming his father's rival. After his father's death in 1849 he merged the two orchestras and inherited his father's audiences, later touring Europe and the United States.

Major works

Strauss's fame rests on waltzes such as The Blue Danube (1866–67), Geschichten aus dem Wienerwald (Tales from the Vienna Woods, 1868), Wein, Weib und Gesang (1869), Wiener Blut (1873), Rosen aus dem Süden (1880), Frühlingsstimmen (Voices of Spring, 1882) and the Kaiser-Walzer (Emperor Waltz, 1889). Encouraged by Offenbach and others, he turned to operetta, producing Indigo und die vierzig Räuber (1871), the enduringly popular Die Fledermaus (1874) and Der Zigeunerbaron (The Gypsy Baron, 1885). His polkas, including the Tritsch-Tratsch-Polka and Unter Donner und Blitz, remain concert favourites.

Legacy

Strauss's music defined the sound of imperial Vienna and entered the standard orchestral repertory; Brahms was an admirer and friend. The Vienna Philharmonic's New Year's Concert is devoted largely to the Strauss family. He died of pneumonia in 1899 and is buried in Vienna's Zentralfriedhof.
