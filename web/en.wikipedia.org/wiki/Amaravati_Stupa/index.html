The Amaravati Stupa, also called the Amaravati Mahachaitya, is a ruined Buddhist stupa at Amaravati in the Palnadu district of Andhra Pradesh, India, on the south bank of the Krishna River. Probably founded in the time of the Mauryan emperor Ashoka (3rd century BCE) and magnificently enlarged under the Satavahana dynasty between roughly the 1st and 3rd centuries CE, it was among the largest stupas in India and the centre of a distinctive school of early Buddhist sculpture. Like Bharhut and Sanchi, it is famous for narrative relief carving; uniquely, much of that carving now resides in the British Museum in London.

History

Amaravati (ancient Dhānyakaṭaka) was a major Buddhist centre for over a millennium, patronised by Satavahana kings and later by the Ikshvakus, whose capital Nagarjunakonda lay upriver. Inscriptions record donations from monks, nuns, merchants, and royal officials. The stupa was progressively encased: a brick core faced, in its final phase, with carved limestone slabs (Palnad marble) and surrounded by a carved railing some 4 metres high, with gateways at the four cardinal points and distinctive āyaka platforms bearing five pillars each. The monastic site remained active into the medieval period before declining by about the 14th century.

Destruction and excavation

The ruin was effectively rediscovered in the 1790s when a local raja quarried the mound for building material and lime. Colin Mackenzie surveyed the site from 1816, and his team's drawings preserve much that was later lost. In the 19th century large quantities of sculpture were shipped to Madras and to London — the "Amaravati marbles" — while other campaigns, including by the Archaeological Survey under Alexander Rea and later by French scholar Gabriel Jouveau-Dubreuil, recorded the plan. Today the site shows a low circular mound with a small museum nearby; a large reconstruction drawing conveys its original splendour.

Art

Amaravati sculpture is celebrated for its sinuous figures, densely packed yet legible narrative scenes of the Buddha's life and the Jataka tales, and its graceful treatment of crowds in flattened relief. Early phases represent the Buddha aniconically (footprints, empty throne, bodhi tree, wheel), while later panels show him in human form. The school influenced art across South and Southeast Asia, and its limestone reliefs — now principally in the British Museum's Asahi Shimbun Gallery and the Government Museum, Chennai — are regarded as masterpieces of Indian art.
