Piezo1 is a mechanically activated ion channel protein in humans, encoded by the PIEZO1 gene. It converts mechanical force — pressure, stretch and shear — into electrical and chemical signals, a process called mechanotransduction. Piezo1 and its relative Piezo2 were identified in 2010 by the group of Ardem Patapoutian, who shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery of these receptors for touch and proprioception.

Structure

Piezo1 is an enormous protein of about 2,500 amino acids. Three subunits assemble into a trimer shaped like a three-bladed propeller, whose curved blades deform the surrounding membrane into a dome. Force that flattens the dome is thought to lever open the central ion-conducting pore — a "force-from-lipids" gating mechanism.

Functions

Piezo1 is widely expressed. In blood-vessel endothelium it senses shear stress from flowing blood and is essential for vascular development in the embryo. In red blood cells it regulates cell volume and hydration. It also functions in the bladder, lungs, skin, bone and the lymphatic system, and in the alignment of cells to flow.

Clinical significance

Gain-of-function mutations in PIEZO1 cause dehydrated hereditary stomatocytosis (hereditary xerocytosis), in which red cells leak ions and lose water; a common gain-of-function variant in African populations appears to protect against severe malaria. Loss-of-function mutations cause a congenital lymphatic dysplasia with non-immune hydrops fetalis. Pharmacological activators such as the small molecule Yoda1 are used experimentally to probe the channel.
