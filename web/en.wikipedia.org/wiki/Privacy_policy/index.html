Privacy policy

A privacy policy is a statement or legal document (in privacy law) that discloses some or all of the ways a party gathers, uses, discloses, and manages a customer or client's data. Personal data can be anything that can be used to identify an individual, not limited to the person's name, address, date of birth, marital status, contact information, ID issue, and expiry date, financial records, credit information, medical history, where one travels, and intentions to acquire goods and services.


A privacy policy describes how an organisation collects and handles personal data.

In the case of a business, it is often a statement that declares a party's policy on how it collects, stores, and releases personal information it collects. It informs the client what specific information is collected, and whether it is kept confidential, shared with partners, or sold to other firms or enterprises. Privacy policies typically represent a broader, more generalized treatment, as opposed to data use statements, which tend to be more detailed and specific.

History

The exact contents of a certain privacy policy will depend upon the applicable law and may need to address the requirements of multiple countries or jurisdictions. While there is no universal guidance for the content of specific privacy policies, a number of organizations provide example forms. The concept gained prominence with the rise of the commercial internet in the 1990s, and privacy policies became a standard feature of websites.

Legal requirements

Many jurisdictions require organisations to publish a privacy policy. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union, the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) in the United States, and similar laws elsewhere impose requirements about transparency, lawful basis for processing, and the rights of data subjects, including rights of access, rectification, and erasure.

Criticism

Privacy policies are frequently criticised for being long, difficult to read, and rarely read by users. Studies have estimated that reading all the privacy policies an average person encounters in a year would take hundreds of hours. Researchers have proposed standardised, machine-readable, or "nutrition label" style formats to make policies easier to compare and understand.
