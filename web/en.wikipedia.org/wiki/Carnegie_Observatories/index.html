The Carnegie Observatories is the astronomy research division of the Carnegie Institution for Science (Carnegie Science), headquartered in Pasadena, California. Founded by George Ellery Hale in 1904 with the establishment of the Mount Wilson Observatory, it has been home to some of the most consequential discoveries in modern astronomy, including the expansion of the universe.

History

Hale founded the Mount Wilson Solar Observatory in 1904 with funding from Andrew Carnegie's institution, installing first the 60-inch and then the 100-inch Hooker telescope—the largest in the world for three decades. At Mount Wilson, Harlow Shapley used Cepheid variables to locate the Sun away from the galactic center, and Edwin Hubble demonstrated that "spiral nebulae" were separate galaxies and that the universe is expanding, laying the observational foundation of modern cosmology. Carnegie astronomers also pioneered studies of stellar populations (Walter Baade) and the chemical composition of stars.

Las Campanas and the Magellan telescopes

Seeking southern skies and darker sites, the observatories established Las Campanas Observatory in the Chilean Atacama Desert in 1969. The site hosts the twin 6.5-meter Magellan telescopes (Baade and Clay), commissioned in 2000 and 2002, the 2.5-meter du Pont telescope, and the 1-meter Swope telescope, with which the first optical counterpart to a gravitational-wave event was discovered in 2017. Las Campanas is also the chosen site of the Giant Magellan Telescope, a 25-meter-class telescope under construction by an international consortium led in part by Carnegie.

Research

Current research at the Carnegie Observatories spans exoplanets, supernovae and transient surveys, galaxy formation, stellar astrophysics, and observational cosmology, supported by its own instrument-building shops in Pasadena.
