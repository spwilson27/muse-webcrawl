Crustaceans (subphylum Crustacea) form a large, diverse group of arthropods including crabs, lobsters, crayfish, shrimp, krill, copepods, barnacles, and woodlice. Most crustaceans are aquatic, living in marine or fresh water, though some groups—such as woodlice and land crabs—are terrestrial. The study of crustaceans is called carcinology.

Anatomy

The crustacean body is divided into a cephalothorax and abdomen, covered by a chitinous exoskeleton that must be shed (molted) for growth. Distinguishing features include two pairs of antennae and biramous (two-branched) appendages. Many species breathe through feathery gills. Decapods such as lobsters and crabs have five pairs of walking legs, the first often modified into claws.

Ecology

Crustaceans occupy nearly every aquatic niche: copepods and krill form a huge share of marine zooplankton and underpin ocean food webs, feeding fish, seabirds, and baleen whales. Many are scavengers or filter feeders; deep-sea and cave species show extreme adaptations such as loss of eyes and pigment.

Human importance

Shrimp, crab, and lobster fisheries are worth billions of dollars annually, and shrimp farming is a major aquaculture industry. Crustacean shells are the source of chitin and its derivative chitosan, used in medicine, cosmetics, and water treatment. However, crustacean shellfish is also one of the most common food allergens. Some invasive crustaceans, such as the Chinese mitten crab and signal crayfish, cause significant ecological damage outside their native ranges.

Evolution

Crustaceans have a fossil record extending to the Cambrian period. Molecular studies now place crustaceans together with insects in the clade Pancrustacea, indicating insects evolved from within crustacean-like ancestors.
