Life table

In actuarial science and demography, a life table (also called a mortality table) is a table which shows, for each age, what the probability is that a person of that age will die before their next birthday (probability of death). In other words, it represents the survivorship of people from a certain population. They can also be explained as a long-term mathematical way to measure a population's longevity. Tables have been created for use by demographers, actuaries, and other interested parties, such as medical researchers, biologists, and historians.

Structure

A life table is typically built for a hypothetical cohort of, by convention, 100,000 newborns (the radix). Standard columns include:

SymbolMeaning
qxProbability of dying between age x and x+1
lxNumber of survivors at exact age x
dxNumber of deaths between age x and x+1
LxPerson-years lived between age x and x+1
TxTotal person-years lived above age x
exLife expectancy at age x

Life expectancy at age x is computed as ex = Tx / lx. A period life table uses mortality rates observed in a single period, while a cohort life table follows an actual birth cohort through time.

History

John Graunt published one of the earliest mortality analyses in 1662, based on London's Bills of Mortality. Edmond Halley constructed a rigorous life table in 1693 using data from the city of Breslau, laying foundations for actuarial science and the pricing of life annuities.

Applications

Life tables underpin life insurance pricing, pension planning, and public-health measures such as life expectancy. In ecology and evolutionary biology, life tables track survivorship and fecundity of populations, giving rise to survivorship curves of Type I, II, and III.
