Pedestrianisation

Pedestrianisation, in the broadest sense, is the process of removing or restricting motor vehicles from an urban area, or parts of it, to enable safe and easy movement of pedestrians, and permitting other activities in that space. Pedestrianised areas are often called pedestrian zones, pedestrian precincts, or pedestrian streets, and in North America are frequently known as pedestrian malls.

History

Early examples include the Strøget shopping street in Copenhagen, pedestrianised in 1962, and Dutch woonerf designs of the 1970s that shared street space between vehicles and residents. German cities developed extensive Fußgängerzonen during post-war reconstruction. Many city centres in Europe, Latin America and Asia have since pedestrianised their historic cores, while North American downtown pedestrian malls of the 1960s–70s had mixed results and many were reopened to traffic.

Advantages and criticism

Advocates cite improved air quality and safety, increased footfall and retail takings, space for cafés and public life, and encouragement of walking and public transport. Critics raise concerns about access for deliveries and disabled people, displacement of traffic to surrounding streets, and effects on businesses dependent on car-borne customers — issues usually addressed through permits, time-windowed delivery access, and design for accessibility.

Related measures

Pedestrianisation is related to car-free development, low-traffic neighbourhoods, congestion charging, and "superblock" schemes such as Barcelona's superilles, and to temporary street closures and open-streets events.
