Tony Awards

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Tony medallion: the comedy and tragedy masks in silver relief on the rotating medallion, stage lights behind]

The Tony Awards (formally the Antoinette Perry Award for Excellence in Broadway Theatre) are the premier honours for live Broadway theatre, presented annually in New York by the American Theatre Wing and the Broadway League. Named for the actress and Wing co-founder Antoinette 'Tony' Perry, they were first awarded in 1947 and first televised nationally in 1967.

The ceremony — 26 competitive categories in recent years spanning plays and musicals — is Broadway's coronation and its best advertisement: a performance-filled broadcast whose 'bump' can rescue a show's box office; a Best Musical win is worth millions in touring and ticket sales.

Records and rituals

The Producers (2001) holds the record haul with 12 wins; Hamilton (2016) holds the nomination record (16) and transformed the ceremony into a national civics lesson watched far beyond theatre audiences. Eligibility turns on the 41 Broadway houses and the season calendar; campaigning is ferocious within its genteel codes. The Wing's other great work — the American Theatre Wing's training and grant programmes — is the award's institutional twin.

EGOT and beyond

The Tony completes, with the Emmy, Grammy and Oscar, the EGOT set — achieved competitively by fewer than two dozen performers and creators. Recent ceremonies have wrestled publicly with the television audience's decline and the post-pandemic economics of Broadway itself, even as individual productions (Hamilton's successors, the British imports, the jukebox debates) keep the form argumentative and alive.
