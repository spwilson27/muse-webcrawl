Drip brew, or filtered coffee, is a method for brewing coffee which involves pouring water over roasted, ground coffee beans contained in a filter, creating the beverage known as drip coffee. Water seeps through the ground coffee, absorbing its constituent chemical compounds, and then passes through the filter. Spent coffee grounds are retained in the filter, while the brewed coffee is collected in a vessel such as a carafe or pot.

History

Paper coffee filters were invented in Germany in 1908 by Melitta Bentz, who punched holes in a brass pot, lined it with blotting paper from her son's school exercise book, and founded the Melitta company the same year. The cone-shaped manual dripper remained common in European homes for decades. In the United States, electric percolators dominated until the introduction of the automatic drip coffeemaker: Vincent Marotta's Mr. Coffee machine, launched in 1972 with Joe DiMaggio as spokesman, displaced the percolator in American kitchens within a few years. German manufacturer Technivorm's Moccamaster (1968) became a benchmark for home machines meeting specialty standards.

Method

For drip brewing, medium-ground coffee is placed in a filter of paper, cloth, or perforated metal. Water just off the boil (roughly 91–96 °C) is poured over the grounds, ideally in a slow spiral; in automatic machines, heated water is distributed by a shower head. A brief "bloom" phase, in which freshly roasted grounds release carbon dioxide, improves extraction. Brew ratios of about 1 part coffee to 15–17 parts water by mass are typical, with total contact times of three to four minutes for a manual pour-over.

Characteristics

Paper filters remove most of the coffee oils and the diterpenes cafestol and kahweol, producing a clean-tasting cup with less body than French press coffee and, according to some studies, a smaller effect on cholesterol. Metal and cloth filters allow more oils and fine particles through. Drip coffee generally contains more caffeine per serving than espresso simply because the serving is much larger, though espresso is more concentrated.

Variants

Manual pour-over devices—the Melitta cone, Hario V60, Kalita Wave, and Chemex (designed in 1941 by Peter Schlumbohm)—are central to specialty coffee culture. Cold drip (such as Kyoto-style slow drip) uses cold water over many hours. Vietnamese cà phê sữa đá uses a small single-cup metal drip filter called a phin.
