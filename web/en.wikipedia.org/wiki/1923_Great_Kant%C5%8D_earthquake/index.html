1923 Great Kantō earthquake

Date1 September 1923, 11:58 local time
Magnitude7.9 Mw
EpicentreSagami Bay, off Izu Ōshima
Casualties~105,000–142,000 dead or missing

The Great Kantō earthquake struck the Kantō Plain on the Japanese island of Honshu at 11:58 on Saturday, 1 September 1923. The earthquake had a magnitude of 7.9 and devastated Tokyo, the port city of Yokohama, and the surrounding prefectures. Firestorms ignited by the quake destroyed much of Tokyo, and more than 100,000 people were killed, making it the deadliest earthquake in Japanese history until the 2011 Tōhoku disaster surpassed it in other respects.

The earthquake

The quake's rupture occurred beneath Sagami Bay, along the Sagami Trough where the Philippine Sea Plate subducts. Shaking lasted between four and ten minutes in places. A tsunami up to 12 metres high struck the coast of Sagami Bay and the Bōsō Peninsula, and landslides buried villages in the mountainous areas of Kanagawa.

Fire and firestorms

Because the quake struck at lunchtime, thousands of charcoal braziers and stoves overturned in wooden houses, and typhoon winds spread the fires. Most deaths were caused by fire. In the single worst incident, about 38,000 people who had fled to an open space at the Honjo Army Clothing Depot in Tokyo were killed when a fire whirl swept the site.

Aftermath and violence

Martial law was declared. In the chaos, rumours that Koreans were poisoning wells and setting fires led vigilantes, police, and soldiers to massacre thousands of Korean residents — estimates of Korean victims range from about 6,000 upward — along with Chinese residents and Japanese mistaken for Koreans. The government suppressed reporting of the Kantō Massacre for decades.

Reconstruction

Rebuilding under Home Minister Gotō Shinpei modernised Tokyo with wider roads, parks designed as firebreaks, and reinforced-concrete buildings. The anniversary of the disaster, 1 September, is now Japan's Disaster Prevention Day.
