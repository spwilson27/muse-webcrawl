Covariance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In probability theory and statistics, covariance is a measure of the joint variability of two random variables. If the greater values of one variable mainly correspond to the greater values of the other, the covariance is positive; if the greater values of one mainly correspond to the lesser values of the other, it is negative. The sign of the covariance therefore shows the tendency of the linear relationship between the variables, while its magnitude depends on the scales of measurement and is consequently hard to interpret — a difficulty addressed by the normalised correlation coefficient.

Scatter plots with positive (left), near-zero (centre) and negative (right) covariance.

Definition

For random variables X and Y with finite second moments, the covariance is defined as the expected product of their deviations from their means:

cov(X, Y) = E[(X − E[X])(Y − E[Y])] = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y]

The covariance of a variable with itself is its variance: cov(X, X) = var(X) = σX². Its units are the product of the units of the two variables.

Sample covariance

Given paired observations (xi, yi), the sample covariance is the average product of the deviations from the sample means, usually with Bessel's correction (dividing by n − 1 rather than n) for an unbiased estimate.

Properties

  • Covariance is symmetric: cov(X, Y) = cov(Y, X).
  • It is bilinear: cov(aX + bZ, Y) = a·cov(X, Y) + b·cov(Z, Y).
  • Uncorrelated variables have covariance zero — but the converse holds only for jointly normal variables, since a strong non-linear dependence may have zero covariance.
  • var(X + Y) = var(X) + var(Y) + 2·cov(X, Y).
  • By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, |cov(X, Y)| ≤ σXσY, which underlies the definition of the correlation coefficient ρ = cov(X, Y) / (σXσY), confined to [−1, 1].

Covariance matrix

For a random vector, the pairwise covariances form the covariance matrix, a symmetric positive-semidefinite matrix central to multivariate statistics, principal component analysis, portfolio theory (where covariance between asset returns determines diversification benefit) and Kalman filtering.
