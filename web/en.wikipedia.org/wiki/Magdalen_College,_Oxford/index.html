Magdalen College (pronounced "Maudlin") is a constituent college of the University of Oxford. Founded in 1458 by William of Waynflete, Bishop of Winchester, it occupies a large site beside the River Cherwell at the eastern end of the High Street, and is noted for its great tower, its deer park and its water meadow.

History

Waynflete first established Magdalen Hall in 1448 and refounded it as a college in 1458 on the site of the Hospital of St John the Baptist, outside Oxford's east gate. The college grew wealthy through endowments and became one of Oxford's largest. Fellows of Magdalen were prominent in the religious disputes of the Reformation; in 1687 James II's attempt to impose a president on the college became a celebrated cause in the lead-up to the Glorious Revolution, when the fellows' resistance forced the king to restore their elected choice.

Buildings and grounds

The Great Tower, completed in 1509, is a landmark of the Oxford skyline; on May Morning the college choir famously sings from its top at dawn. The grounds include Addison's Walk along the Cherwell, a deer park stocked with fallow deer, and a water meadow famous for fritillaries. The New Building of 1733 faces the original cloister across the lawn.

People

Notable alumni include Oscar Wilde, C. S. Lewis (a fellow), Edward Gibbon, John Betjeman, and King Edward VIII, who studied there before the First World War. The college admits around 600 students in total.
