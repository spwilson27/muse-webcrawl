Bicycle-friendly

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bicycle-friendly policies and practices help some people feel more comfortable about choosing cycling as a transportation and recreation option. The level of bicycle-friendliness of a city, town or other community is influenced by many factors, including cycling infrastructure, terrain, weather, attitudes toward cycling, and the legal environment.

Infrastructure

Bicycle-friendly communities typically provide connected networks of cycle tracks, bicycle lanes, bicycle boulevards and shared-use paths, together with secure bicycle parking, bike-sharing systems and integration with public transport (bikes on trains and buses). Design standards from organisations such as the Dutch CROW manual and NACTO emphasise separation from fast or heavy motor traffic, directness and coherence of routes.

Assessment and rankings

The League of American Bicyclists runs a Bicycle Friendly Community programme that awards ratings from bronze to platinum; communities such as Davis, California and Portland, Oregon have achieved the highest levels. International rankings such as the Copenhagenize Index regularly place Copenhagen and Amsterdam at the top, followed by cities such as Utrecht, Antwerp, Strasbourg and Montreal. Studies associate higher cycling mode share with safer outcomes per trip — the "safety in numbers" effect.

Benefits and policy

Supporters cite public-health gains, reduced congestion and emissions, and lower transport costs. Critics sometimes dispute the displacement of parking or road space. Since the 2010s many cities have adopted "complete streets" and Vision Zero policies in which bicycle-friendliness is a component of wider street-safety and climate strategies.
