Shochet

A shochet (Hebrew: שוחט, plural shochtim) is a person trained and licensed in Jewish ritual slaughter (shechita) of animals and birds for food in accordance with Jewish law (halakha). The meat produced is kosher.

Requirements

A shochet must be an observant, pious Jew of good character, knowledgeable in the laws of shechita, and certified by a rabbinic authority after examination. In many communities a shochet must also be trained and examined periodically, and traditionally works under rabbinic supervision (hashgacha).

The slaughter

The shochet uses a razor-sharp, perfectly smooth knife called a chalaf (or sakin). The blade must be free of any nick or imperfection; the shochet inspects it before and after each animal. The cut is made across the throat in a single continuous motion, severing the trachea and esophagus and, in mammals, the carotid arteries and jugular veins, causing rapid unconsciousness and exsanguination. Jewish law forbids five improper techniques: pausing (shehiyah), pressing (derasah), tearing (chaladah), slipping the knife outside the prescribed region (hagramah), and ripping out the organs (ikkur).

After slaughter

After slaughter the animal's internal organs, especially the lungs in cattle, are inspected (bedikah) for defects that would render the animal treif (non-kosher). The shochet recites a blessing before beginning a session of slaughter.
