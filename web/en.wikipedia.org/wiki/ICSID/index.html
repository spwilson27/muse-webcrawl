The International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID) is an international arbitration institution established in 1966 for legal dispute resolution and conciliation between international investors and States. It is part of the World Bank Group and headquartered in Washington, D.C. As of recent counts, more than 160 countries have signed or ratified the ICSID Convention.

The ICSID Convention

The Convention on the Settlement of Investment Disputes between States and Nationals of Other States was drafted under the auspices of the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development and entered into force on 14 October 1966. Jurisdiction requires a legal dispute arising directly out of an investment, between a contracting State and a national of another contracting State, and written consent by both parties. Consent is most often given in advance through bilateral investment treaties, of which thousands exist.

Proceedings

Cases are heard by tribunals of one or three arbitrators. ICSID itself does not arbitrate; it administers proceedings, maintains panels of arbitrators and conciliators, and provides facilities. Awards are binding and enforceable in all contracting states as if they were final judgments of their own courts, subject only to annulment on narrow grounds by an ad hoc committee. ICSID's Additional Facility rules allow it to administer cases where one party is not from a contracting state or the dispute is not directly an investment dispute.

Caseload and criticism

By the 2020s ICSID had registered more than 900 cases, spanning sectors such as energy, mining, construction and finance, with claimants frequently suing over expropriation, fair and equitable treatment, and regulatory change. Investor–state dispute settlement has attracted criticism for constraining public regulation; several countries (Bolivia, Ecuador, Venezuela) withdrew from the Convention in the 2000s–2010s, and reform of the system has been debated in UNCITRAL.
