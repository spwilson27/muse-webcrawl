Aspergillus oryzae, known in Japan as kōji-kin ("koji mould"), is a filamentous fungus of the genus Aspergillus used for centuries in East Asian food fermentation. Grown on steamed rice, barley or soybeans, the mould produces powerful starch- and protein-degrading enzymes that convert grains into sugars and amino acids, forming the basis of sake, miso, soy sauce, mirin and amazake.

Domestication

A. oryzae is regarded as a domesticated form of the wild species Aspergillus flavus, selected over centuries of cultivation for rapid growth, abundant enzyme secretion and, crucially, loss of the ability to produce aflatoxins. Because of its long history of safe use in food, it is generally recognised as safe and is sometimes called Japan's "national fungus".

Uses

In sake brewing, koji rice provides the enzymes that saccharify rice starch so yeast can ferment it. In miso and soy sauce production, koji grown on grains is mixed with soybeans and salt and aged. Industrially, A. oryzae strains are used to produce enzymes such as taka-amylase — historically the first microbial enzyme product, marketed by Jokichi Takamine in the 1890s — and it is a workhorse of modern biotechnology for producing heterologous proteins.

Genome

The genome of strain RIB40 was sequenced in 2005: about 37 million base pairs on eight chromosomes with roughly 12,000 predicted genes, notably expanded families of secreted hydrolases reflecting its fermentative lifestyle.
