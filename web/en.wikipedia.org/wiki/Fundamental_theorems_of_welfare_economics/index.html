Fundamental theorems of welfare economics

The fundamental theorems of welfare economics are two theorems relating competitive market equilibria to Pareto efficiency. They form the theoretical core of the modern justification for the efficiency of markets, and for the separation of efficiency questions from questions of distributional justice.

First fundamental theorem

The first theorem states that, under certain conditions — including complete markets, no externalities, no transaction costs, and price-taking behaviour by all agents — every competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient: no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. The result is sometimes described as a formalisation of Adam Smith's "invisible hand". Notably, the first theorem requires no convexity assumptions.

Second fundamental theorem

The second theorem states the converse, under stronger assumptions including convexity of preferences and production sets: any Pareto-efficient allocation can be achieved as a competitive equilibrium, provided that initial endowments are suitably redistributed through lump-sum transfers. The theorem separates the roles of the market (to deliver efficiency) and the state (to choose among efficient allocations by adjusting endowments).

History

The ideas trace to the work of Vilfredo Pareto and the general equilibrium theory of Léon Walras; rigorous proofs were developed in the mid-twentieth century by Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu, whose Arrow–Debreu model supplied the formal framework in which the theorems are usually stated.

Limitations and interpretation

The theorems are conditional: real markets feature externalities, public goods, asymmetric information and market power, any of which can break the first theorem's conclusion. The second theorem's lump-sum transfers are generally infeasible in practice, since redistribution through real tax systems distorts behaviour. Economists also note that Pareto efficiency is a weak distributional criterion: an allocation in which one person holds nearly everything can still be Pareto efficient.
