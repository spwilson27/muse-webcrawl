Availability heuristic

The availability heuristic, also known as availability bias, is a mental shortcut that relies on immediate examples that come to a given person's mind when evaluating a specific topic, concept, method or decision. This heuristic, operating on the notion that if something can be recalled, it must be important, or at least more important than alternative solutions which are not as readily recalled, is inherently biased toward recently acquired information.

The concept was proposed by psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in 1973.

Overview

Under the availability heuristic, people tend to heavily weigh their judgments toward more recent information, making new opinions biased toward the latest news. The mental availability of an action's consequences is positively related to those actions' perceived magnitude; in other words, the easier it is to recall the consequences of something, the greater those consequences are perceived to be. People are biased by information that is easier to recall, vivid, or emotionally charged, and discount less salient information.

Examples

  • After seeing news reports of plane crashes, people overestimate the risk of flying relative to driving.
  • Judging that words beginning with K are more common than words with K as the third letter, because the former are easier to recall.
  • Overestimating the frequency of dramatic causes of death (homicide, tornadoes) and underestimating common ones (diabetes, stroke).

Applications

The heuristic has been studied in risk perception, media studies, law, medicine and marketing. Media coverage that dwells on unusual events can distort public estimates of their frequency, influencing policy priorities.
