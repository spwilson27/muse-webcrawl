Palace of Nestor

The Palace of Nestor (Greek: Ανάκτορο του Νέστορα) is the best-preserved Mycenaean Greek palace, discovered in 1939 on the hill of Epano Englianos near ancient Pylos in Messenia, southwestern Greece. It takes its modern name from Nestor, the legendary king of Pylos in Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, in whose palace Telemachus is received in the Odyssey.

Excavation and the Linear B tablets

The palace was excavated by Carl Blegen of the University of Cincinnati beginning in 1939 (the very first day of digging yielded clay tablets) and, after a wartime pause, from 1952 onwards. It yielded the largest cache of Linear B tablets found on mainland Greece — more than 600 in the palace's archive rooms — whose decipherment by Michael Ventris in 1952 proved the language of the tablets to be an early form of Greek. The tablets record palace administration: landholdings, rations, bronze, perfumed oil, livestock and offerings to gods.

The palace complex

The main building (about 130 by 105 feet) was two-storeyed, built around a central megaron with a monumental circular hearth flanked by four columns, a throne base, and walls painted with frescoes of griffins, lions, lyre-players and processions. It contained storerooms full of thousands of storage jars and drinking cups, workshops, an archive, and an unusual early bathtub installation. The palace was destroyed by fire around 1180 BCE at the end of the Late Bronze Age, a fire that baked — and thereby preserved — its clay archives.

Recent finds

In 2015, excavators from the University of Cincinnati uncovered near the palace the rich "Griffin Warrior" shaft grave (c. 1450 BCE), containing gold rings, weapons and the intricately carved Pylos Combat Agate, one of the finest known works of Minoan-Mycenaean glyptic art.
