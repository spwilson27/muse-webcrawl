Zwarteleen (Dutch, literally "black fief") is a term from feudal law in the medieval County of Flanders. It denoted a fief (leen) held by a vassal directly from a lord who himself held the land in allod — that is, the lord owed no feudal service upward for it and had no superior lord for that particular holding. Because the chain of tenure ended with that lord, the fief was said to be "black": it carried no onward obligation and could not be traced further up the feudal pyramid.

Background

In medieval Flanders, land tenure formed layered chains: a tenant-in-chief held from the count, his vassals held from him, and sub-vassals held fiefs in turn. Where a lord held land as a free allod (eigen goed) but chose to enfeoff a portion of it to a follower, the resulting fief was a zwarteleen. The distinction mattered for inheritance, relief payments, and the feudal incidents that could be claimed, since obligations on a zwarteleen ran only between the immediate lord and vassal.

Feudal registers and leenboeken (fief books) kept by the Flemish comital administration noted the character of such holdings so that the count's officers could distinguish fiefs that ultimately derived from the count from those that did not.
