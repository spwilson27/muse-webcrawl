Ceefax ("see facts") was a teletext information service developed by the BBC in the United Kingdom, launched in 1974. It was the world's first teletext system and was broadcast in the vertical blanking interval of the analogue television signal, allowing viewers with suitable decoders to browse pages of news, weather, sport, television listings, travel information, and other content on their television sets. Ceefax ran for 38 years before ending with the United Kingdom's digital switchover on 23 October 2012.

History

The BBC began experimenting with transmitting digital information in unused lines of the television signal in the early 1970s. Ceefax was announced in 1972 and launched on 23 September 1974 with an initial set of 30 pages. The system was standardised, together with the rival IBA system ORACLE, in the "UK Teletext" specification of 1976, later developed into World System Teletext. Initially a novelty available only on expensive sets, teletext decoders became standard in British televisions during the 1980s, and Ceefax grew into a major source of instant news and sports results, famous for its blocky Mode 7 graphics, its page 302 football index, and pages-from-Ceefax overnight broadcasts accompanied by music.

Content and use

Pages were numbered in three digits, grouped into "magazines": news in the 100s, sport in the 300s, weather in the 400s, and entertainment and listings elsewhere. Viewers keyed in a page number and waited a few seconds for the continuously cycling broadcast to reach it. Ceefax was celebrated for breaking news flashes, live football scores, cricket scorecards, and its subtitling service on page 888, which made television accessible to deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers.

Closure

As analogue television was switched off region by region, Ceefax disappeared with it; Northern Ireland was the last region to switch, and the service finally closed in the early hours of 23 October 2012, with a special farewell graphic. Its digital successor was the BBC Red Button service, while Ceefax-style pages became widely used objects of nostalgia in British media.
