Hedgehog (weapon)

The Hedgehog (also known as an Anti-Submarine Projector) was a forward-throwing anti-submarine weapon developed by the Royal Navy during the Second World War. It fired a salvo of 24 small contact-fuzed bombs ahead of the attacking ship, in an elliptical or circular pattern, while the ship retained sonar (ASDIC) contact with the target submarine — a decisive advantage over depth charges, which were dropped astern and temporarily blinded the attacker's sonar.

The Hedgehog launcher's 24 spigots gave it its name.

Design

The weapon was developed under the Directorate of Miscellaneous Weapons Development (the "Wheezers and Dodgers"). Based on the spigot mortar principle of Lieutenant Colonel Stewart Blacker's Bombard, the Hedgehog mounted 24 spigots on a fixed platform on the forecastle. Each projectile weighed about 16 kg (35 lb) with a 14 kg (30 lb) Torpex charge and sank at roughly 7 m/s. The bombs were fired in ripple pairs a tenth of a second apart, landing in a pattern about 30–40 metres across at a range of some 230 metres. Because the bombs were contact-fuzed, they exploded only on striking the submarine, so a miss produced no explosion and did not betray the target or disturb the water for following attacks.

Service

The Hedgehog entered service in 1942 and was fitted to hundreds of convoy escorts of the Royal Navy, the Royal Canadian Navy and the United States Navy. Statistics compiled after the war credit the Hedgehog with a significantly higher kill probability per attack than depth charges. Famous successes include the sinking of U-118, U-238 and U-482. In the Pacific, the destroyer escort USS England used Hedgehog in its celebrated destruction of six Japanese submarines in twelve days in May 1944.

Successors

The larger Squid mortar, which fired depth-fuzed projectiles with automatic setting from sonar, supplemented and partly replaced Hedgehog late in the war; Hedgehog itself remained in use into the postwar period, and its concept was echoed by weapons such as the American Mousetrap and later ahead-thrown systems.
