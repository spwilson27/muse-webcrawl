Kinder Scout is a moorland plateau and the highest point in the Peak District of Derbyshire, England, at 636 metres (2,087 ft) above sea level. It is the highest ground in the East Midlands and is famous in the history of access to the countryside as the site of the Mass Trespass of Kinder Scout in 1932, a landmark protest in the campaign for public access to open country.

Geography

Kinder Scout is a plateau of Millstone Grit in the Dark Peak, bounded by steep edges and cloughs. Its blanket peat surface is cut by deep erosion channels known as groughs. The River Kinder rises on the plateau and flows over Kinder Downfall, the tallest waterfall in the Peak District at about 30 metres, visible from the west. The plateau's summit area is broad and featureless, and navigation in mist is notoriously difficult. The Pennine Way long-distance path crosses the plateau on its route from Edale to the Scottish border. Much of the plateau is owned by the National Trust and forms part of the Peak District National Park, England's first national park, designated in 1951.

Mass Trespass of 1932

On 24 April 1932, around 400 ramblers, many from Manchester and organised with the involvement of the British Workers' Sports Federation and the activist Benny Rothman, set out from Hayfield to walk onto the plateau, then a private grouse moor from which the public was excluded. After scuffles with gamekeepers, the trespassers reached the plateau. Six men were arrested and tried at Derby Assizes; five were imprisoned for between two and six months. The harsh sentences caused public outrage, galvanising the access movement. The trespass is credited with helping to bring about the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949 and, eventually, the right to roam provisions of the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000. An annual Trespass celebration is held in Hayfield, and the event was commemorated on the 75th and 90th anniversaries by the Ramblers and the National Trust.

Ecology and conservation

The plateau's blanket bog is an internationally important habitat and carbon store. Decades of air pollution, overgrazing, wildfire and footpath erosion left large areas of bare peat; major restoration projects led by the Moors for the Future partnership have re-vegetated eroded areas with heather, cotton grass and sphagnum moss since the early 2000s. Kinder Scout is designated a Site of Special Scientific Interest, a Special Area of Conservation and a Special Protection Area, supporting upland birds such as golden plover and merlin.
