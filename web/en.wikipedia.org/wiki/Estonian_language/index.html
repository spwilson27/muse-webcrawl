Estonian (endonym: eesti keel) is a Finnic language of the Uralic family, spoken natively by about 1.1 million people, mostly in Estonia, where it is the official language. It is closely related to Finnish and more distantly to Hungarian.

History

Estonian developed from the Finnic dialects spoken along the eastern shore of the Baltic Sea. The oldest written specimens of Estonian date from the 13th century, and the first printed book, a bilingual catechism, appeared in 1535. Under centuries of German and later Russian rule, the language was long confined largely to the peasantry; a written standard based primarily on the North Estonian dialect emerged in the 19th century during the national awakening, and Estonian became the state language of the independent Republic of Estonia in 1918.

Grammar

Estonian is an agglutinative language with 14 noun cases. It has no grammatical gender and no articles. A notable feature is its system of three contrastive phonemic lengths — short, long and "overlong" — which can distinguish words: for example sada "hundred", saada "send!", and saada (overlong) "to get". Vowel harmony, present in Finnish, has largely been lost except in some southern dialects.

Vocabulary

Besides its Finnic core vocabulary, Estonian contains layers of loanwords from Low German, High German, Swedish and Russian. In the early 20th century the linguist Johannes Aavik carried out a programme of deliberate language renewal, introducing new words and forms, some coined artificially, that became part of the standard language.

Writing system

Estonian uses the Latin alphabet with the additional letters ä, ö, ü and õ; the letter õ (a close central unrounded vowel) is distinctive to Estonian among major Finnic languages. The letters š and ž appear in loanwords.
