Hot dry rock geothermal energy

Hot dry rock (HDR) is a type of geothermal energy resource and extraction technique that taps the heat stored in deep, hot, low-permeability crystalline rock — principally granite — which, unlike conventional hydrothermal reservoirs, contains little or no natural fluid or permeability.

Technology

To exploit HDR, water is pumped down an injection well at high pressure to open and extend fractures in the hot rock, creating an artificial reservoir. Water circulated through the fracture network is heated and returned to the surface through production wells, where it drives a turbine or supplies heat directly. The cooled water is reinjected, forming a closed loop. HDR is the conceptual ancestor of the broader category now called enhanced geothermal systems (EGS).

History

The first HDR project was developed at Fenton Hill in New Mexico by Los Alamos National Laboratory in the 1970s. Subsequent projects were built at Rosemanowes in Cornwall, England; Soultz-sous-Forêts in France; and the Cooper Basin in Australia, among others.

The potential resource is enormous: hot rock at drillable depths underlies much of the Earth's land surface, so HDR/EGS could in principle provide geothermal power far beyond the volcanic and tectonic zones used by conventional plants.
