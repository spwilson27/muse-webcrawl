Tethys is a mid-sized moon of Saturn about 1,066 km (662 mi) across. It was discovered by Giovanni Domenico Cassini in 1684 and is named after the Titaness Tethys of Greek mythology. Tethys has the lowest density of all the large moons in the Solar System, indicating that it is made almost entirely of water ice with only a small fraction of rock.

Tethys is heavily cratered and is cut by several large faults. Its largest impact crater, Odysseus, spans about 400 km — roughly two-fifths of the moon's diameter — while a vast canyon system, Ithaca Chasma, stretches some 2,000 km around much of the globe and may be related to the freezing of an ancient subsurface ocean.

Discovery and naming

Cassini discovered Tethys on 21 March 1684, along with Dione. He had previously found Iapetus and Rhea in 1671–72. The name Tethys was suggested by John Herschel in 1847, taken from the sister and wife of Oceanus in Greek myth.

Orbit

Tethys orbits Saturn at a distance of about 294,700 km, completing one orbit every 1.888 days, and is tidally locked. Two much smaller moons, Telesto and Calypso, share Tethys's orbit, occupying its leading and trailing Lagrange points respectively.

Physical characteristics

With a density of only 0.984 g/cm³, Tethys is composed almost entirely of water ice. Its surface is highly reflective, with a geometric albedo above 1.2, making it one of the brightest moons in the Solar System — an effect enhanced by fine ice grains blasted from Enceladus by its geysers and deposited across the Saturn system.

Surface features

The western hemisphere is dominated by the Odysseus crater. The Ithaca Chasma canyon system, up to 100 km wide and 3–5 km deep, runs roughly along a great circle passing near Odysseus's pole, suggesting the two features may be linked to the same period of internal activity. A smoother plains region with fewer craters hints at past resurfacing.

Exploration

Tethys was imaged by Pioneer 11 and the Voyager spacecraft, and extensively by the Cassini orbiter, which made its closest flyby in September 2005 at a distance of about 1,500 km.
