A galley is a type of ship propelled mainly by oars, used for warfare, trade, and piracy from antiquity through the early modern period, particularly in the Mediterranean Sea. Galleys were long, narrow vessels with shallow drafts, typically also carrying one or two masts with lateen sails for cruising, but relying on human muscle — free oarsmen, soldiers, or slaves and convicts — for speed in battle.

History

Oared warships were used by Egyptians, Phoenicians and Greeks in the Bronze and Iron Ages. The classical Greek trireme, with three banks of oars and some 170 rowers, dominated Mediterranean warfare in the 5th and 4th centuries BC. Hellenistic and Roman fleets built still larger polyremes. Roman galleys fought with boarding parties and, later, artillery.

In the medieval and early modern Mediterranean, galleys remained the principal warships of Venice, Genoa, the Ottoman Empire, Spain and the Knights of Malta. Their last great fleet action was the Battle of Lepanto (1571). Galleys persisted in warfare into the 18th century in the Mediterranean and the Baltic, and France maintained a galley corps — manned largely by convicts — until 1748.

Characteristics

Galleys were fast and manoeuvrable for short bursts and could operate in calm weather that becalmed sailing ships, but they were fragile in heavy seas, had limited range and cargo capacity, and required enormous crews with correspondingly large supplies of food and water. Their principal weapons were the bow ram in antiquity, and later bow-mounted cannon and boarding parties.
