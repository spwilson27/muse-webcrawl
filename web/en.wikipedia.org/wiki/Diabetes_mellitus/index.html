Diabetes mellitus

Diabetes mellitus, often known simply as diabetes, is a group of metabolic diseases characterised by chronic high blood sugar (hyperglycaemia) resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. Uncontrolled diabetes leads to serious damage to the heart, blood vessels, eyes, kidneys, and nerves.

Types

Type 1 diabetes results from autoimmune destruction of the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas and requires lifelong insulin therapy. Type 2 diabetes, accounting for over 90% of cases, involves insulin resistance and progressive secretory failure, strongly associated with obesity and inactivity. Gestational diabetes arises during pregnancy and increases later risk of type 2 diabetes.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis uses fasting plasma glucose, an oral glucose tolerance test, or glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c ≥ 6.5%). Management centres on blood-glucose monitoring, diet, exercise, and medications including metformin, newer agents such as GLP-1 receptor agonists, and insulin. Insulin was isolated in 1921 by Banting and Best, transforming type 1 diabetes from a fatal into a manageable disease.

Complications

Long-term complications include cardiovascular disease, diabetic retinopathy (a leading cause of blindness), nephropathy, neuropathy, and foot ulceration leading to amputation. Diabetes roughly doubles the risk of cardiovascular death.
