Race and ethnicity in the United States

Placeholder image: racial and ethnic composition of the United States by county.

The United States has a racially and ethnically diverse population. At the federal level, race and ethnicity have been categorised separately: the United States Census Bureau uses standards set by the Office of Management and Budget (OMB), under which race and ethnicity are treated as distinct concepts, with Hispanic and Latino Americans classified as an ethnic group that may be of any race. In 2024 the OMB revised its standards to combine race and ethnicity into a single question and to add a Middle Eastern or North African (MENA) category.

Census categories

Historically, the census racial categories included White; Black or African American; American Indian and Alaska Native; Asian; Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander; and "some other race", with respondents also able to select two or more races. Self-identification has been the basis of classification since the late twentieth century.

Demographics

The 2020 census recorded a population of about 331 million. Non-Hispanic White Americans remained the largest group, at about 58% of the population, while the multiracial population grew sharply between 2010 and 2020, partly because of changes in question design and coding. Hispanic or Latino Americans, of any race, made up about 18.7%; Black or African American alone about 12%; Asian alone about 6%; with American Indian and Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander, and other groups making up the remainder.

History

Racial classification in the United States dates to the first census of 1790, conducted under a constitutional apportionment rule that counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person. Over the centuries, categories shifted with slavery and emancipation, immigration law (including the Chinese Exclusion Act and the national-origins quotas of 1924), the civil rights era, and the growth of immigration after the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965. Legal doctrines such as the "one-drop rule" and anti-miscegenation statutes, struck down in Loving v. Virginia (1967), shaped historical conceptions of race.
