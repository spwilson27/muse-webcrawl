House of Lords

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The House of Lords is the upper house of the Parliament of the United Kingdom. It comprises about 800 members: life peers appointed under the Life Peerages Act 1958 (the great majority), 92 hereditary peers retained under the House of Lords Act 1999, and 26 Church of England bishops (Lords Spiritual). It meets in the Palace of Westminster, London.

Functions and powers

The Lords scrutinises and revises legislation, holding government to account through questions, debates and influential select committees (on the constitution, Europe, science and economics among them). Its legislative power is limited by the Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949: it cannot block money bills and can only delay most other bills for about a year; by convention (the Salisbury convention) it does not wreck manifesto legislation from the Commons. Until 2009 it was also the UK's final court of appeal, through the Law Lords — a function transferred to the new Supreme Court.

Reform debates

The Lords is the largest legislative chamber in any democracy after China's National People's Congress and the only upper house in the Western world with hereditary and clerical elements — features driving recurrent reform proposals (election, reduction, retirement ages), most only partially enacted. Defenders cite its expertise, independence from the electoral cycle and revising quality; the 2024 government's legislation to remove the remaining hereditary peers reopened the wider question of a senate-style replacement.
