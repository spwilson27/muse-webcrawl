An electric bicycle (also known as an e-bike, powerbike or booster bike) is a bicycle with an integrated electric motor used to assist propulsion. E-bikes range from models whose motor only assists the rider's pedalling (pedelecs) to more powerful machines with throttle control closer to a moped. Most retain functional pedals and are legally treated as bicycles in many jurisdictions when motor power and assisted speed stay below set limits.

Design and components

Modern e-bikes use a rechargeable lithium-ion battery pack, a controller, and either a hub motor built into a wheel or a mid-drive motor acting through the cranks. Sensors measure pedalling force, cadence or throttle input, and some systems recover energy through regenerative braking. Ranges typically run from 25 to over 100 kilometres per charge depending on battery capacity, terrain, rider weight and assistance level. Classes include city commuters, folding bikes, cargo bikes, and electric mountain bikes (eMTBs).

Regulation and adoption

In the European Union, pedelecs limited to 250 W and 25 km/h (15.5 mph) assistance are classed as bicycles, while faster "speed pedelecs" reaching 45 km/h are regulated as mopeds. The United States uses a three-class system based on throttle and top assisted speed (20 or 28 mph). China is the world's largest e-bike market, with hundreds of millions in use, and adoption surged globally in the 2010s and 2020s for commuting, delivery work and leisure. Studies generally find e-bike riders cycle more often and further than conventional cyclists, substituting some car trips, though debates continue over trail access, speed differentials and battery-fire safety.
