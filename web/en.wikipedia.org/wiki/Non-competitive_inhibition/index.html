Non-competitive inhibition

Non-competitive inhibition is a type of enzyme inhibition where the inhibitor reduces the activity of the enzyme without affecting the binding of substrate. The inhibitor binds to a site other than the active site — often an allosteric site — and can bind the enzyme whether or not the substrate has already bound. Because the inhibitor does not compete with the substrate for the active site, increasing substrate concentration cannot overcome the inhibition.

Kinetics

In Michaelis–Menten kinetics, a pure non-competitive inhibitor decreases the maximum reaction rate (Vmax) while leaving the Michaelis constant (Km) unchanged: the enzyme–inhibitor and enzyme–substrate–inhibitor complexes are catalytically inactive, so the effective concentration of working enzyme falls, but the remaining unbound enzyme binds substrate with normal affinity. On a Lineweaver–Burk plot, lines for different inhibitor concentrations intersect on the x-axis (at −1/Km).

Non-competitive inhibition is a special case of mixed inhibition in which the inhibitor binds the free enzyme and the enzyme–substrate complex with equal affinity. If the affinities differ, the inhibition is called mixed, and both Vmax and Km change.

Examples

Classic examples include the inhibition of enzymes by heavy-metal ions such as Ag+ and Hg2+, which bind to sulfhydryl groups away from the active site, and cyanide's inhibition of cytochrome c oxidase. Many allosteric drugs act in a non-competitive (or mixed) fashion; the non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) used against HIV bind a pocket distinct from the active site of reverse transcriptase.
