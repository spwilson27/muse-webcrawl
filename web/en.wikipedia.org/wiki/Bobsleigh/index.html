Bobsleigh (also called bobsled in North America) is a winter racing sport in which teams of two or four make timed runs down a narrow, twisting, banked ice track in a gravity-powered sled. Modern bobsleighs can exceed 150 km/h (93 mph), among the fastest of winter sports. The sport is governed by the International Bobsleigh and Skeleton Federation and has been part of the Winter Olympics since the first Games at Chamonix in 1924.

History

The sport originated in the Swiss resort town of St. Moritz in the late nineteenth century, when the English tobogganing craze led riders to bolt two sleds together and race down the Cresta Run's natural ice track and local roads. The world's first bobsleigh club was founded in St. Moritz in 1897. Early sleds were wooden; steel sleds and dedicated refrigerated tracks followed. Four-man bobsleigh appeared at the 1924 Olympics, two-man in 1932, women's two-woman in 2002, and women's monobob — a single-athlete standardized sled — in 2022.

Competition

A run begins with the crew pushing the sled for roughly 50 metres before loading in; start speed is critical because it largely determines the run. The pilot steers with ropes or a wheel connected to the front runners, while in the four-man event the rear athlete operates the brakes after the finish line. Olympic and World Cup races consist of four heats (two in some formats) over two days, with the lowest aggregate time winning. Sleds are strictly regulated for weight and dimensions, and crews study aerodynamics and runner steel extensively.

Tracks and nations

Permanent artificial tracks exist in places including Oberhof and Winterberg (Germany), Innsbruck-Igls (Austria), Lake Placid (United States), Whistler (Canada), and the Olympic Sliding Centre at Yanqing (China). Germany, Switzerland, and the United States have historically been the leading nations, with Germany dominant in recent decades; notable athletes include Francesco Friedrich, whose crews set records for World Cup and Olympic gold medals.
