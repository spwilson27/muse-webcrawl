The Book of the City of Ladies (French: Le Livre de la Cité des Dames) is a book written by Christine de Pizan, completed in 1405. It is regarded as one of the earliest works of feminist literature and a pioneering defense of women against the misogynistic literary tradition of late medieval Europe.

The work is framed as a dialogue between Christine and three allegorical ladies — Reason, Rectitude, and Justice — who appear to her as she sits reading Matheolus's Lamentations, a work hostile to women. The three ladies instruct her to build an allegorical city, the City of Ladies, in which worthy women may find refuge from the slanders of male authors.

Structure and contents

The book is divided into three parts corresponding to the three guiding virtues. In Part I, Lady Reason helps Christine lay the city's foundations by recounting the lives of powerful and virtuous women, including queens, warriors, and inventors such as Semiramis, the Amazons, and Carmentis. In Part II, Lady Rectitude populates the city with women of integrity, prophets, devoted wives, and women who benefited their communities. In Part III, Lady Justice completes the city with female saints and martyrs, and the Virgin Mary is installed as the city's queen.

Context and influence

Christine de Pizan wrote the book in response to the misogyny she found in works such as Jean de Meun's portion of Roman de la Rose, which she had publicly criticized during the Querelle de la Rose (1401–1403), an early literary debate about the representation of women. Drawing on Boccaccio's De mulieribus claris (On Famous Women) among other sources, Christine reworked the biographical tradition into an argument for women's moral and intellectual capacity.

The book survives in numerous illuminated manuscripts, some produced under Christine's own supervision, and it remained in print into the sixteenth century, with an English translation by Brian Anslay published in 1521.
