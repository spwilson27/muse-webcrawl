Vortex shedding

In fluid dynamics, vortex shedding is an oscillating flow phenomenon that takes place when a fluid such as air or water flows past a bluff (as opposed to streamlined) body at certain velocities, depending on the size and shape of the body. In this flow, vortices are created at the back of the body and detach periodically from either side of the body, forming a Kármán vortex street.

Mechanism

As fluid flows around a bluff body such as a circular cylinder, the boundary layers on either side separate from the surface. Beyond a Reynolds number of roughly 40–50, the wake becomes unstable: vortices roll up and are shed alternately from the two sides, producing a regular, oscillating wake and unsteady side forces on the body. The frequency of shedding is characterised by the dimensionless Strouhal number, first investigated by Vincenc Strouhal in 1878.

Engineering significance

If the shedding frequency coincides with a natural frequency of the structure, resonance — sometimes called vortex-induced vibration — can occur, causing large oscillations. Vortex shedding contributed to the famous collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in 1940 and is a design concern for chimneys, cooling towers, offshore risers, periscopes and transmission lines. Engineers suppress it with helical strakes, perforated shrouds or splitter plates, which disrupt the coherence of the shedding.

Vortex shedding also produces audible tones: the humming of power lines in the wind and the tones of an Aeolian harp both arise from vortices shed by wires and strings.
