Disestablishmentarianism

Disestablishmentarianism is a movement within the Church of England in the 19th century, though originating earlier, that sought the separation of church and state — the removal of the Church's status as the established church of England. The word, at 28 letters, is famous as one of the longest words in the English language and is often cited in discussions of long words.

Background

The Church of England has been the established church since the Reformation, with the monarch as its Supreme Governor and bishops sitting in the House of Lords. From the 19th century, Nonconformists (Protestants outside the established church) and political liberals objected to the privileges of establishment — compulsory church rates, religious tests, and control of education — and campaigners such as Edward Miall, who founded the Liberation Society in 1844, pressed for disestablishment.

Progress of disestablishment

Disestablishment was actually achieved for parts of the United Kingdom other than England: the Church of Ireland was disestablished by the Irish Church Act 1869 (effective 1871), and the Church in Wales was disestablished by the Welsh Church Act 1914 (effective 1920). In England the movement, associated with the term's longer derivative antidisestablishmentarianism for its opponents, never prevailed; the Church of England remains established, though the question recurs periodically in debates over bishops in the Lords and the religious role of the Crown.

The word

"Disestablishmentarianism" belongs to the family of words built on "establishment": disestablishment (the act of removing establishment), disestablishmentarian (an advocate), and antidisestablishmentarianism (opposition to disestablishment), the last often cited as the longest word in English alongside technical coinages.
