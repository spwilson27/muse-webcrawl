Theophoric name

Theophoric name
Definition: a personal name embedding the name (or title) of a god
From: Greek theophoros, "bearing a god"
Examples: Michael ("Who is like God?"), Abdullah ("servant of God"), Apollodorus ("gift of Apollo"), Nebuchadnezzar (invoking Nebo)

A theophoric name (from Greek θεοφόρος, "bearing/giving a god") is a personal name that embeds the name of a god. Theophoric names are among the oldest and most widespread naming practices in the world, and they are a primary source of evidence for ancient religion, since personal names often preserve the gods, epithets, and cults of their era in highly conservative form.

Structure

Theophoric names typically consist of a divine name combined with a verbal or nominal element expressing a wish, statement, or attribute: the god "has given", "protects", "is great", "has heard", or the bearer is the god's "servant" or "gift". Scholars distinguish sentence names ("DN-is-my-strength"), confession names, gratitude names, and names bestowed in hope of divine protection. Shortened "hypocoristic" forms often drop the divine element, which can make the theophoric origin hard to recognise.

In the ancient Near East and the Bible

Hebrew names frequently contain the elements El (God) or Yah/Jah (short for Yahweh): Michael ("Who is like God?"), Gabriel ("God is my strong man"), Isaiah ("Yahweh is salvation"), Elijah ("My God is Yahweh"), and Jonathan ("Yahweh has given"). Elements referring to other deities also appear in the record, such as names formed with Baal in earlier periods. Mesopotamian names such as Nebuchadnezzar (Akkadian Nabu-kudurri-usur, "Nabu, protect the heir") and Babylonian and Assyrian royal names similarly embed their national gods.

Greek, Roman, and other traditions

Greek names like Apollodorus ("gift of Apollo"), Dionysios ("of Dionysus"), Demetrios ("of Demeter"), and Diogenes ("born of Zeus") carried the pattern through the Hellenistic world. Germanic names preserve elements of pre-Christian gods, as in the many names built on Thor. In Arabic, names such as Abdullah ("servant of Allah") and the large family of names compounding Abd al- with the names of God are theophoric. Hindu names very commonly invoke Vishnu, Shiva, Krishna, Lakshmi, and other deities, while Egyptian names like Tutankhamun ("living image of Amun") and Ramesses embed the names of Amun and Ra.

Value to scholarship

Because parents tend to name children after the gods they actually worship, the changing distribution of theophoric elements in dated documents lets historians trace the rise and fall of cults, the spread of religions such as the cult of Yahweh, Isis, or Mithras, and patterns of personal devotion for which temple records alone would be insufficient.
