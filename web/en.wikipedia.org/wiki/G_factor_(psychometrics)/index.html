g factor (psychometrics)

The g factor (also known as general intelligence, general mental ability, or general intelligence factor) is a construct developed in psychometric investigations of cognitive abilities and human intelligence. It is a variable that summarises the positive correlations among different cognitive tasks, reflecting the fact that an individual's performance on one type of cognitive task tends to be comparable to their performance on other kinds of cognitive tasks.

Origins

The English psychologist Charles Spearman first described the concept in 1904, after observing that schoolchildren's grades across seemingly unrelated subjects were positively correlated. Applying his newly developed technique of factor analysis, Spearman proposed a two-factor theory: every cognitive test measures one general factor, g, common to all tests, plus a specific factor unique to that test.

Measurement and models

Modern intelligence test batteries, such as the Wechsler scales and the Stanford–Binet, yield full-scale IQ scores that load heavily on g. Hierarchical models, notably the Cattell–Horn–Carroll theory, place g at the apex above broad abilities such as fluid reasoning, crystallised knowledge, and processing speed. g correlates with outcomes including educational attainment, job performance, and health, and is among the most replicated findings in differential psychology.

Criticism and interpretation

Interpretations of g remain contested. Critics argue that factor analysis alone cannot establish a single underlying cause, that mutualism or sampling of many processes can produce positive correlations, and that theories of multiple intelligences better capture the diversity of cognitive strengths. Defenders respond that g has strong predictive validity whatever its ultimate causal nature.
