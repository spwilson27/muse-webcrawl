Great Officers of State

The Great Officers of State traditionally play ceremonial roles at coronations.

The Great Officers of State are traditional ministers of the Crown in the United Kingdom who rank below the monarch and above all other subjects (except certain members of the royal family and the Archbishop of Canterbury). They date from the Middle Ages, when they were the chief functionaries of the royal household and government. Most of the offices are now purely ceremonial or have been absorbed into modern government posts; several are hereditary or held by the holders of other offices.

The offices

For England, the nine Great Officers, in order of precedence, are:

RankOfficePresent status
1Lord High StewardAppointed for coronations only
2Lord High ChancellorGovernment minister (Secretary of State for Justice)
3Lord High TreasurerIn commission; exercised by the Lords Commissioners of the Treasury
4Lord President of the CouncilGovernment minister
5Lord Privy SealGovernment minister
6Lord Great ChamberlainHereditary, held in rotation among co-heirs
7Lord High ConstableAppointed for coronations only
8Earl MarshalHereditary, held by the Duke of Norfolk
9Lord High AdmiralReverted to the Crown; recently held by the sovereign or consort

Scotland has its own historic great officers, including the Lord Chancellor of Scotland, the Lord High Treasurer and the Keeper of the Great Seal. The Earl Marshal is responsible for state ceremonial, including coronations and state funerals, and presides over the College of Arms. Although their political power has passed to the Cabinet, the Great Officers retain formal precedence and visible roles on state occasions. Not to be confused with the Great Offices of State, the senior ministerial posts of the modern government.
