Shah Abbas I

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Shah Abbas receiving a European ambassador, mural from the Chehel Sotun palace]

Shah Abbas I (1571–1629), known as Abbas the Great, was the fifth Safavid shah of Iran (r. 1588–1629) and the dynasty's most capable ruler. Taking the throne amid military disaster — Ottomans in the north-west, Uzbeks in the east, Qizilbash emirs dominating the court — he rebuilt the army with musket-armed infantry and artillery advised by the English Sherley brothers, created a loyal ghulam corps from converted Georgian and Armenian captives, reconquered the lost provinces, expelled the Portuguese from Hormuz (1622), and made his new capital Isfahan one of the great cities of the early modern world.

Reconquest

Abbas first bought peace with the Ottomans (1590) on humiliating terms to free his hands: he defeated the Uzbeks at Herat (1598), then turned west, retaking Tabriz (1603), Yerevan, Baghdad (1623) and the Caucasus, and pushing the frontier to its Safavid maximum. His scorched-earth deportations moved hundreds of thousands of Armenians and Georgians into Iran — the Armenians of New Julfa, Isfahan's suburb, became pillars of the silk trade that Abbas made a royal monopoly.

Isfahan and character

Isfahan's rebuilding — the Naqsh-e Jahan square, the Shah Mosque, the Ali Qapu palace, the Chaharbagh avenue and its bridges — embodied his programme; 'Isfahan is half the world', ran the saying. Abbas fostered trade with the English and Dutch East India Companies and received embassies from Europe. His greatness was shadowed by cruelty within his family: fearing coups, he killed his eldest son, blinded another, and immured the rest, leaving the succession to a grandson, Safi I, and storing up the dynasty's later weakness.
