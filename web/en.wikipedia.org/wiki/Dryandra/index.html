Dryandra is a former genus of shrubs and small trees in the family Proteaceae, endemic to the south-west of Western Australia. The group comprised roughly 94 species, notable for their finely divided leaves and dome-shaped golden flower heads. In 2007, based on DNA evidence that Banksia was paraphyletic with respect to Dryandra, all Dryandra species were transferred into Banksia as the series Banksia ser. Dryandra.

History

The genus was named by Robert Brown in 1810 in honour of the Swedish botanist Jonas Dryander. George Bentham and later Alex George produced major revisions; George's 1996 arrangement recognized 93 species. The group became a favourite of botanist Kevin Thiele and others studying the Southwest Australian Floristic Region, one of the world's biodiversity hotspots.

Ecology

Dryandras grow on nutrient-poor, lateritic and sandy soils, are pollinated by birds, mammals and insects, and many are killed by the soil-borne pathogen Phytophthora cinnamomi (dieback), which threatens numerous species in the wild.

Taxonomy debate

The merger into Banksia, proposed by Austin Mast and Kevin Thiele, was controversial among Australian botanists and horticulturists, and the Western Australian Herbarium initially resisted it before accepting the new classification.
