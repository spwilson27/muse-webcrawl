British Railways, from 1965 trading as British Rail, was the state-owned railway system of Great Britain from nationalisation in 1948 until privatisation in the 1990s. It was formed from the "Big Four" pre-war companies — the Great Western Railway, the London, Midland and Scottish Railway, the London and North Eastern Railway and the Southern Railway — under the Transport Act 1947, and was overseen first by the British Transport Commission and from 1963 by the British Railways Board.

History

The post-war network faced worn-out infrastructure and growing competition from road transport. The 1955 Modernisation Plan sought to replace steam traction with diesel and electric power; the last main-line steam locomotives were withdrawn in 1968. The 1963 Beeching report, The Reshaping of British Railways, led to the closure of some 5,000 miles of line and about a third of stations in an effort to stem losses. The corporate identity introduced in 1965 — including the famous double-arrow symbol designed by Gerald Barney — unified the railway's image. Electrification of the West Coast Main Line, the introduction of the InterCity 125 high-speed diesel train in 1976, and sectorisation in the 1980s (InterCity, Network SouthEast, Regional Railways, Railfreight) improved commercial performance.

Privatisation

Under the Railways Act 1993, British Rail was broken up and sold off between 1994 and 1997: infrastructure passed to Railtrack, and passenger services were franchised to private train operating companies, while freight operations were sold outright. The British Railways Board survived as a residual body until 2001. The double arrow remains the national symbol of the railway in Britain, used by National Rail. In the 2020s the government moved to reintegrate track and train under a new public body, Great British Railways.
