Dish Stirling

A dish Stirling system is a type of concentrated solar power technology that combines a parabolic reflector (dish) with a Stirling engine located at the dish's focal point. The dish tracks the sun across the sky, concentrating direct sunlight onto a thermal receiver that heats the working gas (usually hydrogen or helium) of the Stirling engine; the engine converts the heat into mechanical work that drives an electric generator.

Characteristics

Dish Stirling systems achieve the highest solar-to-electric conversion efficiency of any solar technology: a record of 31.25% net efficiency was demonstrated by a Stirling Energy Systems SunCatcher unit at Sandia National Laboratories in 2008. Typical units generate 3 to 25 kilowatts each. Because each dish is a self-contained generator, the technology is modular and can be deployed in anything from single off-grid units to multi-megawatt plants, and it uses little or no water for cooling, an advantage in desert environments.

Development and deployment

Development began in the 1970s and 1980s with work by United Stirling of Sweden and test units at Sandia and in Saudi Arabia. Stirling Energy Systems and Tessera Solar planned large plants in California's Mojave Desert, including the 850 MW Calico project, but these were cancelled or converted to photovoltaic technology around 2011 as solar panel prices fell and financing for Stirling plants — which have moving parts and higher maintenance costs — became difficult. Research continues on lower-cost engines, hybrid fuel operation, and use in combined heat and power.
