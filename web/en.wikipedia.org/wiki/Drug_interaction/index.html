Drug interaction

A drug interaction is a change in the action or side effects of a drug caused by its administration with another substance — another drug, food, beverage, supplement, or a medical condition. Interactions can make a drug less effective, increase its effect to dangerous levels, or produce unexpected side effects, and they are a major cause of adverse drug reactions and hospital admissions.

Mechanisms

Pharmacokinetic interactions alter the absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion of a drug. The most important involve the cytochrome P450 enzymes in the liver: enzyme inhibitors (such as grapefruit juice components acting on CYP3A4) raise blood levels of substrate drugs, while inducers (such as St John's wort) lower them. Pharmacodynamic interactions occur when drugs act on the same physiological system, producing synergistic or antagonistic effects — for example, combining anticoagulants with antiplatelet drugs increases bleeding risk, and alcohol adds to the sedation of benzodiazepines.

Management

Clinicians and pharmacists manage interactions by checking prescribing databases and interaction checkers, adjusting doses, spacing administration times, or substituting alternative drugs. The risk rises with the number of medicines taken (polypharmacy), especially in older patients, so medication reviews are a standard part of care.
