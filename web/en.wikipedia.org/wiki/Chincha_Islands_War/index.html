The Chincha Islands War (Spanish: Guerra hispano-sudamericana) was a series of naval engagements and blockades between Spain and its former colonies Peru, Chile, Ecuador and Bolivia between 1864 and 1866. Sparked by a dispute over an incident involving Spanish emigrants at a Peruvian hacienda, the war saw Spain seize the guano-rich Chincha Islands — Peru's chief source of revenue — and bombard the ports of Valparaíso and Callao.

Background and outbreak

Spain, which had never formally recognized Peruvian independence, sent a naval expedition to the Pacific in 1862. After the "Talambo incident" of August 1863, in which Spanish nationals were killed in a brawl, relations collapsed. In April 1864 the Spanish fleet under Admiral Luis Hernández-Pinzón occupied the Chincha Islands. Peru's president Juan Antonio Pezet signed the widely condemned Vivanco–Pareja Treaty conceding Spanish demands; outrage at the treaty provoked a revolution that brought Mariano Ignacio Prado to power and repudiated the agreement.

Course of the war

Chile declared war in September 1865 after Spain blockaded its coast, and Peru, Ecuador and Bolivia joined the alliance. Peruvian-Chilean forces won the naval Battle of Abtao in February 1866. Spain's new commander, Casto Méndez Núñez, bombarded the undefended Chilean port of Valparaíso on 31 March 1866, then attacked the fortified port of Callao on 2 May 1866; the Battle of Callao was hard-fought and inconclusive, and the Spanish squadron withdrew across the Pacific shortly afterward.

Aftermath

The war strained Spain's finances and prestige without restoring its influence in South America; formal peace treaties were concluded only years later (Peru signed in 1879). In the allied republics the war fostered a sense of shared Spanish-American solidarity and prompted naval expansion that shaped the balance later tested in the War of the Pacific.
