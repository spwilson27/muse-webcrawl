Law of sines

A triangle with sides and opposite angles labelled

In trigonometry, the law of sines, also known as the sine rule, is an equation relating the lengths of the sides of a triangle to the sines of its angles. For a triangle with sides a, b, and c opposite angles A, B, and C respectively:

a / sin A = b / sin B = c / sin C = 2R

where R is the radius of the triangle's circumcircle. The common ratio therefore equals the diameter of the circumscribed circle.

Applications

The law of sines is used to solve triangles: given two angles and one side (ASA or AAS), the remaining sides can be computed directly, and given two sides and a non-included angle (SSA), the unknown angles can be found, subject to the ambiguous case — for some values of SSA data there are two possible triangles, one, or none. Together with the law of cosines, it underlies practical methods in surveying, navigation, and astronomy.

History

The theorem was understood in essence by medieval Islamic mathematicians working on spherical trigonometry, including Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, who presented the law of sines for plane triangles in the thirteenth century in his work on the complete quadrilateral. It later appeared in European works by Regiomontanus and others.

Spherical law of sines

For a spherical triangle with sides measured as angles, the corresponding relation is sin a / sin A = sin b / sin B = sin c / sin C, used in celestial navigation and geodesy.
