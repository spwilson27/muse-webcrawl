The Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994 (c. 33) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom. It introduced several changes to the law, most notably in the right to silence of an accused person, public order offences, police powers of stop and search, and measures directed at unauthorised campers, squatters and illegal raves. It was enacted by the Conservative government of John Major, with Home Secretary Michael Howard steering the legislation, and remains a landmark of 1990s criminal-justice policy.

Right to silence

Sections 34–37 curtailed the right to silence in England and Wales by allowing courts to draw "such inferences as appear proper" from an accused person's failure to mention facts when questioned, silence at trial, or refusal to account for objects or presence. Similar provisions already existed in Northern Ireland. The change was controversial with civil-liberties groups and the legal profession.

Public order and protests

Part V of the Act targeted collective trespass. It created offences relating to trespassory assemblies, empowered police to remove persons attending or preparing for a rave — famously defined in section 63 as a gathering at which amplified music "wholly or predominantly characterised by the emission of a succession of repetitive beats" is played during the night — and strengthened powers against unauthorised camping and persons residing on land without consent ("New Age travellers"). The measures followed events such as the Castlemorton Common Festival of 1992.

Other provisions

The Act extended police stop-and-search powers, created offences relating to prisoner custody officers and prison security, increased penalties for certain offences, introduced provisions on indecent photographs of children, and abolished the mandatory death-penalty provision that formally remained for piracy and treason-related offences in some statutes (the death penalty for treason was abolished separately in 1998).

Reception

The Act provoked significant protest, including large marches in London and campaigns by musicians and free-party organisers against the "repetitive beats" clause. Parts of the Act have since been amended or supplemented by later public-order legislation, including the Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act 2022.
