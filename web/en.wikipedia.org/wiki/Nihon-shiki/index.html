Nihon-shiki

Nihon-shiki (日本式, "Japan-style") is a romanization system for transcribing the Japanese language into the Latin alphabet. It was invented by the physicist Aikitsu Tanakadate in 1885 and closely follows the kana orthography of Japanese, with each kana assigned exactly one romanization, making it a strict one-to-one transliteration system.

Characteristics

Nihon-shiki is the most regular of the principal romanization systems. Because it maps each kana to a unique spelling, some spellings differ from the more familiar Hepburn romanization: for example, し is written si (Hepburn: shi), ち is ti (chi), つ is tu (tsu), ふ is hu (fu), じ is zi (ji), and syllables with long vowels and geminate consonants are written by doubling. Small ゃ, ゅ, ょ following another kana are written ya, yu, yo attached to the consonant, and the syllabic ん is always n, written n' before a vowel or y.

Status

The Japanese government briefly adopted a variant of Nihon-shiki (Kunrei-shiki) as the official system, and the ISO 3602 standard is based on the closely related Kunrei-shiki. Nihon-shiki itself is rarely used in general publishing, where Hepburn predominates, but it remains of interest to linguists because of its exact correspondence to the kana syllabary and to the phonological structure of Japanese.
