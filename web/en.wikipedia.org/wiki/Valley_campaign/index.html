Valley campaign

The term Valley campaign may refer to several operations of the American Civil War conducted in the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia, most famously Stonewall Jackson's Valley campaign of 1862 and the Valley campaigns of 1864.

Jackson's Valley campaign (1862)

In the spring of 1862, Confederate Major General Thomas J. "Stonewall" Jackson led about 17,000 men through a rapid series of manoeuvres and battles — including Front Royal, Winchester, Cross Keys and Port Republic — defeating several larger Union forces in detail. The campaign is studied as a classic of military strategy: speed, deception, and use of terrain allowed Jackson to tie down Union troops that might otherwise have reinforced the advance on Richmond.

Campaigns of 1864

The campaigns of 1864 saw Confederate forces under Jubal Early threaten Washington, D.C., before Union Major General Philip Sheridan decisively defeated Early at Third Winchester, Fisher's Hill and Cedar Creek, and devastated the Valley's agricultural capacity in "The Burning".

Strategic importance

The Shenandoah Valley was the breadbasket of the Confederacy and a natural invasion corridor running northeast toward Maryland and Pennsylvania, while shielded by the Blue Ridge Mountains. Control of the Valley was contested throughout the war for these reasons.
