Greenbacks were emergency paper currency issued by the United States during the American Civil War, authorised by the Legal Tender Acts of 1862 and 1863. Officially called United States Notes, they were the first American fiat money — legal tender for most debts but not redeemable in gold or silver on demand. They took their nickname from the green ink printed on their backs, and "greenback" became a general American slang term for paper dollars.

Background

The Union government, facing the enormous costs of the war after banks suspended specie payments in December 1861, turned to direct issue of paper money under Treasury Secretary Salmon P. Chase. The First Legal Tender Act (25 February 1862) authorised $150 million of United States Notes; further acts raised the total to about $450 million. The notes were issued alongside Demand Notes of 1861 and helped finance the war together with bonds and new taxes.

Value and controversy

Because greenbacks were not backed by specie, their value in gold fluctuated with Union military fortunes, falling at worst to about 35 cents on the gold dollar in July 1864. Their constitutionality as legal tender for pre-existing debts was challenged; the Supreme Court first struck them down in Hepburn v. Griswold (1870) and then upheld them in the Legal Tender Cases (1871). After the war, the "greenback question" — whether to contract the currency back to a gold standard or keep it permanently — was a central political controversy, fuelling the Greenback Party of the 1870s–80s. The Specie Payment Resumption Act of 1875 restored gold convertibility from 1 January 1879, and the outstanding stock of United States Notes was frozen at about $346.7 million, where it remained by law for a century.
