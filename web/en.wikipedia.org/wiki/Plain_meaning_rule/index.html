Plain meaning rule

The plain meaning rule, also known as the literal rule, is a rule of statutory interpretation used by courts in common-law jurisdictions. It holds that if the words of a statute are clear and unambiguous, the court must give them their ordinary, everyday meaning, even if the result seems harsh or unintended, and may not resort to external aids such as legislative history.

Application

Under the classic English formulation, courts ask first what the words mean in their ordinary sense, read in context. Only if the literal reading produces an absurdity so glaring that Parliament cannot have intended it — the situation addressed by the related golden rule — may the court modify the meaning. Where ambiguity remains, judges may turn to purposive interpretation or interpretive aids. In contract law the analogous "plain meaning" or "four corners" doctrine limits the use of extrinsic evidence to vary a written agreement that appears complete and unambiguous on its face, a position closely related to the parol evidence rule in the United States.

Debate

The rule is associated with textualism: its defenders argue it respects legislative supremacy, gives fair notice of the law, and constrains judicial discretion. Its critics respond that words rarely have a single plain meaning detached from context and purpose, and that strict literalism can defeat obvious legislative intent — the classic teaching example being a law against "vehicles in the park" applied to ambulances, bicycles, or toy cars. Modern courts in most common-law systems therefore treat plain meaning as the starting point rather than the whole of interpretation, reading text in light of statutory purpose and context.
