Calligrammes: Poèmes de la paix et de la guerre 1913–1916 is a collection of poems by the French poet Guillaume Apollinaire, published in 1918, shortly before his death. The volume is celebrated for its calligrammes — poems in which the typographical arrangement of the words on the page forms a picture related to the poem's subject, making the text simultaneously a verbal and a visual work. The term “calligramme”, a coinage of Apollinaire's combining “calligraphy” and “ideogram”, gave its name to this form of concrete or visual poetry.

Contents and context

The collection gathers poems written between 1913 and 1916, spanning the outbreak of the First World War and Apollinaire's own service as an artilleryman and infantry officer at the front until he was wounded in the head by shrapnel in March 1916. It includes war poems, love poems (many addressed to Madeleine Pagès and Louise de Coligny-Châtillon), and experimental works. Famous calligrammes in the volume include “Il pleut” (“It's Raining”), whose lines stream down the page like rain, poems shaped as the Eiffel Tower, a heart, a watch, and a horse, and the celebrated “La colombe poignardée et le jet d'eau”.

Influence

Calligrammes is a landmark of literary modernism. Building on Apollinaire's earlier collections Alcools (1913) and his championing of cubism and the avant-garde, the shaped poems influenced the concrete poetry movement of the 1950s and 60s, visual and typographic experimentation in Dada and surrealism, and modern graphic design. Apollinaire described the calligramme as an “idéogramme lyrique” and saw in it a synthesis of the arts suited to the machine age.
