Operation Vengeance was the American military operation to kill Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto of the Imperial Japanese Navy on 18 April 1943 during the Solomon Islands campaign of the Pacific War. Yamamoto, the architect of the attack on Pearl Harbor, was shot down by US Army Air Forces P-38 Lightning fighters over Bougainville after American codebreakers intercepted and decrypted his itinerary.

Intelligence

On 14 April 1943, US naval intelligence, using the codebreaking effort known as Magic, decrypted a message giving the detailed schedule of Yamamoto's inspection tour of Japanese bases in the South Pacific. The message specified that on 18 April he would fly from Rabaul to Ballalae airfield, near Bougainville, in a Mitsubishi G4M "Betty" bomber. President Franklin D. Roosevelt authorised the interception, and Admiral Chester Nimitz assigned the mission to the 339th Fighter Squadron, whose twin-engine P-38s were the only fighters with the range to reach the intercept point from Guadalcanal.

The interception

Sixteen P-38s, fitted with drop tanks and led on the 1,000-mile round trip by Major John Mitchell, took off from Henderson Field on the morning of 18 April. They found Yamamoto's formation — two Betty bombers escorted by six Zero fighters — precisely on schedule. In the ensuing dogfight, both bombers were shot down; Yamamoto's aircraft crashed in the jungle, and his body was recovered by a Japanese search party the following day. Rex Barber and Thomas Lanphier both claimed the decisive shots, a controversy that persisted for decades. One American pilot, Raymond Hine, was lost.

Significance

Yamamoto's death was a severe blow to Japanese morale and removed Japan's most capable strategist; the United States concealed the role of codebreaking by attributing the interception to coastwatcher reports. The operation remains a famous example of a targeted killing of an enemy commander based on signals intelligence — carried out exactly one year after the Doolittle Raid, from which it took its name of vengeance.
