p-adic number

In number theory, for a given prime number p, the p-adic numbers form an extension of the rational numbers that is distinct from the real and complex numbers. They are obtained by completing the rationals with respect to a different notion of distance: where the familiar absolute value measures size in the ordinary geometric sense, the p-adic absolute value counts two numbers as close together when their difference is divisible by a high power of p. Introduced by Kurt Hensel in 1897, motivated by the idea of applying power-series methods to number theory, the p-adic numbers (denoted ℚp) have become fundamental in modern number theory, algebraic geometry and representation theory.

Motivation and construction

The real numbers can be constructed from the rationals by completing them under the usual absolute value: sequences that get ever closer together (Cauchy sequences) are assigned limits. Ostrowski's theorem shows that, up to equivalence, the only ways to measure distance on ℚ are the usual absolute value and the p-adic absolute values, one for each prime p. Defining |x|p = p−n when x is a rational whose numerator and denominator are arranged so that x = pn·(a/b) with a, b not divisible by p, completion under this norm yields the field ℚp. Within it, the p-adic integers ℤp are the elements with |x|p ≤ 1; they can also be described as infinite sequences of residues modulo p, p², p³, … that are mutually compatible (an inverse limit).

p-adic expansion

Every p-adic number can be written as a Laurent series in p with digits 0 to p−1, extending infinitely to the left rather than the right: for example, in the 5-adics, the number −1 is represented as …44445, since adding 1 gives …0000. Rational numbers have eventually periodic p-adic expansions, just as they do in decimal notation. This arithmetic, in which divisibility by p makes numbers "small", produces counter-intuitive results, such as the series 1 + p + p² + ⋯ converging to 1/(1−p) in ℚp.

Properties

The p-adic absolute value is non-Archimedean (ultrametric): |x + y|p ≤ max(|x|p, |y|p). This gives p-adic analysis a distinctive character — every triangle is isosceles, every disc is both open and closed, and a series converges if and only if its terms tend to zero. ℚp is locally compact, and its additive and multiplicative groups support a Haar measure used in harmonic analysis. Algebraically closed completions (ℂp) allow a p-adic analogue of complex analysis.

Applications

Hensel's lemma, the p-adic analogue of Newton's method, lets one lift solutions of congruences modulo p to p-adic solutions, and is the basic tool for deciding whether Diophantine equations have rational solutions: by the Hasse–Minkowski theorem and the broader local–global principle, a quadratic form has a rational zero if and only if it has one in ℝ and in every ℚp. P-adic methods are central to the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem's modular machinery, to the Langlands program, to p-adic L-functions and Iwasawa theory, and to p-adic Hodge theory. Beyond pure mathematics, p-adic ideas have found applications in theoretical physics (p-adic string theory) and in models of hierarchical systems in psychology and information science.
