Islamic mythology

Islamic mythology is the body of myths associated with Islam and the Qur'an. Islam is a monotheistic religion, and many of its sacred narratives overlap with those of Judaism and Christianity, including accounts of the creation of the world, Adam and Eve, Noah's flood and the lives of the prophets. Beyond the Qur'an itself, a rich body of legend developed in the Hadith literature, the Qisas al-Anbiya ("Stories of the Prophets") genre, and the folk traditions of Muslim peoples.

Creation and cosmology

In the Qur'an, God (Allah) creates the heavens and the earth in six days. Adam, the first human and first prophet, is formed from clay; the angels are commanded to bow before him, and Iblis (Satan), a jinn made of smokeless fire, refuses and is cast out. Adam and his wife (unnamed in the Qur'an, called Hawwa in tradition) are expelled from the Garden after eating from the forbidden tree. Islamic cosmology also includes the seven heavens, the Throne of God, and the preserved tablet (al-Lawh al-Mahfuz) on which all events are written.

Beings

Alongside humans, Islamic tradition recognises angels (mala'ika) — including Jibril (Gabriel), Mika'il (Michael), Israfil, who will blow the trumpet at the end of time, and Azrael, the angel of death — and the jinn, intelligent beings created from smokeless fire who, like humans, possess free will. Iblis is the most prominent of the rebellious jinn. Folk tradition adds creatures such as the ghoul, the ifrit and the Buraq, the winged steed that carried Muhammad on the Isra and Mi'raj, his night journey from Mecca to Jerusalem and ascension through the seven heavens.

Prophets and narratives

The Qur'an names 25 prophets, many shared with the Bible: Nuh (Noah), Ibrahim (Abraham), Musa (Moses), Dawud (David), Sulayman (Solomon), Isa (Jesus) and Muhammad, regarded as the final prophet. Distinctively Islamic retellings include Abraham's readiness to sacrifice his son (identified in most Islamic tradition as Ishmael), Solomon's command over the jinn and his encounter with the Queen of Sheba, and the Seven Sleepers. The Qisas al-Anbiya collections, such as those of al-Tha'labi and al-Kisa'i, elaborated these stories with additional miraculous and legendary detail.

Eschatology

Islamic eschatology describes the signs of the Hour, the appearance of the Mahdi and the return of Jesus, the resurrection of the dead, the Day of Judgment (Yawm al-Qiyamah), and the weighing of deeds before entry into Jannah (paradise) or Jahannam (hell), with the bridge of Sirat spanning the latter.
