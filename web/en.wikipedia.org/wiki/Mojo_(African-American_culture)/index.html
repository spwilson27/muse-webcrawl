Mojo (African-American culture)

A mojo, in the African-American folk tradition of hoodoo, is a type of magic charm, also called a mojo hand, mojo bag, or gris-gris. It typically consists of a small flannel bag containing one or more magical items — roots, herbs, minerals, coins, or personal effects — which is prepared or "fixed" for a particular purpose such as luck in gambling, love, protection, or success, and is carried on the person or kept in a significant place.

Origins

The mojo derives from West and Central African traditions of charm bags, brought to the Americas by enslaved Africans and adapted in the southern United States. The word itself is thought to derive from African languages of the region, possibly related to terms for medicine or spiritual power. In the related Louisiana tradition, similar bags are called gris-gris, a term of West African origin transmitted through the French colonial world.

Preparation and use

A mojo hand is traditionally made by a rootworker or conjurer for a specific client and purpose. Items are selected according to their reputed properties: High John the Conqueror root for power and luck, lodestones fed with iron filings to attract money or love, or graveyard dirt for protection or harm. The bag is often anointed with oils and "fed" periodically, and it is considered personal — its power is said to be lost if another person touches or sees it.

In popular culture

The mojo entered American popular culture through blues music, where it became a symbol of luck, sex appeal, and personal power; songs such as "Got My Mojo Working" popularised the term far beyond hoodoo practice. In general usage, "mojo" has come to mean a person's charm, talent, or run of good fortune.
