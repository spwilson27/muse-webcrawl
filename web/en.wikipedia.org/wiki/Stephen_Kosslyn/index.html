Stephen Kosslyn

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stephen Michael Kosslyn (born November 30, 1948) is an American psychologist and neuroscientist known for his research on mental imagery, visual cognition, and the design of visual displays. He was a central figure in the "imagery debate" over the format of mental representations, arguing that mental images have depictive, picture-like properties rather than being purely propositional descriptions.

Career

Kosslyn received his bachelor's degree in psychology from UCLA in 1970 and his PhD from Stanford University in 1974. He taught at Johns Hopkins University and Brandeis University before joining the faculty of Harvard University, where he became professor of psychology, chair of the Department of Psychology, and dean of social science. In 2011 he moved to Stanford as director of the Center for Advanced Study in the Behavioral Sciences, and he later served as founding dean and chief academic officer of the Minerva Schools at KGI, an innovative undergraduate institution.

Research

Kosslyn's experiments on mental image scanning, rotation, and inspection showed that the time taken to scan between points in a mental image increases with the represented distance, suggesting that imagery preserves spatial structure. His books Image and Mind (1980) and Image and Brain (1994) developed a computational and neuropsychological theory of imagery, proposing that the visual system reuses perceptual machinery for imagining. His critics, notably Zenon Pylyshyn, argued that such results could be explained by tacit knowledge and propositional representation, and the debate shaped cognitive science for decades.

Later work, including The Case for Mental Imagery (2006, with William Thompson and Giorgio Ganis), drew on neuroimaging evidence that imagery activates topographically organized visual areas. Kosslyn has also written on graph design and visual communication (Graph Design for the Eye and Mind) and, with G. Wayne Miller, the popular book Top Brain, Bottom Brain (2013). He is a member of the National Academy of Sciences and the American Academy of Arts and Sciences.
