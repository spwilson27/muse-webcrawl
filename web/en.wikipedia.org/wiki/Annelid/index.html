The annelids (Annelida, from Latin anellus, "little ring") are a large phylum of segmented worms, comprising over 22,000 living species including earthworms, ragworms, and leeches. Their bodies are divided into ring-like segments (metameres), each typically bearing bristles called chaetae, and they inhabit marine, freshwater, and moist terrestrial environments.

Anatomy

Annelids are bilaterally symmetrical, triploblastic coelomates. The body wall surrounds a fluid-filled coelom that acts as a hydrostatic skeleton. Most segments contain similar sets of organs, including a pair of nephridia for excretion, and the nervous system consists of a cerebral ganglion connected to a ventral nerve cord. The closed circulatory system of many species contains haemoglobin-rich blood.

Classification

  • Polychaeta — mostly marine bristle worms, with parapodia on each segment.
  • Clitellata — characterised by the clitellum used in reproduction; includes Oligochaeta (earthworms) and Hirudinea (leeches).

Ecology

Earthworms are major ecosystem engineers, aerating and enriching soil — a role celebrated by Charles Darwin in his final book (1881). Marine polychaetes are important prey and sediment processors, while leeches include blood-feeding parasites as well as predators. Some annelids, such as the giant Gippsland earthworm, exceed a metre in length, and the marine bootlace worm relatives by length — though bootlace worms belong to another phylum — annelids also include tiny interstitial species less than a millimetre long.
