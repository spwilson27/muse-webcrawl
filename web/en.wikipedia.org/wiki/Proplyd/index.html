Proplyd

A proplyd, a syllabic abbreviation of ionised protoplanetary disc, is an externally photoevaporating protoplanetary disc surrounding a young star. Nearly all known proplyds have been found in the Orion Nebula, where they were revealed by the Hubble Space Telescope in the 1990s. They consist of a disc of gas and dust around a newborn star, enveloped in a bright, teardrop- or comet-shaped cocoon of ionised gas produced as intense ultraviolet radiation from nearby massive stars (chiefly Theta1 Orionis C) boils material off the disc's surface.

Discovery

Astronomers had catalogued a number of compact emission knots in the Orion Nebula from ground-based observations, but their nature was unclear until 1993–94, when Hubble images by C. Robert O'Dell and collaborators showed them to be circumstellar discs, many seen in silhouette against the bright nebula, each with an ionised envelope pointing away from the Trapezium's massive stars. Some 150 proplyds have been catalogued in Orion.

Significance for planet formation

Proplyds provide direct visual evidence that discs — the raw material of planets — are common around young stars. However, they also expose a threat: external photoevaporation by ultraviolet light can strip a disc's gas within a few hundred thousand to a few million years, potentially before giant planets can form. This has driven research into whether the Solar System itself formed in a clustered environment near massive stars (as suggested by traces of short-lived radioactive isotopes such as iron-60 in meteorites), and how planet formation can proceed quickly or in shielded locations. Proplyds and proplyd-like objects have since been reported in other star-forming regions, including the Carina Nebula.
