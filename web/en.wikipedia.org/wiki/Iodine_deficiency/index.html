Iodine deficiency

Iodine deficiency is a lack of the trace element iodine, an essential nutrient required by the thyroid gland to produce the hormones thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). It is the world's most common preventable cause of intellectual disability, and inadequate intake can lead to goitre, hypothyroidism, and a spectrum of developmental disorders collectively called iodine deficiency disorders (IDD).

Causes

Iodine enters the diet mainly from seafood, dairy products, and food grown in iodine-rich soil. Soils in mountainous regions such as the Himalayas, the Alps, and the Andes, and in frequently flooded river plains, are often depleted of iodine because glaciation, snow, and rain leach it away. Populations dependent on locally grown food in these regions are at greatest risk. Recommended intake for adults is about 150 micrograms per day, with higher amounts advised in pregnancy.

Effects

When iodine is scarce, the thyroid cannot make enough hormone; the pituitary responds with more thyroid-stimulating hormone, which enlarges the gland into a goitre. In adults, deficiency can cause hypothyroidism with fatigue, weight gain, and cold intolerance. The most serious effects occur in pregnancy and early childhood: severe maternal deficiency causes congenital iodine deficiency syndrome (historically called cretinism), characterised by profound intellectual disability, stunted growth, deaf-mutism, and motor spasticity. Even mild deficiency in pregnancy is associated with measurably lower IQ in children.

Prevention and control

The principal public-health response is universal salt iodisation — adding potassium iodate or iodide to table salt — a strategy promoted by the WHO, UNICEF, and the Iodine Global Network. Switzerland and the United States introduced iodised salt in the 1920s, nearly eliminating goitre; by the early twenty-first century roughly nine in ten households worldwide consumed iodised salt, though coverage remains incomplete in parts of Africa and Asia, and excess iodine from over-iodisation can also disturb thyroid function. Iodine status is usually monitored through median urinary iodine concentration in school-age children.
