Creditworthiness

Creditworthiness measures how likely a borrower is to repay a debt

Creditworthiness is a lender's assessment of the likelihood that a borrower will default on their debt obligations. It represents a valuation of the borrower's ability and willingness to repay, and it determines both whether credit is granted and on what terms — the amount, interest rate, and security required.

Assessment

Traditional evaluation was summarised by the "three Cs": character (the borrower's reputation and past repayment behaviour), capacity (income and cash flow available to service the debt), and capital (the borrower's own assets and net worth). Some frameworks add collateral and conditions. Modern lenders rely largely on statistical credit scoring, which uses a borrower's credit history — maintained by credit bureaus — together with income, employment, and existing obligations such as debt-to-income ratio.

Corporate and sovereign creditworthiness

For companies and governments, creditworthiness is assessed by credit rating agencies such as Moody's, Standard & Poor's, and Fitch, which assign letter grades — from AAA (highest quality) down to C or D (in or near default). These ratings influence the interest rates issuers must pay on bonds and are based on financial statements, debt levels, economic conditions, and political stability.

Factors affecting creditworthiness

Key factors include payment history, amounts owed and credit utilisation, length of credit history, the mix of credit types, and recent credit applications. For individuals, missed payments, defaults, and bankruptcy sharply reduce assessed creditworthiness, while a long record of timely repayment improves it over time.
