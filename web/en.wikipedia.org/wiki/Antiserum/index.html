Antiserum

An antiserum is a blood serum containing polyclonal antibodies against a particular antigen or pathogen. Antisera are used to confer passive immunity against infectious diseases and toxins and are also widely employed in laboratory diagnostics and research. Because the antibodies are produced by another organism rather than by the patient's own immune system, protection is immediate but temporary.

Antiserum provides immediate, short-lived passive immunity

Production

Antisera are typically produced by immunizing an animal—commonly a horse, sheep, goat, or rabbit—with an antigen, toxin, or venom, allowing its immune system to generate a strong antibody response, and then collecting and purifying the antibody-rich fraction of its blood. Human-derived preparations, called immune globulins, are made from pooled donor plasma.

Medical uses

Antisera have a long history in medicine. In the 1890s Emil von Behring and Shibasaburo Kitasato demonstrated that serum from immunized animals could neutralize the toxins of diphtheria and tetanus, work that founded serum therapy and won von Behring the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901. Modern uses include:

  • Antivenoms for snake, spider, and scorpion envenomation;
  • Antitoxins for diphtheria, tetanus, and botulism;
  • Rabies immune globulin given after exposure to rabies;
  • Hepatitis B and varicella-zoster immune globulins for post-exposure prophylaxis;
  • Rho(D) immune globulin to prevent hemolytic disease of the newborn.

Laboratory uses

In diagnostics and research, antisera serve as reagents for identifying antigens in techniques such as immunodiffusion, agglutination tests, and immunohistochemistry, and for blood typing and tissue typing.

Risks

Because animal-derived antisera contain foreign proteins, recipients may develop serum sickness, a delayed immune-complex reaction, or, rarely, anaphylaxis. These risks, together with the development of monoclonal antibodies and modern vaccines, have narrowed the clinical role of traditional antisera, though antivenoms and certain antitoxins remain irreplaceable.
