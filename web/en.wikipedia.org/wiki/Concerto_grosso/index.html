Concerto grosso

The concerto grosso (Italian for "big concert") is a form of Baroque music in which the musical material is passed between a small group of soloists (the concertino) and a full orchestra (the ripieno or tutti). This contrasts with the solo concerto, which features a single solo instrument, and developed in the late seventeenth century in Italy.

A Baroque orchestra performing a concerto grosso.

History

The concerto grosso emerged around the 1670s–1680s. Alessandro Stradella produced early examples, and Arcangelo Corelli's twelve Concerti grossi, Op. 6, published posthumously in 1714, became the defining model — scored for a concertino of two violins and cello against a string orchestra with harpsichord continuo. The form flourished in the works of George Frideric Handel (Opp. 3 and 6), Antonio Vivaldi, Georg Philipp Telemann and Johann Sebastian Bach, whose six Brandenburg Concertos are related works with varying solo groupings.

Structure

Concerti grossi typically consist of several movements alternating fast and slow tempos, exploiting contrasts of dynamics and texture between the concertino and ripieno. The early Roman type favoured four or more movements, while the Bolognese type settled toward the three-movement pattern (fast–slow–fast) of the later solo concerto.

Modern revivals

Twentieth-century composers including Igor Stravinsky, Ernest Bloch, Bohuslav Martinů, Heitor Villa-Lobos and Alfred Schnittke revived the form as a vehicle for dialogue between instrumental groups.
