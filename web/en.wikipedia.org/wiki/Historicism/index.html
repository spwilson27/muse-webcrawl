Historicism

Historicism
Key figuresJohann Gottfried Herder, Leopold von Ranke, Friedrich Meinecke
Core ideaPhenomena must be understood within their own historical context and development
Related termsHistorism (Historismus); historicity

Historicism is an approach to explaining social and cultural phenomena that attributes their significance primarily to their historical context and development: ideas, institutions and works of art are to be understood as products of their time, place and tradition, rather than judged by universal or timeless standards. The term also has several more specific uses in philosophy, theology, economics and architecture.

Historical thought

The outlook grew out of the late Enlightenment and Romantic reactions against abstract rationalism. Johann Gottfried Herder argued that each culture and epoch has its own centre of value and must be grasped from within. In 19th-century Germany, the historical school associated with Leopold von Ranke and Johann Gustav Droysen made source-critical, contextual history the model of scholarship, insisting that every epoch is "immediate to God" and not a mere step toward the present. Friedrich Meinecke's Die Entstehung des Historismus (1936) traced the movement's rise as one of the great revolutions in Western thought.

Popper's critique

Karl Popper used "historicism" in a distinct, pejorative sense in The Poverty of Historicism (1957) and The Open Society and Its Enemies: the doctrine that history unfolds according to discoverable laws that make large-scale prediction possible, a view he attacked as unscientific and as a foundation of totalitarian ideologies. This usage differs from the German Historismus tradition, which generally denied such laws.

Other uses

In theology, historicism is a method of interpreting prophecy as fulfilled progressively through history. In economics, the German Historical School applied the approach to political economy, opposing the universal claims of classical economics. In architecture and the decorative arts, historicism denotes the 19th-century practice of reviving past styles—Gothic, Renaissance, Baroque—sometimes called the Rundbogenstil and the various revival styles, before modernism rejected historical ornament. In literary studies, the New Historicism of the 1980s, associated with Stephen Greenblatt, reads texts against the power relations and discourses of their period.
