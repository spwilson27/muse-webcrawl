Speakeasy

A speakeasy, also called a blind pig or blind tiger, is an illicit establishment that sells alcoholic beverages. Such establishments came into prominence in the United States during the Prohibition era (1920–1933), when the Eighteenth Amendment and the Volstead Act banned the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquor. The term is said to derive from the practice of speaking quietly — "speaking easy" — about such a place in public, or ordering quietly so as not to alert the police or neighbours.

Prohibition era

Speakeasies ranged from squalid basements serving home-distilled spirits to glamorous nightclubs supplied by bootleggers and protected by bribes. New York City alone was estimated to have had tens of thousands of them; famous establishments included the 21 Club, the Cotton Club, Chumley's, and Sherman Billingsley's Stork Club. Raids by Prohibition agents such as Izzy Einstein and Moe Smith were routine, and premises were often padlocked under the Volstead Act. Speakeasies popularized mixed drinks (in part to mask the taste of poor liquor), helped establish jazz-age nightlife, and — unlike the segregated saloons of the pre-Prohibition era — commonly admitted men and women together.

Legacy

Since repeal in 1933, the word has survived as a stylistic label: since the 2000s craft-cocktail revival, many bars around the world have adopted "speakeasy" styling — hidden entrances, passwords, and period décor — as a marketing theme. The term is also applied to unlicensed bars in other countries and eras.
