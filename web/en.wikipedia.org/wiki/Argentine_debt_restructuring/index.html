The Argentine debt restructuring refers to the processes by which Argentina restructured its sovereign debt following its December 2001 default — then the largest sovereign default in history, on roughly US$82 billion of bonds — amid a severe economic depression and the collapse of the peso's convertibility regime.

The 2005 and 2010 exchanges

In 2005, under President Néstor Kirchner and Economy Minister Roberto Lavagna, Argentina offered an exchange imposing a deep reduction in net-present-value terms; about 76% of bondholders accepted. A second exchange in 2010 under President Cristina Fernández de Kirchner raised total acceptance to about 93%. The deals were controversial for their unilateral "take it or leave it" character, reinforced by a "Lock Law" forbidding better offers to holdouts.

Holdout litigation

Holdout creditors, led by funds including NML Capital (an Elliott Management affiliate), sued in New York. Judge Thomas Griesa ruled that Argentina's pari passu clause barred it from paying exchange bondholders without paying holdouts in full, and injunctions pushed Argentina into a further technical default in July 2014.

2016 settlement

After Mauricio Macri took office in December 2015, Argentina negotiated settlements totaling about US$9.3 billion with holdout creditors; Congress repealed the Lock Law, the injunctions were lifted, and Argentina returned to international capital markets in April 2016 with a US$16.5 billion bond issue. Argentina restructured its debt again in 2020 under President Alberto Fernández.
