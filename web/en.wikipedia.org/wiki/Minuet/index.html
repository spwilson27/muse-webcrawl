Minuet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A minuet (also spelled menuet; French: menuet, from menu, "small" — referring to the small steps) is a social dance of French origin for two people, popular from the later seventeenth century through the eighteenth century. It is also a musical form: a graceful piece in triple time (3/4 or 3/8), at a moderate tempo, with phrases typically in eight-bar periods and a contrasting middle section called the trio. The minuet became a standard movement of the Baroque suite and later of the Classical sonata, symphony and string quartet.

The minuet was the quintessential courtly dance of the Baroque era.

The dance

The minuet rose to prominence at the court of Louis XIV of France, where Jean-Baptiste Lully composed minuets for the king's ballets and operas from the 1660s. Danced by one couple at a time before the assembled court, it demanded upright posture, intricate small steps, bows and courtesies, and elaborate figures traced on the floor, including the characteristic Z-shaped pattern. Mastery of the minuet was an essential social accomplishment, taught by dancing masters across Europe. It gradually lost favour after the French Revolution and was displaced in ballrooms by dances such as the waltz in the early nineteenth century.

The musical form

As music, the minuet is in binary form with repeated sections, usually followed by a trio (so named because it was originally scored for three instruments) and a repeat of the first minuet — "minuet da capo". Haydn and Mozart wrote minuets as the third movement of four-movement symphonies and quartets; Haydn's later minuets became faster and more robust, and Beethoven replaced the minuet with the scherzo, though he retained the same formal scheme. Notable stand-alone examples include the minuet from Boccherini's String Quintet in E major, Op. 11 No. 5, and Paderewski's Minuet in G.
