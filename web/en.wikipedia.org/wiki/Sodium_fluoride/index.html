Sodium fluoride (NaF) is an inorganic compound with the formula NaF. It is a colourless to white, crystalline ionic solid, moderately soluble in water. In nature it occurs as the rare mineral villiaumite. Sodium fluoride is the most common source of fluoride used to prevent dental cavities and is encountered in toothpastes, mouth rinses, dietary supplements, and community water fluoridation.

Production and chemistry

Sodium fluoride is prepared by neutralizing hydrofluoric acid or hexafluorosilicic acid — the latter a byproduct of phosphate fertilizer manufacture — with sodium hydroxide or sodium carbonate. In aqueous solution it releases fluoride ions (F−). Industrially it is also used as a flux in metallurgy, in the manufacture of glass and enamel, and historically as an insecticide and wood preservative.

Medical and dental use

Fluoride strengthens tooth enamel by promoting the formation of fluorapatite, which is more resistant to acid demineralization than the hydroxyapatite it partially replaces, and it enhances remineralization of early carious lesions. Sodium fluoride is applied in toothpaste (typically around 1,000–1,500 ppm fluoride), professionally applied gels and varnishes (up to about 22,600 ppm), and prescription supplements. Fluoride salts, including sodium fluoride, were formerly used at high doses to treat osteoporosis, a practice largely abandoned because of side effects and fracture risk concerns.

Safety

In excessive doses sodium fluoride is toxic: acute poisoning causes gastrointestinal symptoms, hypocalcaemia, and potentially fatal cardiac arrhythmia; the probable lethal dose in adults is on the order of several grams. Chronic overexposure causes dental fluorosis (mottling of enamel) and, at higher levels, skeletal fluorosis. Its use in water fluoridation at recommended concentrations (about 0.7 mg/L in the United States) is supported by health authorities as safe and effective, though the practice remains publicly debated.
