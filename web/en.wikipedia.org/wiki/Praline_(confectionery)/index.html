Praline (confectionery)

Praline is a form of confection containing, at a minimum, culinary nuts — usually almonds, pecans or hazelnuts — and sugar. The term covers several distinct regional varieties. In France and Belgium it often refers to a smooth nut paste or to filled chocolates; in the southern United States, particularly Louisiana, pralines are patties of pecans in a creamy brown-sugar candy.

European praline

The original praline is attributed to the cook of the 17th-century French diplomat César de Choiseul, comte du Plessis-Praslin, after whom the sweet is said to be named. It consisted of whole almonds coated in caramelised sugar. Ground into a paste, it becomes praliné, a filling used in chocolates and pastries. In Belgium, "praline" denotes a chocolate shell with a soft filling, a style developed in the early 20th century by Brussels chocolatiers such as Neuhaus.

American praline

French settlers brought the recipe to Louisiana, where pecans replaced almonds and cream was added. New Orleans pralines — flat, fudge-like discs of brown sugar, butter, cream and pecans — became a signature sweet of the city, traditionally made and sold by Creole women.
