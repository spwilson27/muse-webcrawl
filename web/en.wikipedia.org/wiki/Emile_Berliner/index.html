Emile Berliner

Emile Berliner (May 20, 1851 – August 3, 1929), originally Emil Berliner, was a German-American inventor. He is best known for inventing the lateral-cut flat disc record (called a "gramophone record" in British and American English) used with a gramophone or phonograph, and for founding the United States Gramophone Company in 1894; the Gramophone Company in London, England, in 1897; and Deutsche Grammophon in Hanover, Germany, in 1898. His disc records and gramophones were the first commercially successful means of playing recorded music.

Early life

Berliner was born in Hanover, Germany, in 1851 into a Jewish merchant family. After an apprenticeship as a merchant, he emigrated to the United States in 1870 to avoid being drafted for the Franco-Prussian War, settling in Washington, D.C., where he worked in a dry goods store and later as a chemist.

Inventions

In 1877, Berliner developed a loose-contact carbon microphone (a telephone transmitter) that greatly improved the volume and clarity of early telephones; the Bell Telephone Company purchased his patent for a large sum (often reported as $50,000, with employment continuing to 1886). He patented the gramophone in 1887–1888: unlike Edison's phonograph cylinders, Berliner's system played flat discs recorded in lateral grooves, which could be stamped out cheaply from a metal master, enabling mass production of records. He also experimented with helicopter designs in the early 20th century and worked on acoustic tiles and public-health campaigns promoting clean milk.

Legacy

Berliner's companies and licenses evolved into major record labels including Victor Talking Machine Company (later RCA Victor), His Master's Voice (HMV), and Deutsche Grammophon. The Latin Grammy Award's predecessor institutions and the "gramophone" award name trace to his invention. He was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 1994.
