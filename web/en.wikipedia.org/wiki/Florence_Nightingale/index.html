Florence Nightingale

Florence Nightingale (12 May 1820 – 13 August 1910) was an English social reformer, statistician and the founder of modern nursing. She came to prominence managing nurses during the Crimean War, where her night rounds earned her the name "The Lady with the Lamp"; her statistical campaigning afterwards — including pioneering data visualisation — drove reforms in military and civilian health, and in 1860 she founded the world's first secular nursing school at St Thomas' Hospital in London.

Crimea

Shocked by newspaper reports of soldiers dying untreated at Scutari, Nightingale led 38 nurses to the Barrack Hospital in November 1854. Conditions were appalling — overcrowding, filth, blocked sewers. Her organising, and the Sanitary Commission's flushing of the sewers in 1855, coincided with a dramatic fall in the death rate (historians debate how much was her doing versus the Commission's engineering — and note mortality at her hospital initially ran very high). The "lady with the lamp" image, spread by The Times, made her a national heroine.

Reformer and statistician

Returning in 1856 an invalid — she spent much of the next fifty years largely housebound, possibly with brucellosis contracted in Crimea — Nightingale waged paper warfare: her confidential report and her polar-area "coxcomb" diagrams showing deaths from preventable disease dwarfing battle wounds forced a Royal Commission and army medical reform. Her Notes on Hospitals and Notes on Nursing (1859) reshaped hospital design and professional nursing; from her rooms she advised on India, workhouse infirmaries and district nursing for decades.

Legacy

Nightingale died in 1910 and declined burial in Westminster Abbey; she is buried at East Wellow, Hampshire. International Nurses Day is celebrated on her birthday, 12 May.
