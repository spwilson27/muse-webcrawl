Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser theorem

The Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser (KAM) theorem is a result in dynamical systems about the persistence of quasi-periodic motions under small perturbations. It addresses the stability of integrable Hamiltonian systems, such as the planetary motions of the solar system, against the chaotic behaviour that perturbation might be expected to produce.

Statement

Andrey Kolmogorov announced the theorem in 1954; Vladimir Arnold proved the analytic case and Jürgen Moser the differentiable case in the early 1960s. The theorem says that if an unperturbed system is non-degenerate and integrable, then a large measure set of its invariant tori — those whose frequencies satisfy strong irrationality (Diophantine) conditions — survive small perturbations, slightly deformed. Resonant tori are destroyed, giving rise to zones of chaos, but the surviving tori form a "fat Cantor set" of positive measure.

Significance

KAM theory was a landmark in the theory of Hamiltonian systems and helps explain why the solar system can be stable over very long times despite gravitational interactions among the planets.
