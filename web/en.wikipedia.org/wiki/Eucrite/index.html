Eucrite

Eucrites are achondritic stony meteorites, many of which originate from the surface of the asteroid 4 Vesta. They are the most common type of achondrite and form part of the HED meteorite group, together with the howardites and diogenites. Eucrites are igneous rocks of basaltic composition, consisting mainly of calcium-poor pyroxene (pigeonite) and calcium-rich plagioclase feldspar.

The name derives from the Greek word eukritos, meaning "easily distinguished", referring to the silicate minerals they contain, which can readily be told apart owing to their relatively large grain size. Several hundred eucrites have been found, many of them in Antarctica.

Classification

Eucrites are divided into several subgroups. Cumulate eucrites are coarse-grained rocks that accumulated in magma chambers within the parent body's crust. Basaltic (non-cumulate) eucrites formed as lava flows on the surface and are subdivided into main-series and Stannern-trend groups on the basis of their trace-element chemistry. Polymict eucrites are breccias containing fragments of more than one rock type.

Origin

Spectroscopic observations link the HED meteorites to Vesta: the asteroid's surface spectrum closely matches that of basaltic eucrites, and Vesta's great south-polar impact basin, Rheasilvia, is thought to have excavated the material that eventually reached Earth. NASA's Dawn spacecraft, which orbited Vesta in 2011–2012, confirmed the connection by measuring surface compositions consistent with HED meteorites. Crystallisation ages of about 4.55 billion years make eucrites among the oldest known igneous rocks in the Solar System, recording volcanism on a small differentiated planetesimal within the first few tens of millions of years of Solar System history.

Notable falls

Well-known eucrite falls include Juvinas (France, 1821), which gave the group much of its early petrological definition, Stannern (Moravia, 1808), and Millbillillie (Australia, 1960).
