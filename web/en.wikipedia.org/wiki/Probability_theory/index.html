Probability theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Two dice showing different faces, illustrating randomness and chance]

Probability theory is the branch of mathematics concerned with probability — the analysis of random phenomena. Although the outcomes of a random event cannot be determined before it occurs, the theory describes their statistical regularities through probability distributions, random variables, and limit theorems.

The modern axiomatic foundation is Andrey Kolmogorov's (1933): a probability space consists of a sample space, a σ-algebra of events, and a measure assigning probabilities between 0 and 1 summing to 1 over the whole space. Earlier foundations grew from Pascal and Fermat's 1654 correspondence on gambling problems and the work of Huygens, Bernoulli (law of large numbers), and Laplace.

Core results

The strong law of large numbers and the central limit theorem explain why averages stabilise and why fluctuations are so often normal. Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem govern updating beliefs; expectation and variance summarise distributions.

Branches include stochastic processes (random walks, Markov chains, Brownian motion), measure-theoretic probability, and applied probability in queueing, reliability, and finance (where Itô calculus supports derivatives pricing — the Black–Scholes model).

Interpretations

What probability means remains debated: frequentist (long-run frequency), Bayesian (degree of belief), propensity, and subjectivist views coexist, while the mathematics (Kolmogorov's axioms) is shared.
