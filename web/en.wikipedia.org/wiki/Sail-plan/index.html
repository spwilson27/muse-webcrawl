Sail-plan

A sail-plan is a drawing or description of the sails flown by a sailing vessel, together with the masts and rigging that support them. The term also denotes the particular combination of sails and spars that defines a vessel's rig — for example, sloop, cutter, ketch, schooner or full-rigged ship — and determines how the boat handles in different conditions of wind.

Sail-plan of a gaff-rigged cutter, showing mainsail, topsail and headsails.

Elements of a sail-plan

A sail-plan drawing shows the profile of the hull with each mast, boom and bowsprit in position, and the outline and area of every sail: the mainsail and other fore-and-aft sails set along the vessel's centreline, headsails (jibs and staysails) set forward of the mast, and, on square-rigged vessels, the courses and topsails set on horizontal yards. Designers record the total sail area and the position of its centre of effort, which must be balanced against the hull's centre of lateral resistance for the vessel to steer properly.

Types of rig

  • Sloop — a single mast with a mainsail and one headsail; the most common modern yacht rig.
  • Cutter — a single mast set further aft, carrying two or more headsails.
  • Ketch and yawl — two-masted rigs with a mainmast and a smaller mizzen mast.
  • Schooner — two or more masts, the aft mast tallest; traditionally gaff-rigged.
  • Square rig — primary sails set on horizontal yards, as on brigs, barques and full-rigged ships.
  • Cat rig — a single sail on a mast stepped near the bow, without headsails.

Design considerations

Naval architects choose a sail-plan to suit the vessel's purpose: cruising yachts favour easily handled, subdivided rigs, while racing boats maximise sail area within rating rules. A higher plan gives more power in light airs but raises the centre of effort and heels the boat more; reefing points, roller furling and sail inventories of different weights allow one basic plan to be adapted across a wide range of weather.
