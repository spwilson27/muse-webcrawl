European rabbit

Image: European rabbit (placeholder)

The European rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus) is a species of rabbit native to the Iberian Peninsula, southwestern France, and northwestern Africa. It is the sole living member of the genus Oryctolagus and the ancestor of all domestic rabbit breeds. Widely introduced elsewhere, it has become a major invasive species, most famously in Australia.

Description and behaviour

European rabbits are grey-brown lagomorphs measuring roughly 34–50 cm in length and weighing 1–2.5 kg. They are social animals living in warrens — networks of burrows — in groups with a dominance hierarchy. Females (does) can produce several litters of typically 3–7 young (kittens) per year, explaining the species' rapid population growth. They graze mainly at dawn and dusk, favouring short grasses and herbs.

Introductions and decline

Rabbits were spread by the Romans and Normans for food and fur. In Australia, 24 rabbits released in 1859 multiplied into hundreds of millions within decades, devastating agriculture and native ecosystems; control measures included the rabbit-proof fence and the deliberate release of myxomatosis and rabbit haemorrhagic disease. Ironically, those same diseases, together with habitat loss, have driven steep declines in the native Iberian range, where the species is now listed as Endangered and is a crucial prey species for the Iberian lynx and Spanish imperial eagle.
