Valley Forge was the winter encampment of the Continental Army during the American Revolutionary War, from 19 December 1777 to 19 June 1778. Located about 20 miles (32 km) north-west of Philadelphia, then occupied by the British, the encampment is remembered as a symbol of endurance: the army suffered severely from cold, hunger and disease, but emerged in the spring better trained and organised than before.

The encampment

General George Washington chose Valley Forge for its defensible high ground near the Schuylkill River and its position between the British in Philadelphia and the Continental Congress at York. Some 12,000 soldiers and about 400 women and children camped there. The troops initially lived in tents and makeshift shelters until roughly 1,500 log huts were built, arranged in military streets. Washington made his headquarters in a stone farmhouse owned by Isaac Potts.

Supply failures left many soldiers without adequate food, clothing or blankets. An estimated 1,700 to 2,000 men died, overwhelmingly from diseases such as typhus, typhoid and dysentery rather than from the cold itself. Historians note that conditions, while harsh, were comparable to other eighteenth-century winter quarters, and that the supply crisis owed much to the weak finances and logistics of the young republic.

Training and transformation

In February 1778 the Prussian officer Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben arrived at Valley Forge and, as inspector general, drilled the army in a simplified system of manoeuvre, musket drill and camp sanitation based on his Regulations for the Order and Discipline of the Troops of the United States (the "Blue Book"). News of the Franco-American alliance, signed in February 1778, also raised morale. When the British evacuated Philadelphia in June 1778, the reorganised Continental Army pursued them and fought creditably at the Battle of Monmouth, demonstrating the transformation achieved at Valley Forge.

Legacy

The site is preserved today as Valley Forge National Historical Park, covering about 3,500 acres, with reconstructed huts, Washington's headquarters and commemorative monuments.
