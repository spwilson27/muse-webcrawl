Line of credit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

TypeFlexible borrowing facility
ProvidersBanks and other lenders
Key featureDraw and repay repeatedly up to a limit

A line of credit is a credit facility extended by a bank or another financial institution that enables a customer to draw on the facility when funds are needed, up to a pre-approved maximum amount. Unlike a term loan, which is disbursed as a lump sum, a line of credit works more like a credit card: the borrower draws, repays, and may draw again, paying interest only on the amount actually outstanding.

Types

Personal lines of credit are typically unsecured and carry higher interest rates. A home equity line of credit (HELOC) is secured by the borrower's home and usually offers lower rates and higher limits. Business lines of credit are widely used to manage working capital and smooth out cash-flow fluctuations, and may be secured by inventory or receivables or offered unsecured to established firms.

A credit line may be revolving, with repaid amounts becoming available again, or non-revolving. In a demand line, the lender may require repayment at any time; committed facilities instead run for an agreed term, often with an annual fee or a fee on the unused portion.

Risks

Because rates are usually variable and lenders can reduce or freeze limits in deteriorating conditions, lines of credit are less certain than term loans. For lenders, undrawn commitments still carry capital and liquidity implications, which is why facilities are reviewed periodically against the borrower's creditworthiness.
