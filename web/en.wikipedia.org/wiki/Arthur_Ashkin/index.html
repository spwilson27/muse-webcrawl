Ashkin in 2018.

Arthur Ashkin (2 September 1922 – 21 September 2020) was an American physicist who worked at Bell Laboratories. He received half of the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physics 'for the optical tweezers and their application to biological systems', becoming at 96 the oldest Nobel laureate ever at the time.

Ashkin discovered that laser light's radiation pressure could push, trap and levitate microscopic particles (1970), built the first optical trap for atoms (with the ideas that led to laser cooling — recognised by the 1997 Nobel to Chu, Cohen-Tannoudji and Phillips) and, with Joseph Dziedzic and colleagues, in 1986 used a tightly focused infrared beam to grab individual bacteria and viruses without harming them — the optical tweezers that became a standard instrument of biophysics, able to watch single motor proteins step and DNA molecules stretch. After retiring from Bell Labs in 1992 he set up a home laboratory and worked on solar-energy concentrators; the Nobel call reached him at 96, still experimenting.

Optical tweezers explained

A transparent bead in a focused laser beam is drawn toward the region of highest intensity (the gradient force) while scattering pushes it along the beam; together, in a tight focus, they form a three-dimensional trap with piconewton forces and nanometre position readout. Calibrated, the instrument measures the forces of molecular motors and the elasticity of DNA — physics performed one molecule at a time.
