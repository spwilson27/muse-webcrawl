Superphosphate

Superphosphate is a phosphate fertilizer produced by treating phosphate rock with acid to convert its insoluble calcium phosphate into water-soluble forms that plants can absorb. It was the first commercially manufactured chemical fertilizer and remains widely used in agriculture.

Types and production

Single superphosphate (SSP) is made by reacting ground phosphate rock (typically fluorapatite) with sulfuric acid, yielding a mixture of monocalcium phosphate and gypsum containing about 16–20% available phosphate (as P2O5). Triple superphosphate (TSP) is made with phosphoric acid instead, contains no gypsum, and is roughly three times as concentrated, at about 44–48% P2O5.

History

The process grew from experiments in the 1840s: John Bennet Lawes at Rothamsted in England patented the manufacture of superphosphate from bones and mineral phosphates in 1842 and built the first factory, founding the artificial fertilizer industry. Justus von Liebig had promoted the acid treatment of bones to make phosphate plant-available.

Use

Superphosphate supplies phosphorus, and in the single form also calcium and sulfur. Its solubility makes it effective for pasture top-dressing, notably in Australia and New Zealand. Environmental concerns include cadmium and fluoride impurities from some rock sources and phosphate runoff contributing to eutrophication.
