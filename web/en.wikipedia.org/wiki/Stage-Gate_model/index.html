Phase–gate process

The phase–gate process, also known as the stage-gate process or Stage-Gate model, is a project management technique for driving a new product from idea through to launch. Popularised by Robert G. Cooper from the 1980s onwards, it divides the innovation effort into a series of distinct stages separated by decision points called gates.

A typical five-stage Stage-Gate process

Structure

Within each stage, a cross-functional project team carries out a prescribed set of activities — market research, technical work, financial analysis — designed to gather the information needed for the next decision. At each gate, senior managers ("gatekeepers") review the results against defined criteria and make a decision: go, kill, hold, or recycle the project back for more work.

Typical stages

A full-scale version commonly includes a discovery stage followed by five stages: (1) scoping, a preliminary investigation; (2) building the business case; (3) development; (4) testing and validation; and (5) launch. Scaled-down versions (Stage-Gate Lite or XPress) are used for smaller, lower-risk projects, and later generations of the model have been combined with agile methods in so-called Agile-Stage-Gate hybrids.

Criticism

Critics argue that rigid stage-gate systems can slow innovation, encourage incremental "me-too" projects, and require forecasts that are unreliable for breakthrough products. Supporters respond that the process improves project selection, reduces failure rates and increases the discipline of product development portfolios.
