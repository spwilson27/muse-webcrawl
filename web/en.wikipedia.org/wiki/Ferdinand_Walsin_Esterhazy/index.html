Major Marie-Charles-Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy (16 December 1847 – 21 May 1923) was a French Army officer and German spy, now remembered as the true author of the treasonous bordereau whose attribution to Captain Alfred Dreyfus ignited the Dreyfus affair. Esterhazy was acquitted by a court-martial in 1898 despite overwhelming evidence of his guilt, in a proceeding that provoked Émile Zola's famous open letter J'Accuse…!

Early life and career

Esterhazy was born in Paris to a family claiming descent from the Hungarian Esterházy family — a claim of doubtful legitimacy. Orphaned young, he failed the entrance examination to Saint-Cyr but obtained a commission through service in the French Foreign Legion and as an officer in the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71. He served in counterintelligence and as a translator, and by the 1890s had risen to major. Flamboyant, perpetually in debt from speculation and gambling, and contemptuous of France in his private letters, Esterhazy began selling military secrets to the German military attaché in Paris, Maximilian von Schwartzkoppen, in 1894.

The Dreyfus affair

In September 1894 French intelligence recovered from Schwartzkoppen's wastebasket the torn bordereau, a covering note listing French artillery secrets offered for sale. The army, seeking a culprit, fixed on Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish artillery captain whose handwriting bore a superficial resemblance to that of the note. Dreyfus was convicted of treason and deported to Devil's Island.

From 1896, Colonel Georges Picquart, the new head of intelligence, identified Esterhazy's handwriting as that of the bordereau and uncovered his espionage. The General Staff suppressed the evidence; Picquart was exiled to Tunisia. To quiet mounting public pressure, Esterhazy demanded a court-martial himself: on 11 January 1898, after a hearing lasting two days and deliberations of a few minutes, he was unanimously acquitted and cheered by nationalist crowds. Two days later Zola published J'Accuse…!, accusing the army of a criminal cover-up and forcing the affair into the open.

Flight and later life

Disgraced after the exposure of the forged evidence manufactured by Colonel Henry, Esterhazy was discharged from the army in 1898 and fled to England, settling at Harpenden in Hertfordshire under the name "Count de Voilemont." He lived on a pension quietly supplied through German channels and gave self-justifying interviews, at times effectively admitting his authorship of the bordereau while insisting he had acted under orders — a claim historians regard as unreliable. He died in Harpenden in 1923 and is buried there. Dreyfus, by contrast, had been exonerated and reinstated in 1906.
