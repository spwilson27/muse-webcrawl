Pluralism

Pluralism, broadly, is the recognition and affirmation of diversity within a system—of beliefs, values, interests, or entities—together with the claim that no single one of them need be, or should be, reduced to or dominated by another. The term is used in several distinct but related fields.

Political pluralism

In political science, pluralism is the theory that power in a democracy is dispersed among many competing interest groups rather than concentrated in a single elite. Developed by writers such as Robert Dahl (Who Governs?, 1961), pluralist theory holds that citizens influence policy through group membership, that groups check one another's power, and that outcomes reflect bargaining among them. Critics, including elite theorists and "hyperpluralists," argue that some groups (such as business) are systematically privileged or that group competition can produce gridlock.

Value pluralism

In moral philosophy, value pluralism—associated especially with Isaiah Berlin—holds that there are many genuine, objective values (liberty, equality, justice, mercy, knowledge) that are not reducible to a single measure and may be incommensurable and in real conflict. On this view, some moral dilemmas have no single correct resolution, a position Berlin contrasted with monism, the belief in one ultimate harmonizing value.

Religious pluralism

Religious pluralism is the view that multiple religions can coexist and that truth or salvific value is not the exclusive possession of one faith. Theologians such as John Hick argued that the major religions are different human responses to the same ultimate Reality. It is distinguished from mere religious diversity (a social fact), from tolerance, and from inclusivism or exclusivism. Related ideas include legal pluralism—the coexistence of multiple legal orders—and cultural pluralism, sometimes pictured as a "salad bowl" alternative to the melting pot.

Philosophical pluralism

In metaphysics, pluralism contrasts with monism and dualism, holding that reality consists of many kinds of beings or principles rather than one or two. William James defended a "pluralistic universe," and contemporary metaphysical and scientific pluralists argue that the sciences describe the world through irreducibly many models and levels.
