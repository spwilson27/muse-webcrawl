Indian termination was a policy of the United States government from the mid-1940s to the mid-1960s. It was shaped by a series of laws and practices with a determination to abolish the special political status of Native American tribes, end the federal trust relationship, and subject individual Native Americans to the same laws — and the same taxes — as other Americans.

Development

The policy was formally announced in House Concurrent Resolution 108 of 1953, which declared that certain tribes should be freed from federal supervision "at the earliest possible time." Under Commissioner of Indian Affairs Dillon S. Myer and his successors, Congress passed termination acts for individual tribes, ending federal recognition, distributing tribal assets to members, and removing land from trust. Among the largest nations terminated were the Menominee of Wisconsin and the Klamath of Oregon; more than one hundred tribes and bands lost recognition in all.

Relocation

Termination was paired with relocation: the Indian Relocation Act of 1956 funded programmes encouraging Native Americans to leave reservations for jobs in cities such as Chicago, Denver, and Los Angeles. Tens of thousands moved, and urban Indian communities grew substantially, but many relocatees faced unemployment and poverty, and the promised support often proved inadequate.

Consequences and reversal

Termination proved disastrous for many terminated tribes: the Menominee, once self-supporting, saw their hospital close and their county become the poorest in Wisconsin; the Klamath lost most of their valuable timberland. Growing Native activism and official reassessment led President Nixon's 1970 message to Congress repudiating termination and inaugurating the policy of self-determination, codified in the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975. Several terminated tribes, beginning with the Menominee in 1973, had their federal recognition restored by Congress.
