In electrochemistry, according to an IUPAC definition, the absolute electrode potential is the electrode potential of a metal electrode measured with respect to a universally recognised reference system — the electron at rest in a vacuum, just outside the electrolyte — without any additional metal–solution interface. Unlike the conventional standard electrode potential, which is measured relative to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), the absolute potential places electrode potentials on a universal scale comparable with electronic energy levels in solid-state physics.

Definition

More precisely, the absolute potential of an electrode is the difference in electronic energy between a point just outside the electrolyte in contact with the electrode and the Fermi level of the electrode material, divided by the elementary charge. It can be expressed as the sum of the Galvani (inner) potential difference across the metal–solution interface and the Volta (outer) potential difference between the metal and the point outside the solution.

Value for the standard hydrogen electrode

The most important application is to the absolute potential of the standard hydrogen electrode itself, because adding this value converts every tabulated standard electrode potential onto the absolute (vacuum) scale. Estimates of this quantity varied over the decades, with values between roughly 4.2 and 4.7 V proposed. Work by Sergio Trasatti and others consolidated support for a value of about 4.44 V, and IUPAC adopted 4.44 ± 0.02 V at 25 °C as the recommended value for the absolute electrode potential of the SHE.

Significance

Placing redox levels on the vacuum scale allows direct comparison with the band edges of semiconductors, work functions of metals and ionisation energies, and is routinely used in photoelectrochemistry, solar-cell research, computational electrochemistry and the study of liquid–solid interfaces. The concept underscores that single-electrode potentials cannot be measured directly without an extrathermodynamic assumption, since any measurement requires a complete circuit with a second interface.
