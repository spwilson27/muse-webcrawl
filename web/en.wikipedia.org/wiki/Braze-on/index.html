Braze-on

A braze-on is a permanent fixture attached to a bicycle frame by brazing, welding, bonding, or riveting, used to mount components or accessories. The term originated with hand-built steel frames, where small fittings were literally brazed onto the frame tubes; it is now applied to equivalent fittings on frames of any material, including aluminium and carbon fibre, regardless of the attachment method actually used.

Typical braze-ons: cable stops, a bottle cage mount, and a front derailleur tab

Common braze-ons

  • Cable stops and guides — fittings that route or terminate gear and brake cables, including stops on the down tube, guides under the bottom bracket, and stops along the chainstay and seatstay.
  • Shifter bosses — mounts on the down tube for down-tube shifters, largely superseded by cable stops as integrated brake/shift levers became standard.
  • Bottle cage mounts — pairs of threaded eyelets on the down tube and seat tube for attaching a water-bottle cage; usually two pairs are provided.
  • Rack and mudguard eyelets — fittings near the dropouts and on the seatstay bridge and fork for mounting luggage racks and fenders.
  • Cantilever and V-brake bosses — pivots brazed to the seatstays and fork blades for rim brakes, now largely replaced by disc-brake mounts.
  • Front derailleur braze-on mount — a tab on the seat tube to which a "braze-on" front derailleur bolts; band/clamp-on derailleurs fit frames lacking the tab, and adapters allow each type to be used in place of the other.
  • Pump peg — a small peg, traditionally on the seatstay or head tube, for retaining a frame-fit tyre pump.
  • Dynamo mount, kickstand plate, and chain hanger — utility fittings found chiefly on touring and utility bicycles.

History and usage

Before braze-ons became standard on production frames, accessories were attached with clamps, and racing frames were kept deliberately bare. From the 1970s onward, manufacturers increasingly fitted braze-ons as standard equipment, simplifying assembly and giving a cleaner appearance. The number and type of braze-ons became a quick indicator of a frame's intended purpose: racing frames carry few, while touring frames carry many, including fittings for racks, mudguards, spare spokes, and a third bottle cage. The term survives for fittings on modern welded and moulded frames that have never seen a brazing torch.
