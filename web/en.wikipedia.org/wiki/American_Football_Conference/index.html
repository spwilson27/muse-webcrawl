American Football Conference

The American Football Conference (AFC) is one of the two conferences of the National Football League (NFL), the highest professional level of American football in the United States. The AFC and its counterpart, the National Football Conference (NFC), each comprise 16 teams organised into four divisions.

History

The AFC was created when the American Football League (AFL) merged with the NFL in 1970. All ten AFL teams formed the nucleus of the new conference, joined by three NFL teams — the Baltimore Colts, Cleveland Browns, and Pittsburgh Steelers — to balance the conferences at thirteen teams each. The conference's champion receives the Lamar Hunt Trophy, named for the AFL's founder, and meets the NFC champion in the Super Bowl.

Structure

The AFC is divided into the East, North, South, and West divisions, each with four teams, including franchises such as the Kansas City Chiefs, New England Patriots, Pittsburgh Steelers, Buffalo Bills, and Denver Broncos. The Patriots, with eleven conference titles in the Super Bowl era, are the AFC's most successful team by that measure.

Playoffs

Seven AFC teams qualify for the NFL playoffs each season: the four division winners and three wild cards. The conference champion is determined by the AFC Championship Game, held each January.
