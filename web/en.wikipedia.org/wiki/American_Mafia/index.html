American Mafia

The American Mafia, commonly referred to in North America as the Italian-American Mafia, the Mafia, or La Cosa Nostra, is an Italian-American society of organized crime and a criminal organization. It emerged among Italian immigrants in the eastern United States in the late 19th century, most strongly in New York City, and spread to other cities.

History

Italian immigration in the 1880s–1920s brought Sicilian mafiosi to American port cities. Prohibition (1920–1933) made bootlegging immensely profitable and consolidated gangs. The Castellammarese War (1930–31) ended with Charles "Lucky" Luciano organizing the Five Families of New York — Bonanno, Colombo, Gambino, Genovese and Lucchese — and a national Commission.

Structure

A family is headed by a boss, underboss and consigliere, with caporegimes overseeing crews of soldiers (made men) and associates. Membership historically required Italian descent and an oath of omertà (silence).

Decline

The Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO) of 1970, FBI informants such as Joseph Valachi and Sammy Gravano, and prosecutions in the 1980s–1990s, including the Mafia Commission Trial, sharply weakened the organization, though families remain active in diminished form.

In popular culture

The Mafia has been depicted in The Godfather, Goodfellas and The Sopranos.
