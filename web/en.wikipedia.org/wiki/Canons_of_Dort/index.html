The Canons of Dort are the doctrinal decisions of the Synod of Dort, a national synod of the Dutch Reformed Church held in Dordrecht in 1618–1619 to settle the controversy raised by the followers of Jacobus Arminius (the Remonstrants). They form one of the Three Forms of Unity, together with the Belgic Confession and the Heidelberg Catechism.

Content

The Canons address five heads of doctrine, each answering a point of the Remonstrants' Five Articles of 1610 and each followed by a rejection of errors: (1) divine election and reprobation; (2) the particular redemption of Christ's death; (3/4) the corruption of man, conversion and irresistible grace; and (5) the perseverance of the saints. These five heads were later summarised in English-speaking Calvinism by the acrostic TULIP (total depravity, unconditional election, limited atonement, irresistible grace, perseverance of the saints), though the acrostic's order and shorthand do not fully capture the Canons' pastoral framing.

Context and aftermath

The controversy was entangled with Dutch politics: the Remonstrants' protector Johan van Oldenbarnevelt was executed in 1619 and Hugo Grotius imprisoned. The Canons were adopted by Reformed churches internationally and remain a confessional standard for many Dutch Reformed, Presbyterian and Reformed Baptist bodies.
