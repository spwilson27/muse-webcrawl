Stochastic gradient descent

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stochastic gradient descent (often abbreviated SGD) is an iterative method for optimizing an objective function with suitable smoothness properties (e.g. differentiable or subdifferentiable). It can be regarded as a stochastic approximation of gradient descent optimization, since it replaces the actual gradient (calculated from the entire data set) by an estimate thereof (calculated from a randomly selected subset of the data). Especially in high-dimensional optimization problems this reduces the very high computational burden, achieving faster iterations in trade for a lower convergence rate.

The basic idea behind stochastic approximation can be traced back to the Robbins–Monro algorithm of the 1950s. Today, stochastic gradient descent has become the core training method in machine learning, and in particular for training neural networks by backpropagation.

Method

SGD works by repeatedly sampling a small batch of training examples (a "mini-batch"), computing the gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters on that batch, and updating the parameters by a step in the negative gradient direction:

θt+1 = θt − η ∇θ L(θt; xi, yi)

where η is the learning rate, a hyperparameter controlling the step size. The data are typically shuffled each epoch so that successive batches are approximately independent.

Variations and extensions

Numerous variants improve on plain SGD, including momentum methods, Nesterov accelerated gradient, AdaGrad, RMSProp, and Adam, which adapt the learning rate per parameter. Learning-rate schedules — decreasing the step size over time — are used to satisfy the conditions under which SGD provably converges to a local (or, for convex problems, global) minimum.

Applications

SGD is the standard optimizer for logistic regression, support vector machines trained on large data sets, and essentially all deep learning models, from image classifiers to large language models.
