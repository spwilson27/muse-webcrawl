The Kinsey Reports are two scholarly books on human sexual behaviour, Sexual Behavior in the Human Male (1948) and Sexual Behavior in the Human Female (1953), written by Alfred C. Kinsey, Wardell Pomeroy and Clyde Martin of Indiana University, and (for the second volume) Paul Gebhard. Built on thousands of face-to-face interviews, the reports became unexpected bestsellers and are widely regarded as having helped launch the sexual revolution and the modern scientific study of sexuality.

Findings

The reports documented that behaviours widely considered deviant — including premarital sex, extramarital sex and homosexual experience — were in fact common. Kinsey introduced the seven-point Kinsey scale of sexual orientation, arguing that sexuality lies on a continuum between exclusively heterosexual and exclusively homosexual. His finding that a substantial minority of men reported some homosexual experience caused intense public controversy.

Methods and criticism

Kinsey's team gathered some 18,000 sexual histories. Critics, including the American Statistical Association, questioned the volunteer and non-random character of his samples, and his reliance on populations such as prisoners; later re-analyses by his successor Paul Gebhard, removing suspect subsamples, broadly reproduced the main findings. Kinsey's research institute continues today as the Kinsey Institute at Indiana University.
