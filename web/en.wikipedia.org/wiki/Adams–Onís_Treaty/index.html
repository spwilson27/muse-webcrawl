The Adams–Onís Treaty of 1819, also known as the Transcontinental Treaty or the Florida Purchase Treaty, was an agreement between the United States and Spain that ceded Florida to the United States and defined the boundary between the U.S. and New Spain.

Terms

Negotiated by U.S. Secretary of State John Quincy Adams and Spanish minister Luis de Onís, the treaty provided that Spain cede East Florida and renounce claims to West Florida; the United States assumed up to $5 million in claims of American citizens against Spain. In return, the U.S. renounced its claims to Texas west of the Sabine River. The boundary then ran along the Sabine, Red and Arkansas rivers and westward along the 42nd parallel to the Pacific Ocean, giving the United States a claim to the Pacific coast and making it a "transcontinental" agreement. Spain also renounced claims to the Oregon Country, while the United States recognised Spanish sovereignty in Texas.

Aftermath

Ratification was delayed by Spanish concerns, including American tolerance of privateers and General Andrew Jackson's 1818 incursion into Florida during the First Seminole War, but the treaty took effect in 1821 — the same year Mexico won independence, inheriting the boundary, which was later confirmed in the 1828 Treaty of Limits.
