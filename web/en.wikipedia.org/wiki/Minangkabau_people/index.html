Minangkabau people

A rumah gadang, the traditional Minangkabau longhouse, in West Sumatra.

The Minangkabau (Minangkabau: Urang Minang) are an Austronesian ethnic group native to the Minangkabau Highlands of West Sumatra, Indonesia. Numbering roughly seven million worldwide, they are renowned as the largest matrilineal society in the world, and for a strong tradition of overseas migration (merantau) that has spread Minangkabau communities and cuisine throughout Indonesia and Malaysia.

Matrilineal culture

Minangkabau society is organized by adat (customary law), under which property, clan titles, and ancestral houses (rumah gadang) pass through the female line. Women are the anchors of the household and custodians of rice land, while men traditionally leave home to seek education, trade, or fortune, returning with wealth and experience. Clan leadership titles are held by men (penghulu), but significant decisions require the consensus of senior women, the bundo kanduang. Since the 19th century this matrilineal system has coexisted with Islam, which the Minangkabau profess almost universally.

History and influence

The name Minangkabau is traditionally derived from menang kerbau, "victorious buffalo," recalling a legendary buffalo fight that won the people their land. The Padri War (1803–1837) pitted Islamic reformers against adat traditionalists before Dutch colonial rule. Minangkabau intellectuals were prominent in the Indonesian national movement — Mohammad Hatta, Indonesia's first vice president, and the writer Sutan Takdir Alisjahbana among them. Minangkabau cuisine, especially rendang, is celebrated throughout the region, and the diaspora's Nasi Padang restaurants are found across Indonesia.
