Rescorla–Wagner model

The Rescorla–Wagner model is a model of classical conditioning proposed by Robert A. Rescorla and Allan R. Wagner in 1972. It explains learning in terms of the discrepancy — or "surprise" — between what an animal expects and what actually occurs on each conditioning trial.

The equation

On each trial, the change in the associative strength (ΔV) of a conditioned stimulus is given by:

ΔV = αβ(λ − ΣV)

where α is the salience of the stimulus, β is a learning-rate parameter associated with the unconditioned stimulus, λ is the maximum associative strength supportable by that outcome, and ΣV is the total associative strength of all stimuli present. Learning is greatest when the outcome is unexpected (when ΣV differs most from λ) and ceases when the outcome is fully predicted.

Explanatory successes

The model elegantly explains phenomena that earlier theories could not, including blocking (prior conditioning to one stimulus prevents learning about a redundant added stimulus, because the outcome is no longer surprising), overshadowing, conditioned inhibition and the effects of relative validity.

Limitations and influence

The model does not explain latent inhibition well and treats extinction as unlearning, whereas later work suggests extinction is new, context-dependent learning. Nevertheless, its error-correction rule is mathematically equivalent to the Widrow–Hoff delta rule and anticipated temporal-difference learning in reinforcement learning, making it one of the most influential models in the psychology of learning.
