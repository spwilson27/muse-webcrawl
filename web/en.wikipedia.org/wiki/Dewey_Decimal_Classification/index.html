Dewey Decimal Classification

The Dewey Decimal Classification (DDC), colloquially the Dewey Decimal System, is a proprietary library classification system first published in the United States by Melvil Dewey in 1876. It organises knowledge into ten main classes, each divided into ten divisions and each division into ten sections, so that a book's subject is expressed as a decimal number used to shelve it.

Structure

ClassSubject
000Computer science, information and general works
100Philosophy and psychology
200Religion
300Social sciences
400Language
500Pure sciences
600Technology (applied sciences)
700Arts and recreation
800Literature
900History and geography

Further digits after the decimal point allow finer distinctions: 599, for example, is mammals within 590 (animals) within 500 (science). Auxiliary tables let cataloguers add notation for geography, language, form and other facets.

History and use

Dewey, a librarian at Amherst College, published the scheme at age 24 in a pamphlet of 44 pages. It has been revised through 23 full editions and is maintained by OCLC, which acquired the trademark in 1988. The DDC is used in some 200,000 libraries in at least 135 countries, mainly public and school libraries; many research libraries prefer the Library of Congress Classification. Critics have long noted the scheme's 19th-century biases — for instance the dominance of Christianity in the 200s and Western perspectives in history — and successive editions have attempted to correct them.
