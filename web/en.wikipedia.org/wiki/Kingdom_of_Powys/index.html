The Kingdom of Powys was a Welsh successor state, petty kingdom, and later principality that emerged during the Middle Ages following the end of Roman rule in Britain. It covered roughly the eastern part of central Wales, bordering England along what later became the line of Offa's Dyke.

History

Tradition traces the dynasty to Vortigern and his descendants. In the 9th century, the Powysian king Cyngen ap Cadell erected the Pillar of Eliseg near Valle Crucis Abbey, recording the dynasty's pedigree. Powys was repeatedly pressured by the English kingdoms of Mercia and later by Norman marcher lords, and its rulers often allied with or resisted the other Welsh kingdoms of Gwynedd and Deheubarth as circumstances demanded.

Under Gruffydd ap Llywelyn in the 11th century, Powys was briefly brought under a single Welsh ruler. The last native prince, Gruffydd ap Gwenwynwyn, submitted to Edward I of England after the conquest of Wales in 1282–83, and Powys became a marcher lordship. The name survives in the modern county of Powys, created in 1974 and one of the largest local government areas in Wales.
