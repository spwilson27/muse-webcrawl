Erik Erikson

Erik Homburger Erikson (15 June 1902 – 12 May 1994) was a German-American developmental psychologist and psychoanalyst known for his theory of the psychosocial development of human beings and for coining the phrase identity crisis. His model of eight stages of psychosocial development, set out in Childhood and Society (1950), remains influential in psychology and education.

Life

Born Erik Salomonsen in Frankfurt to Danish parents, he never knew his biological father and took his stepfather's name, Homburger; in America he adopted Erikson — "Erik, son of Erik", a name he gave himself. Trained in Vienna under Anna Freud, he emigrated in 1933, taught at Yale, Berkeley and Harvard, and studied child-rearing among the Sioux and Yurok peoples, grounding his stages in culture as well as biology.

The eight stages

Each stage poses a psychosocial crisis: trust vs. mistrust (infancy), autonomy vs. shame, initiative vs. guilt, industry vs. inferiority, identity vs. role confusion (adolescence), intimacy vs. isolation, generativity vs. stagnation, and ego integrity vs. despair in old age. Successful resolution yields a virtue, such as hope, purpose, or wisdom.

Other work

His psychobiographies Young Man Luther (1958) and Gandhi's Truth (1969, Pulitzer Prize and National Book Award) applied the method to historical figures. With his wife Joan he later proposed a ninth stage for very old age.
