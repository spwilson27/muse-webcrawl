The Boer republics (sometimes also called Boer states) were independent, self-governing polities established by the Dutch-speaking descendants of the Cape Colony settlers, the Boers, principally in the nineteenth century in what is now South Africa. The best known and longest-lived were the South African Republic (the Transvaal) and the Orange Free State; both were conquered by Britain in the Second Boer War (1899–1902) and incorporated into the Union of South Africa in 1910.

Origins

The republics grew out of the Great Trek (c. 1836–1852), in which thousands of Voortrekkers left the British-ruled Cape Colony seeking independence and land. Short-lived states proliferated: Winburg, Potchefstroom, Zoutpansberg, Lydenburg, Utrecht, the Natalia Republic (annexed by Britain in 1843), and the small "mini-republics" of Stellaland and Goshen. Britain recognized Transvaal independence by the Sand River Convention (1852) and the Orange Free State by the Bloemfontein Convention (1854).

The two great republics

The Orange Free State, under presidents such as M. W. Pretorius and Johannes Brand, developed stable institutions. The South African Republic, under Paul Kruger from 1883, was transformed by the discovery of gold on the Witwatersrand (1886) into the region's economic powerhouse — drawing an influx of foreign uitlanders whose civil rights became a casus belli. Britain annexed the Transvaal in 1877, but Boer victory at Majuba Hill in the First Boer War (1880–1881) restored its independence.

Collapse

The Jameson Raid (1895–96) deepened mistrust, and the Second Boer War broke out in October 1899. After conventional defeats, Boer commandos waged a long guerrilla campaign against British forces, who responded with scorched-earth tactics and concentration camps in which many Boer women and children died. The Treaty of Vereeniging (May 31, 1902) ended the republics' independence, though former Boer generals such as Louis Botha and Jan Smuts soon led the unified South Africa.
