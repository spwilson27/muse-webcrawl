Andrew John Volstead (31 October 1860 – 20 January 1947) was an American lawyer and Republican politician from Minnesota, best known as the author of the National Prohibition Act of 1919 — universally called the Volstead Act — which provided for the enforcement of the Eighteenth Amendment and ushered in the era of Prohibition in the United States.

Life and career

Volstead was born in Goodhue County, Minnesota, to Norwegian immigrant parents, and educated at St. Olaf College and the Decorah Institute; he read law, was admitted to the bar in 1884, and practised in Granite Falls, serving as mayor and as a county attorney. He represented Minnesota's 7th congressional district in the US House of Representatives from 1903 to 1923, chairing the House Judiciary Committee. As chairman, he managed the prohibition-enforcement bill drafted largely with Wayne Wheeler of the Anti-Saloon League; Congress passed it over President Woodrow Wilson's veto on 28 October 1919, and prohibition took effect in January 1920.

Other legislation and later life

Volstead also sponsored the Capper–Volstead Act of 1922, which exempted agricultural cooperatives from certain antitrust provisions and remains foundational law for farm marketing cooperatives. Despite his name becoming synonymous with Prohibition, Volstead was not a prominent temperance crusader himself; he acted chiefly as the Judiciary Committee's legislative craftsman. He lost re-election in 1922 amid farm-belt discontent and returned to legal practice in Granite Falls. Prohibition was repealed by the Twenty-first Amendment in 1933, fourteen years before his death.

Legacy

"Volstead" became a byword for prohibition enforcement in American slang and song, and his Granite Falls home is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.
