The lodgepole pine, Pinus contorta, is a common and widespread pine of western North America, ranging from Alaska and the Yukon south through the Rocky Mountains to Colorado, and along the Pacific coast to Baja California. It takes its common name from the long, straight poles cut from the Rocky Mountain subspecies, traditionally used by Native American peoples to support tipis (lodges). The coastal subspecies, known as shore pine, is a small, twisted tree of beaches and bogs — the origin of the Latin epithet contorta, "twisted".

Description

Lodgepole pine is a two-needle pine with needles 3–7 cm long, often twisted, and small, asymmetrical cones 3–6 cm long that point backwards along the branch. Inland trees commonly grow 20–35 m tall with straight, slender trunks and thin, scaly bark. Many populations are serotinous: their cones are sealed shut with resin and open only when heated by fire, releasing a bank of seeds stored in the canopy onto the freshly burned ground.

Ecology

Because of its serotiny and tolerance of poor, cold soils, lodgepole pine is a classic fire-adapted pioneer, forming vast even-aged stands after wildfire — notably in Yellowstone National Park, where lodgepole covers most of the plateau. Since the early 2000s, warmer winters have allowed the mountain pine beetle (Dendroctonus ponderosae) to devastate millions of hectares of lodgepole forest in British Columbia and the Rockies, turning stands red and raising concerns about fire and timber supply. The species is also planted extensively for forestry in Britain, Ireland, Sweden and New Zealand, where it can become invasive.

Uses

Lodgepole pine is a major commercial timber species, used for construction lumber, poles, posts, plywood, pulp and log cabins. Historically its straight poles were traded widely for tipi construction, and its resin, bark and seeds were used by Indigenous peoples for food and medicine.
