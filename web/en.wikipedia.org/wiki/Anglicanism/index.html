Anglicanism is a Western Christian tradition that developed from the Church of England after the English Reformation. Its adherents — in the worldwide Anglican Communion of autonomous provinces, including the Church of England, the Episcopal Church in the United States, and large churches in Africa and Asia — characteristically describe their tradition as both catholic and reformed, a via media ("middle way") between Roman Catholicism and Protestantism.

History

The English Church's break with Rome came under Henry VIII (the Act of Supremacy, 1534), driven initially by his annulment crisis; under Edward VI the church became markedly Protestant (the first Books of Common Prayer, 1549 and 1552), reverted to Rome under Mary I, and settled under Elizabeth I (1559) into the compromise enshrined in the 1662 Book of Common Prayer and the Thirty-nine Articles. Later centuries added the evangelical revivals, the 19th-century Anglo-Catholic Oxford Movement, and global expansion through missions and empire.

Beliefs and practice

Anglican worship is liturgical, centred on the Book of Common Prayer tradition and the Eucharist, with wide latitude of doctrine between Anglo-Catholic, evangelical and liberal wings. The Communion has no central juridical authority: unity is expressed through the Archbishop of Canterbury as a focus of unity, the decennial Lambeth Conference of bishops, and shared formularies. Since the late 20th century, disputes over the ordination of women (first in Hong Kong and North America in the 1970s), women bishops, and same-sex relationships have strained the Communion, producing realignment movements alongside efforts to preserve unity.
