Temporary Protected Status


TPS protects eligible nationals of designated countries

Temporary Protected Status (TPS) is a temporary immigration status provided to nationals of certain countries experiencing problems that make it difficult or unsafe for their nationals to be deported there. The program was created by the United States Congress in the Immigration Act of 1990.

Legal basis

The Secretary of Homeland Security may designate a country for TPS when there is an ongoing armed conflict, an environmental disaster such as an earthquake or hurricane, or other extraordinary and temporary conditions that prevent safe return. Nationals of a designated country who were present in the United States at the time of designation may apply; beneficiaries are protected from removal, may obtain employment authorisation, and may be granted travel permission. TPS does not itself lead to permanent resident status, though beneficiaries may pursue other immigration avenues.

Designations

Designations last 6, 12 or 18 months and are frequently extended. Countries designated over the program's history include El Salvador (after the 2001 earthquakes), Honduras and Nicaragua (after Hurricane Mitch in 1998), Haiti (after the 2010 earthquake), Syria, Somalia, South Sudan, Nepal, Yemen, Venezuela, Ukraine and Afghanistan. Long-running designations have meant that hundreds of thousands of people have lived in the United States under TPS for decades.

Controversy

TPS is a recurring subject of immigration debate. Successive administrations have alternately expanded and attempted to terminate designations, with terminations repeatedly challenged in federal court, and Congress has debated — without enacting — proposals to give long-term beneficiaries a path to permanent residence.
