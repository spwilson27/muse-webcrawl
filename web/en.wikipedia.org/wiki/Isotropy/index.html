Isotropy

Isotropy is uniformity in all orientations — a property of systems, materials, radiation or space whose measured characteristics do not depend on direction. It derives from the Greek isos ("equal") and tropos ("turn, way"). The opposite of isotropy is anisotropy.

Physics and cosmology

In cosmology, isotropy — together with homogeneity — is a foundational assumption of the cosmological principle: the universe, on sufficiently large scales, looks the same in every direction from any location. Observations of the cosmic microwave background show it to be isotropic to about one part in 100,000 once the dipole due to our own motion is removed. In physics more generally, isotropic systems have properties invariant under rotation; an "isotropic radiator" is an idealised antenna that radiates power uniformly in all directions and serves as the reference for antenna gain (dBi).

Materials science

A material is isotropic if properties such as elasticity, thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity and refractive index are the same in all directions. Gases, liquids and amorphous solids (including glass and most unstressed polycrystalline metals) are typically isotropic on average, whereas single crystals, wood, rolled metals, fibre composites and liquid crystals are anisotropic — the basis of the study of birefringence in optics. In elasticity theory, isotropic materials need only two independent elastic constants (such as the Lamé parameters) to describe their response completely.

Mathematics and other fields

In mathematics an isotropic quadratic form is one that takes the value zero for some non-zero vector; isotropic vectors play a role in Lorentzian geometry and relativity. In cell biology, "isotropic" may describe growth or division with no preferred direction; in computer graphics, isotropic filtering improves texture quality symmetrically. A related term, quasi-isotropy, is used for composite laminates engineered to behave approximately isotropically in-plane.
