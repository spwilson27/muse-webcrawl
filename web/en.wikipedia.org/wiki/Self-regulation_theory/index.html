Self-regulation theory

Placeholder image: the feedback loop at the heart of self-regulation.

Self-regulation theory (SRT) is a system of conscious personal management that involves guiding one's own thoughts, behaviours, and feelings to reach goals. Drawing on cybernetic models, it describes behaviour as controlled by feedback loops: a person sets a standard or goal, monitors their current state, compares the two, and acts to reduce any discrepancy. Albert Bandura's social cognitive theory placed self-regulation — self-observation, judgement, and self-reaction — at the centre of human agency, and Charles Carver and Michael Scheier developed an influential control-theory account of self-regulation in psychology.

Components and applications

Self-regulation encompasses goal setting, planning, self-monitoring, self-evaluation, and self-reinforcement or self-correction. In health psychology, Howard Leventhal's self-regulation model explains how people form representations of illness and cope with symptoms. Self-regulation is applied in education (self-regulated learning), addiction treatment, chronic disease management, and sport. Research by Roy Baumeister and others linked failures of self-regulation to problems such as procrastination and impulsive behaviour, and the delay-of-gratification studies of Walter Mischel examined how self-control develops in children.
