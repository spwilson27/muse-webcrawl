The tricuspid valve, or right atrioventricular valve, is a flap-like structure in the mammalian heart situated between the right atrium and the right ventricle. It normally consists of three leaflets (cusps) — anterior, posterior, and septal — attached to a fibrous annulus at the right atrioventricular opening. Its function is to allow deoxygenated blood to flow from the right atrium into the right ventricle during diastole and to prevent backflow (regurgitation) into the atrium when the ventricle contracts during systole.

Structure

The leaflets are thin folds of endocardium reinforced with connective tissue. Their free edges are anchored by chordae tendineae — fibrous cords — to the papillary muscles of the right ventricle (anterior, posterior, and septal papillary muscles). When ventricular pressure rises, the papillary muscles contract to keep the leaflets from prolapsing into the atrium. The tricuspid annulus is somewhat larger and less rigid than that of the mitral valve, and the tricuspid valve is the largest of the four heart valves, with an orifice area of roughly 4–6 cm² in adults.

Function

During ventricular diastole the valve opens passively as blood flows from the systemic veins through the right atrium. During systole, rising right-ventricular pressure closes the leaflets, directing blood out through the pulmonary valve into the pulmonary artery toward the lungs. Because the right side of the heart works at much lower pressures than the left, the tricuspid leaflets are thinner and more delicate than the mitral leaflets.

Clinical significance

Tricuspid regurgitation is leakage of blood backward through the valve. Mild regurgitation is common and usually harmless; severe regurgitation is most often "functional", caused by dilation of the right ventricle and annulus in pulmonary hypertension or left-sided heart disease, but can also result from infective endocarditis (especially in intravenous drug use), rheumatic fever, or carcinoid syndrome. Tricuspid stenosis, narrowing of the valve, is rare and usually rheumatic. Ebstein's anomaly is a congenital malformation in which the septal and posterior leaflets are displaced downward into the ventricle. Treatment of severe disease ranges from diuretics and repair (annuloplasty) to valve replacement and, increasingly, transcatheter devices.
