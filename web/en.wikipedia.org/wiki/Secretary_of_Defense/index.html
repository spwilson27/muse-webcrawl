The United States Secretary of Defense (SecDef) is the head of the United States Department of Defense and the principal assistant to the President in all matters relating to national security and the armed forces. The secretary is a civilian appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate, and by law a person may not be appointed within seven years of relief from active duty as a commissioned officer (a restriction Congress has waived for a small number of nominees, including George Marshall, James Mattis, and Lloyd Austin).

Role and authority

The position was created by the National Security Act of 1947, which unified the military departments under a single National Military Establishment, renamed the Department of Defense in 1949. The secretary exercises authority, direction, and control over the Department of Defense, including the departments of the Army, Navy, and Air Force, though the operational chain of command runs from the President through the Secretary to the combatant commanders — a structure clarified by the Goldwater–Nichols Act of 1986. The Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff is the principal military adviser but holds no command authority.

Office

The secretary ranks sixth in the presidential line of succession and is a statutory member of the National Security Council. The office's second-ranking official is the Deputy Secretary of Defense. Long-serving or notable secretaries include James Forrestal (the first, 1947), Robert McNamara (1961–1968, overseeing the Vietnam War escalation), Caspar Weinberger, Dick Cheney, Robert Gates, and Leon Panetta. The secretary works from the Pentagon, the world's largest office building by floor area.
