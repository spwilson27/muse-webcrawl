Strain rate

In mechanics and materials science, strain rate is the time rate of change of the strain of a material. It is defined as the derivative of the strain with respect to time, or equivalently the rate at which the distance between points within a material changes.

Definition

For a one-dimensional deformation, the strain rate is given by ε̇ = dε/dt, where ε is the strain and t is time. Because strain is dimensionless, strain rate has units of inverse time (s⁻¹). For small strains, the strain rate can be expressed in terms of the velocity gradient: it is the rate at which the length of a line element changes per unit original length. In three dimensions, the strain-rate tensor (rate-of-deformation tensor) is the symmetric part of the velocity gradient tensor.

Strain-rate sensitivity

Many materials, particularly metals at elevated temperature and polymers, have mechanical properties that depend strongly on strain rate. Increasing strain rate generally raises flow stress; the sensitivity is often described by the parameter m = ∂lnσ/∂lnε̇. At very high strain rates (10²–10⁴ s⁻¹), such as those in impact and ballistic events, adiabatic heating and inertia become important, and testing is done with devices such as the split-Hopkinson pressure bar. At very low strain rates, creep dominates in metals at high temperature.

Typical ranges

Quasi-static tensile tests proceed at roughly 10⁻³–10⁻¹ s⁻¹; automotive crash events reach 10² s⁻¹; ballistic impacts can exceed 10⁴ s⁻¹. Geological deformation occurs at extremely low strain rates, around 10⁻¹⁴ s⁻¹.
