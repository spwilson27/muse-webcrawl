Robert Neyland

Robert Reese Neyland
Born: February 17, 1892, Greenville, Texas
Died: March 28, 1962, New Orleans, Louisiana
Career record: 173–31–12 (college football)

Robert Reese Neyland (February 17, 1892 – March 28, 1962) was an American football coach and United States Army officer. He served as the head football coach at the University of Tennessee for 21 seasons, in three stints (1926–1934, 1936–1940 and 1946–1952), compiling a record of 173 wins, 31 losses and 12 ties and leading the Volunteers to four national championships (1938, 1940, 1950 and 1951). Tennessee's stadium, Neyland Stadium, is named in his honour.

Playing and military career

Neyland attended West Point, where he starred in football, baseball and boxing, winning the Army boxing championship and pitching on the baseball team alongside Omar Bradley. Commissioned in the Corps of Engineers, he served in France during the First World War. He returned to West Point as an assistant coach under Charles Daly while completing an engineering degree at MIT, and coached Army's plebe teams. His football coaching was repeatedly interrupted by military service: he served as an ROTC instructor, and during the Second World War he rose to brigadier general, serving in the China-Burma-India theater and receiving the Distinguished Service Medal and Legion of Merit.

Tennessee

Hired by Tennessee in 1925 reportedly with the mission to "even the score" with Vanderbilt, Neyland built the Volunteers into a national power known for disciplined defence and the single-wing offence. His teams had unbeaten streaks of 33 and 28 regular-season games, won Southeastern Conference titles, and produced a string of shutouts — the 1939 team shut out all ten regular-season opponents, an NCAA record. He is remembered for his "Seven Maxims" of football, recited by Tennessee teams to this day, the first of which is "The team that makes the fewest mistakes will win."

Legacy

Neyland was inducted into the College Football Hall of Fame in 1956. Neyland Stadium, expanded many times since, became one of the largest stadiums in the United States. His coaching tree included protégés such as Bowden Wyatt and John Barnhill, and his emphasis on defence, the kicking game and error-free football shaped Southern football for a generation.
