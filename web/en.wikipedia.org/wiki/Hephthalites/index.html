Hephthalites

The Hephthalites, sometimes called the White Huns, were a confederation of nomadic and settled peoples who formed an empire in Central Asia and northern India during the 5th and 6th centuries CE. At its height, the Hephthalite Empire stretched from the Tarim Basin to the Indus valley.

Origins

The origins of the Hephthalites are debated. Chinese sources call them Yanda, and Byzantine writers such as Procopius described them as a branch of the Huns distinguished by their settled cities and fair complexion. Many scholars connect them with the Iranian-speaking peoples of Tokharistan (Bactria), and their surviving coins and documents are largely in the Bactrian language.

Wars with Persia

From the 440s the Hephthalites fought a series of wars with the Sasanian Empire. In 484 they destroyed the army of Shah Peroz I, who was killed in battle, and Persia paid them tribute for decades afterwards. Their conflicts reshaped the balance of power on Persia's eastern frontier.

India and decline

Hephthalite rulers such as Toramana and Mihirakula pushed into northern India in the early 6th century, but were checked by Indian kings including Yasodharman of Malwa. Around 557–565 the empire was destroyed by an alliance of the Sasanian shah Khosrow I and the Göktürks, who divided its territory along the Oxus; smaller Hephthalite principalities survived in the Hindu Kush region for another two centuries.
