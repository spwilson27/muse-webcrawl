Charles's law

Charles's law
StatementAt constant pressure, volume is directly proportional to absolute temperature
FormulaV ∝ T, or V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂
Named afterJacques Charles
Published byJoseph Louis Gay-Lussac (1802)

Charles's law (also known as the law of volumes) is an experimental gas law that describes how gases tend to expand when heated. It states that when the pressure on a sample of a dry gas is held constant, the volume of the gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature (in kelvins): V ∝ T.

History

The relationship was discovered by the French natural philosopher Jacques Charles in the 1780s but remained unpublished; Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac published it in 1802, crediting Charles. Extrapolation of the law implies a temperature at which gas volume would vanish — a route to the concept of absolute zero, formalised by Lord Kelvin in 1848.

Applications

Charles's law explains why hot-air balloons rise: heated air expands, becomes less dense than the surrounding air, and generates lift. Together with Boyle's law and Gay-Lussac's law on pressure and temperature, it forms the combined gas law and is contained in the ideal gas law.
