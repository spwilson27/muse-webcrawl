Mainframe computer

A mainframe computer, informally called a mainframe or big iron, is a computer used primarily by large organizations for critical applications such as bulk data processing for censuses, industry and consumer statistics, enterprise resource planning, and large-scale transaction processing. Mainframes are distinguished by their emphasis on throughput, I/O capacity, reliability, and backward compatibility rather than raw floating-point speed.

Characteristics

Modern mainframes support huge numbers of simultaneous users and transactions, extensive redundancy enabling "five nines" availability, and hardware-assisted virtualization descended from CP/CMS and VM. They excel at batch processing and online transaction processing (OLTP) against large databases.

History

The first mainframes appeared in the early 1950s from makers such as IBM, UNIVAC, and the "BUNCH" vendors. IBM's System/360 (1964) and System/370 defined the category; IBM Z systems remain the dominant mainframes today, alongside Fujitsu's GS21 and Unisys ClearPath. Despite repeated predictions of the mainframe's death, the platform endures in banking, insurance, airlines, and government, processing a large share of the world's credit-card transactions.
