A new religious movement (NRM) is a religious or spiritual group that has modern origins and is peripheral to its society's dominant religious culture. The term was popularised by scholars of religion in the 1970s as a neutral alternative to the pejorative word "cult". Movements described as NRMs range from the Baháʼí Faith (19th century) and the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints to 20th-century groups such as the Hare Krishna movement (ISKCON), the Unification Church, Scientology, Falun Gong, and various neo-pagan, New Age and UFO religions.

Characteristics

New religious movements typically offer a novel synthesis of doctrines, often centred on a charismatic founder, and first-generation converts form a majority of early membership. Sociologists classify them by their stance toward the surrounding society — for example Roy Wallis's distinction between world-rejecting, world-affirming and world-accommodating movements — and study their growth, schisms and gradual transformation into established denominations over generations.

Controversy

Some NRMs have been at the centre of public controversy, including the anti-cult movement, deprogramming controversies, and violent episodes such as the Peoples Temple deaths at Jonestown (1978), the Aum Shinrikyo sarin attack in Tokyo (1995), and the Heaven's Gate suicides (1997). Academic specialists generally caution that such cases are unrepresentative of the thousands of mostly peaceful movements, and debate over terminology — "cult", "sect", "NRM" — remains part of the field. Governments' responses, from China's suppression of Falun Gong to France's monitoring agencies, are also studied within the sociology of religion.
