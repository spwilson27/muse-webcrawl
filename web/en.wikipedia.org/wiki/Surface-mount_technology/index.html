Surface-mount technology

Surface-mount components are soldered directly onto the surface of a printed circuit board.

Surface-mount technology (SMT) is a method in which the electronic components are mounted or placed directly onto the surface of a printed circuit board (PCB). An electronic device so made is called a surface-mount device (SMD). In industry, this approach has largely replaced the through-hole technology construction method of fitting components, in large part because it allows for increased manufacturing automation.

History

Surface mounting was developed in the 1960s and became widespread in the 1980s as component packages shrank and automated pick-and-place machines matured. It enabled smaller, lighter, and cheaper electronics, paving the way for modern compact devices from smartphones to laptop computers.

Process

A typical SMT assembly line first prints solder paste onto the board's pads through a stencil, then pick-and-place machines position components — resistors, capacitors, and integrated circuits in packages such as 0402, SOIC, QFP, and BGA — at rates of tens of thousands per hour. The board then passes through a reflow oven, where the paste melts and forms solder joints. Inspection is performed by automated optical systems and, for hidden joints, X-ray.

Advantages and disadvantages

SMT components are far smaller than their through-hole counterparts, allow components on both sides of the board, and suit high-speed automation; assemblies are also more resistant to vibration. Disadvantages include greater difficulty of manual prototyping and repair, and weaker mechanical joints for connectors subject to physical stress.
