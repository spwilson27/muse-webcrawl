Étienne Bézout (31 March 1730 – 27 September 1783) was a French mathematician best known for his contributions to algebra, in particular the theory of polynomial equations. His name is attached to Bézout's identity in number theory and to Bézout's theorem on the number of intersection points of algebraic curves.

Life and career

Bézout was born in Nemours, the son of a magistrate. Against his family's wishes he pursued mathematics, influenced by the works of Euler and d'Alembert. In 1758 he was elected an adjoint in mechanics of the French Academy of Sciences. He became examiner of the naval guards (gardes de la marine) in 1764 and later of the artillery corps, positions for which he wrote widely used mathematics textbooks, including his Cours de mathématiques and the four-volume course for naval students. His major theoretical work, Théorie générale des équations algébriques (1779), systematised the elimination of unknowns from polynomial systems.

Mathematical contributions

Bézout's identity states that for integers a and b with greatest common divisor d, there exist integers x and y such that ax + by = d; it extends to principal ideal domains and underlies the extended Euclidean algorithm. (Versions for polynomials and integers were known earlier — the identity for integers appears in the work of Bachet — but Bézout gave influential general formulations.)

Bézout's theorem asserts that two projective plane curves of degrees m and n, with no common component, meet in exactly mn points over an algebraically closed field when intersections are counted with multiplicity — a foundational result of algebraic geometry. Bézout also developed the use of determinants and the "Bézout matrix" (Bézoutiant) in elimination theory.
