Complex network

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the context of network theory, a complex network is a graph (network) with non-trivial topological features — features that do not occur in simple models such as lattices or classical random graphs, but do arise in networks representing real systems. The study of complex networks is an interdisciplinary field drawing on graph theory, statistical mechanics, computer science, and the sciences of the systems being modelled, from the Internet and social networks to neural networks and protein interaction maps.

Characteristic features

  • Heavy-tailed degree distributions — many real networks are approximately scale-free, with a few highly connected hubs and many low-degree nodes.
  • Small-world property — most node pairs are connected by short paths, while the clustering coefficient remains high.
  • Community structure — nodes group into densely connected modules with sparser links between them.
  • Hierarchy and assortativity — clustering and connectivity vary systematically with degree, and similar nodes may preferentially connect.

History

Early milestones include Erdős and Rényi's random graph theory, Milgram's small-world experiments, and the Watts–Strogatz model (1998) reconciling clustering with short paths. In 1999 Barabási and Albert proposed the preferential-attachment model explaining scale-free degree distributions, catalysing the modern field of network science.

Models

Standard generative models include the Erdős–Rényi random graph, the Watts–Strogatz small-world model, the Barabási–Albert preferential attachment model, and stochastic block models for community structure. These models are used as benchmarks against which real networks are compared and as null models in statistical tests.

Applications

Complex network methods are applied to epidemiology (disease spread on contact networks), infrastructure resilience, the World Wide Web and citation networks, brain connectivity, and ecological food webs. Centrality measures, community detection algorithms, and percolation analysis are among the core tools.
