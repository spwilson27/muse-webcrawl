Simultaneous interpretation

Simultaneous interpretation (SI) is a mode of interpreting in which the interpreter renders a speaker's message into another language in real time, speaking only a few seconds behind the original. It is one of the principal modes of conference interpreting, alongside consecutive interpretation, in which the interpreter waits for the speaker to pause before rendering a section of speech.

How it works

Simultaneous interpreters usually work in pairs in a soundproof booth, listening to the speaker through headphones and speaking into a microphone connected to listeners' receivers or headsets. Because of the intense concentration required — listening, understanding, reformulating and speaking at once — interpreters typically alternate every 20 to 30 minutes. The lag between source and rendition, called the décalage or ear-voice span, is usually only a few seconds, during which the interpreter must anticipate sentence structure, especially between languages with different word order.

In settings without booths, interpreters may use portable transmitter equipment or whisper the interpretation to a small group of listeners, a variant known as chuchotage.

History

Experimental simultaneous interpretation was attempted at international gatherings in the 1920s and 1930s, requiring early simultaneous interpretation equipment developed by Edward Filene and others for the League of Nations and the International Labour Organization. The mode came of age at the Nuremberg trials of 1945–46, where proceedings in four languages were interpreted simultaneously, proving the technique at scale. Thereafter it became the standard for the United Nations and other international organisations.

Training and professional practice

Professional conference interpreters are usually trained in postgraduate programmes and are represented internationally by AIIC (the International Association of Conference Interpreters), which sets working-condition standards. Since 2020, remote simultaneous interpretation (RSI) platforms have expanded, allowing interpreters to work from home or hubs for online meetings, though the profession continues to debate sound quality and working conditions in remote settings.
