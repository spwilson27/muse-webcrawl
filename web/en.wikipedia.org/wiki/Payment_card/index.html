Payment card

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A payment card is a card, usually plastic and now also issued in metal or virtual form, that can be used by its holder to make payments for goods and services electronically instead of using cash. Payment cards are issued by banks and other financial institutions and are linked to card networks (such as Visa, Mastercard, American Express, UnionPay and Discover) that process transactions between merchants' acquiring banks and cardholders' issuing banks.

Types

Debit cards

Debit cards draw funds directly from the cardholder's deposit account. Purchases are authorised against the available balance, and money leaves the account immediately or within a few days. Debit cards also function as ATM cards for cash withdrawals.

Credit cards

Credit cards let the holder borrow money from the issuer up to a credit limit, repaying later with interest if the balance is not paid in full during the grace period. Charge cards are a variant requiring full payment each month.

Prepaid and stored-value cards

Prepaid cards are loaded with funds in advance and are not linked to a bank account; gift cards, travel money cards and general-purpose reloadable cards are common examples.

Other

Fleet cards, payroll cards, virtual cards (card numbers issued without plastic, often single-use for online purchases) and ATM-only cards serve specialised niches.

Technology

Payment cards historically stored data in a magnetic stripe encoding the primary account number (PAN), cardholder name and expiry date. Since the 1990s, EMV chip cards — which generate a unique cryptogram for each transaction — have largely replaced stripes outside a few markets, and contactless payments using near-field communication (NFC) allow tap-to-pay transactions and tokenised mobile wallets such as Apple Pay and Google Pay. Card numbers are governed by the ISO/IEC 7812 standard: the first digits identify the network and issuer (the IIN/BIN) and the final digit is a Luhn check digit.

Transaction flow and security

A typical card transaction involves authorisation (the merchant requests approval from the issuer via the network), clearing and settlement. Interchange fees paid between banks fund much of the system and are a subject of regulation in the EU and elsewhere. Security features include card verification values (CVV), 3-D Secure authentication for e-commerce, transaction monitoring and liability rules protecting consumers against fraud, such as Regulation E and Regulation Z in the United States.

History

Charge coins and plates issued by department stores and oil companies in the early twentieth century preceded the first general-purpose cards: Diners Club (1950) and American Express (1958) for travel and entertainment, and the bank cards BankAmericard (1958, later Visa) and Master Charge (1966, later Mastercard), which created revolving credit card networks. Cards have since become one of the dominant retail payment methods worldwide, though their role varies: some countries rely heavily on debit cards or on mobile payment systems that bypass card networks.
