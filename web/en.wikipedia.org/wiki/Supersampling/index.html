Supersampling

Supersampling renders at a higher resolution and downsamples, smoothing jagged edges.

Supersampling or supersampling anti-aliasing (SSAA) is a spatial anti-aliasing method used to remove aliasing ("jaggies") from rendered images in computer graphics. The scene is rendered at a higher resolution than the display and then downsampled (downscaled) to the output resolution; the extra samples capture sub-pixel detail, so edges and fine geometry appear smoother in the final image.

Method

In full-scene supersampling, every pixel of the output corresponds to several sample points in a high-resolution framebuffer. Sample positions may follow a regular grid (ordered grid supersampling) or rotated/sparse patterns that distribute aliasing artefacts less visibly. The stored samples are then combined with a reconstruction filter — commonly a box filter, or wider filters such as tent or Gaussian — to produce each output pixel.

Cost and alternatives

SSAA is the reference standard for image quality but is computationally expensive: rendering at four times the pixel count roughly quadruples shading and memory bandwidth costs. Cheaper multisample anti-aliasing (MSAA) oversamples only geometry edges and depth, and post-process methods such as FXAA and SMAA smooth the final image without extra rendering. Temporal methods (TAA) and, more recently, AI-based reconstruction techniques such as DLSS and FSR have largely superseded pure SSAA in real-time graphics, though SSAA remains common in offline rendering and is still offered in games as "render scale" or downsampling options.
