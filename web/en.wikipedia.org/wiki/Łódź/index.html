Łódź

Name meaning: "boat" (Polish)
Piotrkowska Street, the commercial spine of Łódź.

Łódź (pronounced roughly "woodge") is a city in central Poland and the capital of Łódź Voivodeship. The third most populous city in the country, it was once a small agricultural town that exploded in the nineteenth century into one of Europe's great textile-manufacturing centres — the "promised land" (ziemia obiecana) of Władysław Reymont's celebrated novel.

From the 1820s the Congress Kingdom of Poland promoted Łódź as a centre for weavers, and German, Jewish, Polish and Russian industrialists built enormous factory complexes, including the red-brick mills of Karol Scheibler and Izrael Poznański. The city's population grew from a few thousand to over half a million by 1914, and its multi-ethnic textile wealth produced palatial residences along Piotrkowska Street, one of the longest commercial streets in Europe.

Twentieth century and today

During the Second World War, Nazi Germany established the Łódź Ghetto (renaming the city Litzmannstadt), the second-largest Jewish ghetto in occupied Europe; most of its inhabitants were murdered. The textile industry declined after 1989, and Łódź has reinvented itself around services, logistics, and film: the National Film School in Łódź educated directors such as Andrzej Wajda, Roman Polanski and Krzysztof Kieślowski, and the former Poznański factory now houses the Manufaktura shopping and cultural complex.
