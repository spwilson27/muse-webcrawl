John Adams (October 30, 1735 – July 4, 1826) was an American statesman, attorney, diplomat, writer, and Founding Father who served as the second president of the United States from 1797 to 1801. Before his presidency, he was a leader in the American Revolution and succeeded George Washington as president. He was the first vice president of the United States, serving two terms under Washington from 1789 to 1797.

Early life and education

Adams was born in Braintree (now part of Quincy), Massachusetts, the eldest of three sons of John Adams Sr., a farmer and Congregationalist deacon, and Susanna Boylston. He attended Harvard College, graduating in 1755, and afterward studied law under James Putnam in Worcester. He was admitted to the bar in 1758 and built a successful legal practice in Boston.

Role in the Revolution

Adams rose to prominence opposing the Stamp Act of 1765 and defending British soldiers after the Boston Massacre in 1770, a decision he regarded as one of the finest services he ever rendered his country. As a delegate to the Continental Congress, he was a leading advocate of independence and, in June 1776, helped draft the Declaration of Independence alongside Thomas Jefferson. He also wrote the Massachusetts Constitution of 1780, the oldest functioning written constitution in the world, and his pamphlet Thoughts on Government (1776) influenced many state constitutions.

Diplomatic career

During the Revolutionary War, Adams served as a diplomat in France and the Netherlands, secured Dutch loans vital to the war effort, and helped negotiate the Treaty of Paris (1783), which ended the war. He later became the first United States minister to Great Britain.

Presidency

Adams narrowly defeated Jefferson in the election of 1796. His presidency was dominated by the Quasi-War with France, an undeclared naval conflict, and by the controversial Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798, which damaged his popularity. Adams's decision to send a peace mission to France in 1800 — which averted full-scale war — split the Federalist Party and contributed to his defeat by Jefferson in the election of 1800. On his final day in office he appointed John Marshall as Chief Justice, and his "midnight judges" appointments provoked controversy.

Later years and death

Adams retired to his farm, Peacefield, in Quincy, where he corresponded extensively, including a famous late reconciliation with Jefferson. In a striking coincidence, Adams and Jefferson both died on July 4, 1826 — the fiftieth anniversary of the Declaration of Independence. His son, John Quincy Adams, became the sixth president of the United States.

Legacy

Long overshadowed by Washington and Jefferson, Adams received renewed attention in the late 20th and early 21st centuries, notably through David McCullough's Pulitzer Prize-winning biography John Adams (2001) and its 2008 television adaptation. Historians generally rank him as an above-average president and one of the most influential intellectuals among the Founding Fathers.
