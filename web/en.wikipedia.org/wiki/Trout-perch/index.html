The trout-perch (Percopsis omiscomaycus) is a small freshwater fish of North America, the only living member of its family commonly encountered across a broad northern range. Its name reflects a mosaic of features: it has the adipose fin and general appearance of a trout combined with the spiny-rayed fin structure of a perch, although it is closely related to neither. It is a surviving representative of the order Percopsiformes, a once more diverse group known from the fossil record.

Description

Trout-perch are silvery to translucent olive in colour, with rows of dark spots along the back and sides. They rarely exceed about 20 cm (7.9 in) in length, with most adults smaller. The body is moderately deep, the head lacks scales, and the mouth is small. A small adipose fin is present behind the dorsal fin, and the dorsal and anal fins each carry one or two weak spines.

Distribution and habitat

The species ranges across northern North America from Alaska and Hudson Bay drainage south through Canada to the Great Lakes and the upper Mississippi and Missouri basins. It inhabits lakes and slow-moving rivers, typically over sand or gravel, moving into deeper water by day and feeding in the shallows at night.

Ecology

Trout-perch feed on insect larvae, small crustaceans, and other invertebrates, and occasionally on small fish and fish eggs. Spawning occurs in late spring and early summer in shallow water; adults are thought to die after spawning like their relative the pirate perch's distant kin in some populations, though many survive. The trout-perch is an important forage fish for larger predators such as walleye, northern pike, and lake trout, and is used as an indicator species in environmental monitoring of the Great Lakes.
