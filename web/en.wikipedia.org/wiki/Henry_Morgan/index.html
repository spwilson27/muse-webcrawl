Henry Morgan

Sir Henry Morgan (Welsh: Harri Morgan; c. 1635 – 25 August 1688) was a Welsh privateer, plantation owner and, later, Lieutenant Governor of Jamaica. From his base in Port Royal, Jamaica, he raided Spanish settlements and shipping in the Caribbean as part of the Anglo-Spanish War, becoming one of the most notorious and successful privateers of the era. With the tacit (and sometimes explicit) support of the English government, Morgan's expeditions struck Puerto Príncipe and Porto Bello in 1668, Maracaibo and Gibraltar in 1669, and Panama City in 1671.

Early life

Morgan was born in Llanrumney, now part of Cardiff, the eldest son of Robert Morgan, a prosperous farmer descended from a cadet branch of the Morgans of Tredegar. How he reached the Caribbean is uncertain: later accounts claimed he was kidnapped and sold into indentured servitude in Barbados, a claim Morgan himself denied and for which he once sued the publisher of John Esquemeling's buccaneer history for libel — successfully. By the 1660s he was sailing with the privateer fleets operating against Spain under commanders such as Christopher Myngs and Edward Mansvelt.

Raids

Commissioned by Governor Thomas Modyford, Morgan sacked Puerto Príncipe in Cuba before capturing the fortified city of Porto Bello in Panama in 1668 with only some 450 men, holding it for ransom. In 1669 he raided the Venezuelan ports of Maracaibo and Gibraltar, escaping a Spanish squadron by sending a fireship against its flagship. His most famous exploit was the crossing of the Isthmus of Panama in January 1671, defeating a larger Spanish force outside Panama City and sacking and burning the city — although most of its wealth had been shipped away. Because the raid violated the recent Treaty of Madrid between England and Spain, Morgan was arrested and sent to London in 1672, but amid renewed war fears he was never tried; instead he was knighted by Charles II in 1674 and returned to Jamaica as Lieutenant Governor.

Later life and legacy

As a colonial official Morgan turned against the buccaneers he had once led, acquiring large sugar plantations and serving intermittently in government while feuding with rival governors. His health declined amid heavy drinking, and he died in 1688, probably of dropsy or liver disease. He was buried at Palisadoes Cemetery in Port Royal, which sank beneath the sea in the earthquake of 1692. Morgan's name survives in popular culture, notably as the namesake of Captain Morgan rum, and he remains the archetype of the Caribbean buccaneer.
