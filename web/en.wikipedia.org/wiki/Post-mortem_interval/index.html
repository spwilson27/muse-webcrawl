The post-mortem interval (PMI), also called time since death, is the time that has elapsed since an individual died. Estimating the PMI is a central task of forensic pathology: establishing when a death occurred can corroborate or destroy alibis, narrow lists of suspects, and validate witness accounts. No single method is reliable across all intervals; in practice investigators combine the classical post-mortem changes of the first day or two with decomposition studies, entomology, and scene evidence, and express conclusions as ranges rather than exact times.

Early post-mortem changes

Three traditional signs dominate the first 24–36 hours. Algor mortis is the cooling of the body toward ambient temperature; rectal temperature measurement, interpreted with nomograms such as Henssge's, yields the most quantitative early estimates, but is confounded by body mass, clothing, and environment. Livor mortis (lividity, hypostasis) is the gravitational pooling of blood, appearing within a few hours, blanching under pressure early on and becoming "fixed" later. Rigor mortis is the stiffening of muscles as ATP is exhausted: it typically begins within a few hours of death, is complete by roughly 6–12 hours, and passes off over the following day or so, with timing strongly affected by temperature and antemortem exertion. Supplementary early tests include the mechanical and electrical excitability of muscle and chemical changes in the vitreous humour of the eye, notably rising potassium.

Decomposition and entomology

Beyond the first days, estimation relies on the progress of decomposition — fresh, bloat, active decay, advanced decay, and dry/remains stages — scored against temperature history, often using accumulated degree days (ADD) models calibrated at human taphonomy research facilities ("body farms"), the first founded by William Bass at the University of Tennessee in 1981. Forensic entomology provides some of the most precise estimates for bodies colonised by insects: blow flies arrive within minutes to hours, and the age of the oldest maggots, or the succession of insect species present, brackets the minimum PMI. Soil chemistry, plant growth through remains, and — for skeletonised bodies — radiocarbon and other dating techniques extend the toolkit to months and years.

Limitations

All PMI methods are hostage to environment: temperature, immersion in water, burial, wrapping, scavenging, and indoor versus outdoor settings can accelerate or retard every marker. Scientific opinion accordingly presents PMI as an interval estimate, and courts have grown increasingly critical of overconfident testimony. Research continues into biochemical clocks — metabolomic, proteomic, and microbial ("thanatomicrobiome") succession signatures — that might one day narrow the window.
