Frame of reference

In physics and astronomy, a frame of reference (or reference frame) is an abstract coordinate system whose origin, orientation, and scale are specified by a set of reference points whose positions are identified both geometrically and physically. Measurements of position and motion are always made relative to some frame of reference.

Inertial and non-inertial frames

An inertial frame of reference is one in which Newton's laws of motion hold without fictitious forces: a body with no net force moves at constant velocity. Frames that accelerate or rotate are non-inertial, and observers in them must introduce pseudo-forces such as centrifugal and Coriolis forces to apply Newton's laws.

Relativity

In special relativity, the laws of physics — including the speed of light — are the same in all inertial frames, connected by Lorentz transformations. General relativity extends the concept to arbitrary frames and identifies gravity with spacetime curvature. Everyday frames include the Earth-fixed frame used in navigation and the Earth-centered inertial frame used in orbital mechanics.

Other uses

The term is also used in philosophy, cognitive science and social theory for the set of assumptions and values through which a person perceives and interprets the world.
