Elections in Australia take place at federal, state and local levels within a Westminster-style parliamentary system. At the federal level, voters elect members of the House of Representatives and the Senate, which together form the Parliament of Australia. Australia is distinctive for compulsory voting, preferential (ranked) ballot systems, and Saturday polling days, and it pioneered several democratic innovations including the secret ballot — once known abroad as the "Australian ballot".

Features

Voting has been compulsory for eligible citizens aged 18 and over since 1924, and turnout at federal elections consistently exceeds 90 per cent. Enrolment is also compulsory. The House of Representatives, with 150 members elected from single-member divisions, uses instant-runoff voting (full preferential voting): voters number every box, and candidates need an absolute majority after preferences are distributed. The Senate is elected by proportional representation using the single transferable vote, with each state returning 12 senators and the territories two each.

Administration and history

Federal elections are run by the independent Australian Electoral Commission (AEC), established in 1984; states and territories have their own commissions. Australia was a world leader in democratic reform: South Australia granted women's suffrage in 1894, and the Commonwealth Franchise Act 1902 made Australia the first nation to give women both the vote and the right to stand for federal parliament. Federal elections are usually held every three years, on a Saturday, with polling from 8 a.m. to 6 p.m., postal and pre-poll voting widely available, and informal traditions such as the "democracy sausage" sold at polling-place fundraisers.
