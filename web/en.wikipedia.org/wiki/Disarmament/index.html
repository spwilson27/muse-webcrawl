Disarmament

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Disarmament is the act of reducing, limiting or abolishing weapons — of states (general disarmament), of categories (nuclear, chemical, biological disarmament) or of regions and defeated states imposed by treaty. It is distinguished from arms control, which manages arsenals without necessarily reducing them: the distinction structured Cold War debate between abolitionists (general and complete disarmament was the declared UN goal from 1959) and the arms-control school's stabilising reductions.

The institutional record runs from the Hague Conferences (1899, 1907) through the League of Nations' failed World Disarmament Conference (1932–34), the post-1945 nuclear regime (the NPT, 1968; SALT and START; the INF Treaty, 1987, defunct 2019), the near-universal prohibitions of chemical and biological weapons (CWC 1993, BWC 1972), and the humanitarian disarmament of the 1990s (Ottawa Treaty on landmines, 1997; Convention on Cluster Munitions, 2008) to the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2017, in force 2021, joined by no nuclear-armed state). The Conference on Disarmament in Geneva, deadlocked since the 1990s, symbolises the regime's stalled multilateralism amid renewed great-power armament.
