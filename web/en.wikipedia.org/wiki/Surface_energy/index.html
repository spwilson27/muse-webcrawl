Surface energy

Surface energy, or interfacial free energy, quantifies the disruption of intermolecular bonds that occurs when a surface is created. In solid-state physics, surfaces must be intrinsically less energetically favourable than the bulk of a material, because molecules at the surface have fewer neighbours to bond with; the energy required to create a surface is therefore positive, and systems tend to minimise surface area — the origin of surface tension in liquids.

A droplet's shape balances surface and interfacial energies

Measurement

Surface energy is expressed in joules per square metre (equivalently, surface tension in newtons per metre). For liquids it is routinely measured with tensiometers; for solids it is inferred indirectly from contact angle measurements using models such as the Young–Dupré equation and the Owens–Wendt method, which partition surface energy into dispersive and polar components.

Applications

Surface energy governs wetting, adhesion, coating, printing, and gluing: a liquid adhesive spreads spontaneously only on a solid whose surface energy exceeds its own surface tension. Industry therefore raises the surface energy of low-energy plastics such as polyethylene by corona or plasma treatment before printing or bonding. It also underlies crystal growth habits, catalyst behaviour, and the design of hydrophobic and superhydrophobic surfaces.
