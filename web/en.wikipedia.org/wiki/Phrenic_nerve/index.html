Phrenic nerve

The phrenic nerve is a mixed motor and sensory nerve which originates from the C3–C5 spinal nerves in the neck. The nerve is important for breathing because it provides exclusive motor control of the diaphragm, the primary muscle of respiration. In humans, the right and left phrenic nerves are primarily supplied by the C4 spinal nerve, but also receive contributions from the C3 and C5 spinal nerves (summarised by the mnemonic "C3, 4 and 5 keep the diaphragm alive").

Course of the phrenic nerves from the neck to the diaphragm.

Course

From its origin in the cervical plexus, each phrenic nerve descends on the anterior surface of the scalenus anterior muscle, enters the thorax, and passes over the pericardium — the right nerve along the superior vena cava and right atrium, the left over the left ventricle — before reaching the diaphragm, which it supplies from below. Along the way it gives sensory branches to the pericardium, mediastinal pleura and peritoneum beneath the diaphragm.

Clinical significance

Injury to one phrenic nerve, for example during cardiac surgery or from tumour invasion, paralyses the corresponding half of the diaphragm, which then rises paradoxically on inspiration. Irritation of the nerve or of the diaphragm can cause hiccups and referred pain to the shoulder tip, which shares the C3–C5 dermatomes. Pain from gallbladder disease is classically referred to the right shoulder through this pathway. Diaphragmatic pacing may be used in selected patients with bilateral phrenic failure.
