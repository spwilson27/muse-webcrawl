Second Industrial Revolution

The Second Industrial Revolution, also known as the Technological Revolution, was a phase of rapid scientific discovery, standardization, mass production and industrialization from the late 19th century into the early 20th century. It is generally dated from about 1870 to the outbreak of the First World War in 1914. Building on the foundations of the first Industrial Revolution — steam power, coal, and mechanized textile production — it transformed industry and daily life in Western Europe, the United States, and increasingly Japan.

Key developments

Steel and chemicals

The Bessemer process and the Siemens–Martin open hearth process made steel cheap and abundant, enabling taller buildings, longer bridges, railways, ships, and machinery. A new chemical industry produced synthetic dyes, fertilizers, explosives such as dynamite, and pharmaceuticals, with Germany leading in industrial research laboratories.

Electricity and petroleum

Electric generation and distribution — championed by Thomas Edison, Nikola Tesla, and George Westinghouse — brought electric light, motors, trams, and eventually the assembly-line factory. The internal combustion engine, powered by petroleum, revolutionised transport through the automobile and, by 1903, powered flight.

Mass production and communications

Standardized interchangeable parts, scientific management (Taylorism), and moving assembly lines — most famously at Ford's Highland Park plant in 1913 — slashed the cost of manufactured goods. The telegraph, telephone, and undersea cables created near-instant global communication networks, while expanding rail and steamship networks integrated world markets.

Social effects

The era saw explosive urbanization, the rise of large corporations and trusts, organized labour movements, and major advances in public health, sanitation, and medicine. Living standards rose for many, but industrial working conditions provoked reform movements, trade unions, and new political ideologies. By 1914 the Second Industrial Revolution had shifted the balance of industrial power toward the United States and Germany, setting the stage for the geopolitical rivalries of the twentieth century.
