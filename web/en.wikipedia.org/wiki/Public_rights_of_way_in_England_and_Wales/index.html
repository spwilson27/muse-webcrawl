Public rights of way in England and Wales

In England and Wales, a public right of way is a path on which the public have a legally protected right to pass and repass. The network comprises some 225,000 kilometres of rights of way, recorded by local surveying authorities on the definitive map and statement. Once a highway always a highway: a right of way cannot normally be lost by disuse alone, and it may be created by dedication by the landowner or by presumed dedication after twenty years of open public use.

Categories

  • Public footpath — a right of way on foot only.
  • Public bridleway — on foot, on horseback, or leading a horse, and (since 1968) on bicycle, giving way to other users.
  • Restricted byway — on foot, horseback, bicycle, and non-motorised vehicles (created by the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000).
  • Byway open to all traffic (BOAT) — for all traffic including motor vehicles, though often unsurfaced.

Law and history

The modern system stems from the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949, which required counties to survey and map rights of way. The Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 governs modification of the definitive map, which is conclusive evidence of the rights it records. The Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 ("CROW") added a right of access on foot to mapped open country — mountain, moor, heath, down, and registered common land. Landowners must keep paths unobstructed and cut back vegetation; highway authorities must maintain the surface of most paths.

Access campaigns

The rights-of-way network is inseparable from the history of access campaigning, including the 1932 mass trespass of Kinder Scout, which helped spur the creation of national parks. Organisations such as the Ramblers campaign to protect paths and to record historic ways before statutory cut-off dates extinguish unrecorded rights.
