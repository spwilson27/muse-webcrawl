Propeller (aeronautics)

In aeronautics, a propeller is a device with a rotating hub and radiating blades that are set at a pitch to form a helical spiral which, when rotated, exerts linear thrust upon a working fluid such as air. The blades approximate aerofoil sections, and as they rotate they generate aerodynamic lift directed forwards, pulling (or, in pusher configuration, pushing) the aircraft through the air. Propellers convert the torque of a piston engine, turboprop, or electric motor into thrust.

A constant-speed propeller on a turboprop aircraft

Principles

Each blade section behaves like a small wing moving in a helical path. The angle of the blade chord to the plane of rotation — the blade angle or pitch — together with the rotational speed, determines the local angle of attack. Because the tangential speed increases from hub to tip, blades are twisted so that the pitch decreases along the radius, keeping the angle of attack roughly uniform. Efficiency is highest when the blades operate near their best lift-to-drag ratio; tip speeds are limited because compressibility losses and noise rise sharply as tips approach the speed of sound.

Types

  • Fixed-pitch: blade angle set on the ground; simple but efficient only over a narrow speed range.
  • Variable-pitch: blade angle adjustable in flight, typically in two or more positions.
  • Constant-speed: a governor automatically varies pitch to hold a selected rpm, the standard on most high-performance piston and turboprop aircraft. Such propellers can usually be feathered (turned edge-on to the airflow) to minimise drag after an engine failure, and some can produce reverse thrust.
  • Contra-rotating and scimitar designs recover swirl energy or delay tip compressibility effects.

History

The Wright brothers recognised that propeller blades were rotating wings and designed theirs using wind-tunnel data, achieving efficiencies around 66–80% — far ahead of contemporaries. Early propellers were carved from laminated wood; metal and later composite blades followed. The introduction of the constant-speed propeller in the 1930s was a major advance in aircraft performance, and turboprops with large multi-blade propellers remain the most fuel-efficient propulsion for short- and medium-haul aircraft.
