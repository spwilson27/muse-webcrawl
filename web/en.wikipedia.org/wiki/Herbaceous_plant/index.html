Herbaceous plant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Herbaceous plant
Defining traitNon-woody stem; little or no persistent above-ground woody tissue
Life formsAnnuals, biennials, perennials (forbs and graminoids)
ExamplesGrasses, wheat, daisies, ferns, banana (by some definitions)

Herbaceous plants are vascular plants that have no persistent woody stems above ground. This broad category of plants includes many perennials, and nearly all annuals and biennials. Herbaceous plants include forbs (flowering broad-leaved plants) and graminoids (grass-like plants), as well as ferns and clubmosses. Their stems are typically soft, green, and flexible, supported mainly by turgor pressure rather than lignified wood.

Definition and structure

The term "herbaceous" describes the texture and growth habit of the stem — herbaceous, meaning "herb-like", from the Latin herba ("grass" or "herb"). Herbaceous plants may produce a small amount of woody tissue, but not the thick, persistent trunks and branches of trees and shrubs. The banana plant, despite its tree-like size, is effectively a giant herb: its "trunk" is a pseudostem of leaf sheaths, not wood. At the end of the growing season, the above-ground parts of many herbaceous perennials die back to the ground, the plant surviving through roots, rhizomes, bulbs, or tubers.

Life cycles

  • Annuals complete their life cycle — germination, flowering, seed production, and death — within one growing season (e.g. wheat, marigolds).
  • Biennials grow vegetatively in the first year and flower and die in the second (e.g. carrots, foxgloves).
  • Perennials live for more than two years, often dying back in winter and regrowing from underground organs in spring (e.g. hostas, peonies, grasses).

Ecology and uses

Herbaceous plants dominate grasslands, meadows, marshes, and the understorey of woodlands, and include most of humanity's staple food crops — wheat, rice, maize, and other cereals are all grasses — as well as vegetables, culinary and medicinal herbs, and the majority of garden ornamentals. Because they grow fast and reproduce prolifically, herbaceous species are often pioneers in ecological succession, colonising disturbed ground before woody plants arrive.
