Pagan Kingdom

The Pagan Kingdom (Burmese: ပုဂံခေတ်) was the first kingdom to unify the regions that would later constitute modern Myanmar (Burma). Centred on the city of Pagan (Bagan) on the Irrawaddy River, it flourished between the 9th and 13th centuries. Founded, according to tradition, in 849 CE, the kingdom reached its zenith under King Anawrahta (r. 1044–1077) and his successors, and is famed for the thousands of Buddhist temples built on the Bagan plain. It collapsed following the Mongol invasions of the 1280s.

History

The Burmans (Bamar) migrated into the upper Irrawaddy valley from the Nanzhao region by the 9th century and founded the fortified city of Pagan. Anawrahta's conquests — including the capture of the Mon city of Thaton in 1057, traditionally said to have brought Theravada scriptures and relics to Pagan — created the first Burmese empire. Kyansittha (r. 1084–1112/13) consolidated the realm and completed the great Ananda Temple. Over two centuries, Pagan's kings and subjects endowed more than 10,000 religious monuments, of which over 2,000 survive.

Decline and fall

From the mid-13th century, the vast tax-free religious landholdings drained royal revenues and weakened the state. Mongol demands for tribute in the 1270s led to war; Mongol forces defeated the Burmese at Bhamo (1283) and the Battle of Pagan (1287). King Narathihapate fled and was assassinated; the kingdom fragmented into small successor states, and the Mongols eventually withdrew, leaving Pagan a shadow of its former self by 1297 and abandoned as a political centre soon after.

Legacy

Pagan laid the political, cultural and linguistic foundations of Myanmar: the Burmese language and script spread under its rule, Theravada Buddhism became the dominant faith, and Bagan's archaeological zone is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of Southeast Asia's greatest tourist attractions.
