Human Rights Act 1998

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Human Rights Act 1998 is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom which received royal assent on 9 November 1998 and came into force on 2 October 2000. It incorporates the rights contained in the European Convention on Human Rights into UK law — “bringing rights home”, in the government's phrase, so that Convention claims can be heard in British courts instead of requiring applications to Strasbourg.

The Act's machinery is carefully parliamentary: courts must interpret legislation compatibly with Convention rights “so far as it is possible to do so” (s.3); superior courts may issue a declaration of incompatibility (s.4) which does not invalidate the statute but prompts (often) remedial amendment; public authorities act unlawfully if they breach Convention rights (s.6). Cases on privacy (Campbell), prisoners' votes, whole-life tariffs, deportation and counter-terrorism control orders made the Act prominent and controversial; successive governments have proposed replacing it with a British Bill of Rights (a Bill was introduced in 2022 and withdrawn in 2023), while the devolution statutes entrench it in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, complicating repeal. The Act remains the working charter of UK rights law.
