Unreliable narrator in film

An unreliable narrator in film is a narrator — typically a character delivering voice-over narration, framing the story in flashback, or serving as the film's apparent point of view — whose credibility is compromised, so that the audience must doubt, reinterpret, or outright reject the version of events presented. The device derives from literature, where the term was coined by Wayne C. Booth in The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961), but cinema complicates it: because the camera appears to "show" events, filmmakers must cue unreliability through contradiction, exaggeration, or a late revelation.

Types

Film narrators may be unreliable because they deliberately lie (the confessing con artist), because they are mentally ill or cognitively impaired, because they are deluded about themselves, or because they are dead or otherwise ontologically impossible witnesses. Some films sustain ambiguity, never resolving whether the narration was false.

Notable examples

  • The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920) — an early framing twist reveals the narrator is an asylum inmate.
  • Rashomon (1950) — four witnesses give mutually contradictory accounts of the same crime; the film's name became a byword for contradictory testimony.
  • Forrest Gump (1994) — a naive narrator whose limited understanding colors the history he recounts.
  • The Usual Suspects (1995) — Verbal Kint's interrogation narrative is fabricated wholesale.
  • Fight Club (1999) and A Beautiful Mind (2001) — the narrator's perceptions are distorted by mental illness and hallucinated characters.
  • Memento (2000) — an amnesiac protagonist manipulates his own notes and deceives himself.
  • Shutter Island (2010), Gone Girl (2014) and Joker (2019) — later popular examples exploiting delusion and fabricated diaries.
