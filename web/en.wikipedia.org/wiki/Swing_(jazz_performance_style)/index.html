Swing (jazz performance style)

[Image placeholder: swing rhythm]

Swing, in music, is both a style of jazz that developed in the United States in the late 1920s and 1930s and the rhythmic feel that defines it. As a performance style, swing refers to the practice of playing pairs of eighth notes unevenly, so that the first note is lengthened and the second shortened, producing a lilting, propulsive groove often described by the phrase "it don't mean a thing if it ain't got that swing."

The swing feel

In swung eighth notes, the beat is divided unequally — often approximated as a triplet pattern in which the first two triplet-eighths merge into one longer note. The exact ratio varies with tempo and player, from nearly even at fast tempos to about 3:1 at slow ones. Swing also involves subtle timing placement by soloists and rhythm sections relative to the beat.

Swing era

The Swing Era (roughly 1935–1946) was dominated by big bands led by figures such as Benny Goodman, Count Basie, Duke Ellington, Glenn Miller, and Fletcher Henderson, whose danceable arrangements made jazz America's popular music. Rhythm sections anchored by guitar, piano, bass, and drums supplied the characteristic four-beat pulse.

Analysis

Researchers have analysed swing ratios in recordings and studied how drummers' ride cymbal patterns and bassists' walking lines interact to create the feel. Swing remains fundamental to mainstream jazz performance practice.
