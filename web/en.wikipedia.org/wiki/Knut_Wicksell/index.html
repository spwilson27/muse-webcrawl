Johan Gustaf Knut Wicksell (December 20, 1851 – May 3, 1926) was a leading Swedish economist of the early 20th century. His work in monetary theory — especially the distinction between the natural rate and the money rate of interest — profoundly influenced Keynes and the Stockholm School, and his writings on public finance laid foundations for the theory of public goods.

Contributions

In Interest and Prices (Geldzins und Güterpreise, 1898) Wicksell proposed that price levels change cumulatively when the bank (money) rate of interest diverges from the "natural rate" determined by the marginal productivity of capital: if the money rate is held below the natural rate, prices rise in a cumulative expansion, and vice versa. This "cumulative process" made money non-neutral and anticipated later macroeconomic theories of inflation targeting, in which the natural rate survives as the modern concept of r*. In his Lectures on Political Economy (1901–1906) he integrated marginal productivity theory of distribution with Böhm-Bawerk's capital theory. In public finance, his 1896 treatise proposed that taxation be linked to expenditure decisions under an approximate unanimity rule — an early statement of benefit taxation and constitutional economics — and his analysis of voluntary exchange taxation anticipated Lindahl pricing.

Life

Wicksell came to academia late, completing his doctorate in economics at Uppsala at age 48 after studies in mathematics and a controversial public career of lectures on neo-Malthusianism and social reform; he was briefly imprisoned in 1908 for blasphemy after a satirical lecture. He held the chair of economics at Lund University from 1901 to 1916 and remained a prominent, radical voice in Swedish public debate until his death.
