Amide

In organic chemistry, an amide is a compound with the functional group R–C(=O)–NR′R″, where R, R′ and R″ represent organic substituents or hydrogen atoms. Amides are derived from carboxylic acids in which the hydroxyl group is replaced by an amine group; the amide bond is among the most important linkages in chemistry and biology, forming the peptide bonds that join amino acids in proteins and the repeating units of polyamides such as nylon and Kevlar.

Structure and bonding

The amide group is planar because of resonance: the lone pair on nitrogen conjugates with the carbonyl π bond, giving the C–N bond substantial double-bond character. This partial double bond restricts rotation (the barrier is about 15–20 kcal/mol), stabilises proteins' secondary structures, and makes amides much less basic than amines — they are protonated on oxygen, not nitrogen. Simple amides such as formamide and acetamide have high melting and boiling points due to hydrogen bonding.

Classification and nomenclature

Amides are classed as primary, secondary or tertiary according to whether the nitrogen bears one, two or no hydrogen atoms. They are named from the parent carboxylic acid by replacing the suffix -oic acid with -amide (e.g. acetamide from acetic acid). Cyclic amides are called lactams; amides of sulfonic acids are sulfonamides, an important class of antibacterial drugs.

Synthesis and reactions

Amides are commonly prepared by reacting carboxylic acid derivatives (acyl chlorides, anhydrides, esters) with ammonia or amines, or by dehydrating ammonium carboxylate salts. Industrial methods include the Beckmann rearrangement of oximes to lactams (for caprolactam, the nylon-6 precursor) and the hydration of nitriles. Amides are relatively unreactive — they hydrolyse only under forcing acidic or basic conditions, are reduced to amines by lithium aluminium hydride, and can be dehydrated to nitriles.

Importance

Beyond proteins, famous amides include urea (the first organic compound synthesised from inorganic materials, by Wöhler in 1828), the solvent dimethylformamide (DMF), the drug paracetamol (acetaminophen), penicillin antibiotics, and the hallucinogen LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide).
