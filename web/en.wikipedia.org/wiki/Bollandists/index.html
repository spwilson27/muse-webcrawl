Bollandists

The Bollandists (Latin: Bollandistae) are an association of scholars, philologists and historians — originally and for a long period exclusively Jesuits, but now including non-Jesuits — who since the early seventeenth century have studied hagiography and the cult of the saints in Christianity. They are named after the Flemish Jesuit Jean Bolland (1596–1665).

Their most important publication has been the Acta Sanctorum ("Acts of the Saints"), a monumental critical edition of the lives of saints arranged by feast day, begun with the two January volumes published at Antwerp in 1643. The work, continued over more than three centuries, applies rigorous critical methods to separate historical fact from legend in the saints' lives.

History

The project was conceived by the Jesuit Heribert Rosweyde, who planned a systematic collection of hagiographical texts; on his death in 1629, Jean Bolland was charged with continuing it and vastly expanded its scholarly apparatus. Bolland was joined by Godfrey Henschen and Daniel Papebroch, and the "Bollandists" became a byword for critical erudition — Papebroch's questioning of the origins of the Carmelite order provoked a famous controversy with the Spanish Inquisition.

After the suppression of the Society of Jesus in 1773 the work was continued by other religious orders in Belgium; it was revived under a restored Jesuit team in Brussels in 1837. The Society of Bollandists continues to publish the Acta Sanctorum, the journal Analecta Bollandiana (founded 1882) and reference works such as the Bibliotheca Hagiographica Latina.
