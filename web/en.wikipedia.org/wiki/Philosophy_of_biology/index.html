Philosophy of biology


The philosophy of biology examines the concepts, methods and presuppositions of the biological sciences.

The philosophy of biology is a subfield of philosophy of science that addresses philosophical questions raised by biology and biomedicine. It emerged as a distinct professional field in the 1970s and 1980s with figures such as David Hull, Michael Ruse, Elliott Sober and Robert Brandon.

Major topics

Species and classification. Philosophers debate whether species are natural kinds, individuals, or historical entities; David Hull argued that species are individuals — spatiotemporally bounded lineages — rather than classes defined by essences.

Natural selection and adaptation. Questions include the units and levels of selection (genes, organisms, groups), the status of "fitness", whether selection is a causal process, and how adaptationist explanations should be tested — highlighted by Gould and Lewontin's "spandrels" critique.

Function and teleology. Biologists routinely speak of the function of the heart as pumping blood. Philosophers analyse such teleological language through etiological (selected-effects) theories, associated with Ruth Millikan and Larry Wright, or causal-role theories.

Reductionism. Debates concern whether classical genetics reduces to molecular biology, whether biological explanation is autonomous, and the explanatory role of mechanisms and models.

Other topics include the nature of the gene, biological individuality and holobionts, the concept of the organism, race and sex in biology, developmental systems theory, evo-devo, and the ethics of biotechnology, genetics and conservation.
