Sacred grove

A sacred grove or sacred forest is a grove of trees of special religious importance to a particular culture or community. Sacred groves feature in many of the world's religions and folk traditions, including ancient Greek and Roman religion, Celtic and Germanic paganism, Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Shinto, and numerous indigenous traditions in Africa and Asia. Because cutting trees or hunting within them is traditionally forbidden or restricted, sacred groves often survive as islands of old-growth biodiversity in otherwise altered landscapes, and are studied as an early form of nature conservation.

In antiquity

The Greek temenos and Latin lucus designated consecrated enclosures, often wooded, around sanctuaries — famous examples include the grove of the Academy at Athens, the sanctuary of Zeus at Dodona with its prophetic oak, and the grove of Nemi in Italy, associated with Diana and the priest-king Rex Nemorensis described in Frazer's The Golden Bough. Celtic peoples held rites in groves (nemeton), and Germanic and Norse sources describe sacred trees and groves such as the one at Uppsala.

In Asia

In India, sacred groves — called devrai, deorai, sarna, kavu (in Kerala) and many other local names — are dedicated to village deities and are protected by religious taboo; tens of thousands are recorded, notably in the Western Ghats, Maharashtra, Karnataka and Kerala, where they preserve rare and endemic species and protect watersheds. In Japan, chinju no mori are the forests surrounding Shinto shrines. In Mongolia and parts of Siberia, sacred mountains and groves are protected under shamanic and Buddhist custom, and in China feng shui woods are maintained behind villages.

In Africa

The Kaya forests of the Mijikenda peoples of coastal Kenya — fortified village sites in sacred forest — are UNESCO-recognised. In Ghana, Nigeria (e.g. the Osun-Osogbo Sacred Grove, a UNESCO World Heritage Site) and elsewhere in West Africa, sacred groves dedicated to deities have survived as refuges of forest biodiversity.

Ecological significance

Because of long-standing protection, sacred groves frequently conserve relict vegetation, rare species, and locally extinct fauna, and function as seed banks and water sources. Modern conservationists increasingly work with custodial communities to document and protect them, though many groves are threatened by encroachment, weakening taboos and development.
