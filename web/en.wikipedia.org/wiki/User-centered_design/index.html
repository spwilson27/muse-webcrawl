User-centered design


Usability testing with a prototype

User-centered design (UCD), also called human-centered design, is an iterative design philosophy and process in which the needs, wants and limitations of end users are given extensive attention at every stage. Popularised by Donald Norman — whose 1988 book The Design of Everyday Things remains canonical — UCD holds that products should be shaped around how people actually think and behave rather than forcing users to adapt to the artefact.

Process

The international standard ISO 9241-210 describes the cycle as: understand and specify the context of use; specify user requirements; produce design solutions (from sketches and paper prototypes to working software); and evaluate them against the requirements with real users, iterating until goals are met. Techniques include interviews, personas, task analysis, card sorting, heuristic evaluation and usability testing with think-aloud protocols.

Related approaches

UCD overlaps with usability engineering, participatory design (in which users act as co-designers, a Scandinavian tradition), design thinking, and universal/inclusive design, which extends attention to users of all ages and abilities. Critics note that an exclusive focus on current users can entrench existing practices; proponents reply that evidence-based iteration prevents the costly failures of technology-driven design.
