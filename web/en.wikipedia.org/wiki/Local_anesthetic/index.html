Local anesthetic

A local anesthetic (LA) is a medication that causes absence of pain sensation (anaesthesia) in a specific location of the body without necessarily causing loss of consciousness, as opposed to a general anaesthetic which eliminates all sensation in the entire body and causes unconsciousness. Local anesthetics act by reversibly blocking the voltage-gated sodium channels in nerve cell membranes, preventing the initiation and propagation of action potentials and thereby interrupting pain signals to the brain.

Chemical classes

Local anesthetics fall into two chemical classes based on the linkage between their lipophilic aromatic ring and hydrophilic amine group. Ester agents — such as procaine, chloroprocaine, tetracaine and benzocaine — are metabolised by plasma esterases and are more likely to provoke allergic reactions via their metabolite para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA). Amide agents — including lidocaine (lignocaine), bupivacaine, ropivacaine, mepivacaine and prilocaine — are metabolised in the liver and are now dominant in clinical practice.

Techniques

Local anesthetics may be applied topically to skin or mucous membranes (creams, gels, sprays), infiltrated into tissue around a wound or surgical field, injected around a peripheral nerve or plexus (nerve block), or delivered into the spinal or epidural space. Lidocaine is frequently combined with epinephrine (adrenaline), whose vasoconstriction slows systemic absorption, prolongs the block and reduces bleeding.

Onset and duration vary with the agent: lidocaine acts within minutes and lasts one to two hours, while the more potent, lipid-soluble bupivacaine has a slower onset but can provide many hours of anaesthesia, making it popular for postoperative analgesia.

Adverse effects

Most adverse effects arise from excessive plasma concentrations, usually after accidental intravascular injection or overdose. Local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) can cause perioral numbness, tinnitus, agitation and seizures, progressing to cardiovascular collapse; bupivacaine is notably cardiotoxic. Treatment includes stopping the injection, airway support and intravenous lipid emulsion. True allergy is rare and usually attributable to ester agents or preservatives.

History

The first local anesthetic was cocaine, isolated from coca leaves and introduced into ophthalmic surgery by Karl Koller in 1884. Its toxicity and addictiveness drove the search for synthetic alternatives, leading to procaine (Novocain) in 1905 and to lidocaine, synthesised by Nils Löfgren in 1943, which remains a standard agent worldwide.
