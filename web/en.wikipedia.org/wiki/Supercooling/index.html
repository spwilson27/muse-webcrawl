Supercooling, also known as undercooling, is the process of lowering the temperature of a liquid or a gas below its freezing point without it becoming a solid. A liquid cooled below its standard freezing point will normally crystallise in the presence of a seed crystal or nucleus around which a crystal structure can form — a process called nucleation. In the absence of such nuclei, the liquid can remain in a metastable supercooled state down to its homogeneous nucleation temperature, at which crystal formation occurs spontaneously throughout the liquid.

Water

Pure water normally freezes at 0 °C (273.15 K), but in clean containers free of nucleation sites it can be supercooled to about −42 °C before homogeneous nucleation occurs. Supercooling of water is readily demonstrated with a bottle of purified water chilled undisturbed in a freezer: shaking or striking the bottle triggers near-instant crystallisation, releasing latent heat that warms the resulting ice–water mixture back to 0 °C. Freezing rain and aircraft icing involve supercooled droplets in clouds, which freeze on contact with surfaces.

Mechanism

Crystallisation requires the formation of a crystal nucleus of a critical size. Below the freezing point the solid phase is thermodynamically favoured, but forming the initial interface costs energy, so sub-critical clusters tend to redissolve. Foreign particles, container walls or pre-existing crystals lower this barrier (heterogeneous nucleation); without them, the much lower homogeneous nucleation limit applies. When freezing finally begins in a deeply supercooled liquid, it proceeds very rapidly — a phenomenon sometimes called recalescence as the temperature rebounds toward the melting point.

Applications and natural occurrence

Supercooling is exploited in heat-storage hand warmers based on supersaturated sodium acetate, in some refrigeration and cryopreservation techniques, and in cloud seeding research. Many cold-climate animals and plants use antifreeze proteins and controlled nucleation to survive, while freeze-avoiding insects supercool their body fluids to tens of degrees below zero. Supercooling is also central to the formation of metallic glasses and to phase-change materials research.
