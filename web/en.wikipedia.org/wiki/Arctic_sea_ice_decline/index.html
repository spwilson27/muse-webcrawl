Arctic sea ice decline

The Arctic sea ice decline is the observed loss of sea ice in the Arctic Ocean over recent decades, driven by human-caused global warming. Satellite measurements since 1979 show that September sea ice extent — the annual minimum — has fallen at roughly 13% per decade relative to the 1981–2010 average, and the remaining ice has become younger and thinner: thick multi-year ice has largely been replaced by seasonal first-year ice.

Observations

The lowest September extent in the satellite record occurred in 2012, at about 3.4 million square kilometres, roughly half the typical extent of the 1980s. Ice volume declined even faster than extent — by some model-based estimates about three-quarters of September ice volume disappeared between 1979 and 2012. Winter (March) extents have declined more slowly, at around 3% per decade, but winter ice thickness has also decreased markedly.

Causes and feedbacks

The primary cause is the warming of the Arctic, which has proceeded at roughly three to four times the global average rate — a phenomenon called Arctic amplification. Central to it is the ice–albedo feedback: as bright ice melts, darker open water absorbs more solar energy, warming the ocean and melting more ice. The loss is compounded by changes in atmospheric and ocean circulation, black carbon deposition, and poleward heat transport.

Consequences and projections

The decline affects polar ecosystems and species such as polar bears and walruses, Indigenous communities whose travel and hunting depend on ice, and mid-latitude weather, though proposed links to extreme weather remain debated. It also opens Arctic shipping routes and access to offshore resources. Climate models project that the Arctic Ocean will become practically free of summer sea ice before the middle of the 21st century under continued emissions, an outcome that some researchers believe could occur in the 2030s.
