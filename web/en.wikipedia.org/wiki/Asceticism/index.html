Asceticism

Asceticism
PracticesFasting, celibacy, poverty, seclusion, sleep deprivation, self-mortification
Found inHinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Christianity, Islam (Sufism), Judaism, ancient Greek philosophy
EtymologyFrom Greek askēsis, "exercise, training"

Asceticism is a lifestyle characterised by abstinence from sensual pleasures, often for the purpose of pursuing spiritual goals. Ascetics may withdraw from the world, practise celibacy and poverty, fast, and subject the body to hardship, believing that self-denial purifies the mind or brings the practitioner closer to liberation, enlightenment or God. The word derives from the Greek askēsis, originally meaning the disciplined training of an athlete.

In Indian religions

Ascetic practice is central to the śramaṇa traditions of ancient India. Jain monks undertake severe austerities, including fasting and non-possession, to burn off karma. The Buddha, after years of extreme self-mortification, rejected its excesses and taught a "Middle Way", though monastic discipline in Buddhism still involves renunciation of property and sensual indulgence. Hindu traditions include wandering sadhus, some practising extreme austerities (tapas), as well as the structured renunciation of the sannyasa stage of life.

In Christianity

Christian asceticism has roots in the example of Jesus's forty days in the wilderness and in early hermits such as Anthony the Great, whose withdrawal to the Egyptian desert inspired the Desert Fathers and Mothers. Monastic communities — following rules such as that of St Benedict — combined prayer, work, fasting and celibacy. Medieval practice sometimes extended to anchorites immured in cells and stylites living atop pillars. Protestant traditions generally rejected monastic asceticism, while emphasising inner-worldly discipline.

In other traditions

Islam discourages monasticism, but fasting during Ramadan is a pillar of the faith, and Sufi orders developed ascetic practices of remembrance, vigils and poverty. Judaism has known ascetic movements (such as the ancient Essenes and Nazirites) alongside mainstream approval of marriage and worldly enjoyment. In Greek philosophy, the Cynics embraced poverty and shamelessness, the Stoics cultivated indifference to pleasure and pain, and Pythagorean communities observed dietary and behavioural restrictions.

Modern perspectives

Philosophers such as Nietzsche both criticised the "ascetic ideal" and acknowledged its role in giving suffering meaning; psychologists and sociologists study asceticism in relation to self-control and community identity. Simplified forms — voluntary simplicity, digital fasting, minimalist lifestyles — are sometimes described as secular asceticism.
