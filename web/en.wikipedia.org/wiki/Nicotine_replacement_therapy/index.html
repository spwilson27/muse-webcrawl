Nicotine replacement therapy

Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) is a medically approved way to treat nicotine dependence by taking nicotine through means other than tobacco. Products are used to help people stop smoking or stop chewing tobacco by supplying controlled doses of nicotine that relieve withdrawal symptoms and cravings without the harmful combustion products of cigarettes.

Medical uses

NRT products are indicated for the relief of nicotine withdrawal symptoms in people attempting to quit smoking. Meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials show that all licensed forms of NRT increase the rate of quitting by roughly 50–60% compared with placebo or no treatment. NRT may also be used for temporary abstinence in situations where smoking is not permitted, and long-term use is considered for harm reduction in heavily dependent smokers unable to quit completely.

Types

Transdermal patch

The nicotine patch delivers a steady dose of nicotine through the skin over 16 or 24 hours. Patches are sold in several strengths, and treatment typically steps down the dose over 8–12 weeks.

Gum and lozenges

Nicotine gum (2 mg and 4 mg) and lozenges are absorbed through the oral mucosa. They allow users to control dosing in response to cravings. Acidic drinks such as coffee or soda can reduce absorption and should be avoided shortly before and during use.

Inhaler and nasal spray

The nicotine inhaler provides a vapour that is absorbed in the mouth and throat, partly mimicking the hand-to-mouth ritual of smoking. The nasal spray delivers nicotine rapidly through the nasal lining and is among the fastest-acting forms, though it commonly causes local irritation.

Effectiveness

The Cochrane Collaboration has repeatedly concluded that NRT is effective: in a review of over 130 trials involving more than 64,000 participants, NRT increased long-term quit rates. Combining a long-acting form (patch) with a faster-acting form (gum or lozenge) is more effective than a single form. Pre-quit use of the patch and higher-dose gum for highly dependent smokers may further improve outcomes.

Adverse effects

Side effects depend on the delivery system: skin irritation at the patch site, mouth and jaw soreness from gum, hiccups and heartburn from oral products, and nasal irritation from the spray. Nicotine itself is not classified as a human carcinogen, and the risks of NRT are far lower than continued smoking. Use in pregnancy is approached cautiously, but guidelines generally consider NRT preferable to continued smoking.

History

Nicotine gum was developed in Sweden in the late 1960s and 1970s, notably by Ove Fernö at the pharmaceutical company Leo, and was first marketed as Nicorette in the early 1980s. The transdermal patch followed in the late 1980s and early 1990s. NRT products are on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.
