Sunda Islands

The Sunda Islands are a group of islands in the Malay Archipelago, in Southeast Asia. They are divided into the Greater Sunda Islands and the Lesser Sunda Islands. Most of the group lies within Indonesia, with parts belonging to Malaysia, Brunei, and East Timor.

Greater Sunda Islands

The Greater Sunda Islands are the four large islands in the western part of the group: Borneo (the world's third-largest island, shared by Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei), Java (the world's most populous island, part of Indonesia), Sumatra, and Sulawesi. The Sunda Shelf, a shallow extension of the Asian continental shelf, underlies the seas around them; during Pleistocene ice ages, when sea levels were lower, these islands were connected to mainland Asia in the landmass known as Sundaland.

Lesser Sunda Islands

The Lesser Sunda Islands form an arc east of Java, including Bali, Lombok, Sumbawa, Flores, Sumba, Timor, and the Alor archipelago. They are largely of volcanic origin, lying along the Sunda Arc where the Indo-Australian Plate subducts beneath the Eurasian Plate. The deep-water Wallace Line, marking a sharp faunal boundary identified by Alfred Russel Wallace, runs between Bali and Lombok and between Borneo and Sulawesi, separating Asian species from the mixed Wallacea biogeographic zone.

Geology

The islands sit on one of the world's most volcanically active zones. Historic eruptions include Mount Tambora on Sumbawa in 1815 — the largest eruption in recorded history, which caused the "Year Without a Summer" in 1816 — and Krakatoa in 1883. The Sunda Strait separates Java from Sumatra, and the Java Trench offshore marks the subduction zone responsible for the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami.
