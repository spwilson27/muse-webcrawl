Shanghai Jews

The Shanghai Jews were Jewish communities that lived in Shanghai, China, in successive waves from the mid-19th century until the mid-20th century. Most famously, Shanghai was one of the few places in the world that admitted Jewish refugees fleeing Nazi persecution without an entry visa, saving an estimated 20,000 lives during the Holocaust.

Early communities

The first Jews in Shanghai were Baghdadi merchants — families such as the Sassoons, Hardoon and Kadoorie — who arrived under British protection after the city became a treaty port in the 1840s. They built synagogues including Beth El and Ohel Rachel and became major figures in Shanghai commerce. A second community of Russian Jews arrived after the Russian Revolution and during the 1920s–30s, settling largely in the French Concession and Hongkou.

The refugee era

Between 1938 and 1941, some 18,000–20,000 Jewish refugees from Germany, Austria, Poland and elsewhere reached Shanghai, largely because it required no visa; many travelled on ships from Italy or by rail across Siberia. Figures such as the Chinese consul in Vienna, Ho Feng-Shan, issued life-saving visas, and the Japanese consul Chiune Sugihara issued transit visas to thousands. After occupying Shanghai fully, the Japanese authorities, under German pressure, confined stateless refugees in 1943 to a "Designated Area" — the Shanghai Ghetto in Hongkou — where conditions were harsh but the community survived the war intact, protected in part by Japanese officials' refusal to carry out extermination.

Aftermath

After 1945 most refugees emigrated to the United States, Israel, Australia and elsewhere, and the older communities dispersed after 1949. Shanghai's Jewish heritage is now commemorated at the Shanghai Jewish Refugees Museum, housed in the former Ohel Moshe Synagogue.
