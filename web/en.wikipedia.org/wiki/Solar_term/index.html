Solar term

A solar term (or jieqi) is any of twenty-four points on the traditional Chinese lunisolar calendar that divides a year into twenty-four parts. Each solar term corresponds to a specific astronomical position of the Sun on the ecliptic, spaced 15° apart, and was created to guide agricultural activities and reflect seasonal changes in ancient China.

The twenty-four terms

Beginning with the start of spring, the terms are: Lichun (Beginning of Spring), Yushui (Rain Water), Jingzhe (Awakening of Insects), Chunfen (Spring Equinox), Qingming (Pure Brightness), Guyu (Grain Rain), Lixia (Beginning of Summer), Xiaoman (Grain Full), Mangzhong (Grain in Ear), Xiazhi (Summer Solstice), Xiaoshu (Minor Heat), Dashu (Major Heat), Liqiu (Beginning of Autumn), Chushu (Limit of Heat), Bailu (White Dew), Qiufen (Autumn Equinox), Hanlu (Cold Dew), Shuangjiang (Frost Descent), Lidong (Beginning of Winter), Xiaoxue (Minor Snow), Daxue (Major Snow), Dongzhi (Winter Solstice), Xiaohan (Minor Cold), and Dahan (Major Cold).

History and use

The complete system of twenty-four terms was established by the Han dynasty (the list appears in the Huainanzi, 2nd century BC). Because the Chinese calendar is lunisolar, solar terms anchor the calendar to the solar year and schedule leap months. The terms spread to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. In 2016 UNESCO inscribed "The Twenty-Four Solar Terms" on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Significance

Certain terms coincide with festivals: Qingming with the tomb-sweeping festival and Dongzhi with the winter solstice festival. Solar terms still appear in Chinese almanacs and guide traditional farming and dietary customs.
