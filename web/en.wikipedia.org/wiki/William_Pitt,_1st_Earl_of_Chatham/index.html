William Pitt, 1st Earl of Chatham (15 November 1708 – 11 May 1778), known as Pitt the Elder, was a British statesman of the Whig group who served as Prime Minister of Great Britain from 1766 to 1768. He is celebrated as the political leader who guided Britain to victory in the Seven Years' War, laying the foundations of the First British Empire.

Early career

Pitt entered Parliament in 1735 as one of the "Boy Patriots" opposing Robert Walpole, cutting a figure as a brilliant, theatrical orator. As Paymaster of the Forces (1746–55) he won an exceptional reputation for probity by refusing the customary perquisites of the office.

The Seven Years' War

Called to power in the crisis of 1756–57 in coalition with the Duke of Newcastle, Pitt directed strategy as Secretary of State: subsidising Frederick the Great in Europe while conquering in the colonies — "I will conquer America in Germany". The annus mirabilis of 1759 brought victories at Minden, Lagos, Quebec (under General James Wolfe) and Quiberon Bay; by 1763 France had ceded Canada and its position in India was broken. Pitt, the "Great Commoner", was immensely popular with the public.

Later years

Pitt accepted a peerage as Earl of Chatham in 1766 and headed a ministry, but gout and mental illness undermined his premiership, which ended in 1768. In the House of Lords he championed reconciliation with the American colonies, opposing coercive measures, and collapsed while speaking against the war in April 1778, dying a month later. His son, William Pitt the Younger, became prime minister in 1783.
