Poi (food)

Poi
TypePaste / pudding
Place of originPolynesia (Hawaii)
Main ingredientsTaro root (kalo)
Food energy~112 kcal per 100 g

Poi is a traditional staple food in the Polynesian diet, made from the underground plant stem or corm of the taro plant (known in Hawaiian as kalo). It is produced by mashing cooked taro on a wooden pounding board (papa kuʻi ʻai) with a carved pestle (pōhaku kuʻi ʻai) made from basalt, coral, or other dense stone.

Fresh poi, showing its characteristic smooth, paste-like consistency

Preparation

Taro corms are baked or steamed, then peeled and pounded with a small amount of water into a smooth, highly viscous paste. Fresh poi is sweet and edible straight away. As it ferments over the following days it becomes sour; many eaters prefer it at different stages along this progression. Poi is traditionally eaten with the fingers, and its thickness is described by how many fingers are needed to eat it — "one-finger", "two-finger", or "three-finger" poi.

Cultural significance

In Native Hawaiian culture, kalo is regarded as an ancestor: in the genealogy of the Hawaiian people, the stillborn child of the sky father Wākea and the goddess Hoʻohōkūkalani was buried and became the first kalo plant, from which the Hawaiian people descend. The bowl of poi at the table was traditionally never argued over, and the ʻohana (family) gathered around it. Poi is a central dish at a lūʻau, often served alongside kalua pig, lomi salmon, and haupia.

Nutrition

Poi is easily digestible, low in fat, and rich in complex carbohydrates, calcium, and potassium. Its digestibility and hypoallergenic qualities have led to its use as a food for infants and for people with digestive sensitivities.
