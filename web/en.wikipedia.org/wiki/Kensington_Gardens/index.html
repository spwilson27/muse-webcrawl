Kensington Gardens, once the private gardens of Kensington Palace, are among the Royal Parks of London. Covering 265 acres (107 hectares) immediately to the west of Hyde Park, from which they are separated only by the Serpentine Bridge and West Carriage Drive, the gardens are mostly in the City of Westminster, with a small section in the Royal Borough of Kensington and Chelsea.

History

The land was originally part of the hunting grounds of Hyde Park. In 1689 William III bought Nottingham House — the future Kensington Palace — and its grounds, and Queen Mary laid out gardens in the Dutch style. Queen Anne added the Orangery (1704) and a sunken Dutch garden, and Queen Caroline, wife of George II, greatly enlarged the gardens in the 1720s and 1730s, creating the Long Water at the head of the Serpentine and the Round Pond. The gardens were opened to the public gradually during the eighteenth century and fully in the nineteenth.

Features

Landmarks include Kensington Palace, the Albert Memorial (unveiled in 1876 opposite the Royal Albert Hall), the bronze statue of Peter Pan (1912), the Diana, Princess of Wales Memorial Playground, the Serpentine Gallery and its annual summer pavilion, and the Italian Gardens at the head of the Long Water, created in the 1860s with fountains and statuary. The Elfin Oak, a carved and painted tree stump dating from 1911, stands near the playground. The gardens are a Grade I listed landscape on the Register of Historic Parks and Gardens.
