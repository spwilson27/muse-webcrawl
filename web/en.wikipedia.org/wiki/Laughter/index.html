Laughter

Laughter is a human physical reaction consisting of rhythmic contractions of the diaphragm and other parts of the respiratory system, producing a characteristic vocal sound. It is most commonly a response to humour, tickling, or social bonding, and is largely involuntary. The scientific study of laughter and its effects is called gelotology.

Function and social role

Research suggests laughter evolved as a social signal: people are far more likely to laugh in company than alone, and much everyday laughter follows ordinary conversation rather than jokes, serving to signal friendliness and ease tension. Laughter is contagious — hearing it tends to provoke it — a feature exploited by laugh tracks in television comedy.

Health and psychology

Laughter transiently increases heart rate and oxygen uptake and is followed by muscle relaxation; it can raise pain tolerance and is associated with reduced stress hormones. Laughter yoga and humour therapy apply these ideas clinically, though evidence for long-term health benefits is modest. Uncontrollable or inappropriate laughter can also occur as a symptom of neurological conditions such as pseudobulbar affect, and gelastic seizures arise from hypothalamic lesions.

In other animals

Elements of laughter-like behaviour occur in other primates during play and tickling, and rats emit ultrasonic "chirps" during play that some researchers compare to laughter, suggesting deep evolutionary roots for the behaviour.
