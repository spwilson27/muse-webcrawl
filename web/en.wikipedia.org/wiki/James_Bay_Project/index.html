The James Bay Project is a series of hydroelectric power stations on the La Grande River in northwestern Quebec, Canada, built by the provincial utility Hydro-Québec. It is one of the largest hydroelectric complexes in the world, involving the diversion of several major rivers — including the Eastmain, Opinaca, and Caniapiscau — into the La Grande watershed and the creation of vast reservoirs.

History

The project was announced in 1971 by Premier Robert Bourassa as the "project of the century," intended to drive Quebec's economy and assert provincial control over energy resources. Phase I, built between 1974 and 1985, centred on the La Grande river stations LG-2 (later renamed for Bourassa), LG-3, and LG-4. The Cree and Inuit of the region, who had not been consulted, challenged the project in court; after an initial injunction and its reversal on appeal, negotiations produced the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement of 1975 — the first modern Indigenous land-claims settlement in Canada — which granted the Cree and Inuit compensation, land categories, and self-governance institutions in exchange for allowing the project to proceed.

Later phases and controversy

Phase II added stations including La Grande-1, Laforge, and Brisay. The proposed Great Whale (Grande-Baleine) project was shelved in 1994 after sustained Cree opposition led by Grand Chief Matthew Coon Come and the withdrawal of prospective American buyers. The 2002 Paix des Braves agreement between Quebec and the Cree Nation enabled the Eastmain-1 and the Rupert River diversion projects. The complex remains central to Quebec's electricity exports to New England and to debates over Indigenous rights and boreal environmental impacts, including mercury contamination in reservoir fish.
