Islam in China

Islam in China has a history spanning more than 1,300 years. Muslims are a minority group in China, representing about 1–2% of the total population, or roughly 20–25 million people. Islam is recognized by the Chinese government as one of the country's five official religions.

History

According to tradition, Islam was first brought to China in 651 CE, during the Tang dynasty, by an embassy sent by the caliph Uthman, traditionally said to have been led by Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas. Arab and Persian merchants settled in port cities such as Guangzhou, Quanzhou, and Yangzhou, building early mosques such as the Huaisheng Mosque in Guangzhou — among the oldest in the world.

Under the Mongol Yuan dynasty, Muslims from Central and West Asia were brought to China as administrators, soldiers, craftsmen, and merchants, spreading Islam across the country and especially into Yunnan. During the Ming dynasty, Muslims became increasingly sinicized; the Hui ethnic group emerged as Chinese-speaking Muslims. The explorer Zheng He was a Hui Muslim from Yunnan.

Muslim ethnic groups

Nine of China's 56 recognized ethnic groups are predominantly Muslim, including the Hui (the largest), Uyghurs, Kazakhs, Dongxiang, Kyrgyz, Salar, Tajiks, Uzbeks, Bonan, and Tatars. The Hui are concentrated in Ningxia and the northwest, while Uyghurs are a Turkic people native to Xinjiang.

Architecture and culture

Chinese mosques often blend Islamic function with Chinese architectural forms, as in the Great Mosque of Xi'an (founded 742) and the Niujie Mosque in Beijing. Muslim scholars of the Ming and Qing periods produced the Han Kitab, a body of Chinese-language Islamic literature that interpreted Islam through Confucian vocabulary.

Modern era

In the 19th century, major Hui revolts — the Panthay Rebellion in Yunnan and the Dungan revolts in the northwest — caused enormous loss of life. In the People's Republic of China, Islam is administered through the Islamic Association of China. Since the 2010s, international attention has focused on the mass detention of Uyghurs in Xinjiang, which the Chinese government describes as vocational training and counterterrorism measures, while many governments and rights organizations have characterized the policies as severe human rights abuses and, in some assessments, genocide.
