Gesta Regum Anglorum

The Gesta Regum Anglorum ("Deeds of the Kings of the English"), originally titled De Gestis Regum Anglorum, is a history of the kings of England written by the Benedictine monk and historian William of Malmesbury in the early 12th century, around 1125. It is regarded as one of the finest works of medieval English historiography, remarkable for its author's critical attitude towards his sources, his lively style and his relatively impartial judgement of kings and events.

Contents

The work is arranged in five books. The first covers the period from the arrival of the Saxons in 449 to the reign of King Egbert; the second continues to the Norman Conquest of 1066; the third treats William the Conqueror; the fourth William Rufus; and the fifth the reign of Henry I, originally ending in 1120 but later revised and continued to about 1127. William drew on Bede's Ecclesiastical History, the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, charters, oral testimony and earlier chronicles, while also including anecdotes, miracles and classical allusions.

Author and method

William (c. 1095 – c. 1143), librarian of Malmesbury Abbey in Wiltshire, wrote the Gesta Regum as a continuation of Bede, lamenting that after Bede's death English history had been written in a rough and unpolished style. He pledged to give the narrative "the flavour of Roman salt." Unusually for his time, William questioned implausible traditions, weighed conflicting accounts and distinguished between what he had verified and what was merely reported. He also wrote a companion work, the Gesta Pontificum Anglorum, on the bishops and monasteries of England.

Editions and significance

The text survives in many manuscripts reflecting several authorial revisions. The standard modern edition is that of R. A. B. Mynors, R. M. Thomson and M. Winterbottom (Oxford Medieval Texts, 1998–99), with an English translation. Historians value the Gesta Regum as a major source for Anglo-Saxon and Anglo-Norman England, and as a landmark in the development of critical historical writing in medieval Europe.
