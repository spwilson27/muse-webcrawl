Lupus anticoagulant

Lupus anticoagulants (LA) are immunoglobulins that bind to phospholipids and proteins associated with the cell membrane. They belong to the family of antiphospholipid antibodies, which also includes anticardiolipin antibodies and anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies. Despite the name, a lupus anticoagulant is predominantly a prothrombotic agent in vivo: its presence is associated with an increased risk of arterial and venous thrombosis and with adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Nomenclature

The name is a double misnomer. First, most patients with a lupus anticoagulant do not have systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE); the antibodies were first described in patients with SLE in the 1950s. Second, the antibodies act as anticoagulants only in vitro, prolonging phospholipid-dependent clotting assays such as the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), whereas in the body they promote clotting. Two patients with the "lupus anticoagulant paradox" were described by Conley and Hartmann in 1952; the term itself was coined by Feinstein and Rapaport in 1972.

Mechanism

Lupus anticoagulants are directed against epitopes on phospholipid-binding proteins, principally β2-glycoprotein I and prothrombin. In vitro they compete for the phospholipid surface needed to assemble coagulation complexes, prolonging clotting times. In vivo, antibody binding is thought to activate endothelial cells, monocytes and platelets, interfere with the protein C pathway and annexin A5 shield, and thereby favour thrombosis.

Clinical significance

Persistent lupus anticoagulant positivity is one of the laboratory criteria for the diagnosis of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), together with anticardiolipin and anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies. Testing should be repeated at least 12 weeks apart to distinguish persistent antibodies from transient ones, which may appear during infections, including in children, and usually carry little thrombotic risk. A lupus anticoagulant is the antiphospholipid test most strongly associated with thrombosis and pregnancy morbidity.

Diagnosis

International guidelines (ISTH) recommend a screen–mix–confirm algorithm using two test systems, most commonly the dilute Russell's viper venom time (dRVVT) and an LA-sensitive aPTT (such as the silica clotting time). A prolonged screening test that fails to correct on mixing with normal plasma but shortens with excess phospholipid confirms the presence of an LA. Testing is unreliable in patients receiving anticoagulants such as warfarin, heparins or direct oral anticoagulants, which can produce false results.

Treatment

Asymptomatic carriers are generally not treated, though low-dose aspirin is sometimes used. Patients who have had thrombosis or defined pregnancy morbidity with persistent LA are managed as antiphospholipid syndrome, typically with long-term warfarin anticoagulation; direct oral anticoagulants are avoided in high-risk (especially triple-positive) APS.
