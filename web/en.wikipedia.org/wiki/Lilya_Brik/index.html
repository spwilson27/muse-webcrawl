Lilya Yuryevna Brik (Russian: Лиля Юрьевна Брик; 11 November 1891 – 4 August 1978) was a Russian writer, actress and social figure, best known as the muse and lover of the poet Vladimir Mayakovsky. Together with her husband, the critic Osip Brik, she hosted one of the most influential avant-garde salons in Moscow and was closely associated with Russian Futurism and the journal LEF.

Early life

Born Lilya Kagan into a prosperous Jewish family in Moscow, she received a good education, studying architecture briefly before marrying the writer and critic Osip Brik in 1912. Her younger sister, Elsa, later became the French writer Elsa Triolet, wife of Louis Aragon.

Relationship with Mayakovsky

In 1915 Elsa introduced Lilya to Vladimir Mayakovsky, who fell in love with her and became a permanent member of the Brik household in an unconventional arrangement accepted by Osip. Mayakovsky dedicated many of his greatest works to her, including the poems A Cloud in Trousers, The Backbone Flute and the poem "Lilichka! (Instead of a Letter)" (1916), named after her diminutive. He addressed her in his suicide note of 1930, asking that she and his family be provided for, and his final letter referred to the "love boat" having "smashed against convention." After the poet's death, Brik devoted herself to preserving and editing his work; her 1936 letter to Joseph Stalin helped secure Mayakovsky's official canonisation in the Soviet Union.

Later life and death

After Osip Brik's death in 1945, Lilya married the literary scholar Vasily Katanyan. She continued to act as a custodian of the Mayakovsky archive and corresponded with figures across Soviet cultural life, although she fell in and out of official favour. Following a serious fall and in declining health, she died by suicide in Moscow in 1978, aged 86.

Legacy

Brik remains a central and sometimes controversial figure in accounts of the Russian avant-garde; her memoirs and correspondence are major sources for the lives of Mayakovsky and the Futurist circle. Feminist reassessments have stressed her own creative and editorial agency rather than the purely muse-like role assigned to her by earlier biographers.
