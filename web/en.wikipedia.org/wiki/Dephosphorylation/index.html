Dephosphorylation

In biochemistry, dephosphorylation is the removal of a phosphate group from an organic compound by hydrolysis. It is the opposite of phosphorylation. Dephosphorylation and its counterpart, phosphorylation, activate and deactivate enzymes by detaching and attaching phosphoryl groups, forming a major mechanism for regulating protein function in cells.

Enzymes and mechanism

Dephosphorylation is catalyzed by enzymes called phosphatases, which hydrolyze the phosphomonoester bond to release inorganic phosphate. Protein phosphatases are grouped into families including serine/threonine phosphatases (such as PP1, PP2A, and calcineurin/PP2B) and protein tyrosine phosphatases, which reverse the work of protein kinases and thereby switch off — or sometimes switch on — signaling pathways.

Biological roles

The phosphorylation state of proteins controls processes including cell division, metabolism, muscle contraction, and immune signaling. Dephosphorylation also releases energy in the hydrolysis of ATP to ADP (catalyzed by ATPases) and is used deliberately in molecular biology: alkaline phosphatase treatment of linearized plasmid DNA removes 5′ phosphates to prevent self-ligation during cloning.
