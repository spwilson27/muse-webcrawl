Anne Hutchinson (née Marbury; 1591–1643) was a Puritan spiritual adviser and midwife whose religious views precipitated the Antinomian Controversy in the Massachusetts Bay Colony. A charismatic and well-read woman, she held meetings in her Boston home that drew large followings, and her challenge to the colony's ministers and magistrates led to her trial, excommunication and banishment in 1637–1638. She is regarded as an early symbol of religious dissent and freedom of conscience in America.

Early life and emigration

Born in Alford, Lincolnshire, the daughter of clergyman Francis Marbury, she married William Hutchinson, a merchant, and followed the minister John Cotton to Boston in 1634. There she began hosting twice-weekly meetings, initially for women, to discuss sermons — meetings that grew to include prominent men such as the young governor Henry Vane.

The Antinomian Controversy

Hutchinson taught that salvation came by a "covenant of grace" alone and that most of the colony's ministers preached a "covenant of works", lacking the seal of the Spirit. Her brother-in-law John Wheelwright and Governor Vane backed her faction; John Winthrop and most magistrates opposed it. After her party lost the 1637 elections, she was tried by the General Court in November 1637 for "traducing the ministers" and banished; a church trial in March 1638 ended in excommunication, Winthrop calling her "an instrument of Satan".

Exile and death

Hutchinson and her followers founded Portsmouth on Aquidneck Island (in present-day Rhode Island). After William's death she moved to Dutch New Netherland for safety, settling near Pelham Bay in what is now the Bronx. In August 1643, during Kieft's War, she and most of her household were killed by Siwanoy warriors; only her daughter Susanna survived, taken captive.

Legacy

The Hutchinson River and Hutchinson River Parkway in New York are named for her. Historians view her as a key figure in the history of women in ministry and of American religious freedom.
