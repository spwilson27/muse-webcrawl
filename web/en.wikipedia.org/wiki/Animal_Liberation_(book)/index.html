Animal Liberation (book)

Animal Liberation: A New Ethics for Our Treatment of Animals is a 1975 book by the Australian philosopher Peter Singer. It is widely considered the founding philosophical statement of the modern animal liberation movement. Singer argues, on utilitarian grounds, that the interests of all sentient beings deserve equal consideration, and that discriminating against beings on the basis of their species—an attitude he calls "speciesism", following Richard Ryder—is as unjustifiable as discrimination based on race or sex.

Arguments

The book's central claim rests on the capacity to suffer: "If a being suffers, there can be no moral justification for refusing to take that suffering into consideration." Singer surveys the use of animals in factory farming and in scientific experimentation, describing standard industry practices in detail and arguing that the trivial benefits they produce cannot outweigh the suffering imposed. The practical conclusion drawn is that readers should stop eating meat and other animal products produced by such methods.

Singer distinguishes between animals that are self-conscious persons and merely sentient beings, a distinction that has generated extensive debate, particularly regarding his views on killing and on the ethics of replacing animals that live pleasant lives.

Reception and influence

The book helped launch the modern animal rights movement, influencing groups from academic philosophy to activist organisations, and it has been continuously in print, with revised editions in 1990, 2002, and a substantially updated version, Animal Liberation Now, in 2023. Critics have challenged its utilitarian framework, its treatment of personhood, and its neglect of rights-based approaches, notably Tom Regan's alternative in The Case for Animal Rights (1983).
