In mathematics and solid state physics, the first Brillouin zone, named after Léon Brillouin, is a uniquely defined primitive cell in reciprocal space. In the same way that the Bravais lattice is divided up into Wigner–Seitz cells in the real lattice, the reciprocal lattice is broken up into Brillouin zones. The boundaries are the planes at which waves with wavevector on the boundary satisfy the Bragg condition for diffraction by the lattice.

Definition and higher zones

The first Brillouin zone is the locus of points in reciprocal space closer to the origin of the reciprocal lattice than to any other reciprocal lattice point. The n-th Brillouin zone consists of points that are reached by crossing exactly n − 1 Bragg planes; all zones have the same volume, equal to that of the first zone (the volume of the reciprocal primitive cell), though their shapes differ and become increasingly fragmented. Because of Bloch's theorem, any wavevector can be translated by a reciprocal lattice vector into the first zone without changing the physics, so electronic band structures are conventionally plotted only within it.

High-symmetry points

Particular points and lines within the zone are labelled by convention: Γ at the centre, and letters such as X, L, K, M, R, W, Δ and Λ at boundary points and along symmetry lines, depending on the lattice type. The first Brillouin zone of a body-centred cubic lattice is a rhombic dodecahedron, and of a face-centred cubic lattice a truncated octahedron. Band diagrams plot energy along paths connecting these points, giving the standard pictures of a material's electronic band structure — its bands, gaps and density of states — from which conductivity, optical and magnetic behaviour can be understood.
