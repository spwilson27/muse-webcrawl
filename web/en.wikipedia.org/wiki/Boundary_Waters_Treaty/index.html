Boundary Waters Treaty

The Boundary Waters Treaty (formally the Treaty Between the United States and Great Britain Relating to Boundary Waters, and Questions Arising Between the United States and Canada) is an agreement signed in Washington on 11 January 1909 between the United States and the United Kingdom, acting for its then-dominion of Canada. It governs the shared boundary waters between the United States and Canada and established the International Joint Commission (IJC).

Provisions

The treaty provides for free navigation of boundary waters, sets principles for uses and diversions, and requires approval by the IJC for projects that would affect the natural level or flow of boundary waters on the other side of the border. It also gives the two governments the ability to refer questions or disputes to the Commission for investigation and recommendation. A key clause states that boundary waters and waters flowing across the boundary shall not be polluted on either side to the injury of health or property on the other.

Legacy

The IJC created under the treaty has managed disputes over the Great Lakes, the St. Lawrence River, and many transboundary basins, and the treaty's pollution provision laid groundwork for later agreements such as the Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement of 1972. It is frequently cited as a model of peaceful, institutionalised resource sharing between neighbours.
