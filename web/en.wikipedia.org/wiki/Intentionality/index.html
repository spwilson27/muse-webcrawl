Intentionality

Intentionality is the directedness of mental states toward objects.

Intentionality is the power of minds and mental states to be about, to represent, or to stand for, things, properties and states of affairs. To say of a mental state (such as a belief, desire, or perception) that it has intentionality is to say that it has a content, or is directed at an object. The term derives from the Latin intendere, "to stretch toward", and should not be confused with "intention" in the ordinary sense of a purpose or plan.

History

The concept has roots in medieval scholastic philosophy (intentio) and was revived by Franz Brentano in his Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint (1874), where he proposed intentionality — the "intentional inexistence" of an object in every mental phenomenon — as the mark of the mental, distinguishing psychological from physical phenomena. Brentano's student Edmund Husserl made intentionality central to phenomenology, analysing how consciousness constitutes its objects. Through Husserl and later analytic philosophers it became a core topic in the philosophy of mind.

Theories

Philosophers debate whether intentionality can be naturalised — explained in physical, causal or biological terms. Representational theories, associated with Fred Dretske and Ruth Millikan, ground content in causal-informational relations or in the biological function of representation-producing systems. John Searle argued that intentionality is intrinsic to minds and only derived in language and symbols, pressing the point with his Chinese room argument against computational theories of mind. Others, such as Daniel Dennett, treat intentionality as a stance useful for predicting behaviour rather than an intrinsic property.

A classic puzzle is intentional inexistence in the technical sense: thoughts can be about objects that do not exist, such as unicorns or Sherlock Holmes, raising the question of what such thoughts relate to. Intentionality is also linked to the "hard problem" of consciousness and to debates about whether artificial systems can possess genuine understanding.
