Plantigrade

In terrestrial animals, plantigrade locomotion means walking with the toes and metatarsals flat on the ground. It is one of three forms of digit locomotion in mammals, the others being digitigrade (walking on the toes, as in dogs and cats) and unguligrade (walking on the nails of the toes, as in horses and deer). The word "plantigrade" derives from the Latin planta (sole of the foot) and gradi (to step or walk).

Characteristics

The primary advantages of a plantigrade posture are stability and weight-bearing capacity: because the whole sole contacts the ground, weight is distributed over a large area, providing a stable platform for standing — a necessity for animals that rear up, such as bears surveying their surroundings or manipulating food. The plantigrade foot also offers greater versatility for grasping and climbing, which is why it is retained in many arboreal and scansorial mammals, including primates.

The trade-off is speed. Digitigrade and unguligrade limbs effectively lengthen the leg by raising the ankle (or wrist) off the ground, adding an extra segment to the stride and elastic energy storage in tendons. Plantigrade animals are correspondingly slower over open ground; few are built for sustained running, though some — famously humans — compensate with exceptional endurance adaptations, and bears can gallop surprisingly fast over short distances.

Distribution

Plantigrady is regarded as the ancestral condition for mammals: the earliest mammals and their synapsid forebears walked with the sole flat. Examples of plantigrade or semi-plantigrade mammals include:

  • Primates, including humans, whose plantigrade hind feet underpin bipedal standing and walking;
  • Bears (Ursidae), which walk flat-footed and can stand fully upright;
  • Raccoons, skunks and other procyonids and musteloids;
  • Opossums and many other marsupials;
  • Many rodents, hedgehogs and other small mammals;
  • Wolverines and other mustelids that are semi-plantigrade.

Some animals are plantigrade in the hind feet but digitigrade in the front, and many switch posture with gait: at a walk the sole is planted, while at a run the weight shifts forward onto the toes.

Human anatomy

In humans, plantigrade structure — a prominent heel bone (calcaneum), arched midfoot and short toes — is integral to habitual bipedalism. The foot acts first as a shock absorber (heel strike and arch compression) and then as a rigid lever for push-off. Developmental flat feet, high arches and gait disorders are all clinically framed against this plantigrade baseline. In common usage "plantigrade" is occasionally misapplied to footwear or posture in which the heel touches the ground first.
