Nina Simone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Nina Simone at the piano in 1965: high priestess in a dark dress, mid-performance, gaze level at the audience]

Nina Simone (born Eunice Kathleen Waymon; 21 February 1933 – 21 April 2003) was an American singer, pianist, songwriter and civil rights activist. Classically trained and aiming at Carnegie Hall, she was diverted by economics into Atlantic City bars — taking her stage name to hide the work from her preacher mother — and fused Bach, blues, gospel and jazz into a style no category holds. They called her the High Priestess of Soul.

Her recordings include 'I Loves You, Porgy' (1958), 'My Baby Just Cares for Me' (recorded 1957, a UK hit thirty years later), 'Mississippi Goddam' (1964), written in fury after the Birmingham church bombing and the murder of Medgar Evers, 'Four Women' (1966), 'To Be Young, Gifted and Black' (1969) and her readings of 'Strange Fruit', 'Feeling Good' and 'Sinnerman'.

Classical dreams and the movement

Born in Tryon, North Carolina, the sixth child of a Methodist preacher, Simone was a church pianist at three and gave a classical recital at twelve (her parents were moved from the front row for a white couple; she refused to play until they were restored — her first protest, as she told it). Juilliard summer study and a failed Curtis Institute audition — which she believed, not without basis, was racial — rerouted her to the clubs. In the 1960s her music became the movement's conscience, at real cost to her commercial career.

Exile and return

Disillusioned with America, Simone lived in Barbados, Liberia, Switzerland and France, her later years shadowed by bipolar disorder (diagnosed in the 1980s) and financial chaos. A late renaissance — the 1987 Chanel advertisement that revived 'My Baby Just Cares for Me', festival triumphs, an honorary diploma from Curtis two days before her death in 2003 — restored her public. Documentaries and samples keep her voice in the present tense.
