A synchrotron light source is a facility that produces extremely bright beams of synchrotron radiation — electromagnetic radiation emitted when relativistic charged particles, usually electrons, are deflected by magnetic fields. The radiation, spanning infrared through hard X-rays, is delivered to experimental stations called beamlines, where it is used for research in physics, chemistry, biology, materials science, medicine, and engineering.

How it works

Electrons are generated and accelerated in a linac and booster synchrotron, then injected into a storage ring where they circulate for hours at nearly the speed of light. Bending magnets keep the beam on its closed orbit and emit radiation tangentially; insertion devices — undulators and wigglers — force the beam into a wiggly path to produce much brighter, sometimes quasi-monochromatic, radiation. Beamlines transport the light through optics (mirrors, monochromators) to end-stations. Modern "third-generation" sources are optimized for insertion devices, while "fourth-generation" diffraction-limited storage rings, such as MAX IV in Sweden and the ESRF's Extremely Brilliant Source, achieve far higher brilliance using multi-bend achromat lattices.

Properties and techniques

Synchrotron light is intense (many orders of magnitude brighter than laboratory X-ray tubes), collimated, pulsed, polarized, and tunable in energy. Techniques include X-ray crystallography of proteins and materials, X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), small-angle scattering, photoemission spectroscopy, X-ray microscopy and tomography, and macromolecular crystallography, which has determined the structures of ribosomes, viruses, and thousands of drug targets.

History

Synchrotron radiation was first observed in 1947 at a General Electric synchrotron. "First-generation" sources were parasitic on particle-physics accelerators in the 1960s–70s; dedicated second-generation rings such as the NSLS and Daresbury SRS followed in the 1980s; large third-generation facilities — ESRF (France), APS (United States), SPring-8 (Japan) — opened in the 1990s. Today roughly 50 synchrotron light sources operate worldwide, alongside complementary free-electron lasers, which produce even brighter, coherent pulses.
