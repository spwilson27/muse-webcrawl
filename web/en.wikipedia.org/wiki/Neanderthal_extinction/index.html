Neanderthal extinction

The Neanderthal extinction refers to the disappearance of Neanderthals (Homo neanderthalensis) from the fossil and archaeological record roughly 40,000 years ago, following a long period of coexistence and interaction with anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens) in Europe and western Asia. The extinction is one of the most studied events in human evolution, and its causes remain debated.

EventExtinction of Neanderthals
Datec. 42,000–40,000 years ago
RegionEurope and western Asia
Possible causesClimate change, competition, disease, assimilation, small population size

Timeline

Neanderthals evolved in Europe and Asia from earlier populations such as Homo heidelbergensis by about 400,000 years ago. Anatomically modern humans began dispersing out of Africa in significant numbers around 60,000–50,000 years ago and reached Europe by roughly 45,000 years ago. The two populations overlapped for several thousand years. The last secure dates for Neanderthal occupation cluster around 40,000 years ago, although claims of later survival in refugia such as Gibraltar have been proposed and debated.

Hypotheses

Climate instability

The period of the extinction coincided with severe climate oscillations, including Heinrich events and the Campanian Ignimbrite eruption around 39,000 years ago, which may have stressed small, fragmented Neanderthal populations and the ecosystems they depended on.

Competition with modern humans

Modern humans may have outcompeted Neanderthals through larger social networks, more efficient technology, symbolic culture, and higher population densities. Even a slight demographic or reproductive advantage, sustained over millennia, could have driven Neanderthals to extinction.

Assimilation and interbreeding

Genetic evidence shows that Neanderthals interbred with modern humans: present-day non-African genomes carry roughly 1–4% Neanderthal-derived ancestry. Some researchers therefore argue that Neanderthals were partly absorbed into expanding modern human populations rather than simply replaced.

Disease and other factors

Other proposed contributors include novel diseases carried by incoming humans, violence, inbreeding and genetic load in small populations, and the loss of genetic diversity. Most current models combine several of these factors rather than invoking a single cause.

Legacy

Neanderthal DNA survives in modern human populations, influencing traits such as immune function, skin and hair characteristics, and susceptibility to certain diseases. The extinction is thus not absolute: a small fraction of the Neanderthal genome persists in living people.
