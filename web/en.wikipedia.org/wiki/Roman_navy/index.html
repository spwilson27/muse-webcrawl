Roman navy

The Roman navy (Latin: Classis, lit. "fleet") comprised the naval forces of the ancient Roman state. Rome, long an essentially land power, built large war fleets when the Punic Wars forced it to challenge Carthage at sea, and for centuries afterwards Roman fleets policed the Mediterranean — the "mare nostrum" — and the empire's great rivers.

The Punic Wars

At the outbreak of the First Punic War (264–241 BC), Rome had little naval tradition. According to Polybius, the Romans copied a wrecked Carthaginian quinquereme and built a fleet of over a hundred warships, adding the corvus, a spiked boarding bridge that turned sea battles into infantry fights at which Roman marines excelled. Victories at Mylae (260 BC) and the Aegates Islands (241 BC) won Sicily for Rome. Rome lost entire fleets to storms — hundreds of ships and tens of thousands of men — yet rebuilt them repeatedly. In the Second Punic War the fleet blockaded and raided, and later fleets supported campaigns in the Greek east, defeating the navies of Macedon and the Seleucids.

Civil wars and the Empire

Naval power was decisive in Rome's civil wars: Sextus Pompey used his fleet to strangle Rome's grain supply until Agrippa defeated him at Naulochus (36 BC), and Octavian's victory over Mark Antony and Cleopatra at Actium (31 BC) was a sea battle. Under the Empire, Augustus established permanent standing fleets. The two praetorian fleets, the Classis Misenensis at Misenum and the Classis Ravennatis at Ravenna, guarded Italy, while provincial fleets — in Syria, Egypt, the Black Sea (Pontica), Britain (Britannica), and on the Rhine (Germanica) and Danube (Pannonica and Moesica) — patrolled frontiers, escorted grain shipments, and supported army operations. Pliny the Elder commanded the Misenum fleet and died during the eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79 while attempting a rescue.

Ships and personnel

The classic Roman warship was the quinquereme, rowed by some 300 oarsmen in three banks with multiple men per oar, carrying marines, artillery (ballistae), and a bronze ram at the waterline. Lighter liburnians, adapted from Illyrian pirate craft, became the standard imperial warship. Fleets also included transports for troops, horses, and supplies. Sailors (milites classiarii) were recruited largely from the freeborn provincial poor and freedmen, served 26 years, and could gain citizenship on discharge; naval service ranked below the legions in prestige.

Decline

As imperial frontiers stabilised, fleets shrank to coastguard and transport roles. In the third century AD Gothic seaborne raids exposed the weakness of Roman sea power, and Germanic peoples such as the Vandals — who seized North Africa and sacked Rome from the sea in 455 — turned naval warfare against the empire in the west. The eastern empire retained significant fleets, later famous for Greek fire, into the Byzantine period.
