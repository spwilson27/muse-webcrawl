L'Enfant Plan

The L'Enfant Plan for the city of Washington is the urban plan developed in 1791 by Major Pierre (Peter) Charles L'Enfant for George Washington, the first president of the United States, for the capital city of the United States. Prepared under the Residence Act of 1790, the plan laid out a new federal city in the District of Columbia on the Potomac River.

Design

The plan combined a rectilinear street grid with broad diagonal avenues radiating from focal points, an approach influenced by Baroque city planning and the gardens of Versailles. The two principal focal points were the "Congress House" (the Capitol) on Jenkins Hill and the "President's House" (the White House), connected by Pennsylvania Avenue. Avenues were named for the states, and numbered and lettered streets ran east–west and north–south respectively. The plan reserved a "Grand Avenue"—the future National Mall—and sites for public buildings, squares, markets, and a national church and pantheon-like structures.

Implementation and legacy

L'Enfant was dismissed in 1792 after conflicts with the commissioners, and his assistant Andrew Ellicott revised and published the plan; the city nevertheless grew largely according to L'Enfant's vision. A century later, the McMillan Plan of 1901–1902 revived and completed key elements of the design, clearing the Mall and establishing the monumental core. The original manuscript plan is held by the Library of Congress, and the L'Enfant Plan is protected in the National Register of Historic Places, with height limits and vista protections preserving its character.
