Aldosterone is the principal mineralocorticoid steroid hormone. It is produced by the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex and plays a central role in regulating blood pressure and electrolyte balance by controlling the reabsorption of sodium and the excretion of potassium in the kidneys.

Function

Aldosterone acts mainly on the distal tubules and collecting ducts of the nephron. Binding to mineralocorticoid receptors in principal cells, it increases the number of epithelial sodium channels and sodium–potassium pumps, so that more sodium (and, osmotically, water) is reabsorbed into the blood while potassium and hydrogen ions are secreted into the urine. The result is an increase in blood volume and blood pressure.

Regulation

Secretion is governed chiefly by the renin–angiotensin system: when the kidneys sense low blood pressure or low sodium delivery, renin release leads to formation of angiotensin II, which directly stimulates aldosterone production. High plasma potassium also stimulates secretion independently. Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) has only a minor, short-term effect.

Clinical significance

Excess aldosterone (hyperaldosteronism) causes hypertension and low potassium; its most common cause is an adrenal adenoma (Conn's syndrome) or bilateral adrenal hyperplasia, and it is now recognised as a frequent cause of secondary hypertension. Deficiency occurs in Addison's disease, producing low blood pressure and high potassium. Aldosterone-blocking drugs — the mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists spironolactone and eplerenone — are important treatments for hypertension and heart failure.

Aldosterone was isolated and characterised in 1953 by Sylvia Simpson and James Tait, working with Tadeusz Reichstein.
