Supreme Court of the United Kingdom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Supreme Court of the United Kingdom is the final court of appeal in the United Kingdom for all civil cases, and for criminal cases in England, Wales and Northern Ireland (Scotland's High Court of Justiciary remains final for Scottish criminal matters). Established by the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 and sitting since October 2009 in the Middlesex Guildhall in Parliament Square, it replaced the Appellate Committee of the House of Lords — removing the Law Lords from the legislature and completing the separation of the judiciary from the other branches that the old arrangement visibly breached.

Role

The Court hears appeals on points of law of general public importance, decides devolution questions (the competences of the Scottish, Welsh and Northern Irish institutions), and issues declarations of incompatibility under the Human Rights Act 1998 — influential rulings that do not strike down statutes, preserving parliamentary sovereignty in form. Its twelve justices (including its President) are selected by independent commissions. The Court entered national political consciousness with the Miller judgments (2017: parliamentary approval required to trigger Brexit notification; 2019: the prorogation of Parliament was unlawful and void) — decisions defended as orthodox legal principle and attacked as political intervention, crystallising the UK debate about judicial power without a codified constitution.
