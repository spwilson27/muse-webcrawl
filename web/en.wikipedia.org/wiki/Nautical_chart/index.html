Nautical chart

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nautical chart of a coastal area.

A nautical chart or hydrographic chart is a graphic representation of a sea region or water body and adjacent coastal regions. Depending on the scale of the chart, it may show depths of water (bathymetry) and heights of land (topography), natural features of the seabed, details of the coastline, navigational hazards, locations of natural and human-made aids to navigation, information on tides and currents, local details of the Earth's magnetic field, and human-made structures such as harbours, buildings, and bridges. Nautical charts are essential for safe navigation by mariners.

Conventional nautical charts are printed on large sheets of paper at a variety of scales. Mariners generally also carry navigational publications, such as sailing directions, tide tables, notices to mariners, coast pilots and almanacs. Increasingly, charts are produced and used as electronic navigational charts displayed on ECDIS systems.

History

Charting a sea area was one of the earliest applications of surveying. Early navigational charts were produced by Mediterranean pilots; the portolan charts of the thirteenth century showed coastlines, ports and rhumb lines radiating from compass roses. Systematic hydrographic surveying expanded in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and national hydrographic offices — such as the United Kingdom Hydrographic Office (founded 1795) and the United States Coast Survey — began producing standardised charts. The International Hydrographic Organization (IHO), founded in 1921, coordinates standards for chart production worldwide.

Features

Nautical charts include a wide range of information:

  • Depths — soundings in metres or feet/fathoms, referenced to a chart datum, usually a low-water level such as lowest astronomical tide, together with depth contours.
  • Hazards — rocks, wrecks, shoals, reefs, obstructions and prohibited or restricted areas.
  • Aids to navigation — positions and characteristics of lights, buoys, beacons, leading lines and radio aids.
  • Coastline and land features — shore details, conspicuous landmarks, harbours, anchorages and pilot boarding areas.
  • Tidal and current information — tidal stream atlases and diamonds giving the direction and rate of tidal streams.
  • Magnetic information — compass roses showing magnetic variation and its annual change.
  • Seabed quality — the nature of the bottom (sand, mud, rock, etc.), important for anchoring.

Projections and scales

Most nautical charts use the Mercator projection, on which a line of constant bearing (a rhumb line) appears as a straight line, simplifying course plotting. Gnomonic charts, on which great circles appear straight, are used for ocean passage planning at high latitudes. Charts are classified by scale into ocean, general, coastal, approach, harbour and berthing charts.

Chart corrections

Because channels shift, new hazards are found and aids to navigation change, charts must be kept up to date using Notices to Mariners issued by hydrographic offices. Paper charts are corrected by hand; electronic charts are updated with digital update files.

Electronic charts

The Electronic Chart Display and Information System (ECDIS) uses official Electronic Navigational Charts (ENCs) produced to the IHO S-57/S-101 standards. Raster navigational charts (RNCs) are scanned images of paper charts. Since 2012, ECDIS carriage has been progressively mandated for SOLAS vessels.
