The Battle of Maipú (Spanish: Batalla de Maipú) was fought near Santiago, Chile, on 5 April 1818 between the Patriot forces of the United Liberation Army — the combined Army of the Andes and Chilean troops, commanded by José de San Martín — and the Spanish Royal Army under General Mariano Osorio. The decisive Patriot victory destroyed the last major Spanish field force in Chile and effectively secured Chilean independence, making Maipú one of the landmark battles of the Spanish American wars of independence.

Background

After crossing the Andes in 1817, San Martín had defeated the Royalists at Chacabuco and proclaimed Chilean independence, but a Spanish counteroffensive from Peru under Osorio retook much of the country. On the night of 19 March 1818, the Patriots were surprised and routed at the Second Battle of Cancha Rayada, and Bernardo O'Higgins was wounded. San Martín regrouped his shaken army on the plain of Maipú, southwest of Santiago, while reinforcements and new recruits rebuilt its strength to about 5,000 men. Osorio advanced on the capital with a similar number, confident after Cancha Rayada.

The battle

On the morning of 5 April, San Martín deployed on high ground along the Loma Blanca ridge, placing his artillery to command the field. The battle opened around midday. The Patriot right wing under Juan Gregorio de las Heras advanced against the Royalist left, while the Patriot left struggled against the strong Royalist Burgos battalion. San Martín committed his reserve at the critical moment; the Patriot cavalry and infantry broke the Royalist line, and O'Higgins — still recovering from his wound — arrived on the field with militia reinforcements as the Royalist army collapsed. The Royalists lost some 2,000 killed and more than 2,200 captured, including most of their artillery and baggage; Osorio escaped with a small escort. Patriot losses were about 1,000 killed and wounded.

According to tradition, after the battle the wounded O'Higgins greeted San Martín with the famous embrace of Maipú, exclaiming "Glory to the saviour of Chile!" San Martín replied, "Chile will never forget your illustrious name."

Consequences

Maipú ended organised Spanish resistance in central Chile; the remaining Royalists withdrew south and were reduced in the subsequent campaigns in the south of the country. With Chile secured, San Martín could proceed with his plan to attack the viceregal stronghold of Peru by sea, leading to the Chilean naval campaign under Lord Cochrane and the liberation expedition of 1820. A votive church, the Templo Votivo de Maipú, and a museum now stand on the battlefield, and the battle is commemorated as a defining moment in Chilean national identity.
