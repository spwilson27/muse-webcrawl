Sherry

Sherry (Spanish: Jerez) is a fortified wine made from white grapes grown near the city of Jerez de la Frontera in Andalusia, Spain. It is produced in a variety of styles, from bone-dry and pale to dark and sweet, and is protected by Spanish denominación de origen law and EU PDO status.

Production

The principal grape is Palomino, with Pedro Ximénez and Moscatel used for sweet wines. After fermentation the wine is fortified with grape spirit and aged in the solera system — a fractional blending cascade of barrels in which younger wine gradually refreshes older, so the bottled product is a consistent average of many vintages. Two great families of style emerge: wines aged under a veil of yeast called flor (Fino and Manzanilla — the latter from coastal Sanlúcar de Barrameda), which stay pale and dry, and wines aged oxidatively (Amontillado begins under flor then oxidises; Oloroso is fortified higher so flor cannot form, and darkens with age). Sweetened and naturally sweet styles include Pale Cream, Medium, Cream and the raisined Pedro Ximénez.

History

Winemaking around Jerez goes back to the Phoenicians; the region supplied wine to Britain for centuries — Shakespeare's Falstaff praises "sherris sack" — and the very name "sherry" is an English corruption of "Jerez". British merchant houses (Osborne, González Byass, Harvey's of Bristol) shaped the modern trade. The governing body is the Consejo Regulador in Jerez. Sherry is traditionally served chilled in a small tulip-shaped copita glass and is experiencing a revival among sommeliers as a food wine.
