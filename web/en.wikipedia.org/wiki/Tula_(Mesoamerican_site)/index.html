Tula (Mesoamerican site)

Tula (Nahuatl: Tollan) is a Mesoamerican archaeological site in the present-day Mexican state of Hidalgo, about 75 kilometres north of Mexico City. It was the capital of the Toltec culture and one of the largest cities of ancient Mexico, flourishing roughly between 900 and 1150 CE.

The site

The ceremonial centre is dominated by Pyramid B, topped by massive basalt warrior statues known as the Atlantes, each about 4.6 metres tall, which once supported a temple roof. Nearby stand the Burnt Palace (Palacio Quemado), with colonnaded halls and benches carved with processions of warriors, two ball courts, and Pyramid C. A carved frieze, the Coatepantli or serpent wall, depicts rattlesnakes devouring human skeletons.

History

Tula rose after the decline of Teotihuacan and at its height may have had 30,000–60,000 inhabitants. Toltec traditions recorded later by the Aztecs associated Tula with the priest-king Topiltzin Ce Ácatl Quetzalcóatl. The city declined and was burned in the 12th century. Its art and architecture strongly influenced later centres such as Chichén Itzá and Tenochtitlan.
