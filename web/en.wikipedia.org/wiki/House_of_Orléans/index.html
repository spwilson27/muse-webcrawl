The House of Orléans (French: Maison d'Orléans) is a cadet branch of the House of Bourbon, itself a branch of the Capetian dynasty. Founded by Philippe I, Duke of Orléans, younger brother of Louis XIV, the family frequently stood closest to the throne among the princes of the blood, supplied a regent of France, and finally a king, Louis Philippe I, who reigned during the July Monarchy of 1830–1848.

Under the Ancien Régime

Philippe I (1640–1701), known as Monsieur, received the duchy of Orléans and the Palais Royal. His son Philippe II served as Regent of France (1715–1723) for the young Louis XV, governing from the Palais Royal. Later dukes cultivated an image of fashionable, liberal opposition at their Parisian court; Louis Philippe II ("Philippe Égalité") voted for the death of Louis XVI during the Revolution and was himself guillotined in 1793.

The July Monarchy

Philippe Égalité's son Louis Philippe, after decades of exile, was proclaimed "King of the French" following the July Revolution of 1830. His bourgeois July Monarchy combined parliamentary government with censorship and was overthrown by the Revolution of 1848. The family went into exile in England.

Pretenders and modern branches

Orléanist claimants to the French throne descend from Louis Philippe; the current head is Jean, Count of Paris (born 1965), who assumed the position in 2019. Cadet lines include the Brazilian Imperial family (Orléans-Braganza), descended from Gaston d'Orléans, Count of Eu, and the Spanish dukes of Galliera. The Orléanist succession dispute with Legitimist and Bonapartist claimants has continued into the Fifth Republic era, though purely as a matter of dynastic sentiment.
