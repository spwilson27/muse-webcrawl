Selenocysteine

Selenocysteine (symbol Sec or U, in older publications also Se-Cys) is an amino acid that is the selenium analogue of cysteine: it has the same structure except that a selenium atom replaces the usual sulfur atom. It is often called the 21st proteinogenic amino acid because it is incorporated into proteins during translation, although it is not encoded directly in the standard genetic code table.

Biosynthesis and encoding

Selenocysteine is encoded by the codon UGA, which normally functions as a stop codon. Recoding of UGA as selenocysteine requires a specific stem-loop structure in the mRNA — the SECIS element (selenocysteine insertion sequence) — together with a dedicated elongation factor and a special tRNA, tRNASec. The amino acid is synthesised on its own tRNA: serine is first attached to tRNASec and then enzymatically converted to selenocysteine using selenium donated by selenophosphate. Because of this elaborate machinery, all organisms that use selenocysteine possess a recognisable set of dedicated genes.

Selenoproteins

Proteins containing selenocysteine are called selenoproteins. They occur in all three domains of life, though many lineages, including most fungi and higher plants, have lost them. Humans have 25 selenoprotein genes. Well-known examples include the glutathione peroxidases, which protect cells against oxidative damage; the thioredoxin reductases, which maintain redox balance; and the iodothyronine deiodinases, which activate and deactivate thyroid hormones. The selenol group of selenocysteine is more reactive and more readily oxidised and reduced than the thiol of cysteine, making it an excellent catalytic residue for redox enzymes, and it is almost always essential for the enzyme's activity.

Discovery and naming

The amino acid was identified in the 1970s through work by Thressa Stadtman and others on bacterial enzymes such as formate dehydrogenase and on mammalian glutathione peroxidase, which was shown to contain selenium stoichiometrically. The IUPAC/IUBMB three-letter symbol Sec and one-letter symbol U were formally recommended for use in sequence databases.
