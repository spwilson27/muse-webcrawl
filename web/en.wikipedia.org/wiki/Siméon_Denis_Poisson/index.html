Siméon Denis Poisson (21 June 1781 – 25 April 1840) was a French mathematician and physicist who worked on statistics, complex analysis, partial differential equations, the calculus of variations, analytical mechanics, electricity and magnetism, thermodynamics, elasticity, and fluid mechanics. His name is attached to an unusually wide range of concepts, and he is one of the 72 scientists whose names are inscribed on the Eiffel Tower.

Poisson, whose work spans probability, potential theory, and elasticity.

Life

Poisson was born in Pithiviers and entered the École Polytechnique in Paris in 1798, where he impressed Lagrange and Laplace and was exempted from examinations. He became a professor there while still in his twenties and spent his career at Parisian institutions, including the Faculté des Sciences and the Bureau des Longitudes. A prolific author of more than 300 works, he famously declared that "life is good for only two things: doing mathematics and teaching it."

Contributions

In probability, his Recherches sur la probabilité des jugements (1837) introduced the Poisson distribution and applied probability to judicial decisions; the distribution also models rare random events and arises as a limit of the binomial distribution. In potential theory, Poisson's equation ∇²φ = −4πρ generalises Laplace's equation to regions containing mass or charge. In mechanics he introduced the Poisson bracket of Hamiltonian dynamics — later fundamental to quantum mechanics in the form of commutators — and in elasticity Poisson's ratio, the ratio of transverse to axial strain. His name also attaches to the Poisson kernel, the Poisson summation formula, and Poisson regression.

The Poisson spot

Poisson is also remembered for an unintended triumph of wave optics: as a sceptic on the Académie committee judging Fresnel's wave theory of light, he showed that the theory predicted a bright spot at the centre of a circular obstacle's shadow, which he presented as a reductio ad absurdum. Arago promptly observed the spot experimentally — now called the Poisson (or Arago) spot — providing striking confirmation of the wave theory.
