Energy in Japan

Energy infrastructure in Japan

Energy in Japan describes energy production, consumption and policy in Japan. Japan has very limited domestic fossil fuel resources and imports almost all of its oil, coal and liquefied natural gas (LNG), making energy security a defining theme of its policy. Before 2011, nuclear power supplied about 30 percent of Japan's electricity; after the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster, the reactor fleet was shut down, fossil fuel imports surged, and renewables — especially solar — expanded rapidly.

Electricity

Japan's grid is unusual in being split between two frequencies: 50 Hz in the east (Tokyo and northward) and 60 Hz in the west, a legacy of early equipment purchases from Germany and the United States, which limits power transfers between regions. Supply is dominated by ten regional utilities, although retail markets were fully liberalised in 2016.

LNG and coal provide the largest shares of generation, followed by solar, hydro and a recovering nuclear share as reactors are restarted under post-Fukushima safety standards — by the mid-2020s roughly a dozen reactors had returned to service. Japan is one of the world's largest LNG importers and a major market for Australian and Middle Eastern energy.

Policy

Japan's Strategic Energy Plans set targets for the generation mix — the sixth plan (2021) aimed for renewables of 36–38 percent and nuclear of 20–22 percent by 2030 — and Japan has pledged carbon neutrality by 2050, promoting hydrogen and ammonia co-firing under its "Green Transformation" (GX) programme. High dependence on imported fuels makes Japan's trade balance and electricity prices sensitive to global energy prices, as seen after 2022.

History

The 1973 and 1979 oil shocks drove Japan's push into nuclear power and LNG and made it a world leader in energy efficiency. The March 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami and the Fukushima accident reversed the nuclear expansion and reshaped Japanese energy politics for a generation.
