Antiferromagnetism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In materials that exhibit antiferromagnetism, the magnetic moments of atoms or molecules, usually related to the spins of electrons, align in a regular pattern with neighbouring spins pointing in opposite (antiparallel) directions. As a result, unlike ferromagnets, antiferromagnetic materials have no net macroscopic magnetization, even though they are magnetically ordered.

Theory and behaviour

The ordered state appears below a critical temperature called the Néel temperature, named after Louis Néel, who developed the theory in the 1930s and shared the 1970 Nobel Prize in Physics for work on antiferromagnetism and ferrimagnetism. Above the Néel temperature the material is paramagnetic. Magnetic susceptibility shows a characteristic peak at the transition and, unlike ferromagnetic susceptibility, remains small. The antiparallel ordering is usually explained by superexchange through intervening non-magnetic ions.

Materials and applications

Examples include the transition-metal oxides hematite, chromium(III) oxide, manganese(II) oxide and nickel(II) oxide, and the metal chromium. Antiferromagnets are essential in spintronics: in giant-magnetoresistance read heads, an antiferromagnetic layer "pins" an adjacent ferromagnetic layer by exchange bias. Because they produce no stray field and their spin dynamics are extremely fast (terahertz range), antiferromagnets are studied for future memory and computing devices.
