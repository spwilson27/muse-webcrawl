Minimum wage

[Image: a retail worker at a checkout till, among the occupations most affected by minimum wage law]

A minimum wage is the lowest hourly (or monthly) pay that employers may legally pay workers, set by statute or collective agreement. Most countries have one: examples include the US federal minimum of $7.25 an hour (unchanged since 2009, with many states and cities higher), the UK's National Living Wage and Germany's Mindestlohn (introduced 2015).

The economic debate

The elementary supply-and-demand model predicts that a binding minimum wage prices some low-skilled workers out of jobs. Against this, monopsony models — employers with wage-setting power — predict moderate minimums can raise both pay and employment, and influential empirical studies beginning with Card and Krueger (1994) found little employment loss from modest increases; subsequent literature finds small and contested effects, varying with level and place. Debate therefore centres on how high, relative to median wages, a floor can go before disemployment dominates, and on interactions with in-work benefits and the gig economy. Minimum wages are among the most politically charged instruments in labour economics.
