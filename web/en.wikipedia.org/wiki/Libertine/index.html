Libertine

A libertine is a person, especially a man, who behaves without moral principles or a sense of responsibility, in particular one who disregards sexual restraints. The word derives from the Latin libertinus, meaning a freedman — a manumitted slave — and passed through French into English in the late 14th century.

Originally the term carried no strongly negative sense, and in the 16th century it was applied to certain religious dissenters, such as the Anabaptist-adjacent "Libertines" opposed by John Calvin in Geneva. By the 17th and 18th centuries, however, the word had acquired its dominant modern meaning of a debauched, licentious person, particularly one given to sensual indulgence.

In literature and culture

The libertine became a stock figure in Restoration drama and in the fiction of the 18th century. Famous literary libertines include Don Juan, the legendary Spanish seducer; Valmont in Pierre Choderlos de Laclos's Les Liaisons dangereuses (1782); and the rake depicted in William Hogarth's painting series A Rake's Progress (1732–1734). The works of the Marquis de Sade, himself often described as a libertine, gave the figure a philosophical dimension, presenting libertinage as a rejection of conventional morality and religious authority.

In the philosophy of the Enlightenment, libertinism sometimes overlapped with freethinking: the French libertins érudits of the 17th century were scholars who questioned orthodox doctrine, and the term could denote intellectual independence as much as sensual license.

Related concepts

The figure of the libertine is related to the rake in English literature and to the broader phenomenon of hedonism. In modern usage the term is often used somewhat humorously or ironically, though it retains its association with disregard for sexual and social convention.
