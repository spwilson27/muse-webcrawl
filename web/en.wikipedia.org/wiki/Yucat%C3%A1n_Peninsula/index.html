The Yucatán Peninsula (Spanish: Península de Yucatán) is a large peninsula in southeastern Mexico and adjacent portions of Belize and Guatemala. It separates the Gulf of Mexico from the Caribbean Sea, with its northern coastline on the Yucatán Channel. The peninsula is composed almost entirely of limestone, and its flat karst terrain is famous for cenotes — natural sinkholes filled with groundwater that were sacred to the Maya.

Geology

The peninsula sits on the Yucatán Platform, a vast limestone shelf of marine origin. Because rainwater drains through the porous rock, there are almost no surface rivers; instead, underground river systems connect thousands of cenotes. The northwestern coast bears the trace of the Chicxulub impact crater, buried beneath the peninsula, formed by the asteroid impact 66 million years ago that is widely credited with causing the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs; the ring of cenotes around it follows the crater rim.

History

The peninsula was a heartland of the Maya civilisation, home to cities such as Chichén Itzá, Uxmal, Calakmul, and Tikal, and later to post-classic centres like Mayapán. Spanish conquest campaigns in the 1520s–1540s, led by Francisco de Montejo and his son, subdued the region only with difficulty. In the colonial and independence eras the Yucatán was known for henequen (sisal) plantations, and in 1847 the Maya rose in the long Caste War, briefly sustaining the independent state of Chan Santa Cruz in what is now Quintana Roo.

Modern era

Today the Mexican portion of the peninsula comprises the states of Yucatán, Campeche, and Quintana Roo. Tourism dominates the Caribbean coast — Cancún, Playa del Carmen, and Tulum along the "Riviera Maya" — while Mérida, the capital of Yucatán state, is the region's largest city and cultural centre. Campeche's offshore oil fields in the Bay of Campeche are among Mexico's most important sources of petroleum. The population includes a large proportion of Yucatec Maya speakers, and Maya language and traditions remain central to the peninsula's identity.
