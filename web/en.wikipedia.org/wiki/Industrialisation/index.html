Industrialisation

Industrialisation (or industrialization) is the period of social and economic change that transforms a human group from an agrarian society into an industrial society, involving the extensive reorganisation of an economy for the purpose of manufacturing. It entails the shift from manual and craft production to mechanised factory production, powered first by steam and later by electricity and internal combustion.

Industrialisation remade landscapes, cities and working life.

The first industrialisation

Historically, industrialisation is associated with the Industrial Revolution, which began in Britain in the late 18th century in the textile industry and spread through the 19th century to Western Europe and North America. Its hallmarks included the factory system, the growth of coal, iron and steel production, railways, rapid urbanisation, and a demographic transition as populations grew and moved into cities.

Later waves

A "second industrial revolution" from the late 19th century brought steel, chemicals, electricity and mass production. In the 20th century, the Soviet Union pursued forced-draft industrialisation under its Five-Year Plans, and after 1945 many newly independent states adopted import substitution strategies, while the East Asian "tiger" economies industrialised through export-oriented manufacturing. China's reforms after 1978 produced the largest and fastest industrialisation in history.

Effects

Industrialisation raises average incomes and life expectancy in the long run and transforms occupational structure, education and family life, but it has also been accompanied by harsh working conditions, urban squalor in its early phases, and heavy environmental costs — consequences that have given rise to labour movements, social reform, environmental regulation, and, in some wealthy economies, subsequent deindustrialization as production shifts to services or abroad.
