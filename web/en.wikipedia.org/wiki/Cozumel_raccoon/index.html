Cozumel raccoon

Cozumel raccoon
Scientific name: Procyon pygmaeus
Also known as: pygmy raccoon
Family: Procyonidae
Range: Cozumel Island, Mexico
Conservation status: Critically Endangered

The Cozumel raccoon (Procyon pygmaeus), also called the pygmy raccoon, is a critically endangered species of raccoon endemic to Cozumel Island, off the coast of Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula. It is a classic example of insular dwarfism: adults are markedly smaller than the common raccoon (Procyon lotor) of the mainland, typically weighing around 3–4 kg, with proportionally shorter fur and distinctive cranial and dental features.

Taxonomy and description

The species was described by Clinton Hart Merriam in 1901. Although it has sometimes been treated as a subspecies of the common raccoon, morphological and genetic studies support its recognition as a distinct species that has been isolated on Cozumel for thousands of years. Its coat is generally paler and more grizzled than that of mainland raccoons, and the black facial mask and ringed tail characteristic of raccoons are retained.

Distribution and habitat

The Cozumel raccoon is found only on Cozumel Island, where it favours mangrove forests and sandy coastal areas in the north-west of the island, though it also occurs in tropical forests and around the island's lagoons. It is an opportunistic omnivore, feeding heavily on crabs, supplemented by fruits, insects, small vertebrates, and other available food.

Conservation

The total population is estimated at only a few hundred mature individuals. Threats include habitat loss and fragmentation from tourism development, mortality on roads, hurricanes, disease and parasites transmitted by domestic animals, and predation by introduced species such as boas and feral dogs. The species is listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN and is protected under Mexican law; it is one of several endemic Cozumel mammals, alongside the Cozumel coati and the Cozumel harvest mouse, that are of high conservation concern.
