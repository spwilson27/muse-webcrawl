Can opener

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A can opener (also tin opener in British English) is a device used to open metal cans. Although preservation of food in cans began in the early 19th century, dedicated openers were not patented until decades later — early cans were made of thick iron and were opened with a hammer and chisel, as instruction labels of the period suggested.

History

The first can opener, using a lever and a claw-like blade, was patented by Ezra Warner of Connecticut in 1858; it was clumsy and largely confined to grocers' use. William Lyman's 1870 patent introduced the rotating cutting wheel, and the familiar wheel-and-key design followed. The bunker or "butterfly" opener with a turning handle was patented in the 1920s. Because canned rations were issued before convenient openers existed, the U.S. military developed the tiny P-38 can opener during the Second World War, later supplemented by the larger P-51.

Types

Manual openers fall into those that cut the top of the lid inside the rim, leaving a sharp edge, and side-cutting ("safety") openers that sever the lid below the rim for a smooth edge. Electric can openers, first popularised in the 1950s, perform the same operation with a motor. Many cans are now made with ring-pull tabs that require no opener at all.
