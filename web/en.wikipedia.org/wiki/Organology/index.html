Organology

Organology (from Greek: ὄργανον, organon, "instrument" and λόγος, logos, "study") is the science of musical instruments and their classifications. It embraces the history, construction, acoustics, and cultural uses of instruments in all cultures and periods. (In biology, the same word was once used for the study of organs, but that usage is now obsolete.)

History

Systematic descriptions of instruments date to works such as Sebastian Virdung's Musica getutscht (1511), Michael Praetorius's Syntagma Musicum (1619), and Marin Mersenne's Harmonie universelle (1636). In the nineteenth century, museum curators such as Victor-Charles Mahillon of the Brussels conservatory museum developed classification schemes for the world's instruments, and the field coalesced as an academic discipline closely allied with ethnomusicology.

The Hornbostel–Sachs system

In 1914 Erich Moritz von Hornbostel and Curt Sachs published their classification, modelled on the Dewey Decimal system, dividing instruments by the material that vibrates to produce sound: idiophones (the body of the instrument vibrates), membranophones (a stretched membrane), chordophones (strings), and aerophones (a column of air). Sachs later added electrophones (1940). Numerical suffixes describe subdivisions and playing methods, so that any instrument can be given a precise code. The system remains the standard in museums and reference works, though scholars have proposed revisions and culturally situated alternatives.

Scope

Modern organology covers instrument acoustics and craftsmanship, iconography of instruments in art, performance practice, conservation in museum collections, and the social meanings of instruments — from the politics of national instruments to the ergonomics of design.
