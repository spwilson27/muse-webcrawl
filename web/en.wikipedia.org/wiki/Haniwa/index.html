Haniwa (埴輪, "clay rings") are terracotta clay figures made for ritual and funerary use and buried with the dead in Japan during the Kofun period (3rd to 6th centuries AD). They were placed on top of and around the great keyhole-shaped burial mounds (kofun) of the elite.

Forms

The earliest haniwa were simple cylinders (entō haniwa), possibly stands for offering jars. Figurative haniwa (keishō haniwa) developed later and depict houses, boats, animals such as horses, deer and waterfowl, and human figures including warriors in armour, farmers, dancers and shamans. Some figures are over a metre tall and display remarkable detail in dress and equipment, making them an important source for Kofun-period material culture.

Origins and purpose

Haniwa developed from the pottery traditions of the preceding Yayoi period, particularly from special stands and jars used in funerary rituals in the Kibi region. Their purposes are debated: they may have served as retaining walls for the mound, as ritual objects, as markers demarcating sacred space, or as vessels for the soul of the deceased. A later legend, recorded in the Nihon Shoki, claimed they were substitutes for live human sacrifice, but archaeologists reject this account.
