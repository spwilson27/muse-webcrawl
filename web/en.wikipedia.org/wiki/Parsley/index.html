Parsley, or garden parsley (Petroselinum crispum), is a species of flowering plant in the family Apiaceae native to the central and eastern Mediterranean region, naturalised and widely cultivated worldwide as an herb, spice and vegetable. This biennial plant forms a rosette of bright green, tripinnate leaves in its first year and a flowering stem bearing small yellow-green flowers and seeds in its second. Two main cultivar groups dominate: curly-leaf parsley, used chiefly as a garnish, and flat-leaf (Italian) parsley, with a stronger flavour preferred in cooking; a third group, Hamburg parsley, is grown for its thick, parsnip-like root.

Culinary use

Parsley is a cornerstone herb of European, Middle Eastern and American cooking. It is essential to tabbouleh (where it is the bulk ingredient, not a garnish), gremolata, salsa verde, chimichurri and the French persillade and bouquet garni; finely chopped leaves are stirred into soups, sauces and grain dishes, and the stems flavour stocks. Dried parsley is comparatively flavourless. The leaves are rich in vitamins A, C and K, folate and iron, and contain the antioxidant apigenin; concentrated parsley oil and apiol have traditional medicinal uses and are toxic in large doses.

Cultivation

Parsley prefers moist, well-drained soil and full sun to partial shade. Germination is notoriously slow and erratic (two to six weeks) — folklore holds that the seed goes to the devil and back seven times before sprouting; soaking seeds overnight helps. Plants are usually grown as annuals, as leaves become coarse after flowering. Common pests include the carrot fly and aphids; parsley is a host plant for black swallowtail butterfly larvae.

History and folklore

The ancient Greeks associated parsley with death and funerals — it was said to spring from the blood of Archemorus — yet also crowned victors of the Isthmian Games with it; Romans used it as a breath freshener and table decoration. The name derives from Greek petroselinon, "rock celery". In medieval Europe parsley was a monastic garden staple and an emblem of feasting.
