Lobster-tailed pot helmet

The lobster-tailed pot helmet, also known as the zischägge (from German), is a type of combat helmet used by cavalry and some infantry in Europe during the seventeenth century, most famously by troopers in the English Civil Wars and the Thirty Years' War.

Description

The helmet consists of a rounded, one-piece iron skull or "pot", often with a broad brim at the front edge forming a peak. Its most distinctive feature is the defence for the back of the neck: a series of overlapping horizontal lames (plates) that articulate with one another and resemble the tail of a lobster, giving the helmet its name. Cheek pieces protect the sides of the face, and many examples include a face defence of one or three vertical bars, or a sliding nasal bar.

Origins and use

The design derived from Turkish and Eastern European helmets (the shishak), transmitted westward through Poland and Hungary, where similar helmets had been worn by hussars. In England the lobster-tailed pot became the standard headpiece of the harquebusier cavalry — Oliver Cromwell's "Ironsides" are typically depicted wearing it — and it was worn alongside a buff coat and cuirass as part of the characteristic cavalry equipment of the period.
