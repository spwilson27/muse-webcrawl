SOS response

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The SOS response is a global response of bacteria to DNA damage in which the cell cycle is arrested and DNA repair and mutagenesis are induced. It is a key mechanism by which bacteria such as Escherichia coli survive damage caused by ultraviolet light, chemicals and other stresses that stall DNA replication.

Mechanism

The response is controlled by two proteins: the repressor LexA and the sensor RecA. Under normal conditions, LexA binds to operator sequences ("SOS boxes") in the promoters of more than 40 genes and represses them. When DNA is damaged, single-stranded DNA accumulates at stalled replication forks; RecA binds this DNA and becomes activated (RecA*), acting as a co-protease that stimulates the self-cleavage of LexA. Falling LexA levels derepress the SOS regulon in a timed sequence determined by each operator's affinity for LexA.

Effects

Early-induced genes include uvrA and uvrB of the nucleotide excision repair pathway. If damage persists, later genes such as sulA (which inhibits cell division, causing filamentation) and the error-prone polymerases encoded by umuDC (DNA polymerase V) are expressed, enabling translesion synthesis at the cost of increased mutation — a last-resort survival strategy. When the damage is repaired, RecA activation ceases, LexA re-accumulates, and the response switches off. The SOS response, elucidated through work by Miroslav Radman, Evelyn Witkin and others, is also implicated in the development of antibiotic resistance and bacterial evolution under stress.
