Achalasia is a rare disorder of the oesophagus (esophagus) characterised by failure of the lower oesophageal sphincter (LES) to relax during swallowing and by loss of peristalsis in the distal oesophagus. The name derives from the Greek a- (not) + chalasis (relaxation). Food and liquid consequently accumulate in the oesophagus, which may become progressively dilated.

Barium swallow in achalasia showing a dilated oesophagus tapering to a "bird-beak" narrowing at the LES.

Signs and symptoms

The cardinal symptom is dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) for both solids and liquids — a feature distinguishing achalasia from most mechanical obstructions, which affect solids first. Patients commonly report regurgitation of undigested food, retrosternal chest pain, heartburn-like discomfort, coughing or aspiration at night, and weight loss. Symptoms typically develop insidiously over years.

Cause

Achalasia results from degeneration of ganglion cells of the myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus in the oesophageal wall, particularly the inhibitory neurons that release nitric oxide and vasoactive intestinal peptide. In most cases the cause is unknown and presumed autoimmune in susceptible individuals. A secondary form, pseudoachalasia, can occur in Chagas disease (infection with Trypanosoma cruzi) and with malignancy involving the gastro-oesophageal junction.

Diagnosis

High-resolution manometry is the gold standard, demonstrating incomplete LES relaxation and absent or abnormal peristalsis; the Chicago classification distinguishes three subtypes (I–III) with prognostic and therapeutic implications. Barium swallow shows the classic "bird-beak" or "rat-tail" tapering, and endoscopy is performed to exclude malignancy.

Treatment

No treatment restores peristalsis; therapy aims to reduce LES pressure. Options include pneumatic balloon dilation, laparoscopic Heller myotomy (usually with partial fundoplication), per-oral endoscopic myotomy (POEM), and endoscopic botulinum toxin injection, generally reserved for patients unfit for definitive therapy. Pharmacological agents such as calcium channel blockers and nitrates have limited, short-lived benefit.
