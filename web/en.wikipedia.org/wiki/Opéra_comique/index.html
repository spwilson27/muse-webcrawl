Opéra comique

Opéra comique
OriginParis, France, early 18th century
Defining featureSpoken dialogue alternating with sung numbers
Key composersGrétry, Boieldieu, Auber, Bizet, Offenbach
Main venueSalle Favart, Paris

Opéra comique is a genre of French opera that contains spoken dialogue and arias. It emerged in Paris in the early 18th century from the popular theatrical entertainments of the Parisian fairs, where comic plays with songs (vaudevilles) were performed in defiance of the monopolies of the Académie Royale de Musique and the Comédie-Française. Despite its name, opéra comique need not be comic or light in tone — works in the genre include tragedies such as Bizet's Carmen (1875), which was premiered at the Opéra Comique with spoken dialogue before recitatives were added after the composer's death.

History

The institution of the Opéra Comique was formally established in 1714 when fairground theatres gained the right to stage musical plays. In 1762 it merged with the Comédie-Italienne. Composers such as Philidor, Monsigny, and Grétry elevated the genre in the later 18th century, mixing sentiment and comedy. In the 19th century the Salle Favart became its home, and the repertoire ranged from the grand, sometimes serious works of Boieldieu (La dame blanche, 1825), Auber (Fra Diavolo, 1830), and Adam (Le postillon de Lonjumeau, 1836) to Offenbach's satirical works. The genre influenced the development of operetta and the German Singspiel.

Characteristics

The defining technical feature is the alternation of spoken dialogue with sung music, in contrast to the continuous recitative of grand opéra and tragédie lyrique. Stylistically, opéras comiques typically favour clear melodies, couplets, ensembles, and a mixture of comic and sentimental registers; 19th-century examples could run to three acts with large-scale finales and serious dramatic content, the "comique" referring to the company and its conventions rather than to humour.
