Effects of the 2008–2010 automotive industry crisis on the United States

The 2008–2010 automotive industry crisis formed part of the Great Recession and had severe effects on the United States automobile industry. Faced with falling sales, high fuel prices, and a credit crunch, General Motors and Chrysler required federal assistance, while Ford restructured without a government bailout.

Background

By 2008 the Detroit-based "Big Three" automakers had lost market share to foreign competitors and were heavily exposed to declining sales of trucks and SUVs as petrol prices rose. When the financial crisis struck, vehicle sales collapsed and credit for both manufacturers and dealers dried up, threatening the companies with insolvency and the loss of millions of jobs in manufacturing and the supply chain.

Government response

In late 2008 the federal government provided emergency loans through the Troubled Asset Relief Program. In 2009 the administration of President Barack Obama organised managed bankruptcies for General Motors and Chrysler. Chrysler was sold to a group led by the Italian manufacturer Fiat, while GM emerged from Chapter 11 bankruptcy in July 2009 with the United States Treasury as a major shareholder. The federal government also ran the Car Allowance Rebate System ("Cash for Clunkers") to stimulate sales.

Effects

The restructuring led to plant closures, the elimination of brands including Pontiac, Hummer, Saturn, and Saab, the closure of thousands of dealerships, and substantial job losses, particularly in Michigan and the wider Midwest. Concession agreements with the United Auto Workers reduced labour costs, and the industry returned to profitability in the following years as sales recovered.
