Live axle

A solid live axle on leaf springs

A live axle, also called a solid axle or beam axle, is a type of axle and suspension design in which the wheels on either side are connected by a rigid beam, and the axle also transmits driving power to the wheels. The differential and axle shafts are housed within the beam casing, so the whole assembly moves with the suspension as a single unit. The opposite of a live axle is a "dead axle", which carries load but transmits no power, as in an independent suspension.

Design

In a typical live-axle layout, the differential sits in a central housing ("pumpkin"), and semi-floating or full-floating axle shafts run inside tubes to each wheel. The axle is located by leaf springs, trailing arms with coil springs, or a linkage of the Hotchkiss drive type, in which the leaf springs both locate the axle and react to torque. Drive torque reaction and axle tramp are controlled by the springs or by dedicated links such as a Panhard rod and anti-roll bars.

Advantages and disadvantages

Live axles are simple, strong, and inexpensive, and they keep the wheels' camber constant relative to the road and to each other, maintaining a consistent contact patch under load. Ground clearance under the differential is unchanged by suspension compression. These properties make them durable under heavy loads and off-road abuse, and easy to service.

The disadvantages follow from the large unsprung mass of the whole axle assembly: ride quality and roadholding over bumps are poorer than with independent suspension, because a bump at one wheel disturbs the other, and wheel hop can occur under hard acceleration. Steering feel in a live front axle is also comparatively imprecise.

Applications

Live axles were once near-universal in cars and remain standard in heavy trucks, many pickup trucks and off-road vehicles (rear, and front in vehicles such as the Jeep Wrangler and Land Rover Defender classics), buses, and in drag racing, where strength and consistent traction matter most. Front-wheel-drive cars use dead beam axles (torsion beams) at the rear, which are not live axles because they transmit no power.
