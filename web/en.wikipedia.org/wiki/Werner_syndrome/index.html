Werner syndrome

Werner syndrome (WS) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterised by the premature appearance of features associated with normal ageing — a form of progeria. It is named after the German physician Otto Werner, who described the syndrome in his doctoral thesis of 1904, based on four siblings with cataracts and skin changes resembling accelerated ageing.

Signs and symptoms

Affected individuals develop normally through childhood but fail to undergo the adolescent growth spurt. From the second and third decades, signs of premature ageing accumulate: greying and loss of hair, hoarse voice, bilateral cataracts, type 2 diabetes, skin atrophy and ulceration (especially around the ankles), osteoporosis, atherosclerosis and an increased risk of cancers, including soft-tissue sarcomas and thyroid carcinoma. Life expectancy is reduced, typically to the late 40s or 50s, with death most often from cardiovascular disease or cancer.

Genetics

Werner syndrome is caused by loss-of-function mutations in the WRN gene on chromosome 8, which encodes a protein with both helicase and exonuclease activity involved in DNA replication, repair and telomere maintenance. Loss of WRN function leads to genomic instability and accelerated cellular senescence, making the syndrome an important model for the study of ageing. Diagnosis is clinical and confirmed by genetic testing; there is no cure, and management focuses on treating complications.
