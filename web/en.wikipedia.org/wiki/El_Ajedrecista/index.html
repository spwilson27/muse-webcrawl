El Ajedrecista

El Ajedrecista (Spanish: "The Chess Player") was a chess-playing automaton built by the Spanish engineer Leonardo Torres y Quevedo in 1912. It is generally recognised as the first true chess automaton and one of the earliest computer games: unlike the eighteenth-century Mechanical Turk, which concealed a human operator, El Ajedrecista played mechanically and automatically, using an electromechanical algorithm.

El Ajedrecista, the first chess-playing machine, built in 1912.

Operation

The machine played a limited endgame — king and rook against the human player's king — from any starting position, and checkmated reliably, though not always in the minimum number of moves. The pieces were plugged into sockets on the board, allowing the machine to sense their positions through electrical contacts; an internal mechanism computed the move according to a fixed algorithm and moved its pieces with an electromechanical arm. It detected illegal moves by the opponent, signalling them with a light, and announced check and checkmate with a gramophone recording.

History and significance

Torres y Quevedo demonstrated the automaton in Paris in 1914, and his son Gonzalo built an improved version with a moving magnet arm in 1920. The original machine is preserved at the Escuela Técnica Superior de Ingenieros de Caminos in Madrid. Historians of computing regard El Ajedrecista as a landmark: it embodied decision-making rules in machinery decades before electronic computers, and Torres's related work on automata theory and analogue calculating machines anticipated central ideas of computer science.
