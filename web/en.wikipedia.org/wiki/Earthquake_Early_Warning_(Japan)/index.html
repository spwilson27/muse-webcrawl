Earthquake Early Warning (Japan)

Earthquake Early Warning (緊急地震速報, Kinkyū Jishin Sokuhō) is a warning system operated by the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) that alerts the public when an earthquake is detected, seconds before strong shaking arrives. Japan's system, launched for the general public on 1 October 2007, is considered the most advanced nationwide earthquake early warning system in the world.

How it works

A network of more than a thousand seismometers across Japan detects the fast-moving but weak primary (P) waves generated by an earthquake. Computers rapidly estimate the hypocentre, magnitude, and expected intensity of the slower, destructive secondary (S) waves. When shaking of intensity 5-lower or greater on the Japanese seismic intensity scale is predicted, a warning is broadcast. Depending on the distance from the epicentre, the public may receive from a few seconds to more than a minute of advance notice — enough to take cover, stop trains, and shut down industrial equipment.

Distribution

Warnings are carried by television and radio broadcasters, mobile phone networks (through Japan's cell broadcast system and the J-Alert framework), public address systems, and dedicated receivers. Recipients hear a distinctive alert tone developed for NHK broadcasts. The shinkansen high-speed rail network operates its own detection system, which automatically brakes trains when strong shaking is anticipated.

Performance

The system performed notably during the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami, warning residents in Sendai roughly 15 seconds before strong shaking and Tokyo about a minute in advance. Limitations include difficulty with very large earthquakes, where magnitude estimates may saturate, and false alarms, such as the widely distributed false warning of August 2016.
