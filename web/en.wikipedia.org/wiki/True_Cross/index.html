True Cross

True Cross
Type:Christian relic
Associated with:Crucifixion of Jesus
Feast of discovery:3 May (historical); Exaltation 14 September

The True Cross is the cross on which, according to Christian tradition, Jesus was crucified. It is related by numerous historical accounts and legends, including the account of its discovery in Jerusalem in the 4th century by Saint Helena, the mother of the Roman emperor Constantine I. Relics said to be fragments of the True Cross became among the most venerated objects in Christendom.

The discovery by Helena

According to later Christian sources, Helena travelled to the Holy Land in about 326–328 AD and ordered excavations at the site of the Crucifixion, where three crosses were found near the tomb of Jesus. In one version of the legend, the true cross was identified by its miraculous healing of a sick woman, or by reviving a dead man. The Church of the Holy Sepulchre was built over the site. The feast of the Finding of the Holy Cross was traditionally celebrated on 3 May, while the Exaltation of the Cross on 14 September commemorates both the dedication of the church and the recovery of the cross from the Persians.

History of the relic

A large portion of the cross remained in Jerusalem, where it was displayed to pilgrims; silver reliquaries containing fragments were distributed widely. In 614 the cross was carried off to Ctesiphon after the Sasanian capture of Jerusalem, and was recovered by the emperor Heraclius in 628 and restored to Jerusalem. During the Crusades, a relic of the cross was carried into battle by the Kingdom of Jerusalem and was captured by Saladin at the Battle of Hattin in 1187, after which its fate is uncertain. Fragments were also taken to Constantinople and, after 1204, dispersed throughout western Europe.

Criticism and scholarship

During the Reformation, John Calvin famously remarked that the collected fragments of the True Cross would fill a ship's hold. Many churches nonetheless continue to claim fragments. In the 19th century, Charles Rohault de Fleury attempted to catalogue the known fragments and argued their total volume was far less than that of a complete cross. The authenticity of individual relics is generally considered unverifiable by historians.
