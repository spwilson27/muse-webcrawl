Field marshal

Field marshal
Type: Military rank (army)
NATO code: OF-10
Equivalent: Admiral of the fleet; Marshal of the air force
Symbol: Baton

Field marshal is the highest rank attainable in many armies, senior to a full general and equivalent to a five-star rank in the United States system. The title derives from the Old High German marahscalc, "keeper of the horses" — originally the official responsible for a king's cavalry and stables, whose duties grew into command of armies in the field.

History

The rank emerged in the armies of medieval and early modern Europe, notably in the Holy Roman Empire and France, where the Marshal of France became one of the great officers of the crown; Napoleon revived the dignity as Marshal of the Empire. In the German states the Generalfeldmarschall commanded armies in the Franco-Prussian War and both World Wars. Britain adopted the rank in the 18th century — the first British field marshal was the Earl of Orford in 1736 — and it was conferred on such figures as the Duke of Wellington and, in the Second World War, Montgomery and Alexander. British field marshals traditionally remain officers for life, and the rank is now conferred only rarely, chiefly on members of the royal family and chiefs of the defence staff. In India, the rank has been awarded only twice: to Sam Manekshaw (1973) and K. M. Cariappa (1986).

Insignia and privileges

The customary insignia is a baton, often ceremonially presented by the head of state; British insignia show crossed batons in a wreath of laurel beneath a crown. In the Soviet Union and Russia the analogous rank was Marshal of the Soviet Union (later Marshal of the Russian Federation). Many states have abolished the rank or let it fall into abeyance — the United States, for example, has no field marshals, using instead the five-star General of the Army.
