Potsticker

Potstickers (Chinese: 鍋貼, guōtiē, "pot stick") are a style of Chinese dumpling cooked by a combined pan-frying and steaming method: the dumplings — usually jiaozi filled with minced pork and cabbage or chives — are first fried in a little oil until their bottoms are crisp and golden, then water is added, the pan is covered to steam them through, and the water is allowed to evaporate so the bottoms crisp again.

Name and history

The English name is a calque of the Chinese guōtiē: according to a popular (probably apocryphal) story, a Song dynasty chef accidentally let boiled dumplings scorch in a wok, and served them anyway to the emperor's approval. In Japan the same preparation is called yaki gyōza, the most common way of serving gyōza, introduced from China after the Second World War. Korean gun-mandu are the analogous dish.

Preparation

Wheat-flour wrappers are filled, pleated and sealed along one edge so the dumpling has a flat bottom. They are stood upright in a hot oiled skillet or on a griddle, fried briefly, then water is poured in and a lid applied; when the water boils away, the dumplings are fried a moment more and inverted onto a plate so the browned bottoms face up. Street vendors in northern China often cook open-ended cylindrical versions on large flat griddles; in Shandong, potstickers are traditionally served with the crisped undersides up and accompanied by a thin soup.
