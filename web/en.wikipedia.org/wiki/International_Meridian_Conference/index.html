International Meridian Conference

The 1884 conference selected the meridian of Greenwich as the world's prime meridian.

The International Meridian Conference was a conference held in October 1884 in Washington, D.C., in the United States, to determine a prime meridian for international use. The conference was held at the request of US President Chester A. Arthur, and was attended by 41 delegates from 25 nations.

Proceedings

Before the conference, most maritime nations already used the meridian through the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, London, as their reference for charts, while land maps used various national meridians (Paris, Cádiz, and others). Delegates debated proposals for a single "neutral" meridian, such as the Azores or the Bering Strait, but practical considerations — including the fact that the great majority of the world's shipping already used Greenwich-based charts — favoured Greenwich.

Resolutions

The conference adopted seven resolutions. The most important recommended the adoption, as the initial meridian, of "the meridian passing through the centre of the transit instrument at the Observatory of Greenwich"; the vote was 22 to 1, with San Domingo voting against and France and Brazil abstaining. Other resolutions recommended counting longitude up to 180 degrees east and west from Greenwich, and the adoption of a universal day beginning at mean midnight at Greenwich — the basis of Greenwich Mean Time as a world standard. The resolutions were recommendations rather than binding law, and countries converted to the system over the following decades; France retained the Paris meridian for timekeeping until 1911.
