Giant cell arteritis

Giant cell arteritis (GCA), also called temporal arteritis, is an inflammatory autoimmune disease of large blood vessels. Symptoms may include headache, pain over the temples, flu-like symptoms, double vision, and difficulty opening the mouth. Complications can include blockage of the ophthalmic artery, leading to blindness, as well as aortic dissection and aortic aneurysm. GCA is frequently associated with polymyalgia rheumatica.

Specialty: Rheumatology, ophthalmology
Symptoms: Headache, temple pain, jaw claudication, visual loss
Usual onset: Age over 50
Treatment: High-dose glucocorticoids, tocilizumab

Cause and diagnosis

The cause is unclear, but it is believed to involve autoimmune attack by T cells and macrophages on the walls of arteries, with multinucleated giant cells forming in the vessel wall. Diagnosis is suspected based on symptoms and examination, supported by blood tests showing elevated inflammatory markers such as ESR and CRP, and confirmed by biopsy of the temporal artery.

Treatment

GCA is typically treated with high doses of steroids such as prednisone or prednisolone, started urgently when vision is threatened. The interleukin-6 receptor blocker tocilizumab is also used. Long-term steroids may be needed, sometimes for years, and bone-protective treatment is often added because of steroid side effects.

Epidemiology

The disease occurs in about 1 in 15,000 people over the age of 50 per year. It is more common among people of Northern European descent and among women. The average age at onset is over 70, and it almost never occurs before age 50.
