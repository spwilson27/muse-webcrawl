Fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva

SpecialtyMedical genetics, rheumatology
SymptomsProgressive ossification of soft tissues, malformed big toes
CauseMutation in ACVR1 gene
FrequencyAbout 1 in 1.6–2 million people

Fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP), also called Münchmeyer disease or formerly myositis ossificans progressiva, is an extremely rare connective tissue disease in which fibrous connective tissue — such as muscle, tendons and ligaments — is gradually transformed into bone (ossified). It is the only known medical condition in which one organ system changes into another.

Signs and symptoms

Children born with FOP have characteristically malformed big toes, which may be short and abnormally turned. During childhood, painful soft-tissue swellings ("flare-ups"), often triggered by injury, viral illness or intramuscular injections, progressively ossify, forming ribbons and sheets of heterotopic bone that lock joints in place. The process typically proceeds from the neck and shoulders downward and from the trunk outward. By early adulthood many patients are largely immobilized, and the median lifespan is around 56 years, often limited by thoracic insufficiency from bone bridging the chest wall.

Cause

FOP is caused by a heterozygous activating mutation in the ACVR1 gene on chromosome 2q23-24, encoding the activin A receptor type I, a bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) type I receptor. The classic mutation, R206H, makes the receptor inappropriately responsive to activin A. The condition is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, though most cases arise from new mutations.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by the toe malformation and confirmed by genetic testing; biopsies are avoided because tissue trauma accelerates ossification. For decades no effective treatment existed, and management was supportive. In 2023 the retinoid palovarotene (Sohonos) became the first drug approved for FOP, reducing heterotopic ossification; it was authorized in Canada and the United States.

Notable cases

The skeleton of Harry Eastlack, who died in 1973 and donated his body to science, is displayed at the Mütter Museum in Philadelphia and has been invaluable to researchers. Advocacy is led by the International FOP Association (IFOPA), founded by patient Jeannie Peeper.
