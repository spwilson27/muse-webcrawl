Inquiry-based learning

Inquiry-based learning (also spelled enquiry-based learning in British English) is a form of active learning that starts by posing questions, problems or scenarios rather than simply presenting facts. Students identify and research issues and questions to develop knowledge or solutions, with the teacher acting as a facilitator rather than the primary source of information. The approach is rooted in the constructivist ideas of John Dewey, Lev Vygotsky and Jean Piaget, and in the discovery-learning movement of the 1960s associated with Jerome Bruner.

Levels of inquiry

Frameworks commonly distinguish levels by how much structure the teacher provides: in confirmation inquiry, students confirm a principle through an activity with a known outcome; in structured inquiry, they investigate a teacher-presented question using a prescribed procedure; in guided inquiry, they design their own procedures for a given question; and in open inquiry, students formulate the questions themselves. Problem-based learning, developed in medical education at McMaster University, is a prominent relative in which learning is driven by messy, real-world problems.

Evidence and debate

Advocates argue that inquiry approaches improve engagement, retention and skills such as questioning, investigation and reasoning. Critics, including Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006), argue that minimally guided instruction is ineffective for novices because it overloads working memory, and that explicit instruction works better for beginners. Subsequent meta-analyses generally find that guided inquiry — with scaffolding, feedback and explanation — outperforms both pure discovery and, on measures of transfer and process skills, sometimes direct instruction; the consensus position is that the degree and quality of guidance matters more than inquiry as such.

Use in science education

Inquiry is central to many science curricula, including the U.S. Next Generation Science Standards, where students are expected to engage in scientific practices such as asking questions, planning investigations, analysing data and constructing explanations. Similar approaches are used in history (working with primary sources), mathematics and project-based learning across subjects.
