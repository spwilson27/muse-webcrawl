Proper velocity

In relativity, proper velocity (also known as celerity) is the ratio between the distance traveled by an object in a stationary inertial frame and the proper time (time elapsed on a clock carried by the traveling object). Unlike ordinary (coordinate) velocity, proper velocity is not bounded above by the speed of light, because the traveling clock runs slow as seen from the stationary frame.

Definition

For motion in one dimension, proper velocity w is related to coordinate velocity v by:

w = γv = v / √(1 − v²/c²)

where γ is the Lorentz factor. Equivalently, w = c sinh θ, where θ is the rapidity. Proper velocity is the spatial component of the four-velocity.

Usefulness

Proper velocity is useful because momentum is simply rest mass times proper velocity (p = m0w), extending the Newtonian relation p = mv to high speeds. It also gives a natural measure for travelers: the distance covered in the stationary frame per unit of the traveler's own elapsed time, which is what determines, for example, how far a constantly accelerating spacecraft can reach within the crew's lifetime.
