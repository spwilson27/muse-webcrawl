Scots pine
Kingdom:Plantae
Order:Pinales
Family:Pinaceae
Genus:Pinus
Species:P. sylvestris

Pinus sylvestris, the Scots pine (UK), Scotch pine (US), Baltic pine, or European red pine, is a species of tree in the pine family Pinaceae that is native to Eurasia. It is readily identified by its combination of fairly short, blue-green leaves and orange-red bark.

Description

Scots pine is an evergreen coniferous tree growing to 35 m in height and 1 m in trunk diameter when mature, exceptionally to 45 m tall. The bark is thick, scaly and dark grey-brown on the lower trunk, and thin, flaky and orange-red on the upper trunk and branches — a distinctive feature. The leaves ("needles") grow in pairs, are 3–5 cm long, blue-green, and slightly twisted. The seed cones are pointed ovoid, green at first, maturing to grey-brown in the second year.

Distribution and ecology

It has the widest distribution of any pine, ranging from Scotland and northern Spain in the west across the whole of northern Asia to the Pacific coast. It occurs from sea level in the north of its range to about 2,600 m in the south. In Scotland it is the only timber-producing pine native to Britain, and the remnant Caledonian pinewoods of the Highlands are among the country's most valued habitats, supporting species such as the Scottish crossbill, capercaillie and red squirrel. It is the national tree of Scotland.

Uses

Scots pine is one of the most commercially important conifers in northern Europe: its timber, marketed as "red deal" or Baltic redwood, is used in construction, joinery, fencing and paper pulp. It is also widely planted as an ornamental and Christmas tree, and it has naturalised in parts of North America, where it is occasionally considered invasive.
