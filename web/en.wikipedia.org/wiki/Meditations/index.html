Meditations (Ancient Greek: Τὰ εἰς ἑαυτόν, Ta eis heauton, literally "things to one's self") is a series of personal writings by Marcus Aurelius, Roman Emperor from AD 161 to 180, recording his private notes to himself and ideas on Stoic philosophy. Marcus Aurelius wrote the 12 books of the Meditations in Koine Greek as a source for his own guidance and self-improvement. It is possible that large portions of the work were written at Sirmium, where he spent much time planning military campaigns from AD 170 to 180, and on campaign in central Europe during the Marcomannic Wars. The work was not intended for publication and its original title, if any, is unknown.

Content

The writings take the form of short, aphoristic passages ranging from one sentence to long paragraphs. Recurring themes include acceptance of fate and of one's assigned place in the universe, the transience of fame and life, the discipline of perception and judgement, and the duty to act for the common good. The first book, "Debts and Lessons", is a catalogue of gratitude in which Marcus lists what he learned from family members, teachers, friends, and his adoptive father, the emperor Antoninus Pius.

Marcus's Stoicism emphasises that the mind is disturbed not by things themselves but by our judgements about them, and that a rational person can find serenity by living in accordance with nature and the universal reason (logos) that orders the cosmos.

Transmission and reception

The text survived through the Byzantine world; the earliest surviving manuscript dates to the 14th century. The first printed edition, based on a now-lost manuscript, was published in 1558–59 by Wilhelm Xylander. The Meditations has been praised for centuries as a work of philosophy and a manual of self-discipline; modern readers have included statesmen, soldiers, and writers, and it is among the most widely read works of ancient philosophy today.
