Senryū

Senryū (Japanese: 川柳) is a Japanese form of short poetry similar in structure to haiku — conventionally three lines with a total of 17 on (sound units) in a 5–7–5 pattern — but differing in tone and subject matter. Where haiku traditionally deal with nature and the seasons and strive for a moment of insight, senryū tend to be about human nature and everyday life, and are often humorous, satirical, ironic, or cynical.

History

The form takes its name from the Edo-period haikai poet Karai Senryū (1718–1790), whose pen name means "river willow". Senryū selected winning verses from popular poetry contests (maekuzuke) in which entrants completed a given opening line; his published selections established the humorous, people-focused style associated with the form. The contest tradition contributed tens of thousands of verses, most anonymous.

Characteristics

Senryū usually lack the season word (kigo) and cutting word (kireji) associated with haiku, and their subjects include salarymen, family life, politics, and contemporary technology. The form remains popular in Japan, where newspapers run senryū columns and contests, and it is also written in other languages, sometimes in freer syllabic forms.
