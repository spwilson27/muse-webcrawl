Courtesy name

A courtesy name (Chinese: 字; pinyin: zì; literally "character"), also known as a style name, is a name bestowed upon a person at adulthood in addition to one's given name in traditional East Asian cultures, notably in China, and historically also in Korea, Vietnam and Japan. The courtesy name, usually given at the age of twenty for men at the capping ceremony (guanli) and at fifteen for women at the hairpin ceremony (jili), was used by others as a respectful form of address: to call a peer or superior by their given name was considered impolite, while the courtesy name could be used freely by equals and elders.

Literati are commonly referred to in historical texts by their courtesy names rather than their given names.

Usage

The courtesy name typically consists of two characters and is often semantically related to the given name — echoing, complementing or explaining its meaning. It was bestowed by one's parents, a teacher, or chosen by the person themselves. In the Book of Rites, the practice is described as a mark of adulthood: at twenty, a young man was capped and received a courtesy name by which those outside his immediate family should address him. Famous examples include the Three Kingdoms strategist Zhuge Liang, whose courtesy name was Kongming, and the Song dynasty poet Su Shi, whose courtesy name was Zizhan. After the May Fourth Movement of 1919, the custom largely fell out of use in China, though it persists in some artistic and scholarly circles, and comparable practices such as art names (hào) remained common among writers and painters.

Examples

  • Confucius — given name Qiu (丘), courtesy name Zhongni (仲尼).
  • Cao Cao — courtesy name Mengde (孟德).
  • Li Bai — courtesy name Taibai (太白).
  • Sun Yat-sen — courtesy name Zaizhi (載之).
  • Chiang Kai-shek — courtesy name Jieshi (介石).
  • Mao Zedong — courtesy name Runzhi (潤之).

Related naming customs

Courtesy names belong to a wider complex of traditional Chinese naming practices, including milk names given in infancy, school names, art names (hào) adopted by literati, posthumous names conferred after death, and the taboo on using the names of emperors and ancestors (name taboo). In Korea the corresponding courtesy name is called ja (자), in Vietnam tự, and in Japan azana, though the Japanese custom was largely confined to scholars of Chinese learning.
