Turnstile

Image placeholder: turnstile

A turnstile (also called a turnpike, baffle gate, automated gate, or turn gate) is a form of gate which allows one person to pass at a time. Turnstiles can enforce one-way traffic, restrict passage to people who insert a coin, ticket or pass, and provide a count of admissions. They are widely used at railway stations, stadiums, amusement parks, office buildings and other venues requiring controlled access.

History

Turnstiles were originally used, like other forms of stile, to allow human beings to pass while keeping sheep or other livestock penned in. Their use for crowd control spread with public transport and mass entertainment in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Early fare-collection turnstiles on systems such as the New York City Subway accepted coins directly; later designs incorporated token slots, magnetic-stripe card readers and, most recently, contactless smart cards and mobile tickets.

Types

The common tripod turnstile has three arms at 120 degrees that rotate to admit one person per payment. Full-height turnstiles, resembling revolving doors with floor-to-ceiling barriers, provide greater security and are used at unattended entrances. Waist-high and optical turnstiles — the latter using sensors and retractable glass barriers — are popular in office lobbies where appearance matters. Drop-arm and swing-gate variants provide accessible passage for wheelchair users and passengers with luggage.

Uses

Beyond fare collection, turnstiles count visitors for auditing, meter the flow of crowds into stadiums and theme parks, secure factory and office entrances in conjunction with employee badges, and in some ski resorts control access to lifts. Mechanical counting turnstiles historically produced the attendance figures still quoted for many older sporting venues.
