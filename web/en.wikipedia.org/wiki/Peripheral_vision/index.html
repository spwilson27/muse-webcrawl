Peripheral vision

Peripheral vision, or indirect vision, is vision that occurs outside the point of fixation — the ability to see objects and movement beyond the direct line of sight. It corresponds to the great majority of the retina's area and of the visual field, and is specialised for detecting motion and for vision in dim light rather than for fine detail and colour.

Physiology

The central retina (the fovea) is densely packed with cone cells and supports sharp, colour-rich central vision. Towards the periphery, rods dominate and photoreceptors converge onto fewer ganglion cells, so spatial acuity and colour sensitivity fall off steeply while sensitivity to flicker and movement remains comparatively good. The normal human visual field extends to roughly 100 degrees temporally and 60 degrees nasally in each eye, far beyond the few degrees of foveal vision.

Function

Peripheral vision guides eye movements towards objects of interest, supports spatial orientation and balance, and allows monitoring of the environment while attention is directed centrally. It is crucial in driving, sport and crowd navigation, and peripheral stimuli reflexively attract saccades.

Clinical aspects

Loss of peripheral vision produces "tunnel vision", classically in glaucoma and retinitis pigmentosa; it is assessed by perimetry (visual field testing). Conversely, loss of central vision, as in macular degeneration, forces reliance on peripheral retina and eccentric viewing strategies. Neurological lesions can remove one half of the visual field (hemianopia) in both central and peripheral zones.
