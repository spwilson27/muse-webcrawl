The Bendix G-15 is a general-purpose digital computer introduced in 1956 by the Bendix Corporation of Los Angeles, California. It weighed about 966 pounds (438 kg) and measured roughly 5 by 3 by 3 feet, yet it is sometimes described as one of the first personal computers, because a single operator could run it and its purchase price — about $49,500, or roughly $1,300 a month to rent — put computing within reach of small businesses, universities, and engineering firms.

Design

The G-15 was designed by computer pioneer Harry Huskey, who had worked on the ENIAC and the SWAC. It was a serial, drum-memory machine: its main memory was a magnetic drum holding 2,160 words of 29 bits, with an average access time of about 14.5 milliseconds. Input and output were by paper tape, typewriter, and an optional punched-card unit and plotter.

Software and use

Programming systems for the G-15 included Intercom, an early interpretive system, and Algo, an ALGOL-like compiler developed by Donald Knuth while he was a student consultant; Knuth has cited the G-15 fondly in his writings. More than 400 G-15s were installed, and the machine was popular for civil engineering and scientific calculations. Production ended in 1963 after Control Data Corporation acquired the Bendix computer division.
