Food preservative

A food preservative is a substance or process used to prevent the growth of microorganisms such as bacteria, yeasts and moulds in food, or to slow chemical changes such as oxidation that cause spoilage. Preservatives extend shelf life, reduce food waste and help protect consumers from foodborne illness.

Types

Antimicrobial preservatives inhibit the growth of bacteria, yeasts and moulds. Common examples include benzoates (such as sodium benzoate), sorbates (such as potassium sorbate), nitrites and nitrates used in cured meats, sulphur dioxide and sulphites used in dried fruit and wine, and organic acids such as propionic acid used in baked goods.

Antioxidants prevent or delay oxidative rancidity in fats and oils. These include ascorbic acid (vitamin C), tocopherols (vitamin E), and synthetic compounds such as butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA) and butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT).

Traditional methods

Long before chemical preservatives, foods were preserved by drying, salting, smoking, pickling in vinegar or brine, fermentation, and the addition of sugar or honey. Refrigeration and freezing remain among the most widely used preservation techniques.

Regulation and safety

Preservatives are regulated in most countries and are typically identified on labels by name or by E numbers in the European Union. Regulatory agencies evaluate safety and set maximum permitted levels. Some preservatives have been the subject of public debate; sodium nitrite, for example, is valued for preventing botulism in cured meats but can contribute to the formation of nitrosamines under certain conditions.
