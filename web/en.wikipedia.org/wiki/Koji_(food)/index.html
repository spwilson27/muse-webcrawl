Koji (food)

Rice koji, the cultured grain at the heart of Japanese fermentation

Koji (Japanese: 麹, kōji) is a preparation of grains or soybeans — most commonly rice, barley, or soy — that have been inoculated with the filamentous fungus Aspergillus oryzae and allowed to ferment for roughly two days until the grains are covered in white mycelium and fragrant enzymes. Koji is the foundation of much of Japanese fermented cuisine: it is the starter from which sake, miso, soy sauce, mirin, rice vinegar, shochu, and amazake are all produced.

Production

The process begins with polished grain that is washed, soaked, steamed, and cooled. Spores of A. oryzae (called tane-koji, or seed koji, supplied by specialist spore makers known as moyashiya) are dusted over the grain. The inoculated grain is kept in a warm, humid room — the koji muro — traditionally lined with cedar, and spread on wooden trays. As the mold grows its hyphae into the grain, the mass is mixed by hand to control the considerable heat generated by the fermentation. After about two days the grain is matted with white mycelium and smells sweet, faintly like chestnuts.

Enzymes and role

Koji's importance lies in its enzymes. A. oryzae produces abundant amylases, which convert grain starch into sugars that yeast can ferment (essential for sake, since rice has no malt enzymes of its own); proteases, which break proteins into amino acids responsible for umami in miso and soy sauce; and lipases and other enzymes that shape aroma. The sweetness produced by koji alone, without alcohol, is captured in amazake, a sweet drink made from rice koji and water.

History

Mold-cultured grains have been used in China for over two thousand years (the Chinese qu), and the technique reached Japan by the Nara period. By the Muromachi period, professional koji makers in Kyoto supplied temples and brewers, and their spore-making trade became a guarded specialty. In 2006 the Japanese government recognised koji mold as the "national fungus" (kokkin) of Japan, and A. oryzae was designated a symbol of Japanese brewing culture. In the 2010s and 2020s koji spread widely through global restaurant kitchens, where chefs use it for rapid curing, charcuterie, and vegetable ferments, and home cooks can buy dried rice koji in supermarkets.

Variants

Rice koji (kome-koji) is used for sake, miso, mirin, and amazake. Barley koji (mugi-koji) is used for barley miso and barley shochu. Soybean koji (mame-koji) goes into soybean misos such as hatcho miso. A related domesticated species, Aspergillus luchuensis (black koji) and its white mutant, is used in Okinawan awamori and shochu production, valued for its citric acid production which protects the mash in warm climates.
