Danevirke

The Danevirke (modern Danish spelling: Dannevirke; Old Norse: Danavirki) is a system of Danish fortifications in Schleswig-Holstein, Germany. Consisting of earthwork ramparts and ditches stretching across the neck of the Jutland Peninsula, it was built in phases from the early medieval period to defend Denmark's southern border — first against Saxons and Franks, and later in conflicts with German states.

History

Construction of the earliest ramparts may date to around 500 AD, with major phases traditionally attributed to the semi-legendary queen Thyra and to kings including Gudfred (early 9th century) and Harald Bluetooth (10th century). The system was extended in the 12th century under Valdemar I, who added a brick wall. The Danevirke guarded the overland route between the Baltic trading town of Hedeby and the North Sea.

In the 19th century, during the First Schleswig War (1848–51), the Danevirke was a symbol of Danish national resistance. In the Second Schleswig War of 1864, the Danish army evacuated the position in February during severe winter weather, a retreat that shocked Danish public opinion. Minor works were added during the Second World War.

Preservation

Surviving sections of the ramparts, together with Hedeby, were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2018. A museum near the site presents its history.
