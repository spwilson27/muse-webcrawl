The Abbey of Saint-Évroul (French: Abbaye de Saint-Évroul) was a Benedictine monastery in Normandy, in the present commune of Saint-Évroult-Notre-Dame-du-Bois in the department of Orne. Founded in the Merovingian period around the hermitage of Saint Évroul (died c. 596), destroyed by the Vikings, and refounded in 1050, it became one of the great intellectual centres of Norman monasticism and is best known as the home of the chronicler Orderic Vitalis.

History

Évroul, a monk of Jumièges, withdrew to the forest of Ouche around 560 and attracted disciples; a monastery arose at his tomb. The community was scattered by Norse raids in the ninth century and the site lay deserted for two hundred years. Around 1050 the Giroie and Grandmesnil families refounded the abbey, endowing it with lands in Normandy, England and southern Italy. Houses in Apulia and Calabria were dependencies of Saint-Évroul.

Orderic Vitalis and the scriptorium

Sent from England as a child oblate in 1085, Orderic Vitalis spent his life at Saint-Évroul and wrote his thirteen-book Historia Ecclesiastica there, the richest single chronicle of the Anglo-Norman world. The abbey library held about 160 volumes by the twelfth century, and its scriptorium produced finely illuminated manuscripts.

Later centuries and remains

The abbey declined during the Hundred Years' War and the Wars of Religion, passed to the Maurists in the seventeenth century, and was suppressed in the Revolution, after which much of the church was demolished for stone. The surviving remains include the thirteenth-century gateway (the Porte de l'horloge), parts of the abbey church, and the chapter house; the site is protected as a historic monument.
