Han dynasty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A Han dynasty watchtower model in pottery, of the kind buried in tombs]

The Han dynasty (202 BC – AD 220) was the second imperial dynasty of China, founded by Liu Bang after the fall of the Qin. Ruling for four centuries — interrupted briefly by Wang Mang's Xin dynasty (AD 9–23) — the Han consolidated the imperial system, made Confucianism the state orthodoxy, and gave the majority ethnic group of China its enduring name, the Han people. Under Emperor Wu (r. 141–87 BC) the empire expanded into Central Asia, Korea and Vietnam, and the Silk Road trade with the West began.

Government and culture

The Han kept Qin centralisation but tempered Legalist severity with Confucian ethics; from Emperor Wu's reign, officials were increasingly recruited for classical learning, foreshadowing the later examination system. The historian Sima Qian wrote the Records of the Grand Historian, the model for all later dynastic histories; paper was invented (traditionally credited to Cai Lun, AD 105), and advances came in astronomy, medicine, mathematics and iron casting. Buddhism entered China from India along the Silk Road in the 1st century AD.

Decline and fall

The Later (Eastern) Han, with its capital at Luoyang, was plagued by court factionalism between eunuchs and scholar-officials, land concentration, and peasant revolts, above all the Yellow Turban Rebellion (AD 184). Power passed to regional warlords; the last emperor abdicated in AD 220, and China divided into the Three Kingdoms. The Han remains a golden-age reference point in Chinese historical memory.
