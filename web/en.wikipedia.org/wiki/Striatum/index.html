Striatum

Striatum
Part ofBasal ganglia, forebrain
ComponentsCaudate nucleus, putamen, nucleus accumbens
Main inputsCerebral cortex, thalamus, substantia nigra
Key neurotransmitterDopamine (modulatory); GABA (output of projection neurons)
FunctionsMotor control, reward, habit learning, decision-making

The striatum (also called the corpus striatum) is a cluster of interconnected nuclei that make up the largest component of the basal ganglia. It is the main input station of the basal ganglia system, receiving projections from the entire cerebral cortex, and is critical to motor and reward systems. Its name comes from its striped appearance, caused by bundles of white matter (the internal capsule) passing through the grey matter of the caudate nucleus and putamen.

Anatomy

The dorsal striatum consists of the caudate nucleus and putamen, involved in motor and executive functions. The ventral striatum includes the nucleus accumbens, central to reward and motivation. About 95% of striatal neurons are GABAergic medium spiny neurons, divided into direct-pathway (D1 receptor-expressing) and indirect-pathway (D2 receptor-expressing) populations that respectively facilitate and suppress movement.

Function and clinical significance

Dopamine from the substantia nigra modulates striatal activity. Loss of these dopaminergic neurons causes the motor symptoms of Parkinson's disease, while degeneration of striatal neurons themselves produces the involuntary movements of Huntington's disease. The striatum also underlies reinforcement learning, habit formation, and addiction.
