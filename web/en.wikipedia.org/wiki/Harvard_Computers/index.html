The Harvard Computers were a team of women working as skilled workers to process astronomical data at the Harvard College Observatory in Cambridge, Massachusetts, United States. The team was directed by Edward Charles Pickering (1877 to 1919) and, following his death in 1919, by Annie Jump Cannon.

The astronomers were mainly women who analyzed and classified the spectra of stars from photographic plates. Their work produced the Henry Draper Catalogue and the modern system of stellar classification.

History

Pickering began hiring women as "computers" in the late 1870s, partly because their wages were significantly lower than men's. Williamina Fleming, originally Pickering's housekeeper, became the first and most famous of the group, supervising the others and discovering numerous nebulae, novae, and variable stars.

Contributions

Annie Jump Cannon developed the O-B-A-F-G-K-M spectral classification scheme still used today, classifying some 350,000 stars. Henrietta Swan Leavitt discovered the period–luminosity relation for Cepheid variable stars, which became a cornerstone of measuring cosmic distances—a tool later used in establishing the scale of the universe and the Large Magellanic Cloud distance estimates. Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin's 1925 dissertation demonstrated that stars are composed mainly of hydrogen and helium.

Legacy

The Harvard Computers' work laid the foundation of modern observational astrophysics, and their story has become a prominent example of women's contributions to science during an era when they were largely excluded from academic positions.
