Stereotype threat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stereotype threat is a situational predicament in which people are or feel themselves to be at risk of conforming to stereotypes about their social group. It is purportedly a contributing factor to long-standing racial and gender gaps in academic performance.

Origins

The term was coined by social psychologists Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson, whose 1995 experiments found that Black college students performed worse on a standardised test than white students when the test was described as diagnostic of intellectual ability, but performed comparably when it was presented as non-diagnostic. Similar effects were reported for women taking difficult mathematics tests when gender stereotypes were made salient.

Proposed mechanisms

Researchers have proposed that stereotype threat works through increased anxiety, reduced working memory capacity, heightened monitoring of one's performance, and efforts to suppress negative thoughts and emotions, all of which consume cognitive resources needed for the task.

Empirical support and criticism

Hundreds of studies have reported stereotype threat effects across many groups and domains, and interventions based on the idea — such as values-affirmation exercises and reframing tests — have been studied in educational settings. However, the literature has also faced criticism: meta-analyses have suggested that publication bias may inflate the average effect, particularly in the women-and-mathematics literature, and some large preregistered replications have found smaller or null effects. The size and robustness of the phenomenon remain debated.
