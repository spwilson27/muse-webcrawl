Fascioliasis

Fascioliasis is a parasitic infection caused by liver flukes of the genus Fasciola, principally Fasciola hepatica and Fasciola gigantica. It is a plant-borne trematode infection and a zoonosis: the flukes primarily infect livestock such as sheep and cattle, and humans become infected by eating raw aquatic plants, especially watercress, contaminated with larval cysts (metacercariae), or by drinking contaminated water.

Life cycle

Eggs passed in the faeces of an infected mammal hatch in water into miracidia that infect freshwater snails (such as Galba truncatula). After developing in the snail, free-swimming cercariae emerge and encyst as metacercariae on aquatic vegetation. After ingestion, the larvae penetrate the intestinal wall, migrate through the liver tissue, and mature in the bile ducts.

Signs and symptoms

The acute (migratory) phase can cause fever, abdominal pain, hepatomegaly, and urticaria, while the chronic (biliary) phase may cause cholangitis, biliary obstruction, and pancreatitis, or remain asymptomatic. Millions of people are infected worldwide, with high prevalences in parts of South America, Egypt, Iran, and Asia; the disease is also a major cause of economic loss in livestock production.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by detection of eggs in stool or by serological tests. The drug of choice is triclabendazole, which is effective against both immature and adult flukes.
