70 mm film (or 65 mm film) is a wide high-resolution film stock used for filming and exhibiting motion pictures. In the production and camera-negative sense, the film is actually 65 mm wide; release prints are 70 mm wide, the extra space historically used for magnetic audio tracks. It has an aspect ratio of 2.2:1 in its classic form and offers a much larger picture area — and thus finer detail — than standard 35 mm film.

History

Wide film experiments date back to the early days of cinema, including Fox Grandeur in 1929–30. The format became commercially important in the 1950s as studios competed with television: Mike Todd's Todd-AO process launched with Oklahoma! (1955), followed by productions such as Ben-Hur (1959, Ultra Panavision 70), Lawrence of Arabia (1962, Super Panavision 70), and 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968). From the 1970s onward, most "70 mm" releases were actually blow-ups of 35 mm negatives for their superior sound and projection brightness, but the advent of digital projection largely displaced the practice.

IMAX and modern use

The IMAX format uses 70 mm film running horizontally through the camera, yielding a frame 15 perforations wide ("15/70") with roughly ten times the area of a 35 mm frame. A small number of directors — notably Christopher Nolan, Quentin Tarantino (The Hateful Eight, shot in Ultra Panavision 70), and Paul Thomas Anderson — have championed shooting and projecting on real 70 mm in the digital era, with special roadshow engagements in properly equipped cinemas.
