The videotape format war was a period of competition between incompatible home videocassette formats in the late 1970s and 1980s, principally Sony's Betamax and JVC's VHS. It is frequently cited as a classic example of a format war, in which the technically competitive product lost to superior marketing, licensing and recording time.

The formats

Sony launched Betamax in 1975 with a one-hour recording time and slightly higher horizontal resolution. JVC introduced VHS in 1976 with a two-hour recording time — long enough for a feature film or football game — and licensed the format widely to other manufacturers. Philips and Grundig's Video 2000, launched in 1979 and sold only in Europe and a few other markets, offered still longer recording but arrived too late to gain a foothold.

Course of the war

VHS machines quickly became cheaper and more widely available because of JVC's open licensing strategy, and video rental stores stocked the format customers owned, creating a self-reinforcing cycle. By 1984 VHS accounted for the large majority of VCR sales, and in 1988 Sony itself began manufacturing VHS recorders, effectively conceding the consumer market, though its Betacam line remained successful professionally.

Legacy

The outcome is often attributed less to picture quality than to recording length, price, availability and the network effect of the rental market. "Format war" outcomes of this kind have been studied in the economics of standards and network effects, and the phrase is applied to later contests such as HD DVD versus Blu-ray.
