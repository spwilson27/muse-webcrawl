Protein motif

In proteins, a motif is a short sequence pattern of amino acids, or a small three-dimensional structure, that is conserved across related proteins and usually associated with a particular function. Sequence motifs are typically 3–20 residues long and can be described by regular expressions or sequence logos; structural motifs are arrangements of secondary structure elements such as the helix–turn–helix.

Sequence motifs

Well-known sequence motifs include the N-glycosylation motif (Asn–X–Ser/Thr), the ATP-binding Walker A motif (the P-loop), and phosphorylation sites recognised by specific kinases. Databases such as PROSITE catalogue hundreds of motifs used to annotate protein sequences and predict function.

Structural motifs

Structural motifs, also called supersecondary structures, include the zinc finger, the leucine zipper, the helix–turn–helix DNA-binding motif, the EF hand calcium-binding motif, and the Rossmann fold's characteristic β–α–β arrangement. Larger recurring assemblies of motifs form protein domains.

Motifs versus domains

A motif is smaller than a domain: a domain is an independently folding functional unit, whereas a motif may be only part of a domain. Many motifs mediate protein–protein or protein–ligand interactions, such as the SH3-binding proline-rich motif.
