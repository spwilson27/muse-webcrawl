The Hasmonean dynasty was a ruling dynasty of Judea and surrounding regions during classical antiquity, founded in 140 BC by Simon Thassi under semi-autonomous administration from the Seleucid Empire. By 110 BC the dynasty had become fully independent, and its territory eventually reached its greatest extent under Alexander Jannaeus, ruling much of the Levant with its capital at Jerusalem.

The dynasty arose out of the Maccabean Revolt, which began in 167 BC when the priest Mattathias of Modein refused to obey the decree of the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes forbidding Jewish religious practice. Mattathias's sons, led by Judah Maccabee, waged a successful guerrilla war; the rededication of the Second Temple after its desecration is commemorated by the festival of Hanukkah.

History

After Judah's death in battle in 160 BC, leadership passed to his brothers Jonathan and then Simon. Simon negotiated recognition from the Seleucid king Demetrius II, and in 140 BC a great assembly of the people, priests, and elders appointed him high priest and ethnarch, establishing the hereditary dynasty. His son John Hyrcanus (134–104 BC) secured full independence, destroyed the Samaritan temple on Mount Gerizim, and conquered Idumea and Samaria.

John's son Aristobulus I (104–103 BC) was the first Hasmonean to take the title of king (basileus). His brother Alexander Jannaeus (103–76 BC) expanded the kingdom to its greatest territorial extent, conquering the coastal cities and territories east of the Jordan, though his reign was marred by a bitter civil war with the Pharisees.

Decline and fall

Alexander's widow Salome Alexandra (76–67 BC) ruled peacefully, but after her death her sons Hyrcanus II and Aristobulus II fought a civil war that invited Roman intervention. In 63 BC Pompey captured Jerusalem and made Judea a Roman client state. The dynasty's last ruler, Antigonus II Mattathias, was executed in 37 BC after Herod the Great was granted the kingdom by Rome, marking the end of Hasmonean rule.

Legacy

The Hasmoneans remain central to Jewish memory through the festival of Hanukkah and the books of 1 and 2 Maccabees. Their rule represents the last period of independent Jewish sovereignty in the land of Israel until the modern era.
