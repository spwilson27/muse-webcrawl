Metformin

Metformin, sold under the brand name Glucophage among others, is a medication used to treat type 2 diabetes, particularly in people who are overweight. It is generally the first-line drug for the condition. It is also used in the treatment of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and has been investigated for other uses. Metformin is taken by mouth.

Medical uses

Metformin lowers blood glucose primarily by decreasing hepatic glucose production (gluconeogenesis) and by improving insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues. Unlike insulin and sulfonylureas it does not cause weight gain and carries a low risk of hypoglycaemia when used alone. Large outcome studies (notably UKPDS) showed reductions in diabetes-related complications in overweight patients. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is among the most widely prescribed medications in the world.

History

Metformin belongs to the biguanide class, descended from compounds in the plant Galega officinalis (French lilac), used in folk medicine. It was synthesised in 1922 and introduced clinically in France in 1957 by Jean Sterne under the name Glucophage ("glucose eater"). A related biguanide, phenformin, was withdrawn in the 1970s because of lactic acidosis risk; metformin, with a much lower risk, gained wide acceptance in the 1990s in the United States and elsewhere.

Adverse effects

Common side effects are gastrointestinal — nausea, diarrhoea, and abdominal discomfort — which can be reduced by dose titration and extended-release formulations. Long-term use may lower vitamin B12 levels. The most serious adverse effect, lactic acidosis, is rare but is a concern in severe kidney impairment, so metformin is used cautiously or stopped when kidney function is very poor, and temporarily withheld around certain imaging procedures involving iodinated contrast.

Mechanism of action

Metformin acts in part through activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and inhibition of mitochondrial complex I in hepatocytes, reducing hepatic glucose output. It also increases glucose uptake in muscle and alters gut glucose handling and the microbiome.
