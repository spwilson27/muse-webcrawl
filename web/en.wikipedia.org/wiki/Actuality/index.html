Actuality

Actuality is a philosophical term denoting the state of being real, existent, or fully realised, in contrast to potentiality — what a thing could become but is not yet. The distinction originates with Aristotle and has been a central axis of metaphysics, theology, and modal philosophy ever since.

Aristotle

Aristotle used the Greek terms energeia (activity, being-at-work) and entelecheia (having one's end within) for actuality, contrasted with dynamis, potentiality. His framework explains change: a block of marble is potentially a statue; the sculptor actualises that potential. Actuality is prior to potentiality in definition, in time (in a sense), and in substance, and Aristotle's theology culminates in a "prime mover" that is pure actuality with no unrealised potential.

Scholastic philosophy

Medieval thinkers, above all Thomas Aquinas, Latinised the pair as actus and potentia. Aquinas held that in every created thing essence is in potency to the act of existence, while God alone is actus purus, pure act, whose essence is his existence. The distinction also structures accounts of the soul's faculties and of sacramental and moral theology.

Later thought

Hegel recast actuality (Wirklichkeit) as the unity of essence and existence, and famously declared that "what is rational is actual, and what is actual is rational." In 20th-century philosophy the term appears in contexts from Whitehead's process metaphysics to modal logic, where "actual" marks the world that is realised, as opposed to merely possible worlds.
