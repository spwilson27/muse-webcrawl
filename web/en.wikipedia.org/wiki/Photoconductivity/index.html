Photoconductivity

Photoconductivity is an optical and electrical phenomenon in which a material becomes more electrically conductive owing to the absorption of electromagnetic radiation such as visible light, ultraviolet light, infrared light, or gamma radiation. When light of sufficient photon energy is absorbed by a semiconductor or insulator, electrons are excited across the band gap, increasing the number of free charge carriers — electrons and holes — and thus the material's conductivity.

Mechanism

In darkness, a photoconductive material has a certain "dark conductivity". Illumination creates electron–hole pairs; under an applied electric field these carriers transport current. The effect persists for a characteristic carrier lifetime, which may be lengthened by traps that hold one carrier type, producing photoconductive gain — one photon can effectively enable many electrons to pass through the circuit. When the light is removed, the conductivity decays as carriers recombine.

Materials and applications

Classic photoconductors include selenium, cadmium sulfide (CdS) and cadmium selenide used in light-dependent resistors (photoresistors), and lead salts (PbS, PbSe) for infrared detection. Amorphous selenium's photoconductivity underpins xerography (photocopiers and laser printers), in which a charged photoconductive drum is selectively discharged by light. Photoconductive antennas are used to generate and detect terahertz radiation. Some materials show negative photoconductivity, becoming less conductive under illumination, owing to processes such as carrier trapping or light-induced scattering.
