A meteor shower is a celestial event in which a number of meteors are observed to radiate, or originate, from one point in the night sky called the radiant. The showers are caused by streams of debris, called meteoroids, that enter Earth's atmosphere at extremely high speed on parallel trajectories. Most meteoroids are smaller than a grain of sand, so almost all of them disintegrate in the atmosphere and never hit the Earth's surface.

Cause

Meteor showers occur when Earth passes through the trail of debris left by a comet (or, less commonly, an asteroid) orbiting the Sun. As a comet approaches the Sun, solar heat vaporises its ices and releases dust and rocky particles along its orbit. Over many revolutions this debris spreads into a stream. When Earth's orbit intersects the stream, the particles — striking the atmosphere at speeds from about 11 km/s to 72 km/s — burn up as visible meteors. The Leonids, for example, originate from comet 55P/Tempel–Tuttle, while the Perseids come from comet Swift–Tuttle and the Geminids from the asteroid 3200 Phaethon.

Radiant and naming

Because the meteoroids travel on parallel paths, perspective makes their trails appear to diverge from a single point, the radiant. Showers are named after the constellation in which the radiant lies, or sometimes after a nearby bright star: thus the Perseids radiate from Perseus, the Leonids from Leo, and the Quadrantids from the former constellation Quadrans Muralis.

Activity

Shower strength is measured by the zenithal hourly rate (ZHR), the number of meteors a single observer would see under ideal conditions. Major showers such as the Perseids and Geminids reliably produce rates of 60–120 per hour. Occasionally, when Earth encounters a dense, young filament of debris, a meteor storm erupts with rates in the thousands per hour, as happened with the Leonids in 1833, 1866, 1966 and 2001–2002.
