Halley-type comet

1P/Halley, namesake of the Halley-type comets.

Halley-type comets are periodic comets with orbital periods between about 20 and 200 years — intermediate between the short-period Jupiter-family comets and long-period comets from the Oort cloud. They are named after their most famous member, 1P/Halley, which returns roughly every 76 years.

Orbital characteristics

Unlike Jupiter-family comets, whose orbits lie close to the ecliptic plane, Halley-type comets have orbits at all inclinations, and many are retrograde, like Halley's own retrograde (162°) path. Their Tisserand parameter with respect to Jupiter is typically less than 2, and their aphelia reach well beyond the planets. These properties point to an origin in the spherical Oort cloud rather than the flat Kuiper belt and scattered disc: Halley-type comets are thought to be former long-period comets captured into shorter orbits by planetary perturbations.

Members and physical properties

Known Halley-type comets include 1P/Halley, 8P/Tuttle (source of the Ursid meteors), 27P/Crommelin, 55P/Tempel–Tuttle (parent of the Leonids), 109P/Swift–Tuttle (the largest near-Earth object of cometary origin), and 153P/Ikeya–Zhang. Because they arrive from all inclinations and spend most of their time far from the Sun, fewer than a hundred are well catalogued. Their nuclei resemble those of other comets but may retain more primitive material, and some members eventually become dormant, asteroid-like Damocloids.
