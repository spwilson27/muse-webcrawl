Leonids

Leonid meteors radiating from the constellation Leo.

The Leonids are a prolific annual meteor shower associated with the comet 55P/Tempel–Tuttle. They peak around 17–18 November each year, with meteors appearing to radiate from the constellation Leo, from which the shower takes its name.

Characteristics

The Leonids are among the fastest meteors, striking Earth's atmosphere at about 71 km/s, and frequently produce bright fireballs and persistent trains. In ordinary years the zenithal hourly rate is modest (roughly 10–15), but the shower is famous for great meteor storms when Earth passes through dense, fresh debris trails near the comet's perihelion returns, roughly every 33 years — the orbital period of Tempel–Tuttle.

Historic storms

The storm of 12–13 November 1833, visible across North America with rates estimated in the tens of thousands per hour, was a founding event of meteor astronomy, convincing scientists that meteors originate beyond the atmosphere. Comparable storms occurred in 1866 and in 1966, when observers in the southwestern United States reported rates reaching about 40 meteors per second at peak. The returns of 1999–2002 produced strong outbursts and storms over Europe, the Middle East, and Asia, accurately forecast by modern dust-trail models. Smaller enhancements remain possible in future decades when Earth encounters older trails.
