Denim

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Denim is a sturdy cotton warp-faced textile in which the weft passes under two or more warp threads. This twill weaving produces a diagonal ribbing that distinguishes it from cotton duck. The word comes from the name of a sturdy fabric called serge, originally made in Nîmes, France, by the André family. Originally called serge de Nîmes, the name was shortened to denim.

While denim was originally dyed with the finest indigo dye in the world, it has been in use since the 17th century and has been worn as workwear and fashion clothing. Denim is available in a range of colours, but the most commonly used denim is indigo denim in which the warp thread is dyed while the weft thread is left white. As a result of the warp-faced twill weaving, one side of the textile is dominated by the blue warp threads and the other side by the white weft threads. Jeans made from this fabric are thus predominantly white on the inside.

History

Denim precursors date to the 17th century. In the 19th century, denim work clothing became popular in the United States: in 1873, Levi Strauss and Jacob Davis patented rivet-reinforced denim trousers, giving rise to the modern blue jean. Miners, cowboys, railroad workers and farmers adopted jeans for their durability.

In the 20th century, denim moved from workwear to mainstream fashion. Film stars such as James Dean and Marlon Brando popularised jeans in the 1950s, and designer jeans emerged in the 1970s and 1980s. Denim production also globalised, with major mills in Japan, Turkey, India, China and the United States; Japanese selvedge denim became prized by enthusiasts.

Manufacture

Denim is woven using a 3x1 or 2x1 twill. Traditionally, yarn is rope-dyed or slasher-dyed with indigo, which coats the surface of the yarn without penetrating its core — this is why denim fades characteristically with wear. Weights range from lightweight (under 10 oz) to heavyweight (over 16 oz). Blends with elastane create stretch denim.

Uses

Denim is used for jeans, jackets, skirts, shorts, shirts, hats, bags, upholstery and many other items. Global production exceeds several billion metres annually.
