Three-sector model

The three-sector model in economics divides economies into three sectors of activity: extraction of raw materials (primary), manufacturing (secondary), and services (tertiary). The model was developed by Allan Fisher, Colin Clark and Jean Fourastié in the 1930s and 1940s.

The sectors

  • Primary sector: agriculture, mining, fishing, forestry and other extraction of natural resources.
  • Secondary sector: manufacturing, processing and construction, transforming raw materials into finished goods.
  • Tertiary sector: services such as retail, transport, education, health care, finance and entertainment.

Structural change

According to the model — also called the three-sector hypothesis (Fisher–Clark hypothesis) — economies develop through a sequence: labour first shifts from agriculture to industry as productivity rises, and then from industry to services, which come to dominate employment and output in advanced economies. Later authors proposed quaternary (knowledge) and quinary sectors as further subdivisions of tertiary activity.

Criticism

Critics note that the boundaries between sectors are blurred by outsourcing and digital services, and that the supposed universal sequence does not capture economies that moved directly from agriculture to services.
