Maypole

[Image: Dancers with ribbons around a maypole]
Maypole dancing in England

A maypole is a tall wooden pole erected as part of various European folk festivals, around which a maypole dance often takes place. The festivals may occur on 1 May (May Day) or at Midsummer (the summer solstice), and are found across Northern and Central Europe, the British Isles, and in communities of European descent elsewhere.

Origins

The origins of the maypole are debated. Antiquarians once linked it to Germanic pagan tree worship and phallic symbolism; modern historians note that unambiguous references appear only in the later Middle Ages, in Germany and then across Europe. In Britain, maypoles became fixtures of parish May Day celebrations in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. The Puritans condemned them as idolatrous — Parliament ordered maypoles banned in 1644 — but they returned with the Restoration in 1660; the great Strand maypole in London stood over 130 feet tall.

Regional traditions

In Sweden the midsommarstång (midsummer pole), decorated with greenery, flowers and wreaths, is the centrepiece of Midsummer celebrations, with dancing and songs around it. In Germany the Maibaum is raised on 30 April or 1 May, often painted in regional colours (blue and white in Bavaria) and decorated with the crests of local guilds; rival villages traditionally attempt to steal each other's poles. In Britain and Ireland, ribbon dancing — in which dancers weave coloured ribbons attached to the top of the pole into patterns and then unwind them — became popular in the nineteenth century and remains a staple of school and village fêtes.
