Augustine of Hippo (354–430), also known as Saint Augustine, was a theologian and philosopher from Roman North Africa whose writings shaped Western Christianity and philosophy. His Confessions — a spiritual autobiography famous for the garden conversion scene ('take up and read') — and The City of God, written after the sack of Rome in 410, influenced doctrines of original sin, grace, just war and the philosophy of history.

Life and thought

Raised by a Christian mother, Monica, Augustine spent nine years as a Manichaean hearer before turning to Neoplatonism and, in Milan, to Christianity under Bishop Ambrose; he was baptised in 387. As Bishop of Hippo he fought Donatist and Pelagian controversies, arguing against Pelagius that the will is bound by sin and freedom comes by grace. His meditations on time in the Confessions ('What then is time? If no one asks me, I know...') remain a starting point of the subject. He died as the Vandals besieged Hippo.
