Power law of practice


Labelled image placeholder: response time versus practice trials on log–log axes

The power law of practice in psychology states that the logarithm of the reaction time for a particular task decreases linearly with the logarithm of the number of practice trials taken. In other words, performance improves rapidly at first and then ever more slowly, following a power function of the amount of practice.

Formulation

The law is usually written T = aP−b + c, where T is the time to complete the task, P is the number of practice trials, and a, b, and c are constants fitted to the task. Equivalently, on log–log coordinates the learning curve is approximately a straight line.

History and scope

The relationship was described in detail by Allen Newell and Paul Rosenbloom in 1981, who showed that it fits data from a remarkable range of skills, from cigar rolling and mental arithmetic to geometry proof generation and industrial tasks. Snoddy's earlier (1926) work on mirror tracing showed a similar law. The power law is one of the most robust regularities in the study of skill acquisition and is a central modelling target for cognitive architectures such as ACT-R.

Explanations and criticisms

Proposed explanations include chunking of operations, strengthening of memory traces, and selection of faster strategies; in ACT-R, the law emerges from the power-law decay of memory activation and production compilation. Critics, notably Heathcote, Brown, and Mewhort (2000), argue that individual learners often follow exponential rather than power functions, and that the power law at the group level can be an artefact of averaging.
