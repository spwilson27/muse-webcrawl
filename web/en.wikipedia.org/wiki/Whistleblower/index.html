Whistleblower

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A whistleblower is a person, often an employee, who reveals information about activity within a private or public organisation that is deemed illegal, immoral, illicit, unsafe or fraudulent. The term (from referees and police whistles blown to call attention to wrongdoing) covers disclosures made internally, to regulators, to law enforcement, or — most consequentially and controversially — to journalists and the public.

Law and famous cases

Legal regimes try to separate protected disclosure from leaks and espionage: the US Whistleblower Protection Act (1989) and a patchwork of sectoral statutes (with financial bounties under Dodd–Frank's SEC programme paying out hundreds of millions), the EU Whistleblowing Directive (2019), and the UK's Public Interest Disclosure Act (1998). National-security disclosures sit at the hard edge: Daniel Ellsberg (Pentagon Papers, 1971; charges dismissed over government misconduct), Chelsea Manning and Edward Snowden (2013 mass-surveillance disclosures; espionage charges, exile) — prosecuted as leakers despite public-interest arguments, unlike corporate whistleblowers (Enron's Sherron Watkins; WorldCom's Cynthia Cooper; tobacco's Jeffrey Wigand) who are celebrated. Empirical research finds whistleblowers usually report internally first, suffer retaliation at high rates despite legal protections, and are motivated more by organisational loyalty paradoxically than by reward — findings that keep reformers focused on culture and anti-retaliation enforcement rather than bounties alone.
