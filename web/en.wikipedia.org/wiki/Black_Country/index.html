Black Country

The Black Country is an area of the West Midlands in England, west of Birmingham. It became one of the most intensely industrialised regions of Britain during the Industrial Revolution, dense with coal mines, iron foundries, glassworks, and factories. The name is traditionally said to derive from the smoke and soot of its industry — or from the black coal seams exposed at the surface — and was popularized in the 19th century, including by Elihu Burritt, the American consul who described it as "black by day and red by night."

Region: West Midlands, England

Geology and industry

The area sits on the South Staffordshire coalfield, where a thick coal seam (the "Thick Coal," up to about 30 feet) lay close to the surface. Ironstone, limestone, and fireclay were also dug locally, feeding ironworks, foundries, and the famous Stourbridge glass industry, as well as chain-making centered on Cradley Heath and lock-making at Willenhall and Wolverhampton.

Culture and dialect

The Black Country has a distinctive dialect, considered one of the most conservative in England and retaining features from Middle English, and a strong local identity distinct from Birmingham. Its industrial heritage is preserved at the Black Country Living Museum in Dudley, and the region's geology gained recognition when much of it was designated a UNESCO Global Geopark in 2020.

Boundaries

The Black Country has no single official boundary; it is commonly taken to comprise the metropolitan boroughs of Dudley, Sandwell, Walsall, and the city of Wolverhampton. Whether Wolverhampton and certain fringe towns are included is a perennial local debate.
