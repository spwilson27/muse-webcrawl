Mesoamerican Long Count calendar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Mesoamerican Long Count calendar is a non-repeating vigesimal (base-20) and octodecimal (base-18) calendar used by several pre-Columbian Mesoamerican cultures, most famously the Maya. For this reason it is often known as the Maya (or Mayan) Long Count calendar; using a modified base-20 count came from Maya numerals.

Since Long Count dates are not tied to a repeating cycle like the Calendar Round, they can unambiguously identify a day over a span of thousands of years, which makes them valuable for correlating Maya inscriptions with the Gregorian calendar.

Periods

The Long Count counts days from a mythological starting point corresponding to 11 August 3114 BC in the proleptic Gregorian calendar. Days are grouped into periods as follows:

1 Winal (20 Kʼin)20

1 Kʼatun (20 Tun)7,20020
1 Bakʼtun (20 Kʼatun)144,000394
1 Piktun (20 Bakʼtun)2,880,0007,885

Dates are written as five (or more) numbers separated by periods, from the largest unit to the smallest; for example, 9.15.2.0.0 denotes 9 bakʼtuns, 15 kʼatuns, 2 tuns, 0 winals, and 0 kʼins since the start of the current creation.

The base date and correlation

The most widely accepted correlation constant, the Goodman–Martínez–Thompson (GMT) correlation, places the Long Count base date 0.0.0.0.0 on 11 August 3114 BC (Gregorian), so that 13.0.0.0.0 fell on 21 December 2012. That completion of the 13th bakʼtun attracted widespread popular speculation about a supposed 2012 phenomenon, although Maya scholars emphasised that inscriptions project dates far beyond 2012 and no apocalypse was predicted.

Origins

The earliest securely dated Long Count inscriptions, from the late first century BC, come not from the Maya lowlands but from the Pacific coast and Isthmian region (for example Chiapa de Corzo and Tres Zapotes), and are associated with the Epi-Olmec or Isthmian culture. The Maya adopted and greatly elaborated the system, recording dates on stelae, lintels, and in codices, often together with Calendar Round dates and detailed astronomical cycles such as the Venus and lunar tables of the Dresden Codex.
