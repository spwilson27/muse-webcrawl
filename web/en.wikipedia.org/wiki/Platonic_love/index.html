Platonic love

Platonic love is a type of love that is not sexual or romantic in nature. In modern usage it usually denotes a deep, affectionate friendship between two people that lacks physical desire, though the term derives from a richer philosophical account in the works of Plato.

Origin in Plato

In the Symposium, Plato presents love (eros) as a ladder of ascent: beginning with attraction to a single beautiful body, the lover rises to love of beauty in all bodies, then of souls, of laws and knowledge, and finally of Beauty itself — the eternal Form of the Beautiful. In the Phaedrus, the charioteer of the soul restrains the unruly horse of appetite. True Platonic love, in this sense, is not merely the absence of sex but the redirection of desire toward wisdom and virtue.

Later use

The term (Latin amor platonicus) was coined in the 15th century by Marsilio Ficino for the affectionate, non-physical bond between men celebrated in his commentary on the Symposium. By the 17th century "platonic" love was applied to chaste affection between men and women, and today it most often simply describes close non-romantic friendship.
