Regulation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Regulation is the management of complex systems according to a set of rules and trends. In law and government, it refers to rules made by executive agencies under authority delegated by the legislature — administrative law — together with the state supervision of private activity in fields such as finance, utilities, food and drugs, the environment, broadcasting and competition. Regulations have the force of law but are subordinate to statutes.

Modern regulatory states grew from the late nineteenth century (the US Interstate Commerce Commission, 1887, is often counted the first modern federal regulatory agency) through the New Deal agencies to the post-war expansion of social and environmental regulation in all advanced economies. Regulatory theory debates public-interest justifications (market failure, externalities, monopoly, information asymmetry) against public-choice accounts of capture by regulated industries. Instruments range from command-and-control standards to market mechanisms (taxes, tradable permits), disclosure duties and self-regulation under state oversight.

Rulemaking and review

In the United States, agency rules are made under the Administrative Procedure Act (1946) through notice-and-comment rulemaking and are reviewable by courts for legality and rationality; major shifts in judicial deference to agencies (the end of Chevron deference in Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, 2024) recalibrate the balance between agencies, courts and Congress. In the EU, regulations are directly applicable laws of the Union, while agencies issue technical standards under framework legislation. Deregulation movements from the late 1970s (airlines, telecommunications, finance) remain a standing counterpoint.
