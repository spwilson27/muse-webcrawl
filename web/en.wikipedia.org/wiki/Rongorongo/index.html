Rongorongo

Rongorongo (/ˈrɒŋɡəˈrɒŋɡəʊ/) is a system of glyphs discovered in the nineteenth century on Easter Island (Rapa Nui) that appears to be writing or proto-writing. If it is a genuine script, its decipherment has so far resisted all attempts, making it one of the few undeciphered writing systems known. It is one of only a handful of independent inventions of writing in human history if it predates European contact — a point still debated.

The tablets

Twenty-six wooden objects bearing rongorongo texts survive, including tablets, a staff, and ornaments, now scattered among museums worldwide. The glyphs — stylized humans, animals, plants and geometric shapes, roughly 1 cm high — were carved with shark teeth or obsidian flakes and read in reverse boustrophedon: every other line is upside down, and the reader rotated the tablet as they chanted. Oral tradition held that expert chanters (ma'ori rongorongo) recited genealogies, calendars and rituals from them.

Loss and decipherment attempts

The tradition collapsed in the 1860s after Peruvian slave raids and disease devastated Rapa Nui, and the last literate experts died. Bishop Tepano Jaussen collected early testimonies, and scholars from Steven Roger Fischer to Yuri Knorozov have proposed partial readings; a 2024 radiocarbon study dated one tablet's wood to the fifteenth century, lending support to a pre-contact origin. No proposed decipherment has won consensus, and the script's meaning — even whether it encodes language fully — remains unresolved.
