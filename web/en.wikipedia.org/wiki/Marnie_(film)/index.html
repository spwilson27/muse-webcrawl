Marnie is a 1964 American psychological thriller film directed by Alfred Hitchcock. The screenplay by Jay Presson Allen was based on the 1961 novel of the same name by Winston Graham. The film stars Tippi Hedren as Marnie Edgar, a habitual thief with a terror of men, thunderstorms and the colour red, and Sean Connery as Mark Rutland, the wealthy publisher who catches her stealing from his company and blackmails her into marriage while attempting to unravel the childhood trauma behind her behaviour.

Plot

Marnie Edgar repeatedly obtains clerical jobs under false identities, robs her employers' safes and disappears, dyeing her hair between jobs. Widowed publisher Mark Rutland, recognising her when she applies at his firm, hires her and, after she steals from him, forces her to marry him rather than face arrest. Marnie's frigid terror of intimacy and of red — triggered even by the sight of red gladioli — frustrates Mark, who researches her past. After she attempts suicide on their cruise honeymoon, Mark traces her mother in Baltimore. In the climactic confrontation, Marnie recovers a repressed memory: as a small child, during a thunderstorm, she killed a sailor who was molesting her, while her mother took the blame's shadow over the family. Confronted with the truth, Marnie chooses to stay with Mark and face the police rather than flee.

Production

Hitchcock had hoped to cast Grace Kelly, by then Princess Grace of Monaco, in her screen comeback, but the plan collapsed. He cast Tippi Hedren, his discovery from The Birds (1963); their working relationship deteriorated during production. The film was photographed by Robert Burks and scored by Bernard Herrmann, in his last completed collaboration with Hitchcock. Its conspicuous painted backdrops and process photography were criticised at the time but have since been interpreted by some scholars as deliberate expressions of Marnie's subjective state.

Reception

Released in July 1964, Marnie was a modest commercial success but received mixed reviews and was long regarded as a lesser Hitchcock work. Critical opinion has since risen markedly: it is now frequently discussed as one of Hitchcock's most psychologically complex films, and appeared in the BBC's 2015 poll of the greatest American films. Interpretations centre on its treatment of trauma, sexuality, class and control within marriage.
