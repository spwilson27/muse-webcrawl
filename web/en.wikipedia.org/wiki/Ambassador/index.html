Ambassador

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ambassador is an official envoy, especially a high-ranking diplomat who represents a state and is usually accredited to another sovereign state or to an international organisation as the resident representative of their own government, or appointed for a special and often temporary diplomatic assignment. Ambassadors are the highest-ranking diplomats in bilateral relations, heading embassies and speaking formally for their head of state and government.

Appointment and status

Ambassadors are appointed by the sending state (in the US, presidential nominees require Senate confirmation) and must receive the host's agrément before taking post; they present credentials to the host head of state. The Vienna Convention (1961) fixes their privileges and immunities and classes of heads of mission. Most ambassadors today are career foreign-service officers, though some systems appoint political nominees (about a third of US posts are traditionally political appointees, a standing debate). A host may declare an ambassador persona non grata, requiring recall — a standard instrument of diplomatic protest, alongside downgrading or severing relations.

Roles

Beyond representation, ambassadors negotiate, report analysis home, protect citizens, promote national interests and culture, and manage crises. Specialised ambassadors — to the UN, the EU, or for themes such as climate or human rights (ambassadors-at-large) — extend the model multilaterally.
