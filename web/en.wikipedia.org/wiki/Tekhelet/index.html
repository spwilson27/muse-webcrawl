Tekhelet

Tekhelet (Hebrew: תְּכֵלֶת, təḵēleṯ) is a blue dye mentioned in the Hebrew Bible, used in the clothing of the High Priest, the tapestries of the Tabernacle, and in the fringes (tzitzit) attached to the corners of garments. The Torah commands that one thread of each fringe be dyed tekhelet so that wearers will "look at it and recall all the commandments of the Lord" (Numbers 15:39).

Source of the dye

Rabbinic tradition held that tekhelet came from the blood of a marine creature called the ḥillazon. After the dye's production ceased in late antiquity, the custom of wearing a blue thread lapsed for centuries. In the late nineteenth century, Rabbi Gershon Henoch Leiner identified the common cuttlefish as the source, but later research pointed instead to the banded dye-murex (Hexaplex trunculus), a Mediterranean sea snail whose glandular secretion yields a dye that can appear purple or, when processed in sunlight, a sky-blue matching ancient descriptions. Murex-derived dye (chemically related to indigo, dibromoindigo) is now produced in Israel and used by a growing number of observant Jews.

In the Bible and rabbinic literature

Tekhelet is mentioned some fifty times in the Hebrew Bible, always in prestigious contexts: the Tabernacle coverings and priestly vestments, including the robe of the ephod made entirely of tekhelet, and royal or wealthy garments (as in Esther 8:15). The Talmud likens its colour to the sea, the sky, and ultimately to the "Throne of Glory," explaining its role as a mnemonic for the commandments.

Modern revival

Since the 1980s and 1990s, organizations such as Ptil Tekhelet have promoted the murex identification with archaeological and chemical evidence, including dyed textiles from ancient sites in Israel. The question remains debated within Orthodox Judaism, and practice varies by community; many wear all-white fringes, while others include one or more blue threads.
