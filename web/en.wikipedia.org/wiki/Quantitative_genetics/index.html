Quantitative genetics

Quantitative genetics is the study of the inheritance of continuously varying traits — such as height, weight, milk yield, or intelligence — that are influenced by many genes, each of small effect, together with environmental factors. Unlike classical Mendelian genetics, which deals with discrete characters controlled by one or a few loci, quantitative genetics uses statistical methods to relate phenotypic variation in a population to underlying genetic variation that usually cannot be observed directly at the level of individual genes.

Distribution of a typical quantitative trait in a population, approximating a normal curve.

The field was founded in the early twentieth century when R. A. Fisher's 1918 paper, "The Correlation Between Relatives on the Supposition of Mendelian Inheritance", reconciled Mendelian inheritance with the biometric observations of continuous variation, laying the foundations of modern biostatistics and population genetics.

Quantitative traits and polygenic inheritance

A quantitative trait is a phenotype that shows continuous variation and is typically governed by many loci, called quantitative trait loci (QTL), whose individual effects are small. Because each locus contributes a small increment, the combined effect of many segregating loci, plus environmental noise, produces the smooth, approximately normal distribution characteristic of traits such as human stature or grain yield. Traits influenced by only a few loci are sometimes called oligogenic, while those influenced by very many are polygenic.

Partitioning phenotypic variance

The central device of quantitative genetics is the decomposition of total phenotypic variance (VP) into components attributable to genetic and environmental causes:

VP = VG + VE + VG×E

where VG is genetic variance, VE is environmental variance, and VG×E represents genotype-by-environment interaction. Genetic variance is further subdivided into additive variance (VA), arising from the average effects of alleles; dominance variance (VD), arising from interactions between alleles at the same locus; and epistatic variance (VI), arising from interactions between loci.

Heritability

Heritability measures the proportion of phenotypic variance attributable to genetic factors. In the broad sense, H² = VG/VP; in the narrow sense, h² = VA/VP. Narrow-sense heritability is of particular practical importance because it predicts the response of a trait to selection through the breeder's equation:

R = h²S

where R is the response to selection and S is the selection differential. Heritability is a property of a particular population in a particular environment, not of a trait in the abstract, and a high heritability does not imply that a trait is insensitive to environmental change.

Resemblance between relatives

Because relatives share alleles identical by descent, the phenotypic covariance between relatives reflects the genetic variance components. Parent–offspring covariance estimates one-half of the additive genetic variance, while the covariance of full siblings includes additive and dominance components. Such relationships underlie pedigree-based estimation methods, including the "animal model" widely used in livestock breeding, and modern genomic approaches that estimate relatedness from marker data.

Applications

Quantitative genetics underpins plant and animal breeding, where estimated breeding values guide selection decisions. In human genetics it informs the interpretation of twin and family studies and of genome-wide association studies, which have shown that most complex diseases and behavioural traits are highly polygenic. The discrepancy between heritability estimated from pedigrees and that explained by identified loci is known as the "missing heritability" problem. In evolutionary biology, quantitative genetic models describe how selection, drift, and mutation shape the evolution of continuous characters.
