Telerobotics


A remotely operated manipulator arm

Telerobotics is the area of robotics concerned with the control of robots from a distance, chiefly over a communications link. It combines teleoperation — direct human control of a machine's movements — with varying degrees of robot autonomy. Telerobotic systems are used wherever environments are too dangerous, distant or inaccessible for humans, or where a human operator's judgement must be coupled with a machine's strength and endurance.

History

The field traces to mechanical master–slave manipulators developed in the 1940s by Raymond Goertz at Argonne National Laboratory for handling radioactive materials behind shielding. Electrical and servo-controlled versions followed, and the concept expanded to undersea vehicles, nuclear facilities and spaceflight. The Soviet Lunokhod rovers, driven from Earth in the 1970s, demonstrated planetary teleoperation despite signal delay, while NASA's Mars rovers operate under "supervised autonomy" because of long communication lags.

Applications

Modern applications include remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) for deep-sea inspection and repair, bomb-disposal and military robots, remote handling in nuclear decommissioning, robotic surgery (telesurgery) in which a surgeon operates a patient-side robot from a console, and teleoperated drones and warehouse machines. Space station arms such as Canadarm2 can be controlled from the ground as well as by astronauts.

Challenges

Key technical challenges are communication latency, which degrades direct control and motivates predictive displays and shared autonomy; limited sensory feedback, being partially addressed by haptic (force-feedback) interfaces; and reliability of the communications link. Research focuses on balancing human oversight with onboard intelligence so the robot can act safely between commands.
