Supercontinent cycle

Supercontinent cycle
Periodroughly 300–600 million years

The supercontinent cycle is the quasi-periodic aggregation and dispersal of Earth's continental crust, driven by plate tectonics. Over intervals of several hundred million years, the continents drift together into a single supercontinent, which then rifts apart, opening new ocean basins before the fragments eventually reassemble. The cycle is closely related to, but operates on a longer timescale than, the Wilson cycle of ocean opening and closing.

Known supercontinents

The most recent supercontinent was Pangaea, assembled by about 335 million years ago and broken up since roughly 175 million years ago. Earlier ones reconstructed from the geological record include Rodinia (about 1.1 billion years ago), Pannotia, Gondwana (as a large southern landmass), Columbia/Nuna, and the Archaean Kenorland and Ur. The sequence is increasingly uncertain further back in time.

Effects

Assembly and breakup of supercontinents profoundly affect sea level, climate (supercontinents tend to have arid continental interiors and promote global cooling), ocean circulation, and evolution — for example, the breakup of Pangaea isolated faunas and drove diversification, while assembly can trigger extinction through habitat loss and climate change.

Future

Models of the next supercontinent propose configurations such as Pangaea Proxima (Pangaea Ultima), Novopangaea, Aurica, and Amasia, forming 200–300 million years from now depending on whether the Atlantic or Pacific closes or new subduction zones form.
