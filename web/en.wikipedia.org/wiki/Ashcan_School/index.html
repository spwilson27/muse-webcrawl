The Ashcan School, also called the Ash Can School, was an artistic movement in the United States during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. It is best known for works portraying scenes of daily life in New York City, often in the city's poorer neighbourhoods. The movement grew out of the circle of painters associated with Robert Henri, several of whom had worked as newspaper illustrators in Philadelphia.

Origins and The Eight

In 1908 Henri and seven colleagues — John Sloan, George Luks, William Glackens, Everett Shinn, Arthur B. Davies, Ernest Lawson and Maurice Prendergast — exhibited together at the Macbeth Galleries in New York as "The Eight", in protest against the conservative exhibition policies of the National Academy of Design. Their unidealised depictions of tenements, saloons, boxing matches, street crowds and immigrant life scandalised some critics; the derisive label "Ashcan School" (and "revolutionary black gang") was coined by detractors and later adopted with pride.

Style and subjects

Ashcan painters worked in a vigorous, painterly realism, favouring dark palettes and sketch-like immediacy. A younger generation, notably George Bellows and Edward Hopper in his early work, extended the approach. The movement's momentum faded after the Armory Show of 1913 introduced European modernism to America, but its commitment to contemporary urban life shaped later American realism and Social Realism of the 1930s.
