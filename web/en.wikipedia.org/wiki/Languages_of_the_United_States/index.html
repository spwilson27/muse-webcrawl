Languages of the United States

The United States is home to hundreds of languages. English is the most widely spoken language in the country and the language of government at the federal level, although for most of the nation's history it had no official status in federal law. In 2025, an executive order designated English as the official language of the United States. Spanish is by far the second most common language, followed by Chinese languages, Tagalog, Vietnamese, Arabic, and French.

Most common languages

According to the American Community Survey, roughly four out of five residents aged five and older speak only English at home. Spanish is spoken at home by about 13 percent of the population, the legacy both of Spanish colonial settlement in the Southwest and Puerto Rico and of extensive immigration from Latin America.

Indigenous languages

Before European colonization, hundreds of Native American languages were spoken within the present borders of the United States. Many are now endangered or extinct, though revitalization efforts are under way for languages such as Navajo — the most widely spoken Indigenous language north of Mexico — Cherokee, Hawaiian, and numerous others. Alaska and Hawaii recognize Indigenous languages officially; Hawaii lists Hawaiian as an official state language alongside English.

Other languages

Significant historical immigrant languages include German (once the second most printed language in the country), Pennsylvania Dutch, Yiddish, Italian, Polish, and the French varieties of Louisiana (Cajun French and Louisiana Creole). American Sign Language is used by a substantial Deaf community, with estimates of users ranging from several hundred thousand to a few million.
