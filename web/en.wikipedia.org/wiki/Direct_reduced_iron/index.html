Direct reduced iron

Direct reduced iron (DRI), also called sponge iron, is produced from the direct reduction of iron ore (in the form of lumps, pellets or fines) into iron by a reducing gas produced from natural gas or coal. The reducing gas is a mixture of hydrogen and carbon monoxide, obtained for example by reforming natural gas or gasifying coal. Unlike the blast furnace, the ore is reduced in the solid state and never melts; the product is a porous, sponge-like material that is later melted, usually in an electric arc furnace, to make steel.

Process

In the dominant shaft-furnace processes, such as MIDREX and HYL/Energiron, iron ore pellets descend through a furnace while hot reducing gas flows upward. Oxygen is progressively removed from the oxide (hematite and magnetite) at temperatures around 800–1050 °C, converting it to metallic iron without melting. The product, typically 90–94% metallised, is discharged as DRI, charged hot into a furnace, or compacted into dense hot-briquetted iron (HBI) for safe storage and shipping, since fresh DRI is pyrophoric and re-oxidises readily on contact with air and water.

Advantages and use

Direct reduction avoids the need for coking coal and the very large capital cost of blast furnaces, and gas-based DRI emits substantially less CO2 per tonne of iron than the blast furnace route. DRI is used mainly as a high-purity feed for electric arc furnaces, where it dilutes residual elements in scrap steel. Major producers are located where natural gas is cheap or iron ore abundant, including Iran, India, Russia, Mexico and the Middle East; India, which also uses many coal-based rotary kiln plants, has been the world's largest DRI producer.

Hydrogen-based reduction

Because the reducing gas can in principle be pure hydrogen, direct reduction is central to plans for low-carbon "green steel": hydrogen made with renewable electricity would reduce the ore, emitting water instead of carbon dioxide. Pilot plants in Sweden (such as HYBRIT) and elsewhere have demonstrated fossil-free DRI production.
