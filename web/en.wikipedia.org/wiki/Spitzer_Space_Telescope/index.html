The Spitzer Space Telescope, formerly the Space Infrared Telescope Facility (SIRTF), was an infrared space telescope launched in 2003, the fourth and final of NASA's Great Observatories (alongside Hubble, Compton, and Chandra). It was retired on 30 January 2020 after more than 16 years of operation, far beyond its planned 2.5-to-5-year lifetime. The telescope is named after astrophysicist Lyman Spitzer Jr., an early advocate of space telescopes.

Design and orbit

Spitzer carried an 85-centimetre beryllium primary mirror cooled by liquid helium to about 5.6 K, allowing it to observe the infrared universe with extreme sensitivity at wavelengths of roughly 3 to 180 micrometres. Rather than orbiting the Earth, Spitzer was placed in an Earth-trailing heliocentric orbit, drifting slowly away from Earth (roughly 0.1 AU per year); this kept it far from Earth's heat and allowed its warm side to face the Sun while the telescope itself remained cryogenically cold, extending the mission with a relatively small helium supply.

Instruments

  • IRAC (Infrared Array Camera): four-channel camera imaging at 3.6, 4.5, 5.8, and 8.0 micrometres.
  • IRS (Infrared Spectrograph): low- and high-resolution spectroscopy from 5 to 38 micrometres.
  • MIPS (Multiband Imaging Photometer): imaging and photometry at 24, 70, and 160 micrometres.

Cryogenic and warm missions

The liquid helium coolant was exhausted in May 2009, ending the "cryogenic mission." The observatory then began its "warm mission": the telescope settled at about 28 K, too warm for the longer-wavelength instruments, but the two shortest IRAC channels (3.6 and 4.5 micrometres) continued to operate at near-original sensitivity for another decade of science.

Science results

Spitzer's observations included mapping some of the most distant galaxies known, studying star formation hidden inside dust clouds, characterizing the atmospheres and temperatures of exoplanets, producing a comprehensive infrared survey of the Milky Way (the GLIMPSE survey), and observing the coldest and most distant objects of the early universe. Its precise photometry was essential to the characterization of the seven Earth-sized planets of the TRAPPIST-1 system, announced in 2017. Over its lifetime the telescope logged more than 36,000 hours of observations and its data underpin many thousands of scientific papers.

End of mission

As Spitzer drifted ever farther from Earth, communication became increasingly constrained by its fixed antenna orientation and the geometry of its solar panels. NASA ended the mission on 30 January 2020, sending the final commands to place the spacecraft in a permanent safe mode. The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021, is its successor in infrared astronomy.
