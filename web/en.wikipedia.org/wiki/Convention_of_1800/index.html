The Convention of 1800, also known as the Treaty of Mortefontaine, was a treaty between the United States and the French Republic that ended the Quasi-War, the undeclared naval conflict fought between 1798 and 1800. Signed on 30 September 1800 at Mortefontaine, France, it peaceably terminated the Franco-American alliance of 1778 — the only formal alliance the United States entered in its first century and a half — and restored commercial relations between the two countries.

[Image: Mortefontaine, France]

Background

During the French Revolutionary Wars, France seized American merchant ships trading with Britain, prompting the United States to abrogate the 1778 treaties, build up the Navy, and fight an undeclared naval war in the Caribbean and Atlantic. President John Adams, resisting pressure for a full declaration of war, sent envoys to France in 1799; Napoleon Bonaparte, newly in power as First Consul, was eager to end the conflict and free France's hand in Europe.

Terms

The convention restored peace and commerce, provided for the return of captured public ships, and allowed each side to appoint consuls. The contentious questions — American claims for French spoliations and the formal status of the 1778 Treaty of Alliance and Treaty of Amity and Commerce — were deferred to future negotiation, and the Senate ratified the convention only after striking the article that suspended rather than terminated the old treaties. Both sides effectively agreed that the alliance was at an end and that the indemnity claims would lapse.

Significance

The convention freed the United States from its sole entangling alliance; the country would not enter another formal military alliance until the twentieth century. It also cleared the way for improved relations with France, culminating in the Louisiana Purchase of 1803.
