Petronilla de Meath (c. 1300 – 3 November 1324) was the maidservant of Dame Alice Kyteler, a wealthy Anglo-Irish noblewoman of Kilkenny, Ireland. Petronilla was tortured and forced to confess to witchcraft before being burned at the stake. She is widely regarded as the first person in Ireland to be executed for witchcraft, and her case is one of the earliest recorded witch trials in the British Isles.

The Kyteler case

In 1324, Alice Kyteler was accused of witchcraft by her stepchildren, who charged her with poisoning their father and her subsequent husbands, and with practicing sorcery, including sacrificing animals to a demon. The Bishop of Ossory, Richard de Ledrede, a Franciscan known for his zeal against heresy, pursued the case vigorously despite resistance from local nobility and officials who protected Kyteler.

Petronilla, as Kyteler's servant, was arrested and tortured — reportedly flogged repeatedly — until she confessed that she and her mistress had practiced witchcraft. Her confession included claims of demonic pacts, magical ointments, and sacrifices, and it provided the evidence used against the accused group.

Execution and aftermath

While Kyteler escaped, reportedly fleeing to England with the help of powerful allies, Petronilla had no such protection. She was burned at the stake in Kilkenny on 3 November 1324. Other members of Kyteler's household and associates were variously imprisoned, whipped, or released. The case is notable for blending charges of heresy, sorcery, and demonic pacts decades before the great European witch-hunts of the early modern period.

Petronilla's fate is often cited as an example of the vulnerability of lower-status defendants in medieval witchcraft proceedings: the wealthy accused escaped, while her servant was executed.
