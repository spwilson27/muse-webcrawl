The National Health Service in England is the publicly funded healthcare system serving the population of England, and the largest of the four national health services of the United Kingdom. Founded in 1948 on the principle that care should be free at the point of use and based on clinical need, not ability to pay, it is one of the world's largest employers and a central institution of British public life.

Structure

The Department of Health and Social Care sets policy and funds the system; NHS England commissions and oversees care. Since the Health and Care Act 2022, services have been planned locally by 42 Integrated Care Boards, which buy care from NHS hospital trusts and foundation trusts, GP practices, dentists, pharmacists and independent providers. Primary care through general practitioners acts as the gatekeeper to most specialist and hospital services. Prescriptions in England carry a flat charge (with many exemptions), while prescriptions are free in Scotland and Wales — a famous asymmetry of devolved health policy.

Challenges

The NHS faces chronic pressures: an ageing population, rising chronic disease, workforce shortages and, after the COVID-19 pandemic, record waiting lists for elective treatment exceeding seven million pathways in England. Funding grows more slowly than demand, and successive reorganisations — the internal market of the 1990s, foundation trusts, the Lansley reforms of 2012 and the integration agenda of 2022 — have been repeatedly debated. Public attachment to the service remains exceptionally high, and its protection is a perennial pledge of all major parties.
