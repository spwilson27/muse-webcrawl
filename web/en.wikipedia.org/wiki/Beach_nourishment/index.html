Beach nourishment

Type: Soft coastal engineering
Purpose: Counter beach erosion, storm protection, recreation

Beach nourishment (also referred to as beach fill or beach replenishment) describes a process by which sediment, usually sand, lost through longshore drift or erosion is replaced from sources outside the eroding beach. It is a "soft" engineering approach to coastal defence, in contrast to hard structures such as seawalls and groynes.

Process

Sand is typically dredged from offshore deposits or quarried inland, transported by pipeline, barge, or truck, and spread along the upper beach and nearshore to widen and heighten the shore. The first recorded nourishment project in the United States was at Coney Island, New York, in 1922–23; large-scale programmes followed in the Netherlands, Germany, Florida, and Australia (including the Gold Coast).

Advantages and disadvantages

Nourishment restores recreational beach width and provides storm-buffering without the downdrift erosion caused by hard structures, and it can be adjusted as conditions change. Its drawbacks are cost, the need for repeated renourishment every few years as sand continues to move, disturbance of benthic and nesting wildlife (such as sea turtles), and the difficulty of finding borrow sand that matches the native beach in grain size and colour.
