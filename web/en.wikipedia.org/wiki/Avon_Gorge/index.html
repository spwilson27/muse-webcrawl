Avon Gorge

The Avon Gorge is a 1.5-mile (2.4 km) long gorge on the River Avon in Bristol, England. The gorge runs south to north through a limestone ridge close to the centre of the city, and is spanned by Isambard Kingdom Brunel's Clifton Suspension Bridge, completed in 1864, which is its best-known landmark.

Geology and landscape

The gorge was cut through Carboniferous limestone during the last ice age, probably by meltwater diverted when ice blocked the river's previous course. Steep wooded slopes rise on both sides; the western side carries the Leigh Woods National Nature Reserve, while the eastern side is overlooked by Clifton and the Downs. The river through the gorge is tidal, with one of the largest tidal ranges in the world, leaving wide mud banks at low water.

The gorge is a Site of Special Scientific Interest, noted for rare plants that grow on its cliffs, including the Bristol onion and endemic whitebeams found almost nowhere else. Peregrine falcons nest on the gorge walls, and the woods support dormice and a nationally important assemblage of ancient woodland plants.

The gorge was historically the route of Bristol's port traffic, with ships towed up to the city docks; the Portway road and a railway now run along its eastern bank.
