Decision theory

Decision theory is a branch of applied probability theory and analytic philosophy concerned with the logic, reasoning and optimality of choices. It studies how agents should (normative theory) and do (descriptive theory) choose among actions under uncertainty, risk and competing values.

Normative theory

The standard model maximises expected utility: following John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern (1944) and Leonard Savage (1954), a rational agent with preferences satisfying axioms (completeness, transitivity, independence) can be represented as maximising expected utility given probabilities, as in Bayes' theorem for belief updating. Related criteria include maximin and minimax regret under ignorance, and prospect theory's challenge is descriptive.

Descriptive theory

Behavioural research by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed systematic deviations—framing effects, loss aversion, probability weighting—summarised in prospect theory (1979). Bounded rationality (Herbert Simon) studies satisficing heuristics under limited information and computation.

Applications and extensions

Decision theory underlies economics, game theory (interdependent decisions), statistics (Bayesian decision rules, loss functions), medicine, law, AI alignment and public policy. Open problems include paradoxes (Allais, Ellsberg, Newcomb's problem, Pascal's mugging), evidential vs causal decision theory, and decisions under deep uncertainty and by groups.
