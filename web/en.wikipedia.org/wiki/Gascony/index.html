Gascony

Gascony (French: Gascogne; Gascon: Gasconha) is a historical and cultural region in southwest France, roughly corresponding to the area between the Garonne River and the Pyrenees, bounded by the Atlantic Ocean to the west. Its name derives from the Vascones, the ancestors of the Basques who settled there in late antiquity. Gascony is famed for its gastronomy — foie gras, Armagnac brandy, and duck dishes — and as the homeland of the musketeer d'Artagnan.

History

In the Roman era the region was part of Novempopulania. The Duchy of Gascony emerged under Frankish and then effectively independent rule, and in the 11th–12th centuries it passed to the Duchy of Aquitaine. Through Eleanor of Aquitaine's marriage to Henry II, Gascony came under English rule in 1154 and remained an English possession, with interruptions, until the end of the Hundred Years' War in 1453. Wine from Bordeaux and Bayonne was a major export to England throughout the period.

Language and culture

The traditional language is Gascon, a distinctive variety of Occitan with Basque substrate influence; it is now endangered but still spoken and taught. Gascony was historically administered from Auch, its ecclesiastical capital, while Bordeaux — on its northern fringe — was the great commercial port. The old province now spans parts of the Gers, Landes, Hautes-Pyrénées, and neighbouring departments.
