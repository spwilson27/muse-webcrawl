Cretan hieroglyphs

A seal stone bearing Cretan hieroglyphic signs

Cretan hieroglyphs are an undeciphered writing system used in early Bronze Age Crete during the Minoan civilisation, from roughly 2100 to 1450 BC. The script, named for its pictorial appearance reminiscent of Egyptian hieroglyphs, is attested on seals, medallions, and a small number of clay documents. It is one of three writing systems of ancient Crete, alongside Linear A and Linear B, and may be an ancestor of Linear A.

Corpus

The corpus comprises a few hundred documents, mostly seals and seal impressions, with notable concentrations at Knossos (the Hieroglyphic Deposit), Malia, and Petras. Unlike the clay-tablet archives of Linear A and B, Cretan hieroglyphic inscriptions are usually short, many apparently serving as seal legends or marks of ownership and authority. The standard catalogue of the corpus is the Corpus Hieroglyphicarum Inscriptionum Cretae (CHIC), published from 1996 onward.

Characteristics

Signs include human and animal figures, body parts, tools, vessels, plants, and celestial symbols. Scholars distinguish probable phonetic (syllabic) signs from logograms, some of which likely stand for commodities. Recurring sign groups on seals have been interpreted as names, titles, or formulaic phrases, but without a bilingual text or a known underlying language, decipherment has not been achieved; the language written is presumed to be the unknown "Minoan" language also written in Linear A.

Relation to other scripts

Cretan hieroglyphs and Linear A were in use simultaneously for a period, possibly for different purposes or regions. Some signs resemble Linear A signs, suggesting the hieroglyphic script contributed to the development of the linear scripts. The famous Phaistos Disc, stamped with pictorial signs, is sometimes compared with the hieroglyphs but is generally treated as a separate, unique artefact.
