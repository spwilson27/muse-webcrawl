Thought-terminating cliché

A thought-terminating cliché (also known as a semantic stop-sign) is a form of loaded language, often passing as folk wisdom, used to end an argument or enquiry, not by addressing it, but by making further discussion appear unnecessary or impossible. The term was popularised by psychiatrist Robert Jay Lifton in his 1961 book Thought Reform and the Psychology of Totalism.

Definition

Lifton described phrases such as "the broadest and most complex of human problems are compressed into brief, highly selective, definitive-sounding phrases, easily memorised and easily expressed" as part of the "language of non-thought" used by totalist environments. Examples in modern usage include "it is what it is", "boys will be boys", "everything happens for a reason", and "that's just your opinion".

Uses

The concept is used in the analysis of propaganda, cults, corporate jargon, and everyday rhetoric. Critics note that such phrases can serve benign conversational purposes, but that their repeated use in debate can shut down analysis, shift burdens of proof, and render critics inarticulate.
