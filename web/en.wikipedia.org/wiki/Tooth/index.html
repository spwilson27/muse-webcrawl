A tooth (pl. teeth) is a hard, calcified structure found in the jaws (or mouths) of many vertebrates, used to break down food, and sometimes as a weapon. Teeth are made of multiple tissues of varying density and hardness. In humans they are essential to chewing (mastication), speech, and appearance.

Structure

A human tooth consists of a crown, visible above the gum line, and one or more roots embedded in the jawbone. The outer layer of the crown is enamel, the hardest substance in the human body, composed almost entirely of hydroxyapatite crystals. Beneath it lies dentine, a softer calcified tissue that forms the bulk of the tooth. The centre contains the pulp, soft connective tissue carrying nerves and blood vessels. The root is covered by cementum, and the tooth is anchored in its socket by the periodontal ligament.

Types of human teeth

  • Incisors – eight sharp front teeth for cutting.
  • Canines – four pointed teeth for tearing.
  • Premolars – eight teeth for crushing and grinding.
  • Molars – twelve teeth (including wisdom teeth) with broad surfaces for grinding.

Humans develop two sets of teeth: 20 deciduous ("baby") teeth, which erupt in infancy and are shed in childhood, and 32 permanent teeth.

Development

Tooth development (odontogenesis) begins in the embryo. Primary teeth start erupting around six months of age; permanent teeth begin replacing them at about six years, with third molars ("wisdom teeth") erupting in late adolescence or adulthood, if at all.

Dental health

Common disorders include tooth decay (caries), caused by acid-producing bacteria feeding on sugars; gum disease (periodontitis); malocclusion; and tooth wear from erosion or grinding. Preventive care includes tooth brushing with fluoride toothpaste, interdental cleaning, and regular dental examinations.

Teeth in other animals

Tooth form reflects diet: carnivores have prominent canines and shearing carnassials, herbivores have large flat molars, and rodents have ever-growing incisors. Some animals, such as sharks, replace teeth continuously throughout life (polyphyodonty), while mammals are typically diphyodont, with two sets.
