A czapka (Polish pronunciation: [ˈt͡ʂapka]; plural: czapki) is a military cap of Polish origin, distinguished by its flat, square top. It became one of the most recognisable items of cavalry uniform in 19th-century Europe, worn by uhlans and lancers in many armies, and survives today in ceremonial dress, most famously in the rogatywka worn by the modern Polish armed forces.

History

The czapka developed in 18th-century Poland from traditional Polish headgear, in particular the square-topped caps worn by Polish nobility and soldiers. During the Napoleonic Wars, Polish lancers serving in French and allied armies made the cap famous across Europe. Its striking silhouette — a tall cap with a flat square top, a peak, and often a badge or sunburst plate at the front — was widely copied.

Uhlan regiments in the Prussian, Austrian, Russian, French and British armies all adopted versions of the czapka. The British lancer regiments wore it throughout the 19th century, and it featured in the Charge of the Light Brigade at Balaclava in 1854. In Germany it was known as the Tschapka.

Design

The classic czapka consists of a stiffened body rising from a band around the head, topped by a flat square "mortarboard" top that may overhang the body. A leather peak shades the eyes, and the front often bears a regimental badge, frequently in the form of a sunburst plate. Colours varied by regiment, and a coloured cloth top and piping were common distinctions. A chin chain or scales protected the wearer's face to a degree.

Modern use

Although the czapka disappeared from field service after the First World War, it survives ceremonially. The Polish Army's rogatywka — a soft version of the square-topped cap — is standard service dress headgear in Poland. Czapkas are also worn by ceremonial units and re-enactors, and by lancer-style regiments preserved in several countries.
