Clash of Civilizations

The Clash of Civilizations is a thesis in international relations proposed by political scientist Samuel P. Huntington, arguing that after the Cold War, people's cultural and religious identities would be the primary source of conflict in the world. First presented in a 1992 lecture and expanded in a 1993 article in Foreign Affairs titled "The Clash of Civilizations?", the argument was developed at book length in The Clash of Civilizations and the Remaking of World Order (1996).

The thesis

Huntington contended that the fundamental divisions of world politics would no longer be primarily ideological or economic, as during the Cold War, but cultural. He divided the world into major civilizations — including Western, Latin American, African, Islamic, Sinic (Chinese), Hindu, Orthodox, Buddhist, and Japanese — defined largely by religion, language, and shared history. Conflicts, he predicted, would occur most violently along the "fault lines" between civilizations, and states would rally to "kin countries" of the same civilization. He warned in particular of conflict between the West and the Islamic and Sinic worlds, and described Ukraine as a "cleft country" divided between Western and Orthodox civilizations.

Context and debate

The thesis was written partly in response to Francis Fukuyama's "end of history" argument that liberal democracy had triumphed as the final form of government. It attracted enormous attention, especially after the September 11 attacks, which many commentators read through Huntington's framework, though Huntington himself cautioned against treating the attacks as a war between civilizations.

Criticism

Critics, including Edward Said, accused Huntington of essentializing cultures, exaggerating the unity of civilizations, and ignoring conflicts within them; Said called the thesis a recycling of Orientalist stereotypes. Empirical studies of post–Cold War conflicts have produced mixed results, with many finding that civilizational differences are no stronger a predictor of conflict than other factors. Others have argued that states continue to act mainly from political and economic interest, and that the idea of a clash risks becoming a self-fulfilling prophecy.
