White Company

The White Company (Italian: Compagnia Bianca) was a company of mercenaries active in Italy in the fourteenth century. Although the name was used by several companies, the most famous was the English-led force commanded by Sir John Hawkwood from 1363, which became one of the most formidable and feared military units in the Italian peninsula during the era of the condottieri.

History

The original White Company formed in 1361 from soldiers discharged after the Treaty of Brétigny ended the first phase of the Hundred Years' War. Many were English and Gascon veterans, distinguished — according to one tradition — by polished armour and white surcoats, or by white banners. The company crossed the Alps into Piedmont and was hired by Italian city-states in their wars.

Under Hawkwood, the company fought for Pisa, Milan, the Papal States, and Florence, among others, switching employers as contracts demanded. Hawkwood's campaigns included the Battle of Cascina (1364). The company's discipline, combined-arms tactics with longbowmen and dismounted men-at-arms, and skilful manoeuvre made it unusually effective and rich through pay, ransoms, and blackmail payments (so-called "donatives") offered to spare cities from attack.

Composition

The company was organised in "lances," each centred on a man-at-arms with attendant archers and a page, and could field several thousand men at its peak. Its name inspired Arthur Conan Doyle's 1891 historical novel The White Company, about the Hundred Years' War.
