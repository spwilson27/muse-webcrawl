Fingal's Cave

Fingal's Cave is a sea cave on the uninhabited island of Staffa in the Inner Hebrides of Scotland, known for its extraordinary structure of hexagonal basalt columns and its natural acoustics. Formed from a Palaeocene lava flow, the cave is about 72 feet (22 m) high and 270 feet (82 m) deep.

Name and legend

The cave is named for the hero Fingal (Fionn mac Cumhaill) of Irish and Scottish Gaelic mythology, popularised by James Macpherson's Ossian poems in the 18th century. Its Gaelic name, An Uaimh Bhinn, means "the melodious cave", referring to the echoing sounds of the waves inside.

Cultural influence

Brought to wider attention by Sir Joseph Banks in 1772, the cave became a Romantic-era destination visited by Sir Walter Scott, William Wordsworth, and Queen Victoria. Felix Mendelssohn's 1829 visit inspired his concert overture The Hebrides (also known as Fingal's Cave), one of his most famous works. The similar columnar structure of the Giant's Causeway in Northern Ireland shares the same ancient lava field.
