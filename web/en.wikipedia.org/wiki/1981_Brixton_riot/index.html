The 1981 Brixton riot was a confrontation between the Metropolitan Police and predominantly Black protesters in Brixton, South London, between April 10 and 12, 1981. It was the most serious riot in London of the 20th century to that date, and it triggered an official inquiry whose report reshaped British thinking about policing and race.

Background

Brixton had a large Afro-Caribbean community and suffered high unemployment and poor housing. Relations with the police were poisoned by the "sus" law, under which officers could arrest people on suspicion of intent to commit an offence; in the week before the riot, Operation Swamp 81 flooded the area with plainclothes officers who stopped nearly a thousand people, most of them young Black men. Resentment was also fueled by the unresolved New Cross house fire of January 1981, in which 13 young Black people died.

The riot

Violence ignited on Friday, April 10, when a crowd gathered around police attending a stabbed Black youth, believing he was being arrested rather than helped. On Saturday, April 11, rioting escalated along Railton Road: police were pelted with bricks and petrol bombs—used for the first time on mainland Britain—and buildings and vehicles were set ablaze. By the end of the weekend, 279 police officers and at least 45 members of the public had been injured, 282 people arrested, and dozens of buildings damaged or destroyed, with damage estimated around £7.5 million.

Scarman Report and aftermath

Lord Scarman's inquiry, published in November 1981, attributed the disorder to policing failures, racial disadvantage, and social deprivation, though it rejected the charge of institutional racism. The "sus" law was repealed in 1981, and the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 later codified stop-and-search powers. Further riots followed in Brixton in 1985 and 1995.
