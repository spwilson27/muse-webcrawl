Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Nintendo Co., Ltd.

Case
Court: U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York
Decided: 1983–1984
Holding: For Nintendo; King Kong in the public domain

Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Nintendo Co., Ltd. was a 1983 United States District Court case in which Universal Studios sued Nintendo, claiming the arcade game Donkey Kong infringed Universal's trademark and copyright in King Kong. The court ruled for Nintendo.

Background

Nintendo released Donkey Kong in 1981; it became an enormous arcade success. In 1982 Universal demanded Nintendo cease selling the game and pay royalties. Nintendo's attorney, John Kirby, discovered that Universal had itself argued in a 1975 case (Universal City Studios v. RKO General) that the original 1933 King Kong story was in the public domain.

Decision

Judge Robert W. Sweet ruled in 1983 that Universal acted in bad faith, that King Kong's story was public domain material, and that in any event consumers were unlikely to confuse the comical Donkey Kong with King Kong. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit affirmed in 1984 and Nintendo recovered its legal costs. Nintendo later named its character Kirby after its victorious lawyer.

Significance

The case secured Nintendo of America's position in the U.S. video game market and is a frequently cited example of the risks of overreaching trademark claims against works based on public-domain material.
