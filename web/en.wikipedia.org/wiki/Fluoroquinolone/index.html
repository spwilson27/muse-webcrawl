Fluoroquinolone

Fluoroquinolones
ClassQuinolone antibiotics
ExamplesCiprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, ofloxacin

SpectrumBroad (gram-positive and gram-negative)

Fluoroquinolones are a class of broad-spectrum antibiotics that are effective against both gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria. They are distinguished from earlier quinolones by a fluorine atom at the 6-position of the quinolone ring. Important members include ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, and ofloxacin.

Mechanism of action

Fluoroquinolones kill bacteria by inhibiting two essential bacterial enzymes: DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) and topoisomerase IV. By stabilizing the enzyme–DNA cleavage complex, they block DNA replication and transcription, producing double-strand breaks and rapid bactericidal activity. Their action is concentration-dependent.

Medical uses

Fluoroquinolones have been widely used for urinary tract infections, respiratory tract infections, sexually transmitted infections, gastrointestinal infections, and anthrax. Because of safety concerns, regulators such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration advise reserving them for cases in which no alternative treatment options exist for uncomplicated infections such as sinusitis, bronchitis, and uncomplicated urinary tract infections.

Adverse effects

Fluoroquinolones carry boxed warnings for potentially disabling and irreversible adverse reactions, including tendinitis and tendon rupture, peripheral neuropathy, and central nervous system effects. They have also been associated with QT prolongation, dysglycemia, photosensitivity, Clostridioides difficile infection, and an increased risk of aortic aneurysm and dissection. Resistance to fluoroquinolones has risen steadily worldwide, driven by target-site mutations and plasmid-mediated resistance genes.

History

The class descends from nalidixic acid, introduced in the 1960s. The addition of a fluorine atom and a piperazine ring produced norfloxacin and then ciprofloxacin (1987), greatly expanding potency and tissue penetration. Successive "generations" broadened coverage against gram-positive and anaerobic organisms.
