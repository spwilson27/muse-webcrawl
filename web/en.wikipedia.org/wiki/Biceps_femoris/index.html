Biceps femoris

OriginLong head: ischial tuberosity; Short head: linea aspera of femur
InsertionHead of the fibula
ArteryPerforating arteries, inferior gluteal artery
NerveLong head: tibial division of sciatic nerve; Short head: common fibular division
ActionsFlexes knee, laterally rotates tibia, extends hip (long head)
AntagonistQuadriceps femoris

The biceps femoris is a muscle of the posterior compartment of the thigh. As its name implies, it has two heads — a long head and a short head — which unite to form a single tendon. Together with the semitendinosus and semimembranosus, it forms the group of muscles known as the hamstrings.

Structure

The long head arises from the inferomedial part of the ischial tuberosity of the pelvis, sharing a common tendon with the semitendinosus. The short head arises from the lateral lip of the linea aspera and the lateral supracondylar line of the femur. The two heads converge into a tendon that inserts on the lateral side of the head of the fibula, splitting around the fibular collateral ligament of the knee.

Nerve supply

Unusually for a single muscle, the two heads have different nerve supplies: the long head is supplied by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve (root values S1–S3), while the short head is supplied by the common fibular (peroneal) division (L5–S2). This dual innervation reflects the composite developmental origin of the muscle.

Function

Both heads flex the knee joint. The long head, crossing the hip joint, also extends the hip and assists in lateral rotation of the thigh. When the knee is flexed, the biceps femoris laterally rotates the tibia on the femur. The muscle is important in walking, running, and jumping, and helps stabilise the pelvis during trunk flexion.

Clinical significance

Hamstring strains involving the biceps femoris are among the most common injuries in sprinting and field sports; the long head is the most frequently injured hamstring muscle. Avulsion of the fibular head insertion can be associated with injury to the common fibular nerve, which winds around the neck of the fibula nearby.
