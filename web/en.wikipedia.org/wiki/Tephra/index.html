Tephra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Distinct layers of tephra from successive eruptions preserved in a deposit.

Tephra is fragmental material produced by a volcanic eruption regardless of composition, fragment size, or emplacement mechanism. Volcanologists also refer to airborne fragments as pyroclasts. Once clasts have fallen to the ground, they remain as tephra unless hot enough to fuse into pyroclastic rock or tuff.

Classification

Tephra fragments are classified by size:

Clast sizePyroclastMainly unconsolidated (tephra)Mainly consolidated
> 64 mmBomb, blockBlocks; bombsAgglomerate; breccia
2–64 mmLapillusLapilliLapillistone
< 2 mmAshAshAsh tuff

Volcanic ash consists of fragments smaller than 2 mm; lapilli are pea- to walnut-sized fragments; and bombs and blocks exceed 64 mm, bombs having been molten or plastic while airborne.

Formation and dispersal

Tephra is produced when magma inside a volcano is fragmented by the explosive expansion of volcanic gases. Explosive eruptions can inject tephra into the stratosphere, where fine ash may circle the globe and affect climate. Deposits thin and fine downwind, forming distinctive layers that geologists use as marker horizons.

Tephrochronology

Because each eruption produces tephra with a distinctive chemical fingerprint, buried tephra layers can be used to date and correlate sediments and archaeological sites across wide regions — a technique called tephrochronology. Well-known marker layers include the Mazama ash from the eruption that formed Crater Lake and the widespread deposits of the Toba supereruption.
