Black-necked stilt

Black-necked stilt (Himantopus mexicanus).
Scientific nameHimantopus mexicanus
FamilyRecurvirostridae

The black-necked stilt (Himantopus mexicanus) is a locally abundant shorebird of American wetlands and coastlines. Taxonomically, some treatments place it as a subspecies of the black-winged stilt (Himantopus himantopus mexicanus), while others recognise it as a full species.

Description

Adults are 35–39 cm long with strikingly long, thin pink-to-red legs — proportionally among the longest of any bird relative to body length. They have black upperparts (including the back of the neck and a cap above the eye), white underparts, white cheeks and forehead, a long, thin, straight black bill, and red eyes. Males have a glossy greenish sheen to the black; females are tinged brown. Immatures are browner above.

Distribution and behaviour

The species breeds in shallow wetlands — salt ponds, marshes, mudflats and flooded fields — from the western and southern United States through Mexico, the Caribbean and Central America to northern South America, with a distinct Hawaiian population, the ae'o (H. m. knudseni), which is federally endangered. Northern populations migrate south in winter. Black-necked stilts wade in shallow water picking aquatic invertebrates from the surface or probing in mud, and they feed in loose flocks. They nest on the ground near water on small islands or tussocks, laying typically four eggs; colony members mob predators noisily to defend nests and chicks.
