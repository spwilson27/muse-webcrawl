During the Second World War, the Waffen-SS recruited significant numbers of foreign volunteers and conscripts. From "Germanic" volunteers from Northern and Western Europe in the early war years, recruitment expanded to include ethnic Germans (Volksdeutsche) from Eastern and Southeastern Europe, and later — increasingly by conscription — Latvians, Estonians, Ukrainians, Russians, and others, as well as small units drawn from more distant populations. By 1944, a large share of Waffen-SS strength was non-German. Heinrich Himmler promoted the vision of a pan-European, anti-Bolshevik army, although racial ideology, pragmatism, and manpower needs shaped recruitment in contradictory ways.

Recruitment by region

Early foreign formations included the Wiking Division, manned partly by Dutch, Flemish, Danish, Norwegian, Swedish, and Finnish volunteers. Separate national legions were raised in the Netherlands, Flanders, Denmark (Frikorps Danmark), Norway, France (later the Charlemagne Division), and Wallonia under Léon Degrelle. In the Baltic states, the Latvian Legion and the 20th Waffen Grenadier Division (Estonian) were formed largely by conscription from 1943. In the Balkans and Eastern Europe, divisions were raised among Bosnian Muslims (Handschar), Albanians (Skanderbeg), ethnic Germans, Hungarians, and others, while the Galician Division recruited Ukrainians and the Kaminski and Russian (RONA) formations drew on Soviet citizens. A small Indian Legion (attached to the Wehrmacht, later transferred to the Waffen-SS) and a British Free Corps of a few dozen men also existed.

Motivations and coercion

Motivations ranged from ideological sympathy with Nazism or anti-communism, nationalism and hopes of independence, adventure, and material incentives, to outright compulsion: in many occupied territories, especially from 1943, "volunteering" shaded into conscription, forced labour drafts, or enlistment under duress. Many units were used primarily against partisans in the Balkans and Eastern Europe, where Waffen-SS and associated formations committed numerous war crimes; some foreign units also fought at the front, including in the final battles of 1945.

Aftermath

At the Nuremberg trials, the SS, including the Waffen-SS, was declared a criminal organisation, with an exemption for conscripts who had committed no crimes. The fate of foreign personnel varied widely: Western European volunteers were tried for collaboration in their home countries; many Baltic and Ukrainian veterans emigrated to Western countries, where their service remains politically contentious; Soviet citizens were often repatriated to the USSR and punished. The memory politics of foreign SS service remain debated in several countries.
