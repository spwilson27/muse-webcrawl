History of cities

The history of cities traces the development of human settlements from small agricultural villages into large, dense urban centres. Cities arose independently in several regions of the world — Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley, China, Mesoamerica and the Andes — typically where fertile land, water supplies and trade routes converged. The study of urban history overlaps with archaeology, geography and sociology.

Origins

The Neolithic Revolution, beginning around 10,000 BCE, allowed permanent settlements such as Jericho and Çatalhöyük. By the fourth millennium BCE, Sumerian cities such as Uruk had grown into true cities with temples, administrative bureaucracies, specialised labour and populations in the tens of thousands. Writing, first developed in Mesopotamia, appears to have been driven partly by the administrative needs of these early urban societies.

Classical antiquity

Greek city-states (poleis) such as Athens and Sparta developed distinctive political institutions, while Rome grew into an empire whose capital exceeded one million inhabitants. Roman urban planning — grids, aqueducts, sewers, forums and baths — set templates copied across Europe, North Africa and the Near East. In China, Chang'an became one of the largest cities of the early medieval world, and in Mesoamerica, Teotihuacan and later Tenochtitlan were among the largest cities on Earth.

Medieval and early modern cities

After the fall of Rome, European urban populations contracted, while cities in the Islamic world — Baghdad, Córdoba, Cairo — flourished as centres of trade and learning. From the eleventh century, European towns revived around markets, cathedrals and universities; many obtained charters of self-government. The growth of long-distance trade enriched port cities such as Venice, Genoa and the Hanseatic towns of northern Europe.

Industrialisation

The Industrial Revolution transformed cities on an unprecedented scale. Factory production drew rural migrants into manufacturing towns such as Manchester, and populations exploded: London grew from about one million in 1800 to over six million by 1900. Crowding, pollution and disease provoked reforms in sanitation, housing and municipal government, and inspired planned suburbs, garden cities and the first modern town planning movements.

Twentieth century and beyond

In the twentieth century urbanisation became global. Motor transport enabled suburbanisation and sprawl, while high-rise construction reshaped skylines. After the Second World War, the fastest urban growth shifted to Asia, Africa and Latin America, producing megacities of more than ten million people. In 2008 the world's urban population surpassed its rural population for the first time. Contemporary issues include sustainability, informal settlements, gentrification and the rise of the global city as a node in international finance and culture.
