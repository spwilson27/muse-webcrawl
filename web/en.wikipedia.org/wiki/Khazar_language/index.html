The Khazar language is the extinct language spoken by the Khazars, a semi-nomadic Turkic people who ruled a large empire in the Pontic–Caspian steppe between the 7th and 10th centuries. The language is poorly attested: no continuous texts in Khazar survive, and it is known only from personal names, tribal names, titles and place names recorded in Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, Greek and other sources, plus a handful of words glossed by medieval authors.

Classification

Khazar is conventionally classified as a Turkic language. Historians debate whether it belonged to the Oghuric branch (like Bulgar and modern Chuvash) or the Common Turkic branch, or whether the Khaganate was multilingual, with different tribes speaking different Turkic varieties alongside Iranian and other languages. The 10th-century Persian geographer al-Istakhri noted that the Khazar language differed from Turkish and Persian and resembled that of the Bulgars.

Attestation

Sources include the correspondence around Khazaria in Hebrew (such as the Kievan Letter, c. 930, which contains a Turkic runiform word possibly reading "I have read [it]"), names of Khazar rulers and officials (e.g. Bulan, Obadiah, and titles such as khagan, beg, tudun), and words preserved by the geographer al-Masudi and others. The Khazars reportedly used a runic-type script derived from the Old Turkic runiform tradition, though almost no inscriptions are securely Khazar.

History

After the fall of the Khaganate to Kievan Rus' and other powers in the late 10th century, the Khazar language disappeared, its speakers assimilated into surrounding Turkic, Slavic and other populations. Theories connecting Khazar to the origins of some Jewish communities in Eastern Europe remain speculative and contested.
