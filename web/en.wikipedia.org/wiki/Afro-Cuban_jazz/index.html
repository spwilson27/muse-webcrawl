Afro-Cuban jazz

Afro-Cuban jazz
Other namesCubop
OriginsCuban music, jazz (1940s)
Typical instrumentsConga, timbales, bongó, piano, trumpet, saxophone, bass
Key figuresMachito, Dizzy Gillespie, Chano Pozo, Mario Bauzá, Mongo Santamaría

Afro-Cuban jazz is the earliest form of Latin jazz, a genre developed in the 1940s from the fusion of jazz with the Afro-Cuban rhythms of son, mambo, rumba, and cha-cha-chá. Its foundation is the clave — a two-bar rhythmic pattern of African origin that organizes the interplay of all the parts.

Origins

The fusion crystallized in New York in 1947 when bebop trumpeter Dizzy Gillespie collaborated with the Cuban conga virtuoso Chano Pozo in Gillespie's big band, producing pieces such as "Manteca", "A Night in Tunisia", and "Cubana Be/Cubana Bop" (arranged by George Russell). Machito and his Afro-Cubans, with musical director Mario Bauzá, had already been blending Cuban rhythms with jazz phrasing in Harlem since the early 1940s.

Development

The 1950s saw the mambo craze bring figures such as Pérez Prado, Tito Puente, and Mongo Santamaría to wider audiences, while hard-bop musicians including Art Blakey absorbed Afro-Cuban elements (notably on Orgy in Rhythm and "A Night in Tunisia"). In the 1960s and 1970s, bossa nova and later salsa and Latin jazz ensembles — Irakere in Cuba, Cal Tjader and Eddie Palmieri in New York — carried the tradition forward, alongside the descarga (jam session) culture.

Characteristics

Afro-Cuban jazz is distinguished by layered polyrhythmic percussion (conga, timbales, bongó, güiro, claves), piano montunos, syncopated bass lines (tumbao), and jazz improvisation and harmony played over clave-based forms. Standard instrumentation adds the Cuban percussion battery to a conventional jazz horn section.

Later developments

From the 1980s the music expanded into the broader Latin jazz umbrella, incorporating Brazilian, Caribbean, and American influences. Artists such as Arturo Sandoval, Paquito D'Rivera, Chucho Valdés, and Gonzalo Rubalcaba have carried Afro-Cuban jazz into the 21st century, and it remains a staple of jazz education and festival programming worldwide.
