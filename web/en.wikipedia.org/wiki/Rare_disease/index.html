Rare disease

A rare disease is any disease that affects only a small percentage of the population. Definitions vary by jurisdiction: in the United States, the Orphan Drug Act of 1983 defines a rare disease as one affecting fewer than 200,000 people nationally; in the European Union, a disease is rare if it affects fewer than 1 in 2,000 people.

Scale

There are an estimated 5,000–8,000 distinct rare diseases, most of them genetic in origin. Although each is individually uncommon, collectively they affect tens of millions of people: roughly 1 in 10 Americans and about 30 million people in the EU are estimated to live with a rare disease. Examples include cystic fibrosis, Huntington's disease, Duchenne muscular dystrophy and many rare cancers and metabolic disorders.

Orphan drugs

Because small patient populations make drug development commercially unattractive, many jurisdictions offer incentives for "orphan drugs" — fee reductions, tax credits and periods of market exclusivity — under legislation such as the US Orphan Drug Act and the EU orphan medicinal products regulation.

Awareness

Rare Disease Day is observed annually on the last day of February to raise awareness among policymakers and the public. Patient organisations such as NORD (US) and EURORDIS (Europe) coordinate advocacy, registries and research.
