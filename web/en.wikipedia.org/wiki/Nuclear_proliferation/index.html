Nuclear proliferation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nuclear proliferation is the spread of nuclear weapons, fissionable material and weapons-applicable nuclear technology and information to nations not recognised as "Nuclear Weapon States" by the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT, 1968), or more broadly any spread beyond the original five possessors. The NPT — the cornerstone of the nonproliferation regime — pairs non-possessors' renunciation with disarmament obligations on the five recognised states (China, France, Russia, the UK, the US) and guarantees of peaceful nuclear technology under IAEA safeguards.

History

After the US monopoly (1945–49), the USSR, UK, France and China followed. Israel developed an undeclared capability (Dimona) outside the NPT; India (1974/1998), Pakistan (1998) and North Korea (2006, after withdrawing from the NPT) tested outside or against the regime; South Africa built and dismantled weapons before joining. Programmes in Iraq, Libya, Syria and Iran generated crises handled by inspections, sanctions, diplomacy (the 2015 JCPOA on Iran, from which the US withdrew in 2018) and, in Iraq's case (2003), a war justified on erroneous proliferation claims. A. Q. Khan's network exposed the role of clandestine supply chains; counter-proliferation tools include export controls (Nuclear Suppliers Group), interdiction, security assurances and extended deterrence — which itself embeds "virtual" proliferation choices by allies.

Debates

Analysts dispute whether proliferation stabilises (mutual deterrence optimists, after Kenneth Waltz) or multiplies accident and escalation risks (organisational pessimists, after Scott Sagan), and whether the regime's discriminatory structure can hold as arsenals modernise and norms against use (the "nuclear taboo") are tested by threats in recent wars.
