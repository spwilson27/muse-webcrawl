The Guaporé River (Portuguese: Rio Guaporé; Spanish: Río Iténez) is a river in western Brazil and northeastern Bolivia. It is a tributary of the Mamoré River, and rises on the Parecis plateau in the Brazilian state of Mato Grosso, about 150 kilometres from Pontes e Lacerda. It flows westward and then northwest, forming a long stretch of the border between Brazil and Bolivia, before joining the Mamoré near the Bolivian town of Guayaramerín's upriver reach.

Course

The river runs for about 1,530 kilometres. From its headwaters in the Chapada dos Parecis it descends to the Amazon lowlands and meanders through seasonally flooded savanna and forest, including the region of Brazil's Guaporé Biological Reserve and Bolivia's Noel Kempff Mercado hinterland. Important tributaries include the Rio Branco, the Rio Corumbiara and the Río Blanco and Río Negro in Bolivia.

History

The Guaporé valley was a contested frontier between the Spanish and Portuguese empires; the Treaty of Madrid (1750) drew the boundary largely along the river, a line broadly retained in the modern Brazil–Bolivia border. In the 18th century the Portuguese built the fortress of Forte Príncipe da Beira on its banks to guard the route to the goldfields of Mato Grosso. The river later served rubber tappers during the Amazon rubber boom.

Ecology

The Guaporé is noted for its rich aquatic fauna, including the Amazon river dolphin (boto), giant otter, caimans and a great diversity of fish. Its relatively undisturbed floodplains are an important refuge for wildlife in the southwestern Amazon.
