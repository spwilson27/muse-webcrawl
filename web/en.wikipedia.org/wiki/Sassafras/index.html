Sassafras

Sassafras is a genus of three extant (and one extinct) species of deciduous trees in the family Lauraceae, native to eastern North America and eastern Asia. The best-known species, Sassafras albidum, is famous for its aromatic bark and roots — once the defining flavour of root beer — and for its unusual leaves, which occur in three shapes on the same tree: oval, mitten-shaped, and three-lobed.

Sassafras leaves, showing the genus's characteristic varied shapes, turn brilliant orange and red in autumn.

Description

Sassafras trees grow 9–35 m tall with spreading, often suckering habits. All parts of the plant are fragrant. The small yellow-green flowers appear in spring before the leaves; female trees bear dark blue drupes on red stalks. In autumn the foliage colours vividly. The leaves are an important food plant for the spicebush swallowtail butterfly.

Uses

Native American peoples used sassafras medicinally and as a spice long before Europeans, who in the 16th and 17th centuries exported it to Europe as a supposed cure-all — for a time it was among the most valuable American exports after tobacco. Root bark tea and root beer were flavoured with sassafras oil, whose main component, safrole, was found to be carcinogenic in animal studies; the U.S. Food and Drug Administration banned safrole-containing sassafras oil as a food additive in 1960, and modern root beers use safrole-free extracts or artificial flavourings. Ground sassafras leaves — filé powder — remain a traditional thickener and seasoning in Creole gumbo.
