School of Alexandria

Alexandria, centre of Hellenistic and early Christian learning

The School of Alexandria is a term used for several related traditions of learning centred in Alexandria, Egypt — first the Hellenistic scholarship associated with the great Library and Mouseion, and later the Christian catechetical school that flourished from the 2nd to the 5th centuries AD.

Hellenistic scholarship

Under the Ptolemies, Alexandria became the intellectual capital of the Greek world. Scholars at the Library, including Zenodotus, Callimachus, Eratosthenes, and Aristarchus of Samothrace, pioneered textual criticism of Homer, bibliography, geography, and astronomy.

The catechetical school

Tradition holds that a Christian teaching institution, the Didascalium, was founded in Alexandria in the late 2nd century. Its celebrated heads included Pantaenus, Clement of Alexandria, and Origen. The Alexandrian school favoured an allegorical interpretation of Scripture and sought to reconcile Christian faith with Greek philosophy, particularly Platonism.

Rivalry with Antioch

Alexandria's allegorical, Christologically "high" approach contrasted with the literal-historical exegesis of the School of Antioch. Their rivalry shaped the Christological controversies of the 4th and 5th centuries, culminating in the Council of Ephesus (431) and the Council of Chalcedon (451).
