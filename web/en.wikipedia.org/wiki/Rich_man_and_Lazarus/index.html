Rich man and Lazarus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The rich man and Lazarus (also called the parable of Dives and Lazarus) is a parable of Jesus told in the Gospel of Luke (16:19–31). It describes a wealthy man who feasts daily while a beggar named Lazarus lies hungry at his gate; after death their fortunes are reversed, Lazarus being comforted in Abraham's bosom while the rich man suffers torment and is refused relief.

The story

In the parable, the rich man — in later tradition called Dives, Latin for "rich" — is clothed in purple and fine linen and feasts every day. Lazarus, covered with sores, longs for the crumbs from his table, and dogs lick his sores. Both die. Lazarus is carried by angels to Abraham's side; the rich man, in Hades, sees him from afar and begs Abraham first for a drop of water, and then to send Lazarus to warn his five brothers. Abraham refuses both requests, explaining the great chasm fixed between them and answering the second plea: "They have Moses and the Prophets; let them listen to them… If they do not listen to Moses and the Prophets, they will not be convinced even if someone rises from the dead."

Interpretation

The parable is unusual among Jesus's parables in naming a character, and in being set partly in the afterlife. It is often read as a warning about wealth, indifference to the poor, and the irreversibility of choices after death; its closing line is frequently connected by interpreters with the resurrection. The story shares motifs with earlier folk tales, including an Egyptian tale and Jewish stories of the afterlife reversal of a rich tax collector and a poor scholar.

Legacy

The name Lazarus passed into common usage: medieval lazar houses (leper hospitals) and the word lazaretto derive from it, as does the beggars' order of St Lazarus. The parable has been depicted frequently in medieval art and is commemorated in hymns and in the name of the military and hospitaller Order of Saint Lazarus.
