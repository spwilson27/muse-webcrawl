Gallon

The gallon is a unit of volume in the British imperial and United States customary systems of measurement. Three significantly different sizes are in current use: the imperial gallon (4.54609 litres), defined in the United Kingdom and used in some Commonwealth and Caribbean countries; the US gallon (3.785411784 litres), used in the United States and Latin America; and the US dry gallon (4.40488377086 litres), which is little used.

The imperial gallon was originally defined as the volume of 10 pounds of distilled water weighed in air at 62 °F; it was redefined in the United Kingdom in 1985 as exactly 4.54609 litres. The US gallon is legally defined as 231 cubic inches. In both systems the gallon is divided into four quarts and eight pints, but the sizes of those subdivisions differ between the systems, as do the fluid ounces they contain.

History

The term derives from Old Northern French galun, a liquid measure. Historically, many different gallons coexisted in England, including the wine gallon of 231 cubic inches (standardised by Queen Anne in 1707 and inherited by the United States), the ale gallon of 282 cubic inches, and the Winchester corn gallon. The British Weights and Measures Act 1824 abolished these in favour of a single imperial gallon. Canada converted to litres for most purposes in the 1970s, but the imperial gallon survives in everyday use in parts of the Caribbean and in fuel-economy ratings.

Usage

Petrol is sold by the gallon in the United States, Liberia, and a number of Latin American and Caribbean countries, while most former British territories have adopted the litre. Fuel economy in the US is expressed in miles per gallon (mpg); in the UK the same unit is used but refers to the larger imperial gallon, causing figures that differ from American ones for the same vehicle.
