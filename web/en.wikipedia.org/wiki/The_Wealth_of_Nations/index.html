The Wealth of Nations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, generally referred to by its shortened title The Wealth of Nations, is the magnum opus of the Scottish economist and moral philosopher Adam Smith (1723–1790). Published on 9 March 1776, during the Scottish Enlightenment and the beginning of the Industrial Revolution, it founded modern economics as a discipline and remains its most famous classic.

Contents

Book I grounds wealth in the division of labour (the pin factory), exchange and the extent of the market, and develops a cost-of-production theory of price; Book II treats capital accumulation; Book III the historical progress of opulence; Book IV attacks the mercantile system and the physiocrats, arguing that consumption is the sole end of production and that trade restrictions serve producers against consumers; Book V addresses the proper duties of the sovereign — defence, justice, public works and institutions (including education) that profit could not supply. Its butcher–brewer–baker passage on self-interest, and the single famous use of the "invisible hand", have been quoted ever since — the latter inflated in popular retelling far beyond Smith's own emphasis, as scholars note; Smith himself assigned government substantial roles and distrusted merchants' conspiracies against the public.
