Chukchi is a Chukotko-Kamchatkan language spoken by the Chukchi people in the easternmost extremity of Siberia, mainly in Chukotka Autonomous Okrug. According to Russian census figures, a minority of ethnic Chukchi — roughly five thousand people — speak the language, and fluency is concentrated among older generations; UNESCO classifies Chukchi as endangered.

Classification and related languages

Chukchi is the largest member of the Chukotkan branch of the Chukotko-Kamchatkan family, closely related to Koryak, Alutor, and Kerek. The family is sometimes grouped with other Siberian languages under the typological label "Paleo-Siberian", which does not imply a genetic relationship.

Grammar

Chukchi is a polysynthetic and incorporating language with ergative–absolutive alignment. Nouns incorporate into verbs, producing complex single words expressing what other languages render as whole sentences. The language distinguishes men's and women's pronunciations, which differ systematically in certain consonants — a well-known sociolinguistic feature in which women's speech replaces some sounds (for example /rk/ where men have /cc/). Chukchi has vowel harmony and a relatively small consonant inventory with ejective consonants.

Writing system

A written form of Chukchi was created in the early 1930s, first in a Latin-based alphabet associated with the scholar Vladimir Bogoraz, and converted to Cyrillic in 1937. The Cyrillic alphabet includes the special letters Ԓ and ԓ for the voiceless lateral fricative. Literature, school textbooks, and a newspaper have been published in Chukchi, though Russian dominates education and media.

Status

Intergenerational transmission has weakened since the Soviet era, when boarding-school education in Russian interrupted home use of the language. Revitalisation efforts include Chukchi-language teaching in some schools, broadcasting, and documentation projects by linguists.
