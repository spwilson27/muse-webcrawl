Climate of India

The climate of India consists of a wide range of weather conditions across its vast geographic scale and varied topography. Under the Köppen classification, India hosts six major climatic subtypes, from arid desert in the west and alpine tundra in the Himalayas to humid tropical regions supporting rainforests in the southwest and the island territories. The country's climate is dominated by the Indian monsoon, a seasonal reversal of winds that delivers the great majority of the annual rainfall.

Seasons

The India Meteorological Department recognizes four seasons: winter (December to February), summer or pre-monsoon (March to May), the southwest monsoon season (June to September), and the post-monsoon or northeast monsoon season (October to November). Summer temperatures on the northern plains regularly exceed 45 °C, and Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in Meghalaya, on the windward side of the Khasi Hills, are among the wettest places on Earth, receiving around 11,000 mm of rain annually.

The monsoon

The southwest monsoon, drawn by the intense heating of the subcontinent and the Tibetan Plateau, arrives at the Kerala coast around the beginning of June and advances northward, supplying about three-quarters of India's annual rainfall; agriculture, which employs a large share of the population, depends heavily on its timing and adequacy. The retreating northeast monsoon brings significant rain to Tamil Nadu and the southeastern coast in autumn, while western disturbances from the Mediterranean bring winter rain and Himalayan snow to the northwest.

Extremes and hazards

India is exposed to tropical cyclones in the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea, devastating floods along its great rivers, and droughts in its rain-shadow interiors. The Thar Desert in Rajasthan is among the hottest regions on Earth, while Dras in Ladakh records some of the coldest inhabited temperatures in the world. Climate change is intensifying heatwaves and extreme rainfall events across the subcontinent.
