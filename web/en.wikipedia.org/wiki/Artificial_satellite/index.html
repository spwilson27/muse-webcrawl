Artificial satellite

An artificial satellite is an object intentionally placed into orbit by human beings. Satellites are launched by rockets to altitudes ranging from a few hundred kilometres (low Earth orbit) to 35,786 km (geostationary orbit) and beyond, and are used for communications, Earth observation, navigation, weather forecasting, scientific research and military purposes. The first artificial satellite was the Soviet Union's Sputnik 1, launched on 4 October 1957.

History

The idea of an artificial satellite was explored in fiction by Edward Everett Hale ("The Brick Moon," 1869) and given practical form by Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, Hermann Oberth and others in the early twentieth century; in 1945 Arthur C. Clarke proposed geostationary communications relays. Sputnik 1's launch opened the Space Race. Explorer 1 (1958) was the first United States satellite; Telstar 1 (1962) pioneered active communications relay; and the 1960s–70s saw the first weather, navigation and reconnaissance systems. Since the 2010s, large commercial constellations of small satellites, such as Starlink, have sharply increased the number of objects launched.

Types and orbits

Satellites are classified by function: communications satellites relay television, telephone and internet traffic; Earth-observation satellites image the surface for mapping, environmental monitoring and intelligence; navigation satellites (such as GPS, GLONASS, Galileo and BeiDou) provide positioning; weather satellites monitor the atmosphere; and scientific satellites include space telescopes. Common orbits include low Earth orbit (LEO), medium Earth orbit (MEO), geostationary orbit (GEO), polar and sun-synchronous orbits, and highly elliptical Molniya orbits.

Structure

A satellite consists of a bus (structure, power from solar panels and batteries, propulsion, thermal control and attitude control) and a payload specific to its mission. Satellites are tracked by networks of ground stations; thousands of active satellites and far larger numbers of pieces of debris are catalogued in orbit.

End of life and debris

Defunct satellites and fragments pose a growing collision hazard known as space debris. Guidelines call for deorbiting low-orbit satellites into the atmosphere or moving geostationary satellites to a "graveyard orbit" at end of life. The 2009 collision between Iridium 33 and Kosmos 2251 illustrated the risks of orbital congestion.
