The FIS Alpine Ski World Cup is the top international circuit of alpine skiing competitions, held annually since 1967 under the International Ski and Snowboard Federation (FIS). Skiers accumulate points across a season of races — downhill, super-G, giant slalom and slalom — held mainly at European and North American resorts from late October to March. The skier with the most points in each discipline, and overall, wins a crystal globe trophy.

History

The circuit was conceived in 1966 by French journalist Serge Lang, French coach Honoré Bonnet and American coach Bob Beattie, and launched in January 1967 with FIS endorsement from president Marc Hodler. Jean-Claude Killy of France and Nancy Greene of Canada won the first overall titles. Iconic venues include the Hahnenkamm downhill at Kitzbühel, Austria — widely considered the most demanding course — the Lauberhorn at Wengen, Switzerland, and the season-opening glacier races at Sölden. The World Cup sits above the biennial World Championships and the quadrennial Winter Olympics in frequency, and for many racers the overall globe is the sport's most prestigious season-long prize.

Scoring

Points are awarded to the top 30 finishers in each race on a scale running 100–80–60–50–45–40–36–32–29–26–24–22 down to 1 point for 30th place. Separate standings are kept for each discipline, and the overall title combines all events. Since the 1990s the circuit has also awarded a Nations Cup for the best-performing national team.

Records

Sweden's Ingemar Stenmark won a record 86 World Cup races (1974–1989), a mark approached and passed in the 2020s by American Mikaela Shiffrin, who became the most successful World Cup skier of all time. Austria's Marcel Hirscher won eight consecutive men's overall titles (2012–2019), while Annemarie Moser-Pröll and Shiffrin headline the women's overall records; Lindsey Vonn won 82 races and four overall titles. Hermann Maier, Aksel Lund Svindal and, in the 2020s, Marco Odermatt are among the dominant men's racers of their eras.
