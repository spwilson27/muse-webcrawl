Indus script

The Indus script, also called the Harappan script, is a corpus of symbols produced by the Indus Valley Civilisation of the Bronze Age (roughly 2600–1900 BCE). The symbols appear most often on small steatite seals, as well as on pottery, tablets, and other artefacts. Despite more than a century of study since their discovery in the 1920s, the script remains undeciphered.

Characteristics

Scholars have identified between about 400 and 600 distinct signs, a number that suggests a logosyllabic system — one combining word signs and syllabic signs — rather than a pure alphabet or syllabary. Inscriptions are very short: the average inscription has about five signs, and the longest known has only about 26. Most inscriptions appear to read right to left, based on the spacing and overlapping of signs. A famous large inscription, sometimes called the Dholavira signboard, consists of ten large signs set into a wooden board above a city gate.

Decipherment attempts

No bilingual inscription (such as the Rosetta Stone for Egyptian hieroglyphs) has been found, the underlying language is unknown, and the brevity of the texts makes decipherment exceptionally difficult. Proposed readings have linked the script to Dravidian languages, to early Indo-Aryan, to Munda languages, and to a lost language isolate; none has won scholarly consensus. Some researchers have even argued that the symbols do not encode language at all but form a non-linguistic sign system, a claim other specialists dispute.

Corpus

Several thousand inscribed objects have been catalogued, mainly from the major cities of Mohenjo-daro and Harappa and from sites across modern Pakistan and north-western India. The seals, often carved with animal motifs such as the so-called "unicorn", are thought to have been used in trade and administration.
