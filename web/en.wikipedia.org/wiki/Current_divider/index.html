Current divider

In electronics, a current divider is a simple linear circuit that produces an output current which is a fraction of its input current. Current division is the act of splitting the current between the branches of the divider. The currents in the various branches of such a circuit will always divide in such a way as to minimize the total energy expended.

Current divider rule

For two impedances Z1 and Z2 connected in parallel, with total current I entering the combination, the current through Z1 is:

I1 = I · Z2 / (Z1 + Z2)

That is, the current through a branch equals the total current multiplied by the ratio of the other branch's impedance to the sum of the impedances. For purely resistive branches this becomes I1 = I · R2 / (R1 + R2). Equivalently, in terms of conductances, current divides in direct proportion to conductance — current prefers the path of least resistance.

General case

The rule generalizes to any number of parallel branches: the current through branch X is the total current times the equivalent impedance of all the other branches in parallel, divided by the sum of that equivalent impedance and the impedance of branch X. The formula is derived from Kirchhoff's current law together with the fact that all parallel branches share the same voltage.

Applications

Current dividers are used in measurement circuits, where a shunt resistor diverts a known fraction of current around a sensitive galvanometer or ammeter movement so that large currents can be measured; in biasing networks; and in current mirrors and sensor interfaces in analog integrated circuits. The concept is the dual of the voltage divider.
