Caste system in India

Depiction of caste groups in 19th-century India

The caste system in India is the paradigmatic ethnographic example of a system of social stratification based on caste. It has its origins in ancient India and was transformed by various ruling elites in medieval, early-modern, and modern India, especially the Mughal Empire and the British Raj. The system broadly divides Hindus into four hierarchical varnas — Brahmins (priests and scholars), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and landowners), and Shudras (labourers and service providers) — with a fifth category, the Dalits (formerly "untouchables"), placed outside the system entirely.

Varna and jati

In practice, Indian society has been organised less by the fourfold varna scheme than by thousands of jatis — endogamous birth-groups traditionally associated with occupations and ranked locally. Caste has historically determined occupation, diet, marriage partners, and social interaction, and analogous stratifications exist among Indian Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, and other communities, though often in less rigid forms.

History

Texts such as the Manusmriti codified varna ideology in the early centuries CE. Under British colonial rule, administrators used caste categories in censuses and governance, which many scholars argue hardened previously more fluid identities. Reformers including Jyotirao Phule and B. R. Ambedkar, himself a Dalit, campaigned against caste discrimination; Mahatma Gandhi also opposed untouchability while defending an idealised varna.

Modern India

The Constitution of India (1950) abolished untouchability, prohibited caste discrimination, and instituted reservations — quotas in education, government employment, and legislatures — for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and later Other Backward Classes. Caste nevertheless remains a major factor in Indian politics, marriage, and social life, and caste-based violence and discrimination persist despite legal prohibitions.
