The St. Lawrence Seaway (French: la Voie Maritime du Saint-Laurent) is a system of rivers, locks, canals, and channels in eastern North America that permits ocean-going vessels to travel from the Atlantic Ocean to the Great Lakes of North America, as far inland as Duluth, Minnesota, at the western end of Lake Superior. Legally, the Seaway extends from Montreal, Quebec, to Lake Erie, and includes the Welland Canal.

History

The Seaway was built jointly by Canada and the United States between 1954 and 1959, following decades of negotiation. It replaced older, smaller canal systems that limited traffic to shallow-draft vessels. The project involved the flooding of several Ontario communities — the "Lost Villages" — and the construction of the Moses-Saunders Power Dam for hydroelectric generation. Queen Elizabeth II and President Dwight D. Eisenhower formally opened the Seaway on 26 June 1959.

Navigation

The St. Lawrence River portion includes seven locks between Montreal and Lake Ontario, raising ships about 75 metres; the Welland Canal's eight locks lift vessels a further 99.5 metres to bypass Niagara Falls. The maximum vessel size is known as Seawaymax. Bulk cargoes — iron ore, grain, coal, steel — dominate traffic, and annual cargo totals have historically ranged between 30 and 50 million tonnes.

Impact

The Seaway spurred industrial development around the Great Lakes and gave landlocked ports such as Chicago, Toronto, and Duluth direct access to world shipping. It also facilitated the spread of aquatic invasive species, including the zebra mussel and sea lamprey, prompting ballast-water regulations. The waterway generally closes during winter because of ice.
