Forfeiture (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, forfeiture is the loss of rights or property without compensation as a consequence of a crime, breach of legal obligation, or failure to perform a required act. Forfeiture has roots in English common law, where property connected with an offence could be seized by the Crown.

Types

Criminal forfeiture is imposed as part of a sentence following conviction, and acts against the person (in personam). Civil forfeiture, by contrast, is an action against the property itself (in rem) and may proceed without a criminal conviction — a practice that is extensive in the United States, where assets suspected of being involved in crime, such as drug trafficking or money laundering, may be seized.

Debate

Civil asset forfeiture in the United States has attracted significant criticism and reform efforts, with opponents arguing that low burdens of proof and financial incentives for seizing agencies raise due process concerns. The United States Supreme Court addressed aspects of the practice in cases such as Timbs v. Indiana (2019), which held that the Excessive Fines Clause of the Eighth Amendment applies to the states.
