Authoritarian personality


Image placeholder: The Authoritarian Personality (1950)

The authoritarian personality is a personality type characterised by a disposition to treat authority figures with unquestioning obedience and respect, combined with hostility toward out-groups. The concept derives principally from The Authoritarian Personality (1950), a study by Theodor W. Adorno, Else Frenkel-Brunswik, Daniel Levinson and Nevitt Sanford, researchers associated with the Frankfurt School and the University of California, Berkeley.

The F-scale

The researchers developed the F-scale (F for "fascism") to measure an underlying authoritarian predisposition without asking directly about fascist sympathies. Nine traits were identified: conventionalism, authoritarian submission, authoritarian aggression, anti-intraception (opposition to introspection), superstition and stereotypy, power and toughness, destructiveness and cynicism, projectivity, and exaggerated concern with sexuality. High F-scale scorers tended to express ethnic prejudice and antidemocratic attitudes.

Criticism and development

The study was criticised on methodological grounds — including acquiescence response bias, since all F-scale items were worded in the authoritarian direction — and for conflating authoritarianism with right-wing politics. Bob Altemeyer's later work on right-wing authoritarianism (RWA) narrowed the construct to three clusters: authoritarian submission, authoritarian aggression and conventionalism, measured by a balanced scale. Subsequent research has also examined left-wing authoritarianism and the role of social dominance orientation.
