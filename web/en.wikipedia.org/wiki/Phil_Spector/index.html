Harvey Philip Spector (26 December 1939 – 16 January 2021) was an American record producer and songwriter who is best known for his innovative recording practices and entrepreneurship in the 1960s and his two trials and conviction for the murder of actress Lana Clarkson in the 2000s. Spector developed the Wall of Sound, a production style that involves overdubbing multiple musicians playing in unison to create a dense, reverberant orchestral effect. He is considered among the most influential figures in the history of pop music.

Career

Spector first found success as a teenager with the Teddy Bears, whose "To Know Him Is to Love Him" (1958) — its title taken from his father's gravestone inscription — topped the US charts. Moving into production, he co-founded Philles Records in 1961 and produced a string of hits for the Crystals ("Da Doo Ron Ron", "Then He Kissed Me"), the Ronettes ("Be My Baby"), Darlene Love and the Righteous Brothers ("You've Lost That Lovin' Feelin'"). His lavish production of Ike & Tina Turner's "River Deep – Mountain High" (1966), a commercial disappointment in America, marked the end of his hit-making peak.

After a period of semi-retirement he returned to co-produce the Beatles' Let It Be (1970) and solo albums by John Lennon (Imagine) and George Harrison (All Things Must Pass), and later worked with Leonard Cohen, the Ramones and others. His erratic behaviour, reclusiveness and reported intimidation of musicians with firearms became notorious. He was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1989.

Murder conviction and death

On 3 February 2003 actress Lana Clarkson was found shot dead at Spector's mansion in Alhambra, California. Spector claimed the shooting was an accidental suicide; prosecutors presented evidence of a pattern of threats against women. His first trial in 2007 ended in a hung jury; at retrial in 2009 he was convicted of second-degree murder and sentenced to 19 years to life. He died in prison custody in 2021 of complications from COVID-19.
