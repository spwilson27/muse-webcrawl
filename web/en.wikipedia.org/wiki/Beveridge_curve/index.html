The Beveridge curve, or UV curve, is a graphical representation of the relationship between unemployment and the job vacancy rate, the number of unfilled jobs expressed as a proportion of the labour force. Typically, vacancies are plotted on the vertical axis and unemployment on the horizontal axis. The curve is named after the British economist William Beveridge, who discussed the relationship in his 1944 report Full Employment in a Free Society.

Shape and interpretation

The curve is characteristically downward-sloping and convex to the origin: when unemployment is high, vacancies are usually scarce, and when unemployment is low, employers report many unfilled openings. Movements along the curve reflect the business cycle — during recessions the economy slides down and to the right, and during expansions it moves up and to the left. Shifts of the curve, by contrast, indicate changes in the efficiency with which unemployed workers are matched to jobs: an outward shift suggests worsening mismatch, for example because of skill or geographic mismatch, longer unemployment durations, or less effective job search.

History and use

The relationship was analysed systematically in the 1950s and 1960s and became a standard tool of labour-market analysis, often paired with the Phillips curve. Economists use the Beveridge curve to assess whether unemployment is cyclical or structural. Following the Great Recession, the United States Beveridge curve shifted notably outward, prompting debate about skill mismatch versus weak aggregate demand. During the COVID-19 pandemic and the subsequent recovery, the curve shifted sharply outward again as vacancies surged to record levels while unemployment fell, a configuration sometimes called the "Beveridge curve shift" of 2020–2022.
