Wave function collapse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Graph of a spread-out wave function abruptly narrowing to a spike upon measurement]
Collapse: from a spread of possibilities to one outcome.

In quantum mechanics, wave function collapse (also called reduction of the state vector) is the process by which a system initially in a superposition of several states appears to reduce to a single state upon interaction with the external world — traditionally, upon measurement or observation. The probabilities of the outcomes are given by the Born rule (Max Born, 1926), proportional to |ψ|².

Collapse is one of the two modes of change in textbook quantum mechanics (von Neumann's 'Process 1'), the other being smooth unitary evolution ('Process 2'); the tension between them is the Measurement problem. Interpretations divide over collapse: Copenhagen takes it as a primitive update tied to measurement; many-worlds denies it (branches instead); de Broglie–Bohm theory dispenses with it (the wave guides, never collapses, though 'effective collapse' occurs); objective-collapse models (GRW, CSL, Penrose's gravity-induced collapse) make it a real physical process with parameters that experiments increasingly constrain. In quantum information practice, collapse is simply the rule used to compute post-measurement states — its ontological status bracketed.

Decoherence's role

Quantum decoherence explains why collapse, when it appears to happen, selects position-like pointer states and why interference vanishes — converting a coherent superposition into what looks exactly like a classical statistical mixture. Whether a definite outcome still requires something more (an interpretation's extra postulate) is the live debate.
