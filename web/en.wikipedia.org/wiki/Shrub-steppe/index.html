Shrub-steppe

A shrub-steppe is a type of low-rainfall natural grassland. Similar to other types of steppe, shrub-steppes are arid grasslands with insufficient rain to support a forest but not dry enough to be a true desert. They are characterized by a mixture of shrubs — most famously big sagebrush (Artemisia tridentata) in western North America — and perennial bunchgrasses such as bluebunch wheatgrass and Idaho fescue.

Distribution

The largest shrub-steppe region in North America lies in the Great Basin and Columbia Plateau of the Intermountain West, covering parts of Washington, Oregon, Idaho, Nevada, Utah, and Wyoming. Notable remnants include the Hanford Reach and Arid Lands Ecology Reserve in Washington and the sagebrush steppe of the Snake River Plain in Idaho. Shrub-steppe habitats also occur in other dry continental interiors worldwide.

Ecology

Shrub-steppe supports a distinctive fauna. Obligate species include the greater sage-grouse, sagebrush sparrow, Brewer's sparrow, sage thrasher, pygmy rabbit, and pronghorn. The sagebrush canopy shelters grasses and wildflowers from wind and grazing, and biological soil crusts of lichens, mosses, and cyanobacteria stabilize the soil between plants.

Threats and conservation

More than half of the historical North American shrub-steppe has been converted to agriculture or degraded. Key threats are invasion by cheatgrass (Bromus tectorum) and other annual grasses, which promote frequent wildfire that sagebrush (killed by fire and slow to re-establish) cannot tolerate; energy development; urban expansion; and off-road vehicles. Extensive conservation efforts, including the Sage Grouse Initiative and Bureau of Land Management habitat programs, aim to preserve the remaining large tracts.
