Molybdenum-99 (99Mo) is an isotope of molybdenum that decays by beta emission to the metastable nuclear isomer technetium-99m (99mTc), with a half-life of about 66 hours. Technetium-99m is the most commonly used radioisotope in diagnostic nuclear medicine, employed in tens of millions of procedures each year worldwide, so molybdenum-99 is one of the most commercially important radioisotopes in the world.

A molybdenum-99/technetium-99m generator ("technetium cow") in a nuclear medicine department.

Medical use

Hospitals receive 99Mo adsorbed on alumina in shielded devices called technetium generators. As the molybdenum decays, the daughter 99mTc (half-life about 6 hours) is periodically washed off ("milked") with saline and combined with pharmaceutical kits to make radiotracers for scintigraphy and SPECT imaging of the heart, bones, brain, thyroid, lungs and other organs.

Production and supply

Nearly all 99Mo is produced by the fission of uranium-235 targets in a small number of research reactors, historically including NRU (Canada), HFR (Netherlands), BR-2 (Belgium), OSIRIS (France), Safari-1 (South Africa), OPAL (Australia) and Maria (Poland). Because the 66-hour half-life makes stockpiling impossible and the supply chain depends on aging reactors, unplanned outages — notably in 2007–2010 — caused worldwide medical isotope shortages. These events spurred international efforts, coordinated by the OECD Nuclear Energy Agency, to build new production capacity, to convert targets from highly enriched uranium (HEU) to low-enriched uranium (LEU) for non-proliferation reasons, and to develop alternative production routes such as neutron capture on molybdenum-98 and accelerator-based methods.
