Age of Enlightenment

[Image: a candlelit eighteenth-century Parisian salon, where philosophers and writers gather to debate ideas]

The Age of Enlightenment, or simply the Enlightenment, was an intellectual and cultural movement in Europe, mainly in the eighteenth century, which emphasised reason, science, individual liberty and scepticism toward traditional authority. Its ideas underpinned major political revolutions and reshaped philosophy, science and society.

Enlightenment thinkers argued that human beings could understand and improve the world through rational inquiry and the scientific method, challenging the inherited authority of monarchy and church.

Ideas and thinkers

Central figures included John Locke, whose writings on natural rights and government by consent influenced liberalism; Voltaire, who championed freedom of speech and religion; Jean-Jacques Rousseau, author of The Social Contract; and Immanuel Kant, whose 1784 essay answered the question 'What is Enlightenment?' with the motto sapere aude — 'dare to know'.

Other major contributors included Adam Smith, whose The Wealth of Nations (1776) founded modern economics; Montesquieu, advocate of the separation of powers; Mary Wollstonecraft, who argued for women's education and rights; and the editors of the French Encyclopédie, Diderot and d'Alembert.

Impact

Enlightenment ideas fed directly into the American Revolution and the French Revolution, and into the constitutions and declarations of rights that followed them. They also promoted the expansion of science, secular education and the abolitionist and feminist movements.

Historians debate the Enlightenment's unity, period and legacy, including its relationship to colonialism and slavery, which many of its thinkers opposed in principle while others accommodated or profited from them.
