Dolly zoom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The dolly zoom is an in-camera effect in filmmaking in which the camera moves toward or away from a subject (dollies) while the lens simultaneously zooms in the opposite direction. The subject remains the same size in the frame while the background appears to stretch or compress, producing a disorienting sensation of vertigo or sudden realisation. It is also known as the trombone shot, contra-zoom, or Vertigo effect.

History

The technique was devised by cameraman Irmin Roberts for Alfred Hitchcock's Vertigo (1958), to convey the protagonist's acrophobia as he looked down a stairwell. It has since been used in films including Jaws (1975), where it marks Chief Brody's shock on the beach, Goodfellas (1990), The Fellowship of the Ring (2001), and Poltergeist (1982).

Technique

Executing the shot requires precise coordination between the dolly movement and the zoom so that the subject's framing stays constant. The change in focal length alters perspective and depth: zooming out while dollying in makes the background appear to swell toward the viewer, while zooming in while dollying out flattens and recedes it. The effect is usually reserved for moments of fear, shock, or psychological disturbance.
