Overview

Theories of humor are attempts to explain what humour is, why people laugh, and what social and cognitive functions laughter serves. Although thinkers have discussed laughter since antiquity, most accounts fall into three classical families: superiority theory, relief theory, and incongruity theory. Contemporary research adds approaches such as benign violation theory and evolutionary accounts of play and social bonding.

Major theories

Superiority theory, associated with Plato, Aristotle, and Thomas Hobbes, holds that laughter expresses a feeling of sudden glory arising from perceiving others' misfortunes or defects as inferior to oneself. Relief theory, developed by figures such as Herbert Spencer and Sigmund Freud, treats laughter as the release of built-up nervous or psychic energy, for example from repressed thoughts or social taboos.

Incongruity theory, advanced by Kant, Schopenhauer, and Arthur Koestler, argues that humour arises from perceiving something that violates our mental patterns and expectations, and then resolving — or playfully failing to resolve — that incongruity. It is the most widely accepted framework in modern humour research. Benign violation theory, proposed by Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren, refines this by holding that humour occurs when something is perceived simultaneously as a violation and as benign.

Other approaches examine humour's role in social bonding, mate selection, and group cohesion, and researchers study its effects on health and coping. No single theory explains all cases, and scholars generally treat the theories as complementary.
