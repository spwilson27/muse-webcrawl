Analytic set

In descriptive set theory, an analytic set is a subset of a Polish space that is the continuous image of a Borel subset of another Polish space — equivalently, the projection of a Borel set, or the continuous image of the Baire space. Analytic sets, denoted Σ11, form the first level of the projective hierarchy beyond the Borel sets.

An analytic set as the projection of a Borel set in a product space

Definition and equivalent characterizations

For a Polish space X, a set A ⊆ X is analytic if any of the following equivalent conditions holds: A is the continuous image of a Borel subset of a Polish space; A is the projection of a Borel subset of X × Y for some Polish space Y; A is the result of applying the Souslin operation (operation A) to a family of closed sets; or A is the continuous image of the Baire space ℕℕ.

Properties

Every Borel set is analytic, and every analytic set is universally measurable and has the property of Baire and the perfect set property. The class of analytic sets is closed under countable unions, countable intersections, and continuous images, but not under complementation; complements of analytic sets are called coanalytic sets (Π11). A set that is both analytic and coanalytic is Borel, by Souslin's theorem.

History

Analytic sets were introduced by Mikhail Suslin (Souslin) in 1917, who discovered an error in a purported theorem of Lebesgue by constructing a Borel set whose projection need not be Borel. Nikolai Lusin developed the theory further, establishing the regularity properties of analytic sets. The study of analytic and projective sets became a central theme linking set theory, topology, and mathematical logic.
