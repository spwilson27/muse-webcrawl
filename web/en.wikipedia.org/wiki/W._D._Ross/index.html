Sir William David Ross (15 April 1877 – 5 May 1971), known as W. D. Ross, was a Scottish philosopher who spent his career at the University of Oxford. He is renowned as a translator and commentator on Aristotle and for his pluralist deontological ethics, set out in The Right and the Good (1930) and The Foundations of Ethics (1939), which continues to influence moral theory.

Life

Ross was educated at the Royal High School, Edinburgh, and at Edinburgh University and Balliol College, Oxford. He became a fellow of Oriel College in 1902, later serving as provost of Oriel (1929–1947) and vice-chancellor of the University of Oxford (1941–1944). During the First World War he worked at the Ministry of Munitions; he was knighted in 1938. Ross was also a leading administrator of academic institutions, including service as president of the British Academy (1936–1940).

Ethics: prima facie duties

Ross rejected both the utilitarian reduction of rightness to the production of good outcomes and any single absolute principle. Instead, he held that we apprehend a plurality of prima facie duties — duties that hold "at first glance" and bind us unless overridden by a stronger duty in the circumstances. His list includes duties of fidelity (keeping promises), reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement, and non-maleficence (not harming others). When duties conflict, judgment — Ross compared it to aesthetic perception — determines our actual "duty proper". This intuitionist pluralism has been widely taken up by contemporary deontologists and writers on moral particularism.

Aristotle scholarship

Ross edited and translated the standard Oxford edition of Aristotle's works; his translation of the Nicomachean Ethics (1925) and editions of the Metaphysics and Physics, with extensive commentaries, remain standard scholarly resources. His study Aristotle (1923) is a classic introduction to the philosopher's thought.
