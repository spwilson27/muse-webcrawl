Roulette (French for "little wheel") is a casino game in which a croupier spins a wheel in one direction and a small ball in the other; players bet on which numbered, coloured pocket the ball will land in. Bets may be placed on single numbers, groups of numbers, red or black, odd or even, and high or low ranges.

History

The game evolved in 18th-century France from earlier games such as roly-poly and biribi; Blaise Pascal's failed perpetual-motion wheel of 1655 is an oft-cited ancestor. The modern form was described in Paris by 1796. François and Louis Blanc brought roulette to Monte Carlo in 1863. The double-zero wheel became standard in America, increasing the house edge, while French roulette's la partage rule — returning half of even-money bets on zero — reduces it to about 1.35%.

The wheel and bets

A European wheel has 37 pockets (0–36); an American wheel has 38 (0, 00, 1–36). "Inside" bets on small groups pay up to 35-to-1 for a straight-up number; "outside" even-money bets cover 18 numbers. No betting system can overcome the house edge, a fact demonstrated by the gambler's fallacy and by the ban on many attempted wheel-bias exploits. In 1873 Joseph Jagger famously "broke the bank at Monte Carlo" by exploiting a biased wheel, and in 2004 the Ritz laser-scanner case revived interest in prediction devices.
