Hill equation (biochemistry)

In biochemistry and pharmacology, the Hill equation is an empirical formula describing the fraction of a macromolecule saturated with a ligand as a function of ligand concentration, widely used to characterise cooperative binding. It was proposed by Archibald Vivian Hill in 1910 to account for the sigmoidal oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve.

θ = [L]n / ( Kd + [L]n )

where θ is the fractional saturation, [L] the free ligand concentration, Kd an apparent dissociation constant, and n the Hill coefficient.

The Hill coefficient

The Hill coefficient nH measures the steepness of the binding curve. A value of 1 indicates independent, non-cooperative binding, in which case the equation reduces to a form equivalent to Michaelis–Menten kinetics. Values greater than 1 indicate positive cooperativity — binding of one ligand increases the affinity for subsequent ligands — while values below 1 indicate negative cooperativity. For haemoglobin, which has four oxygen-binding subunits, nH is about 2.8, close to but less than the theoretical maximum set by the number of sites.

The Hill plot

Taking logarithms converts the equation into a straight line: log[θ/(1−θ)] plotted against log[L] gives a line whose slope is the Hill coefficient. This Hill plot is a standard diagnostic for cooperativity and is the forerunner of the more general dose–response curves used in pharmacology, where analogous equations (such as the Hill–Langmuir equation) describe drug response versus dose, with the IC50 or EC50 marking the half-maximal concentration.
