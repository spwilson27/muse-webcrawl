Mamluk Sultanate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Mosque-Madrasa of Sultan Hasan in Cairo, masterpiece of Mamluk architecture]

The Mamluk Sultanate (1250–1517) was a state ruling Egypt, Syria and the Hejaz from Cairo, governed by mamluks — slave-soldiers of mainly Kipchak Turkic and later Circassian origin, imported as boys, converted, trained and manumitted into a military aristocracy that reproduced itself by importation rather than inheritance. Founded when the Bahri mamluks seized power amid the Seventh Crusade, the sultanate destroyed the last Crusader states (Acre, 1291), defeated the Mongols repeatedly (Ain Jalut 1260, Homs, Elbistan), held the Indian Ocean trade, and made Cairo the Islamic world's cultural capital — a city of mosques, madrasas and hospitals whose skyline still proclaims the era. The Ottomans of Selim I conquered it in 1516–17, though mamluk households dominated Egypt's politics until Muhammad Ali's massacre of 1811.

Bahri and Burji

The sultanate's two eras are named for its regiments' quarters: the Bahriyya (River Island barracks; Kipchak era, 1250–1382 — Baybars, Qalawun, al-Nasir Muhammad) and the Burji (Citadel towers; Circassian era, 1382–1517 — Barquq, Barsbay, Qaitbay). Succession was notionally elective among the great emirs, practically violent: few sultans died uncontested in bed, yet the system delivered striking durability — a professional army, an iqta' revenue machine, chancery and postal services, and commercial treaties with Venice and Genoa that made Cairo the entrepôt between the Indian Ocean and Europe.

Downfall

By 1500 the sultanate's finances — squeezed by Portuguese disruption of the spice routes after 1498, plague demographics, and debasement — could not fund the gunpowder-age army its Ottoman rival fielded. Selim I destroyed the Mamluk field armies at Marj Dabiq (1516) and Ridaniya (1517) and hanged the last sultan's deputy Tumanbay II at Cairo's gate. The regime's art and architecture — Qaitbay's citadel and mosques, the great madrasa complexes — survived as Cairo's 'city of a thousand minarets' identity, and the mamluk system itself outlived the sultanate by three centuries in Ottoman Egypt.
