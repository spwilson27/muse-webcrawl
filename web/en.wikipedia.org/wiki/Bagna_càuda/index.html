Bagna càuda

TypeDip
Place of originItaly
RegionPiedmont
Main ingredientsGarlic, anchovies, olive oil, butter
Serving temperatureHot

Bagna càuda (Piedmontese for "hot dip" or "hot gravy") is a hot dish made from garlic and anchovies, originating in Piedmont, Italy, during the 16th century. The dish is served and consumed in a manner similar to fondue, most often as an appetizer, and is kept warm in a pot or earthenware bowl over a small flame at the table.

Preparation

Garlic is simmered gently in olive oil — sometimes first softened in milk — until it becomes sweet and mellow, then anchovy fillets are stirred in until they dissolve, and butter is often added to finish the sauce. The result is a pungent, savoury emulsion that must be kept warm but never allowed to boil. Raw and cooked vegetables are dipped into it, classically including cardoon (a local speciality of Piedmont), peppers, cabbage, cauliflower, Jerusalem artichokes, carrots, celery, and bread.

History

The dish is traditionally associated with the grape harvest, when vineyard workers would gather to celebrate the new wine with a communal, warming meal. Its main ingredients reflect Piedmont's historic salt trade: anchovies, preserved in salt, were traded inland from the Ligurian coast in barrels that also served to smuggle salt past customs duties. Bagna càuda is eaten throughout late autumn and winter and has become a symbol of Piedmontese conviviality; since 2012 an annual festival, the Bagna Cauda Day, has been held in Asti and across Piedmont.

Variations

Recipes vary considerably between families and towns: some include walnut oil, cream, or chopped walnuts; in the Monferrato some cooks add Barolo wine. A milk-based version softens the garlic's bite. Immigrants from Piedmont carried the dish to Argentina, where baña cauda remains popular in the provinces of Córdoba and Santa Fe during Holy Week.
