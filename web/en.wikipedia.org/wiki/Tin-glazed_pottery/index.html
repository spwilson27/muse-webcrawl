Tin-glazed pottery

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tin-glazed pottery is earthenware covered with a glaze containing tin oxide, which produces a brilliant opaque white surface that serves as an ideal ground for painted decoration. Regional varieties include Italian maiolica, French, Spanish and Portuguese faience, Dutch and English delftware, and Mexican talavera.

Technique

The fired clay body is dipped in a lead glaze opacified with tin oxide (cassiterite). Metallic-oxide pigments are painted onto the raw, absorbent glaze before a second firing, so the decoration fuses into the surface. The technique demands confidence, since mistakes cannot be corrected once the pigment touches the glaze.

History

Tin glazing was developed in the Islamic Middle East by the 9th century, possibly in imitation of Chinese white porcelain, and spread through Spain to Italy, where maiolica flourished during the Renaissance in centres such as Faenza — the source of the word "faience". In the 17th century Delft in the Netherlands became the great northern centre, producing blue-and-white wares in response to imports of Chinese porcelain. By the late 18th century, refined creamwares and industrial porcelain largely displaced tin-glazed earthenware for everyday use, though the tradition survives in art pottery and in Mexican talavera.
