IFRS 16

IFRS 16 — Leases
Issued: January 2016 by the IASB
Effective: 1 January 2019
Replaced: IAS 17
Key change: Nearly all leases on the lessee's balance sheet

IFRS 16 is an International Financial Reporting Standard issued by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) that prescribes how leases are recognized, measured, presented, and disclosed. It became effective for reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2019 and replaced IAS 17, IFRIC 4, SIC-15, and SIC-27.

The single lessee model

The standard's central reform is the abolition, for lessees, of the old distinction between finance leases (on balance sheet) and operating leases (off balance sheet). Under IFRS 16, a lessee recognizes almost every lease as a right-of-use asset and a corresponding lease liability measured at the present value of future lease payments. In the income statement, the single straight-line operating lease expense is replaced by depreciation of the asset and interest on the liability, which front-loads total expense early in the lease and raises reported EBITDA while increasing reported debt.

Two recognition exemptions are permitted: leases of 12 months or less ("short-term leases") and leases of low-value assets (such as laptops and office furniture), for which payments may still be expensed as incurred.

Rationale and impact

The IASB estimated that listed companies carried around US$3 trillion in off-balance-sheet operating lease commitments under IAS 17 — famously, airlines, retailers, and shipping companies appeared far less leveraged than they were. IFRS 16 was developed in parallel with the FASB's ASC 842 in the United States; the two largely converged on putting leases on the balance sheet, though ASC 842 retained a dual expense model for lessees. Lessor accounting under IFRS 16 remains substantially unchanged from IAS 17, retaining the finance/operating lease classification.

Sectors most affected included airlines (aircraft leases), retail (store leases), and logistics. Adoption forced companies to inventory their leases, choose discount rates, and renegotiate debt covenants whose ratios changed mechanically.
