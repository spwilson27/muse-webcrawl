Double bass

The double bass, also called the string bass, upright bass or contrabass, is the largest and lowest-pitched member of the violin family in common use in the modern symphony orchestra. It stands about 1.8 metres (6 feet) tall and is tuned in fourths (E–A–D–G), unlike the other string instruments, which are tuned in fifths. Its music is written an octave higher than it sounds.

Construction and playing

The double bass has features of both the violin and viol families, including sloped shoulders and, often, a flat back. It is played standing or sitting on a high stool, with a bow (arco) or plucked (pizzicato). Two bow types are used: the French (overhand) and German (underhand) grips.

History

The instrument emerged in Europe in the 16th century from the violone, the largest member of the viol family. It became a standard part of the orchestra by the 18th century, and virtuosi such as Domenico Dragonetti expanded its solo possibilities in the early 19th century.

Uses

In classical music the double bass anchors the orchestra's bass line, usually doubling the cellos an octave lower. In jazz it is fundamental to the rhythm section, typically played pizzicato in a "walking" bass line; notable jazz bassists include Charles Mingus and Ray Brown. It is also used in bluegrass, rockabilly, tango and other genres.
