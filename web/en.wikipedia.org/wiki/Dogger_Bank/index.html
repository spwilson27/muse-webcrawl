Dogger Bank

Dogger Bank (Dutch: Doggersbank, German: Doggerbank, Danish: Doggerbanke) is a large sandbank in a shallow area of the North Sea about 100 kilometres (62 mi) off the east coast of England. It extends over approximately 17,600 square kilometres (6,800 sq mi), with its dimensions being about 260 kilometres (160 mi) long and up to 97 kilometres (60 mi) broad. The water depth ranges from 15 to 36 metres (49 to 118 ft), about 20 metres (66 ft) shallower than the surrounding sea floor.

Geologically it is a moraine, formed during the last ice age. During the last glaciation the bank was part of a land bridge between mainland Europe and the British Isles, now known as Doggerland. It has long been known by fishermen to be a productive fishing bank; it was named after the doggers, old Dutch fishing boats especially used for catching cod.

History

The area was the site of the Battle of Dogger Bank in 1781 during the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War, a battle in 1915 during the First World War, and the Dogger Bank incident of 1904, when the Russian Baltic Fleet mistook British trawlers for Japanese torpedo boats and fired on them. In 2024 a prehistoric worked timber structure was reported from the area, evidence of Mesolithic settlement of Doggerland.

Wind farms

In the 21st century the shallow waters made Dogger Bank a major site for offshore wind development. The Dogger Bank Wind Farm, developed in phases, is among the largest offshore wind projects in the world, with a planned capacity of several gigawatts supplying millions of homes in the United Kingdom.
