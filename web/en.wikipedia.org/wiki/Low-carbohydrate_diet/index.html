Low-carbohydrate diet

Low-carbohydrate diets restrict carbohydrate intake relative to the average diet, emphasising foods high in protein and fat. They are used for weight loss and for managing diabetes and other metabolic conditions. There is no standard definition of how low a diet must be to qualify: studies variously define low-carbohydrate diets as providing less than 26% of energy from carbohydrate, or fewer than 130 grams per day, and very-low-carbohydrate ketogenic diets restrict intake to about 20–50 grams per day.

Types

The Atkins diet, popularised by Robert Atkins from 1972, is the best-known commercial example, beginning with an almost carbohydrate-free induction phase. The ketogenic diet, originally developed in the 1920s to treat epilepsy in children, induces ketosis, in which the body burns fat-derived ketone bodies for fuel. Other variants include the paleo diet and low-glycaemic-index approaches.

Health effects

Clinical trials generally find low-carbohydrate diets produce weight loss comparable to, and in the short term sometimes greater than, low-fat calorie-restricted diets; their long-term advantage over other diets is modest. Carbohydrate restriction can improve blood-glucose control in type 2 diabetes. Concerns raised by critics include possible rises in LDL cholesterol in some individuals, the nutritional quality of heavily animal-based regimens, and constipation from low fibre intake. Major dietary guidelines take differing positions on the approach.
