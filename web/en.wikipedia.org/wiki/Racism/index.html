Racism

Racism
DefinitionPrejudice, discrimination or antagonism based on race or ethnicity
FormsInterpersonal, institutional, structural/systemic, scientific racism (historical)
Key legal instrumentsInternational Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (1965); national civil-rights and hate-crime laws
Related conceptsXenophobia, ethnocentrism, racial segregation, white supremacy

Racism is the belief that groups of humans possess different behavioural traits corresponding to inherited attributes, and can be divided on the basis of the superiority of one race over another. It also describes prejudice, discrimination or antagonism directed against other people because they are of a different racial or ethnic origin. Modern variants are often grounded in social perceptions of biological difference, while scholars emphasise that race itself is primarily a social construct rather than a biological reality.

Definitions and forms

The United Nations' International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (1965) defines racial discrimination as any distinction based on race, colour, descent or national or ethnic origin that impairs the equal enjoyment of human rights. Analysts commonly distinguish interpersonal racism (individual attitudes and acts) from institutional and structural racism — policies and accumulated disadvantages embedded in systems such as housing, employment, education and criminal justice, sometimes captured in concepts like institutional racism and environmental racism.

History

Although ethnic hostility is ancient, modern racism took shape with European colonialism and the transatlantic slave trade, when hierarchies of race were elaborated to justify enslavement and conquest. In the 19th and early 20th centuries, "scientific racism" — craniometry, phrenology and eugenics — lent pseudo-scientific authority to these hierarchies; its logic culminated in Nazi racial ideology and the Holocaust, and underpinned systems such as Jim Crow segregation in the United States and apartheid in South Africa. After 1945, UNESCO statements by leading scientists rejected biological race hierarchies, and decolonisation and civil-rights movements dismantled many formal structures of racial domination, though discrimination and disparities persist worldwide.

Contemporary issues

Contemporary debates concern racial profiling, hate crimes, disparities in health and wealth, representation, and the legacies of slavery and colonialism, including reparations. Surveys and audit studies continue to document discrimination in hiring, lending and policing in many countries.
