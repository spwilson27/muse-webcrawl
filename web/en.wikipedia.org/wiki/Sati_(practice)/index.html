Sati (practice)

Sati was a historical practice in parts of India in which a widow sacrificed herself by sitting atop her deceased husband's funeral pyre. The term derives from the Sanskrit satī, meaning a virtuous woman, and is associated with the goddess Sati, who in Hindu mythology self-immolated in protest against her father's dishonouring of her husband Shiva.

History

References to widow-burning appear in sources from the first millennium CE onwards, and Greek accounts of Alexander the Great's era may describe related customs. The practice was never universal in India; it was concentrated in particular regions and communities, notably among some Rajput clans of Rajasthan and in Bengal, where records from the early 19th century counted several hundred cases per year. Some widows were glorified as sati mata (sati mothers) and commemorated with memorial stones and temples.

Abolition

Indian reformers, most prominently Raja Ram Mohan Roy, campaigned against sati in the early 19th century. In 1829 the Bengal Sati Regulation, enacted under Governor-General Lord William Bentinck, declared the practice illegal and punishable in the territories under British East India Company rule; similar bans followed in other presidencies. After independence, the Indian government strengthened the law following the 1987 immolation of Roop Kanwar in Deorala, Rajasthan, which led to the Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act, 1987, criminalising both the practice and its glorification.

Debate and interpretation

Historians and postcolonial scholars debate the extent to which individual acts were voluntary expressions of devotion versus the result of social, familial, and economic coercion. The practice figures prominently in discussions of colonial reform, Indian nationalism, and women's rights.
