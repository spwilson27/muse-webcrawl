Companion planting

Companion planting in gardening and agriculture is the planting of different crops in proximity for any of a number of different reasons, including weed suppression, pest control, pollination, providing habitat for beneficial insects, maximizing use of space, and otherwise increasing crop productivity. Companion planting is a form of polyculture.

Techniques

Well-known examples include the Three Sisters method used by Indigenous peoples of North America, in which maize provides a structure for climbing beans, beans fix nitrogen in the soil, and squash leaves shade the ground, suppressing weeds and retaining moisture. Other mechanisms used by companion plants include trap cropping (luring pests away from the main crop), nurse cropping (taller plants sheltering smaller ones), and biochemical pest suppression, in which root exudates or strong scents — such as marigolds planted with tomatoes — deter harmful insects or nematodes.

History and evidence

Companion planting has been practised for thousands of years in Asia and the Americas. While some combinations are well supported by field research in intercropping, parts of the companion-planting literature rest on traditional knowledge and anecdotal observation, and specific recommended pairings vary between sources.
