Armillaria is a genus of fungi that includes the Armillaria mellea species known as honey fungi, so called because of the sweet taste and honey colour of their fruiting bodies. Long considered a single species, Armillaria is now known to comprise several dozen biological species distributed worldwide. Many are plant pathogens that cause Armillaria root disease (honey fungus disease) of trees, shrubs and garden plants; others are saprotrophs on dead wood.

Description and biology

Armillaria spreads by black, bootlace-like cords called rhizomorphs, which grow through soil and under bark and can extend several metres. Under the bark of infected trees it forms characteristic white mycelial fans. Fruiting bodies — clusters of honey-coloured mushrooms with a ring on the stem — appear in autumn. The genus also includes one of the largest and oldest known organisms on Earth: a specimen of Armillaria ostoyae in Oregon's Malheur National Forest covers roughly 8.9 km² (about 2,200 acres) and is estimated to be thousands of years old.

Significance

Honey fungus is among the most destructive fungal diseases of gardens and forests in temperate regions, killing trees by rotting their roots. Some species' mushrooms are eaten (after thorough cooking) in parts of Europe, though they can cause gastric upset; the genus is also mildly bioluminescent — its mycelium produces the "foxfire" glow of rotting wood.
