Performance-related pay

Performance-related pay (PRP), also known as merit pay or pay for performance, is a system of remuneration in which some or all of an employee's pay depends on assessments of their individual performance, or on the performance of their team or organisation. It is used as a management tool intended to motivate employees, reward high performers, and align individual effort with organisational goals.

Forms

Performance-related pay takes several forms:

  • Merit increases — permanent rises to base salary awarded on the basis of a performance appraisal.
  • Bonuses — one-off payments for meeting or exceeding targets, not consolidated into base pay.
  • Piece rates — payment per unit of output produced, historically common in manufacturing.
  • Commission — a percentage of sales value, common in sales roles.
  • Profit-sharing and gainsharing — payments linked to the financial results of the whole organisation or a unit within it.

Rationale and theory

PRP is grounded in expectancy theory and principal–agent theory: if employees believe that extra effort will lead to valued rewards, they will work harder. Employers also use PRP to attract and retain high performers and to make labour costs partly variable with results.

Criticism

Critics argue that the link between individual effort and measured outcomes is often weak, that appraisal ratings are subjective and prone to bias, and that PRP can damage teamwork, encourage gaming of targets, and crowd out intrinsic motivation. Empirical studies find modest and inconsistent effects on performance, with stronger results for simple, easily measured tasks than for complex professional work. Performance-related pay has been widely adopted, and widely debated, in public services such as teaching, healthcare, and the civil service.
