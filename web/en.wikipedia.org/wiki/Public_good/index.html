Public good

[Image: a lighthouse beam sweeping over a harbour at dusk, the classic example of a public good]

In Economics, a public good is a good that is non-excludable (no one can feasibly be prevented from using it) and non-rivalrous (one person's use does not diminish another's). Paul Samuelson formalised the concept in 1954. Pure examples include national defence, a lighthouse's beam, flood defences, street lighting and broadcast signals.

The free-rider problem

Because non-payers cannot be excluded, individuals have an incentive to free-ride — enjoy the good without contributing — so markets under-provide public goods or fail to provide them at all: a canonical Market failure. Governments step in by funding provision from Taxation. Goods can be classified on the two dimensions: private goods (rival, excludable), club goods (excludable, non-rival — streaming services), common-pool resources (rival, non-excludable — fisheries, where Elinor Ostrom showed communities often devise their own governance), and public goods. Knowledge and basic research have strong public-good character, shaping arguments for public science funding and for the design of patent systems.
