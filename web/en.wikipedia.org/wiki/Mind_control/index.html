Mind control

Mind control (also known as brainwashing, reeducation, coercive persuasion, thought control, or thought reform) is the concept that human actions or thinking can be indoctrinated, subverted or controlled by an external influence. The term applies to any tactic, psychological or otherwise, used to subvert an individual's sense of control over their own thinking, behavior, emotions or decision making.

History

The term "brainwashing" was popularized in the 1950s by journalist Edward Hunter, translating the Chinese term used during the Korean War to describe the coercive indoctrination of American prisoners of war. Studies of former POWs by Robert Jay Lifton and Edgar Schein described techniques of "thought reform" involving isolation, confession, and ideological resocialization. During the Cold War, the CIA investigated the feasibility of mind control in programs such as Project MKUltra, which experimented with drugs including LSD, hypnosis, and sensory deprivation, largely without subjects' consent.

Models and debate

In the 1970s and 1980s, psychologist Margaret Singer and others argued that some new religious movements ("cults") used coercive persuasion to recruit and retain members. The scientific community remains divided: the American Psychological Association declined to endorse a task-force report on "deceptive and indirect methods of persuasion and control" in 1987, citing insufficient scientific rigor. Critics of the brainwashing concept argue that conversion and commitment can usually be explained by ordinary social influence, and that the concept has been used to justify forced "deprogramming."

In popular culture

Mind control is a staple of fiction, from Richard Condon's novel The Manchurian Candidate (1959) to hypnosis, implanted commands, and superpowered mind controllers in film and comics.
