Peregrine Phillips


Born: 1800, England
Died: 1888
Known for: Contact process for sulphuric acid (1831 patent)

Peregrine Phillips (1800–1888) was an English chemist and vinegar maker from Bristol who in 1831 patented the contact process for manufacturing sulphuric acid—one of the foundational inventions of the modern chemical industry.

The 1831 patent

Working in Bristol, Phillips took out British patent No. 6096 in 1831 for oxidising sulphur dioxide to sulphur trioxide by passing the gas, mixed with air, over a heated platinum catalyst, then absorbing the product in water or existing acid to make concentrated sulphuric acid. This is the essence of the contact process used worldwide today. Phillips also recognised that the catalyst could be poisoned by impurities such as arsenic in the gas stream—an insight industry would grapple with for decades.

Why it was not exploited

Although Phillips's patent described a workable process, the lead chamber process then dominant was cheaper for the dilute acid most users needed, and platinum was expensive, so the invention lay largely dormant. Only in the 1870s, with demand for concentrated acid for dyes and explosives, did engineers such as Rudolf Messel in London commercialise contact plants, reviving Phillips's principles with vanadium catalysts eventually replacing platinum.

Life

Little is recorded of Phillips's life beyond his Bristol vinegar business and a small number of patents; historians have noted the contrast between his obscurity and the enormous later importance of his invention, by which sulphuric acid became the world's highest-volume industrial chemical.
