Papal name

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A papal name or pontifical name is the regnal name taken by a pope upon his election. Both the head of the Catholic Church (usually known as the pope) and the pope of the Coptic Orthodox Church choose papal names. A pope's regnal name is given in Latin in official documents and is often accompanied by a regnal number (for example, Benedictus XVI).

Catholic Church

Immediately after a new pope is elected, the Dean of the College of Cardinals asks him, in Latin, "By what name shall you be called?" (Quo nomine vis vocari?). The pope-elect chooses the name by which he will be known. The choice is then announced to the world with the Habemus Papam proclamation.

History

The first pope known to have changed his name was John II (533–535), who had been born Mercurius and considered it inappropriate to bear the name of a Roman god. The practice became common from the late 10th century; Sergius IV (1009–1012), born Pietro, is often cited as the first to adopt a new name as a matter of course. The last pope to reign under his baptismal name was Marcellus II (1555).

Symbolism

Popes often choose names to honour a predecessor, signal continuity or a programme of reform, or express devotion to a saint. The most commonly chosen names include John (used 23 times), Gregory, Benedict, Clement, Innocent, Leo, and Pius. No pope has taken the name Peter out of reverence for Saint Peter. In 1978 Albino Luciani took the double name John Paul, the first double papal name, honouring John XXIII and Paul VI; his successor Karol Wojtyła retained it as John Paul II. Pope Francis, elected in 2013, was the first to choose the name Francis, after Saint Francis of Assisi.

Coptic Orthodox Church

The Coptic pope also takes a regnal name upon enthronement, typically the name of a predecessor such as Shenouda or Cyril, usually with a regnal number.
