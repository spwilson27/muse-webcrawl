The quadrille is a square dance that was fashionable in late eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Europe and later in its colonies. It is performed by four couples arranged in a square, each couple facing the centre, and consists of a suite of figures danced to a sequence of contrasting musical sections.

History

The term originally referred to a card game, and the dance developed in France from the earlier cotillion: where the cotillion had nine or more figures, the quadrille standardised at five figures (conventionally Le Pantalon, L'Été, La Poule, La Pastourelle or La Trénis, and a finale). The quadrille reached London in the early nineteenth century — it was famously performed at Almack's Assembly Rooms — and by the 1820s it had largely displaced the minuet and country dance at fashionable balls. Dance masters published elaborate rules, and composers such as Johann Strauss I and Joseph Lanner supplied quadrilles arranged from operatic hits.

Later forms and legacy

Variants proliferated throughout the century: the Lancers (a militaristic variant with more vigorous figures), the Caledonians, and quadrilles on ice skates. The dance crossed the Atlantic, where in North America it fed into the tradition of square dancing, and in the Caribbean it became a creole folk form, surviving in the traditional music and dance of islands such as Saint Lucia and Dominica. In military usage, "quadrille" also survives as a ceremonial cavalry display, and in dressage as a team performance of horses moving in formation.
