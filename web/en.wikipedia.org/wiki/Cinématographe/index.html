Cinématographe

The Cinématographe of the Lumière brothers

The Cinématographe was a motion picture film camera and projector invented by the French brothers Auguste and Louis Lumière. Patented in 1895, the device combined three functions in a single hand-cranked unit: it could record film, print copies, and project the finished film onto a screen. Its name — from the Greek kinema (movement) and graphein (to write) — gave rise to the word "cinema" in many languages.

Design

The Cinématographe used 35 mm film with a single round perforation on each side of every frame, advanced by an intermittent claw mechanism adapted from the movement of a sewing machine. Running at about sixteen frames per second, the machine was far lighter than Edison's Kinetograph — weighing roughly five kilograms — and could be carried anywhere and operated by one person without electricity. By using the same apparatus for shooting and projection, the Lumières could take their invention around the world.

First screenings

The brothers gave the first paying public screening of projected films on 28 December 1895 at the Salon Indien of the Grand Café in Paris, a date often taken as the birth of cinema. The programme of short actuality films included Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory, The Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat, and the trick of projecting a reversed film of a wall being demolished. Lumière operators were dispatched worldwide to shoot and exhibit films, creating a global catalogue of more than a thousand titles within a few years.

Legacy

The Cinématographe's portability and all-in-one design set the pattern for cinema's first decades and helped establish projected motion pictures as mass entertainment. Louis Lumière regarded the invention as a scientific curiosity without a commercial future, and the firm gradually withdrew from production, but the name and the device remain emblematic of the origins of film.
