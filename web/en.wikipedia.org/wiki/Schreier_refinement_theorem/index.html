Schreier refinement theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the Schreier refinement theorem of group theory states that any two subnormal series of subgroups of a given group have equivalent refinements, where two series are called equivalent if there is a bijection between their factor groups that sends each factor group to an isomorphic one.

The theorem is named after the Austrian mathematician Otto Schreier, who proved it in 1928. It is a foundational result in the theory of composition series: it implies the Jordan–Hölder theorem, which asserts the essential uniqueness of composition series of finite groups.

Statement

Let G be a group and consider two subnormal series

1 = G0 ⊲ G1 ⊲ ⋯ ⊲ Gm = G,    and    1 = H0 ⊲ H1 ⊲ ⋯ ⊲ Hn = G.

Each series can be refined by inserting additional subgroups between consecutive terms. The Schreier refinement theorem says that the two series possess refinements that are equivalent: the refined series have the same length, and after reordering, corresponding factor groups are isomorphic.

Proof idea

The standard proof uses the Zassenhaus lemma (the "butterfly lemma"). Given two adjacent terms of the two series, one inserts into the first series the terms Gi(Gi+1 ∩ Hj) and into the second the symmetric terms Hj(Hj+1 ∩ Gi). The Zassenhaus lemma shows that the factors of these two inserted chains are pairwise isomorphic, so the two constructed refinements are equivalent.

Consequences

  • Jordan–Hölder theorem: any two composition series of a group (subnormal series whose factors are simple) have the same length and the same factors up to permutation and isomorphism, since a composition series admits no proper refinement.
  • The theorem generalises beyond groups: an analogous refinement theorem holds for modules, and the Schreier refinement property is one of the features captured in the theory of modular lattices.

History

Otto Schreier published the theorem in 1928 in his paper on composition series. Hans Zassenhaus later devised the elegant lemma that bears his name, simplifying the proof. The result unified earlier uniqueness results for finite groups going back to the work of Camille Jordan and Otto Hölder in the nineteenth century.
