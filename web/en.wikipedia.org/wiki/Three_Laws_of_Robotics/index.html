Three Laws of Robotics

The Three Laws of Robotics (often shortened to the Three Laws or Asimov's Laws) are a set of rules devised by science fiction author Isaac Asimov to govern the behaviour of robots in his fiction. They were introduced in his 1942 short story "Runaround", although earlier stories had implied them:

Three Laws of Robotics
Created byIsaac Asimov
First stated"Runaround" (1942)
  1. First Law: A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.
  2. Second Law: A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.
  3. Third Law: A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law.

In Asimov's fiction

Asimov's robot stories and novels — collected in I, Robot (1950) and developed in the Robot series — are largely built around the unexpected consequences and edge cases of the Laws, explored by robopsychologist Susan Calvin and engineers Powell and Donovan. In later works Asimov introduced a "Zeroth Law" — a robot may not harm humanity, or, by inaction, allow humanity to come to harm — which takes precedence over the First Law, most prominently in Robots and Empire (1985).

Influence

The Three Laws shaped public and literary discussion of robot ethics and machine safety for decades and are frequently invoked — often critically — in real debates about artificial intelligence and autonomous weapons. Ethicists in robotics note that the Laws are literary devices illustrating the difficulty of rule-based ethics rather than workable engineering specifications, but they remain the best-known fictional attempt to codify machine morality.
