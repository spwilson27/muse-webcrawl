Hypocapnia

Hypocapnia (from the Greek words ὑπό meaning "below normal" and καπνός meaning "smoke"), also known as hypocarbia, is a state of reduced carbon dioxide in the blood. Hypocapnia usually results from deep or rapid breathing, known as hyperventilation. It is the opposite of hypercapnia, the state of excessive carbon dioxide in the blood.

Effects

Because carbon dioxide in the blood is in equilibrium with carbonic acid, a fall in CO2 raises blood pH, producing a respiratory alkalosis. Hypocapnia also causes cerebral vasoconstriction, reducing blood flow to the brain, which can lead to light-headedness, visual disturbances, and fainting. Other effects include tingling of the extremities (paraesthesia), muscle cramps and tetany — partly because alkalosis reduces the concentration of free ionized calcium — and a shift of the oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve (the Bohr effect) that reduces oxygen release to tissues.

Even though the carbon dioxide level falls, hypocapnia during hyperventilation is usually driven by anxiety, pain, fever, or conditions such as pulmonary embolism, asthma, or sepsis. Deliberate hyperventilation by swimmers and free-divers before a dive is dangerous: it suppresses the urge to breathe without significantly increasing oxygen stores, and can cause shallow water blackout.

Diagnosis and treatment

Hypocapnia is identified from an arterial or venous blood gas measurement showing a partial pressure of CO2 below the normal range (approximately 35–45 mmHg). Treatment is directed at the underlying cause of the hyperventilation; in the acute anxiety setting, reassurance and controlled breathing may be used.
