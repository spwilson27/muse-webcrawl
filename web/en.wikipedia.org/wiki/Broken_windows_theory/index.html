Broken windows theory

[Image: a run-down urban street with a broken window]
Broken windows theory
FieldCriminology
Introduced1982
ProponentsJames Q. Wilson, George L. Kelling

The broken windows theory is a criminological theory that states that visible signs of crime, anti-social behaviour and civil disorder create an urban environment that encourages further crime and disorder, including serious crimes. The theory suggests that policing methods that target minor crimes, such as vandalism, loitering, public drinking and fare evasion, help to create an atmosphere of order and lawfulness.

The theory was introduced in a 1982 article by social scientists James Q. Wilson and George L. Kelling. It was further popularised in the 1990s by New York City police commissioner William Bratton and Mayor Rudy Giuliani, whose policing policies were influenced by it.

Origin

Wilson and Kelling used the image of broken windows to illustrate how neighbourhoods may decay into disorder: if a window in a building is broken and left unrepaired, other windows will soon be broken, because an unrepaired window signals that no one cares about the building. The theory builds on earlier work by Philip Zimbardo, whose 1969 experiments with abandoned cars in the Bronx and Palo Alto showed that vandalism spread much faster in areas perceived as uncared for.

Policy applications

The theory underpinned zero tolerance policing strategies and order-maintenance policing in cities such as New York. Crime rates fell sharply in New York during the 1990s, and supporters credited broken-windows policing; critics noted that crime fell across the United States during the same period, in cities that used very different strategies.

Criticism

Scholars have criticised the theory for weak causal evidence and for legitimising aggressive enforcement against minor offences, with disproportionate effects on poor and minority communities. Research by Robert Sampson and Stephen Raudenbush found that disorder and crime were largely explained by common underlying factors such as concentrated poverty and collective efficacy, rather than disorder directly causing crime.
