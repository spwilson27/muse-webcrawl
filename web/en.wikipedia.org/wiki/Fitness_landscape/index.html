Fitness landscape

Fitness landscape
A rugged fitness landscape with multiple peaks
Introduced bySewall Wright (1932)
FieldEvolutionary biology, evolutionary computation

A fitness landscape is a way of visualising the relationship between genotypes (or phenotypes) and reproductive success, or fitness. The concept was introduced by Sewall Wright in 1932: every possible genotype is assigned a position in a high-dimensional space of genetic variation, and a "height" equal to its fitness. Evolution by natural selection is then imagined as a population moving over this surface — uphill toward fitness peaks — while genetic drift can carry it across valleys, a central idea in Wright's shifting balance theory.

Features

A rugged landscape has many local peaks separated by valleys of low fitness, arising when genes interact (epistasis); a smooth landscape has a single accessible summit. On rugged landscapes, a hill-climbing population can become trapped on a low local peak, and crossing a valley may require drift, large populations, recombination, or changes in the environment that deform the landscape itself. John Maynard Smith's related idea of protein space showed that functional proteins form networks connected by single mutations, making gradual evolution possible even in vast sequence spaces. Related metaphors include fitness "seascapes", in which the surface itself changes over time, and the adaptive landscape models used in evolutionary computation and optimisation, where fitness landscapes describe the difficulty of search problems for genetic algorithms.

Criticism

The metaphor has been criticised because real genotype spaces are far too high-dimensional for two- or three-dimensional pictures, because fitness is frequency-dependent and environment-dependent rather than a fixed height, and because the picture can mislead by suggesting peaks are unique goals. Nevertheless it remains one of the most widely used organising images in evolutionary theory and a working tool in experimental evolution, where landscapes of antibiotic resistance and viral fitness are measured directly.
