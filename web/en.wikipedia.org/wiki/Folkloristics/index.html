Folkloristics

Folklorists document and analyse the traditional expressive culture of groups.

Folkloristics, also known as folklore studies, is the academic discipline devoted to the study of folklore. It emerged in 19th-century Europe and flourished in connection with romantic nationalism, as scholars collected fairy tales, ballads, myths and customs that they understood as expressions of a people's spirit. The Brothers Grimm in Germany and Elias Lönnrot, compiler of Finland's Kalevala, are foundational figures.

The English word "folklore" was coined by William Thoms in 1846 to replace "popular antiquities". The discipline studies the full range of traditional expressive culture: oral narratives (myths, legends, folktales, jokes, proverbs), traditional music and dance, material culture (folk art, vernacular architecture, traditional crafts), foodways, rituals, festivals and beliefs.

Approaches

Early approaches were comparative and historical: the Finnish historic-geographic method traced tale types across regions to reconstruct their origins, work that produced the Aarne–Thompson–Uther tale-type index. Vladimir Propp's Morphology of the Folktale (1928) analysed narrative structure. In the mid-20th century, especially in the United States, the performance approach shifted attention from texts to the event of performance, studying how stories are told, to whom, and with what artistry. Folklorists such as Alan Dundes broadened the definition of "folk" to any group sharing a common factor, and the study of folklore to any traditional expressive behaviour within it.

Institutions and fieldwork

Folkloristics is studied at universities in Europe (often as part of ethnology) and North America (folklore programmes at Indiana University, UC Berkeley and elsewhere). The American Folklore Society was founded in 1888. Fieldwork — recording informants, with attention to context and ethics — is central, and archives such as the Archive of Folk Culture at the Library of Congress preserve vast collections. Public folklore applies the discipline in museums, festivals and cultural-heritage work; UNESCO's intangible cultural heritage lists draw heavily on folkloristic research.
