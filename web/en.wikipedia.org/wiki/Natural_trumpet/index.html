Natural trumpet

A natural trumpet is a valveless brass instrument that is able to play the notes of the harmonic series. Such trumpets were used from the late medieval period through the Baroque and Classical eras, and they remain in use today in historically informed performance of early music.

Construction

The natural trumpet consists of a mouthpiece, a long cylindrical leadpipe, and a gradually expanding bell section, with the tubing folded or coiled for manageability. Unlike the modern valved trumpet, it has no valves, slides, or keys to alter the effective tube length while playing. Instruments were commonly built in D or C, with crooks inserted to change the fundamental pitch for different keys.

Technique

Because pitch selection depends entirely on the player's embouchure choosing among the harmonics of the tube, natural trumpet playing demands exceptional control, particularly in the high "clarino" register where harmonics lie close enough together to form scales. Notes outside the harmonic series — the so-called "forbidden" notes — could be lipped into tune or, in some twentieth-century reproductions, corrected with small finger holes, though such vent holes are not historically authentic.

History

Trumpets signalled in battle and ceremony for centuries before becoming art instruments. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, composers such as Johann Sebastian Bach, George Frideric Handel, and Henry Purcell wrote virtuoso clarino parts for the instrument. The invention of valves in the early nineteenth century displaced the natural trumpet in orchestras, though its brilliant, penetrating tone has been revived by period-instrument ensembles.
