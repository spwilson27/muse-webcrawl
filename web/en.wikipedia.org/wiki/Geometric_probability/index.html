Geometric probability

Geometric probability is a branch of probability theory concerned with problems in which outcomes are points in a geometric space — a line segment, a plane region or a solid — and probabilities are computed from lengths, areas or volumes. If a point is chosen uniformly at random from a region, the probability that it falls in a subregion is proportional to the measure of that subregion.

Classic problems

The most famous problem of geometric probability is Buffon's needle problem, posed in 1733 and solved by Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon: the probability that a needle dropped on a floor of parallel strips crosses a line between strips depends on π, allowing Monte Carlo estimation of π. Bertrand's paradox highlights the subtlety of defining "uniformly at random" for geometric objects such as chords of a circle.

Applications

Geometric probability underlies stochastic geometry, which models random spatial patterns in telecommunications, forestry, materials science and astronomy. Integral geometry provides tools, such as Crofton's formula, for computing expected values of geometric quantities.
