Hearing aid

Hearing aid
TypeElectroacoustic medical device
PurposeAmplify sound for people with hearing loss
Common stylesBehind-the-ear (BTE), in-the-ear (ITE), receiver-in-canal (RIC), completely-in-canal (CIC)

A hearing aid is a small electronic device worn in or behind the ear that amplifies sound to make it more intelligible for a person with hearing loss. All hearing aids contain a microphone, an amplifier (in modern devices a digital signal processor), a miniature loudspeaker (receiver), and a battery. Digital aids analyse incoming sound, applying frequency-specific gain, compression, feedback cancellation, and noise reduction according to the wearer's audiogram.

History

Non-electric aids such as ear trumpets were used from the 17th century. The first electric hearing aid, Miller Reese Hutchison's Akouphone (1898), used a carbon transmitter; vacuum-tube models followed in the 1920s, transistor aids in the 1950s made devices small enough to fit behind or in the ear, and fully digital hearing aids arrived commercially in 1996.

Types and fitting

Styles include behind-the-ear, receiver-in-canal, in-the-ear, in-the-canal, and completely-in-canal devices, as well as body-worn aids and bone-conduction devices for conductive losses. Hearing aids are usually fitted by audiologists or hearing-instrument specialists after hearing testing; in the United States, a 2022 FDA rule created a category of over-the-counter hearing aids for adults with perceived mild-to-moderate loss, substantially lowering cost barriers.

Limitations

Hearing aids do not restore normal hearing: they cannot fully separate speech from background noise, and benefit varies with the type and severity of loss. Severe-to-profound sensorineural loss may instead be treated with a cochlear implant. Despite their effectiveness, only a minority of people who could benefit from hearing aids use them, owing to cost, stigma, and discomfort.
