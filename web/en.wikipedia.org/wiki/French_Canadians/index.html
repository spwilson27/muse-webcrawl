French Canadians (French: Canadiens français) are an ethnic and linguistic group descended principally from the French colonists who settled New France in the 17th and 18th centuries. Today the term generally refers to French-speaking Canadians of that colonial ancestry, most of them concentrated in the province of Quebec, where they form the majority, with historic communities in Ontario, Manitoba, the Atlantic provinces and the northeastern United States.

Quebec City, founded in 1608, is the historic heart of French Canada.

History

French settlement began with the founding of Quebec City by Samuel de Champlain in 1608. Under the seigneurial system, a small founding population — some 10,000 immigrants in total — grew by natural increase into a distinct colonial society of farmers, fur traders and voyageurs, bound together by the Catholic Church and the French language. After Britain conquered New France in 1763, the Quebec Act of 1774 preserved French civil law and Catholic practice, allowing the community to maintain its identity within British North America. In the 19th and early 20th centuries, hundreds of thousands emigrated to New England mill towns, while those who stayed defined themselves by la survivance — the survival of French-Canadian culture.

Identity and politics

The Quiet Revolution of the 1960s secularised Quebec society and recast French-Canadian identity around the Quebec state; since then, many prefer the term Québécois within the province, while French Canadian and francophone remain in use for the broader national group, including the distinct Acadian people of the Maritimes. French-language rights outside Quebec have been contested through episodes such as Manitoba's schools question and Ontario's Regulation 17, and are now protected by the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and official bilingualism.

Culture

French-Canadian culture is marked by its dialect of French, a rich tradition of folk music and storytelling, cuisine including tourtière and poutine, winter carnival celebrations, and literature from authors such as Gabrielle Roy and Michel Tremblay. The flag of Quebec — the fleurdelisé — and the June 24 Saint-Jean-Baptiste Day (Quebec's national holiday) are central symbols.
