Kore (sculpture)

A kore (Greek: κόρη, "maiden"; plural korai) is a type of free-standing statue of a young woman produced in Archaic Greece, roughly between 650 and 480 BCE. Korai are the female counterparts of the nude male kouroi, and like them they stand frontally, one foot slightly advanced, with the enigmatic "Archaic smile". Unlike the nude kouros, the kore is always clothed, typically in a peplos or a chiton with an overfold, and the sculptor lavished attention on the patterned folds of drapery and the elaborate braided hairstyles.

Function

Korai were set up in sanctuaries as votive offerings to goddesses — especially Athena, Artemis, and Hera — and also served as grave markers for wealthy women. Inscribed bases sometimes name the dedicator, the sculptor, or the girl commemorated. It is generally not possible to say in an individual case whether a kore represents a goddess, a priestess, or an ordinary dedicant.

Style and famous examples

The type evolved over two centuries from stiff, blocky figures with wig-like hair to more naturalistic works with diagonal drapery patterns and supple modelling. In antiquity the statues were painted in bright colours, traces of which survive on several examples.

The most famous group comes from the Athenian Acropolis, where dozens of korai dedicated before the Persian sack of 480 BCE were buried in the Persian debris (Perserschutt) and excavated in the 1880s; they are now the pride of the Acropolis Museum. Celebrated individual pieces include the Peplos Kore (c. 530 BCE), the colourful Chian Kore, and the Kore of Lyons. A celebrated non-Athenian example is the offering of Nikandre from Delos (c. 650 BCE), among the earliest large-scale Greek marble statues.
