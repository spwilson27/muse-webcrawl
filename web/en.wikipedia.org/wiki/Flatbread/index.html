Flatbread

A flatbread is a bread made with flour, water, and salt, and then rolled into flattened dough. Many flatbreads are unleavened, although some are leavened, like pita bread. Flatbreads range from below one millimetre to a few centimetres thick, so they can be easily eaten without being sliced.

History

Flatbreads are among the earliest processed foods, with evidence of flatbread production from ancient Egypt and Sumer. Because they require no oven in some cases — being cooked on a hot stone, griddle or the wall of a clay oven — they suited both settled and nomadic peoples. Unleavened flatbread also has ritual significance, as in the matzo of Passover.

Varieties

Well-known flatbreads include naan and roti from South Asia, pita and lavash from the Middle East, tortillas from Mesoamerica (traditionally of maize, later also wheat), focaccia and piadina from Italy, crisp flatbrød and lefse from Scandinavia, and injera, a fermented flatbread of Ethiopia and Eritrea. Flatbreads serve as wraps for kebabs and sandwiches, as scoops for dips such as hummus, and as the base for dishes such as pizza and lahmacun.

Preparation

The dough is typically divided into balls, rolled thin, and cooked quickly at high heat, so that steam puffs leavened and some unleavened breads into a hollow pocket. Baking surfaces include the tandoor, tava, saj and comal.
