Japanese Americans (日系アメリカ人, Nikkei Amerikajin) are Americans of Japanese ancestry. Significant immigration from Japan to the United States began in the late 19th century, first to Hawaii and then to the West Coast, making Japanese Americans one of the oldest and most established Asian American communities.

Immigration history

Japanese labourers began arriving in Hawaii in 1885 and on the mainland soon after, working in agriculture, railroads, and fishing. The 1907 Gentlemen's Agreement and the Immigration Act of 1924 halted most Japanese immigration, creating a distinctive generational structure: the immigrant Issei, their U.S.-born Nisei children, and subsequent Sansei and Yonsei generations. Immigration resumed after the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952 allowed naturalisation and the 1965 act removed national-origins quotas.

Internment

Following the attack on Pearl Harbor, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066 in February 1942. Around 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, most of them U.S. citizens, were forcibly removed from the West Coast and incarcerated in camps such as Manzanar, Tule Lake, and Heart Mountain. Japanese American soldiers of the 442nd Regimental Combat Team and the 100th Infantry Battalion nevertheless became among the most decorated American units of the Second World War. In 1988 the Civil Liberties Act formally apologised and provided redress payments to surviving internees.

Culture and contributions

Japanese Americans have been prominent in politics (including Senators Daniel Inouye and Spark Matsunaga and Secretary Norman Mineta), science, the arts, and business. Community institutions such as Buddhist temples, Japanese-language schools, and festivals like Obon and Nisei Week remain centred in historic Japantowns in Los Angeles, San Francisco, and San Jose and in Hawaii, where people of Japanese ancestry form a large share of the population.
