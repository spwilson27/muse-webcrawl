Ice sheet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ice sheet is a mass of glacial ice covering more than 50,000 km² of land, thick enough to bury the landscape beneath it and flow under its own weight. Only two exist today: the Antarctic ice sheet (about 14 million km², up to 4.8 km thick, holding roughly 60% of the world's fresh water) and the Greenland ice sheet (about 1.7 million km²).

Ice sheets grow from accumulated snow compressed over millennia; their layered ice is a climate archive — bubbles of ancient air in cores from Antarctica record 800,000 years of atmosphere (see climate). They flow outward as glaciers and ice streams, calving icebergs where they meet the sea. During the last glacial maximum, ice sheets kilometres thick covered Canada and northern Europe, depressing the crust and lowering sea level by about 120 metres; their retreat sculpted the landscapes of the fjords and the Great Lakes.

Today both sheets are losing mass, contributing measurably to sea-level rise; the West Antarctic sheet, grounded below sea level, is the focus of concern about marine ice-sheet instability and multi-metre long-term sea-level commitment.
