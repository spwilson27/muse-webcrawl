Ali ibn Abi Talib (Arabic: علي بن أبي طالب; c. 600 – 661 CE), often called Imam Ali by Shia Muslims, was a cousin and son-in-law of the Islamic prophet Muhammad, and one of the most important figures in Islamic history. He was the fourth caliph of the Rashidun Caliphate (656–661) in Sunni tradition, and in Shia Islam he is the first Imam, regarded as Muhammad's rightful successor.

Life

Ali was born in Mecca, traditionally said to be inside the Kaaba, the son of Abu Talib, Muhammad's uncle and protector. Raised in Muhammad's household, he was among the very first converts to Islam — often counted the first male Muslim. He married Muhammad's daughter Fatima, with whom he had sons Hasan and Husayn. Ali distinguished himself in the early battles of the community, including Badr, Uhud and the Battle of the Trench, and is celebrated in tradition for his courage, eloquence and knowledge.

Caliphate

After the assassination of the caliph Uthman in 656, Ali was elected caliph in Medina. His reign was consumed by the First Fitna (civil war): he fought the rebellion of Aisha, Talha and Zubayr at the Battle of the Camel (656), and Mu'awiya, governor of Syria, at the Battle of Siffin (657), which ended inconclusively in arbitration. A faction that rejected the arbitration, the Kharijites, broke away, and one of them, Ibn Muljam, assassinated Ali in the Great Mosque of Kufa in 661. He is buried at Najaf, where the Imam Ali Shrine is one of Shia Islam's holiest sites.

Legacy

Ali is revered across Islam for his piety, justice and learning; the collection of sermons and sayings attributed to him, the Nahj al-Balagha ("Peak of Eloquence"), is a classic of Arabic literature. For Shia Muslims he is the central figure of the Imamate; Sufi orders also trace their spiritual lineages through him.
