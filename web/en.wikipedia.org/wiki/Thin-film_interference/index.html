Thin-film interference

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Colours on a soap film are produced by thin-film interference

Thin-film interference is a phenomenon in which light waves reflected by the upper and lower boundaries of a thin film interfere with one another, either reinforcing or cancelling particular wavelengths. It produces the iridescent colours seen in soap bubbles, oil films on water, and the wings of certain insects, and it underlies technologies such as anti-reflective coatings.

Mechanism

When light strikes a film whose thickness is comparable to the wavelength of light, part of the wave reflects from the top surface and part is transmitted, reflects from the bottom surface, and re-emerges. The two reflected waves have travelled different optical path lengths; whether they interfere constructively or destructively depends on the film thickness, the angle of incidence, the refractive indices involved, and the wavelength. A phase change of half a wavelength occurs when light reflects from a boundary with a medium of higher refractive index, which must be included in the calculation.

2 n d cos θ = m λ (for constructive interference, subject to phase changes)

Applications and examples

Because the colours depend sensitively on film thickness, interference has long been used to measure very small thicknesses and surface irregularities, for example in Newton's rings. Engineered multilayer films serve as dichroic mirrors, optical filters, and the low-emissivity coatings on modern glazing. Structural colour in butterfly wings and beetle elytra arises from analogous interference in microscopic layered structures.
