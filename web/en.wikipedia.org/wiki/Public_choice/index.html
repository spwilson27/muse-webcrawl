Public choice

Public choice, or public choice theory, is the application of economic methods to political science: it studies how self-interested voters, politicians, bureaucrats and interest groups behave within democratic institutions, rather than assuming public officials act purely for the public good. James M. Buchanan, a founder of the field, described it as "politics without romance".

History

Precursors include the Marquis de Condorcet's work on voting cycles, Knut Wicksell's analysis of taxation and unanimity, and Duncan Black's committee theory in the 1940s. Kenneth Arrow's Social Choice and Individual Values (1951) founded modern social choice theory. Anthony Downs's An Economic Theory of Democracy (1957) modelled rational voter ignorance and the median voter. Buchanan and Gordon Tullock's The Calculus of Consent (1962) launched constitutional political economy; Buchanan shared the 1986 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics for this work. Mancur Olson's The Logic of Collective Action (1965) explained why small concentrated interest groups organise more effectively than large diffuse ones, and William Niskanen modelled budget-maximising bureaucracy.

Main themes

Key topics include rent-seeking — Tullock's insight that resources spent lobbying for transfers are themselves a social cost; regulatory capture, developed by George Stigler; the principal–agent problems of bureaucracy; fiscal illusion; logrolling; and constitutional rules as constraints on majoritarian cycling. Public choice examines government failure alongside market failure, and has strongly influenced the study of constitutions, federalism and institutional design.

Criticism

Critics argue the theory overstates self-interest, understates ideology and civic virtue, and that its constitutional prescriptions smuggle in normative preferences for smaller government. Empirical work on voter behaviour finds expressive and group motives alongside instrumental ones, which public choice scholars have increasingly incorporated.
