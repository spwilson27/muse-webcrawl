Electrical brain stimulation

Electrical brain stimulation is the application of electric currents to the brain, by implanted electrodes or by electrodes applied to the scalp, in order to modulate neural activity. Techniques include deep brain stimulation (DBS), transcranial direct-current stimulation (tDCS), electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) and direct cortical stimulation used in neurosurgery and research.

History

In the nineteenth century, experiments by Eduard Hitzig and Gustav Fritsch, and later David Ferrier, showed that electrical stimulation of the cerebral cortex produced movements, founding the localisation of motor function. Wilder Penfield's stimulation mapping during epilepsy surgery in the twentieth century produced detailed maps of motor and sensory cortex and remains a clinical technique for preserving function during operations.

Methods

Deep brain stimulation implants electrodes in deep structures such as the subthalamic nucleus and is an established treatment for Parkinson's disease, essential tremor and dystonia. Transcranial direct-current stimulation passes weak currents through the scalp and is studied for depression, rehabilitation and cognitive effects. Electroconvulsive therapy induces a controlled seizure under anaesthesia and remains effective for severe, treatment-resistant depression and catatonia.

Applications and ethics

Research uses stimulation to probe perception, memory and consciousness, while reported effects on mood and personality raise ethical questions about identity, consent and enhancement.
