Pharmacovigilance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pharmacovigilance (PV), also known as drug safety, is the pharmaceutical science relating to the surveillance, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or any other medicine/vaccine-related problem. All medicines and vaccines undergo rigorous testing for safety and efficacy through clinical trials before they are authorised for use. However, processes in clinical trials can only detect the most common adverse reactions, so post-marketing surveillance is essential: pharmacovigilance continues throughout a product's life cycle.

The World Health Organization defines pharmacovigilance as "the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problem."

History

Modern pharmacovigilance took shape after the thalidomide tragedy of the early 1960s, in which a sedative prescribed in pregnancy caused severe birth defects in thousands of children. The disaster led to systematic adverse-drug-reaction reporting schemes, such as the United Kingdom's Yellow Card system (1964), and to the WHO Programme for International Drug Monitoring, established in 1968, whose global database is maintained by the Uppsala Monitoring Centre in Sweden.

Adverse event reporting

The core activity of pharmacovigilance is the collection and analysis of spontaneous reports of suspected adverse drug reactions from health professionals, patients, and manufacturers. Reports are coded using standard terminologies such as MedDRA and stored in databases including EudraVigilance (European Union), FAERS (United States), and VigiBase (WHO). Statistical signal-detection methods, such as disproportionality analysis, search these databases for drug–event combinations reported more often than expected.

Risk management

Marketing authorisation holders are required to maintain pharmacovigilance systems, submit periodic safety update reports, and prepare risk-management plans describing known and potential risks and how they will be minimised. Regulatory actions arising from safety signals range from updating product information to restricting indications, adding warnings, or withdrawing a medicine — as happened with rofecoxib (Vioxx) in 2004.

Actors and regulation

Key regulators include the European Medicines Agency and its Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee (PRAC), the US Food and Drug Administration, and national authorities such as the UK's MHRA. International harmonisation is promoted by the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH), whose E2 series of guidelines standardises safety reporting.
