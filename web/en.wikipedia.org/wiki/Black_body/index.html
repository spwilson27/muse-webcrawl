Black body

A black body or blackbody is an idealized physical body that absorbs all incident electromagnetic radiation, regardless of frequency or angle of incidence. The name "black body" is given because it absorbs all colors of light. In thermal equilibrium, a black body emits radiation—called black-body radiation—whose spectrum depends only on its temperature, not on its composition or shape.

Thermal radiation

The spectral radiance of a black body is described by Planck's law:

B(ν, T) = (2hν³/c²) · 1/(e^(hν/kT) − 1)

Integrating over all frequencies gives the Stefan–Boltzmann law, which states that the total power radiated per unit area is proportional to the fourth power of the absolute temperature. Wien's displacement law gives the wavelength at which the emission peaks, shifting to shorter wavelengths as temperature increases. Max Planck's derivation of his law in 1900, introducing the quantization of energy, is regarded as the birth of quantum mechanics.

Approximations in nature

No real material is a perfect black body, but many approximate one closely. A cavity with a small hole is the classic laboratory realization: radiation entering the hole is reflected internally until absorbed, and the radiation escaping the hole is characteristic black-body radiation. Stars, including the Sun (effective temperature about 5,778 K), approximate black bodies, as does the cosmic microwave background, which fits a black-body spectrum of 2.725 K to extraordinary precision.

Applications

Black-body theory underpins radiometry, pyrometry, climate science (the Earth's energy budget and greenhouse effect), and astrophysics, where stellar temperatures and luminosities are inferred from spectra.
