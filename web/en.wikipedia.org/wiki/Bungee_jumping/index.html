Bungee jumping

Bungee jumping is an activity involving jumping from a tall structure while connected to a large elastic cord. The tall structure is usually a fixed object such as a building, bridge or crane, but jumping from a movable platform such as a hot air balloon or helicopter is also possible. The thrill comes from the free fall and the rebound, when the cord stretches and the jumper flies upwards.

History

The practice is inspired by the land diving ritual of Pentecost Island in Vanuatu, where divers jump from wooden towers with vines tied to their ankles. Modern bungee jumping began on 1 April 1979 at the Clifton Suspension Bridge in Bristol, England, by members of the Oxford University Dangerous Sports Club, including David Kirke and Simon Keeling, inspired by a BBC film about the Vanuatu ritual. Commercial bungee jumping was popularised in New Zealand by A. J. Hackett, who opened the Kawarau Bridge bungy site near Queenstown in 1988.

Equipment and safety

The elastic cord is made of many strands of natural latex rubber encased in a woven outer cover. Harnesses may be body harnesses or ankle harnesses. Reputable operators follow strict safety standards, use redundant attachments, and calculate cord length and weight ranges carefully. Injuries are rare but can include whiplash, eye trauma from increased intraocular pressure, and dislocations if equipment is misused.

Records and culture

The highest commercial bungee jump is from the Macau Tower at 233 metres. Bungee jumping has featured in films such as the James Bond film GoldenEye (1995), whose opening jump from the Verzasca Dam in Switzerland was performed by stuntman Wayne Michaels.
