Induction motor

An induction motor, or asynchronous motor, is an AC electric motor in which the electric current in the rotor needed to produce torque is obtained by electromagnetic induction from the magnetic field of the stator winding. It is the most widely used type of electric motor in industry and in domestic appliances because of its simplicity, ruggedness and low cost.

Principle of operation

A polyphase supply applied to the stator windings creates a rotating magnetic field. The relative motion between this field and the stationary rotor conductors induces currents in the rotor, which produce a torque tending to rotate the rotor in the direction of the field. The rotor always turns slightly slower than the synchronous speed of the stator field; the difference, expressed as a percentage of synchronous speed, is called slip. If the rotor turned at synchronous speed there would be no relative motion, no induced current, and no torque.

Types

The most common rotor design is the squirrel-cage rotor, consisting of aluminium or copper bars short-circuited by end rings. A wound rotor has windings connected to slip rings, allowing external resistance to be added for starting or speed control. Single-phase induction motors require a starting arrangement such as a capacitor or shaded pole.

History

The induction motor was developed independently in the 1880s by Galileo Ferraris and Nikola Tesla, and was commercialised by companies such as Westinghouse. It became the workhorse of industrial electrification, driving pumps, fans, compressors, machine tools and conveyor systems.

Speed control

Historically run at nearly constant speed determined by supply frequency and pole count, induction motors are now commonly controlled by variable-frequency drives (VFDs), which adjust the supply frequency and voltage to vary speed efficiently.
