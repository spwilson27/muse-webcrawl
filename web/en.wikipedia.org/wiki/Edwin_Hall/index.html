Edwin Herbert Hall (November 7, 1855 – November 20, 1938) was an American physicist who discovered the Hall effect in 1879, while still a graduate student. The effect — the production of a voltage across a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field — is today measured in laboratories worldwide and used in countless sensors and semiconductor devices.

Life and career

Hall was born in Gorham, Maine, and graduated from Bowdoin College in 1875. He pursued graduate study at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore, where in 1879, prompted by a passage in Maxwell's Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism and encouraged by his supervisor Henry Rowland, he found that a magnetic field deflects the current in a thin gold leaf, producing a transverse voltage proportional to the field. The result, published in the American Journal of Mathematics, became the subject of his doctoral dissertation (1880).

After a year of research in Europe, Hall joined Harvard University in 1881, rising to full professor of physics in 1895 and remaining until his retirement in 1921. He became a respected teacher and textbook author — his Elementary Lessons in Physics and laboratory manuals with Joseph Bergen shaped American secondary-school physics teaching — and was elected to the National Academy of Sciences. He died in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1938.

The Hall effect

The sign and magnitude of the Hall voltage reveal the sign and density of charge carriers in a material, making the effect an indispensable diagnostic in solid-state physics; its quantised form, discovered in 1980, won a Nobel Prize and defines the standard of electrical resistance. Hall himself pursued related magneto-electric phenomena throughout his career and took a lifelong interest in the kinetic theory of heat conduction in metals.
