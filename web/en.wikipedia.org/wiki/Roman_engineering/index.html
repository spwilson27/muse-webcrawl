Roman engineering

Roman engineering refers to the engineering achievements of ancient Rome, which drew on earlier Etruscan, Greek and Hellenistic knowledge but applied it with unprecedented organisational scale and durability. Roman engineers built roads, aqueducts, bridges, harbours, fortifications and public buildings across an empire stretching from Britain to Mesopotamia, many of which remain in use or survive as ruins today.

Water supply

The Romans constructed hundreds of aqueducts, some over 60 km long, that carried water by gravity along carefully surveyed gradients of as little as 15–30 cm per kilometre. Rome itself was served by eleven aqueducts delivering over a million cubic metres of water daily to baths, fountains, latrines and private houses. Engineers used inverted siphons to cross valleys, settling tanks to clarify water, and the castellum divisorium to distribute it. Frontinus, water commissioner of Rome, recorded the system in De aquaeductu.

Roads and bridges

The Roman road network eventually covered some 80,000 km of paved roads, built in layers of rubble, gravel and paving stones over a cambered, drained foundation. Roads such as the Via Appia (begun 312 BC) allowed rapid movement of armies and administration. Roman bridges, built with semicircular stone arches on concrete piers, include the Alcántara Bridge in Spain and the Pons Fabricius in Rome (62 BC), still in use.

Materials and techniques

Key materials included Roman concrete, fired brick, dressed stone, lead piping and timber. Roman engineers perfected the arch, vault and dome; developed cranes powered by treadwheels; used the groma and chorobates for surveying; and applied hydraulic mining (ruina montium) on an industrial scale, as at Las Médulas in Spain. The geographer Strabo remarked that the Romans excelled in the practical arts the Greeks neglected: roads, aqueducts and sewers.

Military engineering

Roman legions carried their own engineering capability: marching camps were fortified nightly, siege works such as Caesar's double circumvallation at Alesia enclosed entire armies, and artillery (ballistae, onagers) was standardised. Military engineers (architecti, immunes) maintained frontier works including Hadrian's Wall and the Danube limes.
