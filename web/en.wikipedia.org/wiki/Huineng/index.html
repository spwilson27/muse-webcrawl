Dajian Huineng (Chinese: 大鑒惠能; pinyin: Dàjiàn Huìnéng; Wade–Giles: Ta-chien Hui-neng; Japanese: Daikan Enō; Korean: Hyeneung; 638–713), commonly known as the Sixth Patriarch, was a Chinese Chan (Zen) Buddhist monk, recognised as the Sixth and Last Patriarch of Chan Buddhism. His teachings and the Platform Sutra — the only Chinese Buddhist work traditionally honoured with the title "sutra" reserved for the Buddha's own discourses — form the doctrinal foundation of the Southern School of Chan.

Life

According to the traditional accounts preserved in the Platform Sutra, Huineng was born to a poor family in Xinzhou (modern Guangdong), was illiterate, and supported his widowed mother by selling firewood. Hearing a customer recite the Diamond Sutra, he experienced awakening and travelled north to the monastery of the Fifth Patriarch, Hongren, at Huangmei. Assigned to thresh rice and split wood, the newcomer from the south was derided as an illiterate "barbarian".

When Hongren invited verses expressing insight, his head monk Shenxiu wrote: "The body is the bodhi tree, / The mind is like a bright mirror's stand. / At all times we must strive to polish it / And must not let dust collect." Huineng replied: "Bodhi originally has no tree. / The mirror also has no stand. / The Buddha-nature is always clear and pure; / Where is there room for dust?" Hongren secretly recognised him as his successor, transmitted the robe and bowl, and sent him south by night for his safety.

Teachings

Huineng taught at Caoxi (Baolin Temple, modern Nanhua Temple) near Shaoguan and in Guangzhou, ordaining only later in life. His doctrine of sudden enlightenment — that awakening is not the cumulative result of gradual polishing but the direct seeing of one's own nature — was proclaimed against the "gradual" Northern School of Shenxiu in a later succession dispute, and the Platform Sutra as edited by his disciple Fahai (with later revisions) became the Southern School's charter. Core themes include "no-thought" (wunian), "non-form" and "non-abiding", and the identity of meditation (ding) and wisdom (hui).

Legacy

Huineng's remarkably preserved body is enshrined at Nanhua Temple in Shaoguan, where it remains an object of veneration. All surviving lineages of Chan/Zen trace their transmission through Huineng, and his verse contest with Shenxiu remains the most famous parable in Zen literature.
