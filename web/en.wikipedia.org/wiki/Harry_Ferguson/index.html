Henry George "Harry" Ferguson (4 November 1884 – 25 October 1960) was an Irish engineer and inventor who is noted for his role in the development of the modern agricultural tractor and its three-point linkage system, and for building the first four-wheel-drive Formula One car, the Ferguson P99.

Early life

Ferguson grew up on his family's farm near Dromore, County Down. He worked in his brother's cycle and motor repair business in Belfast, became a racing motorcyclist, and in 1909 designed and flew his own aeroplane, making one of the first powered flights in Ireland. He then set up a garage business in Belfast selling cars and tractors.

The Ferguson System

Dissatisfied with the tractors of the time, Ferguson developed a system of mounting implements directly on the tractor through a three-point linkage with hydraulic draft control, patented from 1926. After building tractors with David Brown in the 1930s (the Ferguson-Brown), he entered the famous "handshake agreement" with Henry Ford in 1938, under which Ford built the Ford 9N (1939) and 2N using Ferguson's patents. Disputes ended the partnership in 1947; Ferguson sued and received a substantial settlement in 1952, while his own Ferguson TE20 — the beloved "Little Grey Fergie" — was built by the Standard Motor Company at Coventry from 1946 and transformed small farming worldwide.

Later years

Ferguson's company merged with Massey-Harris in 1953 to form Massey Ferguson. Through Harry Ferguson Research he also pursued four-wheel-drive systems, culminating in the P99 racing car, which won the 1961 Oulton Park Gold Cup with Stirling Moss — the only victory of a four-wheel-drive car in Formula One.
