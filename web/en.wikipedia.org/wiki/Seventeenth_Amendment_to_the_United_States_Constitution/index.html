Seventeenth Amendment to the United States Constitution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Seventeenth Amendment to the United States Constitution provides for the direct election of United States senators by the people of each state, replacing the original system of election by state legislatures. Proposed by Congress in 1912 and ratified on 8 April 1913 (proclaimed 31 May 1913), it retains equal representation of states (two senators each, now serving six-year terms as before) and provides that vacancies be filled by popular election, with governors empowered to make temporary appointments if state law permits.

The amendment was the Progressive Era's flagship democratic reform, driven by decades of deadlock and corruption in legislative elections (bribery scandals such as the Lorimer case), by state-level workarounds (the “Oregon system” of advisory primaries binding legislators), and by the House's repeated passage of direct-election resolutions from the 1890s. Its effects are debated: it nationalised senatorial politics and removed a federalism check (Repeal movements surface periodically in conservative legal thought), while defenders note the pre-1913 Senate's documented corruption and empty seats. It completed, with the Sixteenth (income tax) and Nineteenth (women's suffrage) Amendments, the Progressive remaking of the constitutional order.
