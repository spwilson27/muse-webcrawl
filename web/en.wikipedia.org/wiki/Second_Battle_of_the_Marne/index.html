The Second Battle of the Marne (15 July – 6 August 1918) was the last major German offensive of the First World War on the Western Front and the turning point that began the Allied advance leading to the Armistice. The German attack failed to break the Allied lines east and west of Reims, and a powerful Franco-American counter-offensive erased the Marne salient.

Background

Germany's Spring Offensive of 1918 had won ground but not decision; by July, Erich Ludendorff planned a final effort — the Friedensturm ("Peace Offensive") — attacking across the Marne around Château-Thierry and towards Épernay and Châlons, hoping to draw Allied reserves south before a decisive blow in Flanders. Allied commander Ferdinand Foch, forewarned by intelligence and prisoner interrogation, prepared a defence in depth and massed reserves for a counterstroke.

The German attack

On 15 July, German forces attacked east of Reims against French Fourth Army under Henri Gouraud, whose elastic defence — lightly held forward positions, then a strong main line — blunted the assault within hours. West of Reims the Germans crossed the Marne and advanced several miles before being contained by French, American and Italian divisions; the U.S. 3rd Division earned the name "Rock of the Marne" for its stand on the river.

The Allied counter-offensive

On 18 July, Foch launched his counterattack against the western face of the salient. French Tenth Army under Charles Mangin, spearheaded by hundreds of Renault FT tanks and supported by American divisions including the 1st and 2nd, struck near Villers-Cotterêts, achieving surprise and advancing up to five miles on the first day. German forces began a fighting withdrawal from the salient; Soissons was retaken on 2 August, and by 6 August the battle ended with the front restored roughly to the line before the offensive.

Significance

The battle cost the Germans some 168,000 casualties, including around 29,000 prisoners and 600 guns captured by the Allies, against roughly 140,000 Allied casualties. More importantly, it ended Germany's capacity for offensive operations: the initiative passed permanently to the Allies, who followed up on 8 August at Amiens, opening the Hundred Days Offensive that ended the war in November 1918.
