Acquiescence bias

Acquiescence bias is a category of response bias in which respondents to a survey have a tendency to agree with all the questions, indicating a positive connotation. Also known as agreement bias or "yea-saying", it is a well-documented problem in survey research, personality assessment, and opinion polling, because it threatens the validity of measurements: if a respondent agrees both with a statement and with its negation, the responses cannot reflect the respondent's true position.

Causes

Researchers have proposed several explanations for acquiescence. Respondents may be deferential to the perceived authority of the interviewer or institution; they may be satisficing — giving a low-effort answer rather than thinking carefully — especially when questions are ambiguous or the topic is uninteresting; or agreement may simply be the socially desirable response in their culture. Studies have found acquiescence to vary with education level, age, and culture.

Mitigation

Survey designers counter acquiescence by balancing questionnaires with equal numbers of positively and negatively worded items (reverse scoring), so that indiscriminate agreement cancels out. Forced-choice formats, in which respondents must choose between statements rather than agree or disagree, are also used. Analysts sometimes compute an acquiescence index from pairs of logically opposed items to detect and correct the bias statistically.

Related biases

Acquiescence is related to, but distinct from, social desirability bias (answering to look good), the tendency to choose extreme responses, and cultural response styles documented in cross-national surveys.
