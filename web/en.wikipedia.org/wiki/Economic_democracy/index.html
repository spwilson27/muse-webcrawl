Economic democracy is a socioeconomic philosophy that proposes to shift decision-making power in the economy from a small group of corporate shareholders and managers to a larger group of stakeholders — above all workers — thereby extending the democratic principles of political life ("one person, one vote") into workplaces and economic institutions. Proponents argue that political democracy is incomplete or unstable while economic power remains concentrated; critics respond that dispersing ownership and control can blunt investment, efficiency and accountability.

Forms and proposals

  • Worker cooperatives — firms owned and governed by their employees, exemplified by Spain's Mondragon Corporation, the world's largest cooperative federation.
  • Codetermination — legal rights for employees to elect representatives to company boards, most developed in Germany (Mitbestimmung), where large firms reserve up to half of supervisory-board seats for worker representatives.
  • Employee ownership — share schemes such as ESOPs in the United States or Britain's John Lewis Partnership trust model.
  • Wage-earner funds — Sweden's Meidner plan (1970s) proposed gradually transferring corporate ownership to collective employee funds; enacted in heavily diluted form, it remains the classic social-democratic scheme of gradual socialisation.
  • Stakeholder and social-wealth models — David Schweickart's "economic democracy" blueprint combines worker self-management, markets for goods, and social control of investment; other variants include participatory economics (parecon) and community land trusts and public banking.

History

The idea has roots in 19th-century cooperative and guild-socialist thought — J. S. Mill predicted worker cooperatives would eventually outcompete conventional firms — and in German and Scandinavian labour movements after 1945. It resurfaced in debates over industrial democracy in the 1960s–70s (Britain's Bullock Report of 1977 recommended board-level worker representation but was not implemented) and in contemporary discussions of inequality, precarious work and the governance of large technology companies.

Arguments

Supporters claim economic democracy improves job satisfaction, information flow, income equality and democratic culture, and cite evidence that cooperatives are at least as productive as conventional firms and more resilient in downturns. Opponents argue that capital markets discipline managers more effectively than workplace electorates, that worker-owned firms under-invest and struggle to raise capital, and that employees' jobs and savings concentrated in one firm is poor risk diversification. Empirical debate continues, with most real economies mixing elements of both models.
