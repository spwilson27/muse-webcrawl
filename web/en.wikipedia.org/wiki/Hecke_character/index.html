Hecke character

In number theory, a Hecke character is a generalisation of a Dirichlet character in which the underlying group is the ideal class group, or more generally the idele class group, of a number field. Hecke characters were introduced by Erich Hecke in 1918–1920 in his work extending the theory of L-functions beyond Dirichlet's original setting; in the German literature they are called Größencharaktere.

Definition

For a number field K and a modulus (an ideal together with conditions at the infinite places), a Hecke character is a homomorphism from the group of fractional ideals coprime to the modulus into the non-zero complex numbers, which on principal ideals is given by a fixed continuous character of the infinite components — allowing, crucially, values that are not roots of unity, such as powers of the norm. In modern language, algebraic Hecke characters are characters of the idele class group of K whose infinite component is algebraic.

Hecke L-functions

To a Hecke character χ one associates a Hecke L-function, defined by an Euler product over prime ideals, L(s, χ) = Π (1 − χ(𝔭) N𝔭−s)−1. Hecke proved that these L-functions have analytic continuation to the whole complex plane (except for a possible pole of the trivial character) and satisfy a functional equation relating s to 1 − s. For the rational numbers, Hecke characters reduce to Dirichlet characters and their L-functions to Dirichlet L-functions.

Examples and applications

A classical example comes from imaginary quadratic fields: for the field ℚ(i), a Hecke character built from the geometry of the Gaussian integers yields an L-function whose special values count representations of integers connected with the arithmetic of elliptic curves with complex multiplication. Hecke characters parametrise the abelian extensions and L-functions treated by class field theory, and they are the GL(1) case of automorphic forms — the foundation on which the general Langlands program builds.

Hecke's theory of L-functions for characters of infinite order also modelled the analytic machinery later used by Tate in his 1950 thesis, which recast Hecke's proofs using Fourier analysis on the adeles.
