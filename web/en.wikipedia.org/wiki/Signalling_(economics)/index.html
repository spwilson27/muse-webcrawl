Signalling (economics)

In contract theory and economics, signalling is the idea that one party (the agent) credibly conveys information about itself to another party (the principal) in order to overcome information asymmetry. The field was pioneered by Michael Spence, whose 1973 job-market model showed how education can serve as a signal of worker ability even if it adds nothing to productivity.

Spence's job-market model

Employers cannot observe a worker's productivity before hiring. Workers can acquire education at a cost that is lower for high-ability workers (the "single-crossing" condition). In a separating equilibrium, high-ability workers obtain education, low-ability workers do not, and employers rationally pay higher wages to the educated — the signal is credible precisely because it is too costly for low-ability types to imitate. Spence shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics with George Akerlof and Joseph Stiglitz for analyses of markets with asymmetric information.

Other applications

  • Corporate finance: dividends and debt levels as signals of firm quality (Ross, 1977); retaining equity in an IPO as a signal (Leland & Pyle).
  • Advertising: heavy advertising expenditure signalling product quality, since only firms expecting repeat purchases recoup it.
  • Insurance and credit markets, where signalling complements screening by the uninformed party.
  • Biology: costly signals such as the peacock's tail (the handicap principle), which economists and biologists developed in parallel.
