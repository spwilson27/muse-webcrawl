Baritone

A baritone is a type of classical male singing voice whose vocal range lies between the bass and tenor voice types. The typical baritone range is from the second F below middle C (F2) to the F above middle C (F4). The term comes from the Greek barytonos, meaning "heavy sounding." Composers typically write music for this voice in the range from the second A below middle C to the A above middle C (A2 to A4) in choral music, and from the second G below middle C to the G above middle C (G2 to G4) in operatic music.

History

The baritone voice emerged as a distinct category during the 18th and 19th centuries, as operatic writing increasingly differentiated the higher male voices. In early music, parts now sung by baritones were often assigned to basses. By the era of Mozart, a lighter, higher male voice was commonly used for younger or more agile characters, while the mature baritone category solidified in the works of composers such as Rossini, Donizetti, and Verdi, the latter writing many of his most complex male roles for baritones.

Subtypes

Baritone subtypes include the lyric baritone, a lighter and sweeter voice suited to roles such as Papageno in The Magic Flute; the dramatic baritone, heavier and more powerful, used for roles such as Scarpia in Tosca; the Verdi baritone, combining legato phrasing with high tessitura; the baritenor and the bass-baritone, whose ranges extend toward those of the tenor and bass respectively. In musical theater and popular music, the baritone range is also the most common male voice type.

Roles

Famous baritone roles include Figaro in Rossini's The Barber of Seville, Rigoletto and Macbeth in Verdi's operas, Don Giovanni in Mozart's opera of that name, and Escamillo in Bizet's Carmen. Celebrated baritones have included Mattia Battistini, Lawrence Tibbett, Leonard Warren, Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau, Thomas Hampson, and Bryn Terfel.
