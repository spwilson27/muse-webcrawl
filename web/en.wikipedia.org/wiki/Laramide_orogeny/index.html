Laramide orogeny

The Laramide orogeny was a period of mountain building in western North America that began in the Late Cretaceous, about 80 to 70 million years ago, and ended around 35 to 55 million years ago in the Eocene. It produced the Rocky Mountains and a series of basement-cored uplifts and intermontane basins from Montana to New Mexico, and related deformation in Mexico.

Mechanism

The orogeny is attributed to changes in subduction of the Farallon Plate beneath the North American Plate: the subducting slab flattened, so that magmatism and deformation jumped far inland, lifting deep Precambrian basement along steep reverse faults rather than folding only sedimentary strata, as in the earlier, thin-skinned Sevier orogeny.

Effects

Laramide uplifts divided the Western Interior Seaway's former basin into smaller intermontane basins that filled with sediment and hosted rich fossil faunas. Uplifts such as the Wind River, Bighorn and Front Ranges created the modern topography of the Rockies. The end of the orogeny is associated with the slab steepening again, igniting widespread ignimbrite volcanism in the mid-Tertiary.
