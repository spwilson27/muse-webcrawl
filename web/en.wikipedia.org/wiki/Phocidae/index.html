KingdomAnimalia
PhylumChordata
ClassMammalia
OrderCarnivora

FamilyPhocidae (Gray, 1821)

Phocidae is the family of earless seals, also called true seals or phocids. Together with the eared seals (Otariidae — sea lions and fur seals) and the walrus (Odobenidae), they make up the pinnipeds. The family comprises about 19 living species in two subfamilies, Phocinae (northern seals) and Monachinae (southern seals, including elephant seals, monk seals, and the Antarctic seals).

Characteristics

Earless seals lack external ear flaps, having only a small ear opening on the side of the head. Their hind flippers cannot be rotated forward under the body, so on land they move awkwardly by wriggling and bouncing, while in the water the hind flippers provide the main propulsion. Their foreflippers are short, furred, and clawed, used mainly for steering while swimming. Phocids generally have shorter, hairier flippers and a rounder body than otariids, and many are strongly built for life in cold water.

Distribution and species

Phocids are widely distributed along coastlines of the Northern Hemisphere and Antarctica, with some species in inland seas and lakes, such as the Baikal seal and the Caspian seal. The largest is the southern elephant seal, whose males can exceed 4 metres and 2 tonnes; the smallest is the ringed seal. Familiar species include the harbour seal, grey seal, harp seal, leopard seal, Weddell seal, and the critically endangered Mediterranean and Hawaiian monk seals. The Caribbean monk seal was declared extinct in the twentieth century.

Evolution

Fossil phocids are known from the Miocene. Early pinnipeds such as Puijila and Enaliarctos document the transition from terrestrial carnivoran ancestors to semiaquatic life. Phocids are thought to have spread from northern oceans, with monachines reaching the Southern Hemisphere.
