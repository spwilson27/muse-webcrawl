Lepton

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Chart of the six leptons: electron, muon, tau and their neutrinos, with masses and charges]
The six leptons of the Standard Model.

In particle physics, a lepton is an elementary particle of half-integer spin (spin 1/2) that does not undergo strong interactions. Two main classes of leptons exist: charged leptons (also known as electron-like leptons) and neutral leptons, better known as neutrinos.

The charged leptons are the electron, the muon and the tau, carrying electric charge −e; each pairs with a neutrino (electron neutrino, muon neutrino, tau neutrino) to form a generation corresponding to the three quark generations. Unlike quarks, leptons are observed as free particles, and lepton flavour numbers are separately conserved in most processes — violated only by neutrino oscillations.

History

The electron was identified by J. J. Thomson in 1897. The muon, discovered in cosmic rays in 1936, prompted I. I. Rabi's quip 'Who ordered that?' The tau followed in the 1970s (Martin Perl). Neutrinos were postulated by Wolfgang Pauli in 1930 to rescue energy conservation in beta decay, and detected in 1956 (electron antineutrino, Reines and Cowan), 1962 (muon neutrino) and 2000 (tau neutrino, DONUT).

Properties

Lepton masses span an enormous range: the electron at 0.511 MeV, the muon 207 times heavier, the tau 3,477 MeV, while neutrinos have masses below about 1 eV — so tiny that their exact values and ordering remain unknown. Charged leptons interact electromagnetically and weakly; neutrinos interact only weakly (and gravitationally), so they pass through light-years of matter almost unimpeded, making them extraordinarily hard to detect but valuable as astronomical messengers.
