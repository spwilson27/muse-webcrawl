Malaria vaccine

A malaria vaccine is a vaccine used to prevent malaria, a mosquito-borne infectious disease caused by Plasmodium parasites that kills several hundred thousand people each year, most of them young children in sub-Saharan Africa. Developing such a vaccine has been exceptionally difficult because of the parasite's complex life cycle and antigenic variability. Two vaccines are now recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) for children in areas of moderate to high transmission of Plasmodium falciparum: RTS,S/AS01 (Mosquirix) and R21/Matrix-M.


A child receives a malaria vaccination in a pilot programme in Africa.

RTS,S

RTS,S, developed by GSK in partnership with PATH over more than three decades, targets the circumsporozoite protein of P. falciparum, aiming to prevent the parasite from infecting the liver. In its large phase 3 trial, efficacy against clinical malaria in young children was roughly 36–39% over four years with a booster dose. Following pilot introductions in Ghana, Kenya, and Malawi from 2019 — in which over two million children were vaccinated and a significant reduction in severe malaria and child deaths was observed — the WHO recommended RTS,S for widespread use in October 2021, making it the first vaccine ever recommended against a parasitic disease in humans.

R21/Matrix-M

R21, developed by the University of Oxford's Jenner Institute with the Serum Institute of India, uses a similar circumsporozoite construct with a higher proportion of the target antigen and the Matrix-M adjuvant. In trials in seasonal transmission settings it showed efficacy of about 75% over the year following a booster — meeting the WHO's long-standing 75% efficacy goal. The WHO recommended R21 in October 2023, and its large, low-cost manufacturing capacity is expected to relieve the supply constraints that limited RTS,S rollout.

Challenges and future directions

Both vaccines require four doses and provide partial, waning protection, so they are deployed alongside insecticide-treated nets, chemoprevention, and prompt treatment rather than replacing them. Research continues on whole-sporozoite vaccines (such as PfSPZ), blood-stage and transmission-blocking vaccines, and mRNA platforms, aiming for higher and more durable efficacy including against Plasmodium vivax.
