Catiline Orations

The Catiline Orations (Latin: Orationes in Catilinam) are a set of four speeches given in 63 BC by Marcus Tullius Cicero, the consul of Rome that year, accusing the senator Lucius Sergius Catilina (Catiline) of plotting to overthrow the Roman Republic. They are among the most famous surviving examples of Latin oratory and have been staples of Latin education for centuries.

Background

Catiline, a disgruntled aristocrat who had twice failed to win the consulship, organised a conspiracy to seize power with the backing of indebted veterans and discontented nobles. After an attempted plot was exposed, Cicero, acting on information from informants and from Catiline's associates, addressed the Senate and the people to unmask the conspiracy.

The four speeches

  • First Oration — delivered in the Senate, in the Temple of Jupiter Stator, on 8 November 63 BC, with Catiline present. It opens with the famous words Quo usque tandem abutere, Catilina, patientia nostra? ("How long, Catiline, will you abuse our patience?") and demands that Catiline leave the city. Catiline fled Rome that night to join his army in Etruria.
  • Second Oration — delivered to the people in the Forum the following day, announcing Catiline's departure and characterising his followers as debtors, gamblers, and ruined aristocrats.
  • Third Oration — delivered to the people on 3 December, after the arrest in Rome of the conspirators Lentulus, Cethegus, and others, who had been caught through the interception of their letters to the Allobroges, a Gallic tribe whose envoys they had tried to enlist.
  • Fourth Oration — delivered in the Senate on 5 December, during the debate on the punishment of the arrested conspirators. Cicero guided the Senate toward the death sentence advocated by Cato the Younger, against Julius Caesar's proposal of imprisonment; the conspirators were executed that evening in the Tullianum.

Aftermath and publication

The conspiracy collapsed, and Catiline was killed in battle at Pistoria in January 62 BC. Cicero published the speeches in 60 BC, in revised form, as part of a collection of his consular speeches. His execution of Roman citizens without trial later became a charge against him and contributed to his exile in 58 BC. The orations remain models of rhetorical structure and invective, and phrases from them — including O tempora, o mores! ("Oh the times! Oh the customs!") — are still widely quoted.
