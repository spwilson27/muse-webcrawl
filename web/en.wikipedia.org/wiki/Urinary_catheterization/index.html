Urinary catheterization

In urinary catheterization, a latex, polyurethane, or silicone tube known as a urinary catheter is inserted into the urinary bladder through the urethra to allow urine to drain from the bladder for collection. It may also be used to inject liquids used for treatment or diagnosis of bladder conditions.

Types

The most common indwelling catheter is the Foley catheter, held in place by a balloon inflated with sterile water inside the bladder. Intermittent (short-term) catheters are inserted and removed as needed, often by the patient. External catheters, such as condom catheters for men, collect urine without entering the urethra, and suprapubic catheters are placed through the abdominal wall directly into the bladder.

Indications

Common indications include acute or chronic urinary retention, monitoring of urine output in critically ill patients, drainage during and after certain surgeries, management of urinary incontinence when other measures fail, and administration of medication directly into the bladder.

Complications

The most significant complication is catheter-associated urinary tract infection, one of the most common healthcare-associated infections. Other risks include urethral trauma, bleeding, bladder spasms, and, with long-term use, bladder stones and an increased risk of bladder cancer. Aseptic insertion technique and prompt removal when no longer needed are central to prevention.
