Turgor pressure

Turgor pressure
FieldPlant physiology, cell biology
Typical magnitude0.1–1.0 MPa in plant cells
RelatedOsmosis, water potential, plasmolysis

Turgor pressure is the force within the cell that pushes the plasma membrane against the cell wall. It is caused by the osmotic flow of water from an area of lower solute concentration outside the cell into the cell's vacuole, which has a higher solute concentration. Turgor is the main determinant of the rigidity of plant tissues: when turgor is high, stems and leaves are firm; when it falls, plants wilt.

Mechanism

Plant cells are hypertonic relative to their surroundings in fresh water conditions, so water enters by osmosis across the semipermeable plasma membrane and tonoplast into the vacuole. The swelling protoplast presses against the relatively rigid cellulose cell wall, generating hydrostatic pressure. Equilibrium is reached when the pressure potential balances the osmotic (solute) potential, so the cell's total water potential equals that of its surroundings.

Functions

Turgor pressure provides structural support to non-woody plants, drives cell expansion during growth, and powers movements such as the opening and closing of stomata by guard cells and the rapid leaf movements of sensitive plants such as Mimosa pudica. Loss of turgor in guard cells closes stomata, conserving water.

Measurement and loss

Turgor can be measured with a pressure probe inserted into individual cells, or estimated from water potential components. When a cell is placed in a hypertonic solution, water leaves, turgor falls to zero, and the membrane pulls away from the wall — a process called plasmolysis. Severe or prolonged turgor loss in tissues causes wilting.
