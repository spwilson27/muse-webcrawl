Aden Colony

Image placeholder: the harbour and crater town of Aden

The Aden Colony (Arabic: مستعمرة عدن) was a British Crown colony from 1937 to 1963, consisting of the port city of Aden and its immediate surroundings on the southern coast of the Arabian Peninsula. Before 1937 the territory had been administered as part of British India, first under the Bombay Presidency and later as the Aden Province under a Chief Commissioner.

History

Aden was occupied by forces of the British East India Company in January 1839, valued as a coaling station on the sea route between Britain and India. Its importance grew greatly after the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869, and Aden became one of the busiest bunkering ports in the world.

In 1937 Aden was detached from India and became a Crown colony directly under the Colonial Office. The surrounding tribal hinterland was organised separately as the Aden Protectorate, divided into western and eastern protectorates whose rulers had treaty relations with Britain.

Economy

The colony's economy rested on its free port, its oil refinery at Little Aden (completed in 1954 by British Petroleum), bunkering services, and an entrepôt trade. Aden was also a major station on imperial air routes and submarine telegraph cables.

End of the colony

Nationalist unrest, centred on the Aden Trades Union Congress and later on armed groups such as the National Liberation Front, intensified in the late 1950s and 1960s. In 1963 the colony was merged with the protectorates to form the Federation of South Arabia. Following the Aden Emergency, British forces withdrew in November 1967 and the territory became part of the independent People's Republic of South Yemen.
