Headless Horseman

The Headless Horseman is a mythical figure who has appeared in folklore around the world for centuries. He is traditionally said to ride a horse while holding his own severed head, or to be missing his head entirely, searching for it. The figure is associated particularly with Irish folklore — the dullahan — and with German and Scandinavian tales, and it entered American literature through Washington Irving's 1820 short story "The Legend of Sleepy Hollow".

In folklore

In Irish tradition the dullahan is a headless rider, usually on a black horse, who carries his head under his arm; his arrival is said to foretell or bring death. Similar figures appear in British tradition, including legends attached to various battlefields and country roads, and in German folklore, where headless riders are sometimes associated with warning travellers not to continue on a dangerous path.

The Legend of Sleepy Hollow

Irving's story, published in The Sketch Book of Geoffrey Crayon, Gent. (1820), made the figure famous. In the tale, the schoolmaster Ichabod Crane is pursued at night by the Headless Horseman, said to be the ghost of a Hessian trooper who lost his head to a cannonball during the American Revolutionary War and rides nightly in search of it. Crane vanishes after the chase; the story leaves ambiguous whether the Horseman was supernatural or his rival Brom Bones in disguise. The story has been adapted many times, including the 1949 Disney animated film The Adventures of Ichabod and Mr. Toad and Tim Burton's 1999 film Sleepy Hollow.
