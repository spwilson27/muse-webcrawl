El Nino-Southern Oscillation

The El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is a climate phenomenon of irregular periodic variation in winds and sea surface temperatures over the tropical Pacific Ocean that affects the climate of much of the tropics and subtropics. The warming phase of the sea temperature is known as El Niño and the cooling phase as La Niña.

The Southern Oscillation is the accompanying atmospheric component, coupled with the sea temperature change. During an El Niño, the trade winds weaken or reverse, warm water spreads eastward across the Pacific, and rainfall patterns shift: the eastern Pacific and western Americas become wetter, while Indonesia and Australia become drier. During La Niña, the trade winds strengthen, cold water upwells in the eastern Pacific, and the pattern reverses.

Phases and effects

ENSO phases typically last 9–12 months for El Niño and sometimes longer for La Niña, recurring every two to seven years. Major El Niño events, such as those of 1982–83, 1997–98, 2015–16 and 2023–24, have caused global weather extremes including droughts, floods, heat waves, and disruptions to fisheries off the coast of Peru, where the collapse of anchoveta catches first drew scientific attention to the phenomenon.

The name El Niño ("The Boy Child", referring to the Christ child) was given by Peruvian fishermen who noticed warm currents arriving around Christmas. ENSO is monitored using indices such as the Oceanic Niño Index, based on sea surface temperature anomalies in the Niño 3.4 region of the central Pacific.
