Genomic selection

Genomic selection is a form of marker-assisted selection in plant and animal breeding in which a statistical model fitted across the whole genome predicts the breeding value of individuals from their DNA markers alone. Proposed by Meuwissen, Hayes and Goddard in 2001, it became practical as dense SNP genotyping grew cheap.

Method

A training population is both genotyped and phenotyped, and the effects of thousands of markers are estimated simultaneously, typically with ridge regression, GBLUP or Bayesian methods. Candidates in later generations are then genotyped as seedlings or calves, and their genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs) predict performance without waiting for progeny testing.

Applications

Genomic selection transformed dairy cattle breeding by shortening generation intervals and roughly doubling rates of genetic gain for milk traits. It is now widely used in beef cattle, pigs, poultry, and crops including maize, wheat and rice, and increasingly in forestry and aquaculture.

Comparison with marker-assisted selection

Traditional marker-assisted selection uses a few markers of large effect, whereas genomic selection uses all markers at once, capturing small-effect loci that together explain much of the genetic variation for complex traits.
