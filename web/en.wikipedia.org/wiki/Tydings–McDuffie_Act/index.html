Tydings–McDuffie Act

Image placeholder: Tydings–McDuffie Act

The Tydings–McDuffie Act, officially the Philippine Independence Act, is an Act of the United States Congress, signed on 24 March 1934, that provided for Philippine self-government and for the independence of the Philippines from the United States after a ten-year transitional period. It is named for its sponsors, Senator Millard Tydings of Maryland and Representative John McDuffie of Alabama.

Provisions

The Act directed the Philippines to elect a constitutional convention to draft a constitution for a new Commonwealth of the Philippines, which would govern the islands during the transition, after which the United States would withdraw its sovereignty and recognise full independence. It also reclassified Filipinos — previously US nationals — as aliens for immigration purposes, imposing an annual quota of just 50 immigrants, a provision that sharply curtailed Filipino migration to the United States and reflected the exclusionist politics behind much American support for independence.

Background and aftermath

A Filipino delegation led by Manuel L. Quezon negotiated the Act after rejecting the earlier Hare–Hawes–Cutting Act, largely over its provisions on US military bases. The new constitution was drafted in 1934–35, the Commonwealth was inaugurated in November 1935 with Quezon as president, and although the Second World War and the Japanese occupation intervened, the timetable was fulfilled: the United States recognised Philippine independence on 4 July 1946, when the Republic of the Philippines was proclaimed.
