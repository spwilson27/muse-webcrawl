War of the Spanish Succession

The War of the Spanish Succession was a European great-power conflict fought from 1701 to 1714, triggered by the death of the childless Charles II of Spain in November 1700. Charles bequeathed his entire empire to Philip, Duke of Anjou, grandson of Louis XIV of France, threatening a union of the French and Spanish crowns and a dramatic shift in the balance of power. A Grand Alliance of England (later Great Britain), the Dutch Republic, the Holy Roman Empire and others backed the Habsburg claimant, Archduke Charles of Austria.

Causes

Throughout the 1690s the European powers negotiated partition treaties to dispose of the Spanish inheritance, but Charles II's will left the whole monarchy to Philip of Anjou on condition the crowns of France and Spain never be united. Louis XIV accepted the will, then provoked his neighbours by occupying barrier fortresses in the Spanish Netherlands, recognising the exiled James Francis Edward Stuart as king of England, and granting French merchants the lucrative asiento slave-trading contract.

Course of the war

Fighting spread across Flanders, the Rhine, Italy, Spain and the colonies (where it overlapped with Queen Anne's War in North America). The Duke of Marlborough and Prince Eugene won a series of famous victories: Blenheim (1704), Ramillies (1706), Oudenarde (1708) and Malplaquet (1709). In Spain, Allied forces twice occupied Madrid but could not dislodge Philip, whose position was secured by the Franco-Spanish victory at Almansa (1707). The war in Spain became a civil conflict between Bourbon centralism and the defenders of the Catalan and Aragonese fueros.

Peace settlements

The Treaty of Utrecht (1713) recognised Philip V as king of Spain, on condition he renounce his place in the French succession. Britain gained Gibraltar, Menorca, Newfoundland, Acadia and the asiento; Savoy gained Sicily; Prussia was recognised as a kingdom. Austria, still fighting, settled by the Treaties of Rastatt and Baden (1714), gaining the Spanish Netherlands, Milan, Naples and Sardinia. The fall of Barcelona on 11 September 1714 ended organised resistance in Catalonia.

Significance

The war established the principle of the balance of power in European diplomacy, confirmed Britain's rise as a maritime and financial power, and left Spain's empire partitioned. It also entrenched Bourbon rule in Spain, where Philip V's Nueva Planta decrees abolished the historic privileges of the Crown of Aragon.
