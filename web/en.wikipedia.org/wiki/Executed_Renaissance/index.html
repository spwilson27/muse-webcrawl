Executed Renaissance

The Executed Renaissance (Ukrainian: Розстріляне відродження, Rozstriliane vidrodzhennia) is the term for the generation of Ukrainian writers, artists, and intellectuals of the 1920s and early 1930s who flourished during the period of Ukrainisation in the Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic and were then destroyed during Joseph Stalin's Great Terror. The name comes from the title of an anthology compiled by the Polish publicist Jerzy Giedroyc, who in 1959 published the works of the repressed generation in Paris under that title, from a suggestion by the literary scholar Yuriy Lavrinenko.

Background

Following the Bolshevik policy of korenizatsiya (indigenisation), Ukrainian culture experienced a remarkable revival in the 1920s. Writers' groups such as VAPLITE, led by Mykola Khvylovy, debated Ukraine's cultural orientation; Khvylovy's slogan "Away from Moscow!" symbolised the desire for a European, independent Ukrainian literature. Figures of the generation include the poets Pavlo Tychyna (an early survivor who conformed), Maksym Rylsky, Mykola Zerov and the neoclassicists, the prose writers Valerian Pidmohylny, Hryhorii Kosynka, and Mykola Kulish, and the theatre director Les Kurbas.

Repression

From the early 1930s the cultural policy reversed. Khvylovy committed suicide in May 1933 as arrests mounted; the famine of the Holodomor devastated the countryside. During the Great Terror of 1937–38, hundreds of Ukrainian cultural figures were arrested and shot. On 3 November 1937, a large group including Les Kurbas and Mykola Kulish was executed at Sandarmokh in Karelia. Others died in camps or by suicide; by the end of the decade the renaissance had been annihilated almost in its entirety.

Legacy

The works of the executed writers were banned in the Soviet Union for decades and recovered only during and after glasnost. The term has become central to Ukrainian memory of the Terror and to the modern understanding of twentieth-century Ukrainian literature.
