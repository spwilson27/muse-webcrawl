Labor Zionism (Hebrew: ציונות סוציאליסטית, Tsiyonut sotsyalistit) is the left-wing current of the Zionist movement. It held that a Jewish state could only arise through the labour of Jewish workers settling the Land of Israel themselves, and it combined national revival with socialist ideals of equality and collective ownership. Labor Zionism built the institutions of the pre-state Jewish community (the Yishuv) and dominated Israeli politics from independence in 1948 until 1977.

Ideology

Marxist theorist Ber Borochov argued that Jewish economic life in the diaspora was an "inverted pyramid" of middlemen without a working class, and that normalisation required Jewish labour in a territory of their own. A. D. Gordon gave the movement its moral philosophy of the "religion of labour" — redemption through physical work on the land — while rejecting Marxist class struggle. Central slogans included the "conquest of labour" (kibbush ha'avoda), the insistence that the new society be built by Jewish workers rather than by Jewish employers hiring cheap Arab labour.

Institutions

Immigrants of the Second and Third Aliyot (1904–1923) founded the movement's pillars: the first kibbutzim (collective settlements) beginning with Degania in 1909/1910; the Histadrut labour federation (1920), which functioned as employer, health fund, and welfare state-in-the-making; the Haganah defence organisation; and parties that merged in 1930 into Mapai, led by David Ben-Gurion. Labor governments built the state, absorbed mass immigration, and ran a largely socialised economy through the 1960s.

Decline

The movement's hegemony ended with the Likud victory of 1977, driven by demographic change, the aftermath of the 1973 war, and liberalisation of the economy. Its successor, the Israeli Labor Party, declined further in the 21st century, while kibbutzim largely privatised. Labor Zionism's legacy — the kibbutz, the Histadrut, and the founding leadership of Israel — remains foundational to the state's history.
