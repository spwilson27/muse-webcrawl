Benedict Arnold (January 14, 1741 – June 14, 1801) was an American-born British military officer who served during the American Revolutionary War. He fought with distinction for the American Continental Army and rose to the rank of major general, before defecting to the British side in 1780. His name has become a byword for treason in the United States.

Service to the American cause

A Connecticut merchant and militia captain, Arnold distinguished himself early in the war: he helped capture Fort Ticonderoga in May 1775, led an arduous expedition through the Maine wilderness against Quebec, prevented a British advance at the Battle of Valcour Island in 1776, and played a conspicuous role at the Battles of Ridgefield and Saratoga in 1777, where he was severely wounded in the leg. Repeatedly passed over for promotion and embroiled in disputes with Congress and fellow officers, Arnold accumulated grievances and debts. As military governor of Philadelphia he married Peggy Shippen, from a family with Loyalist connections, and was court-martialled for minor offences, receiving a reprimand from George Washington.

Defection

In 1779 Arnold opened secret correspondence with the British, negotiating through Major John André. Given command of the fort at West Point in 1780, he plotted to surrender it to the British in return for £20,000 and a commission. The plot was exposed when André was captured carrying incriminating papers; Arnold escaped to the British warship Vulture, while André was hanged as a spy. Arnold subsequently led British forces in destructive raids on Richmond, Virginia, and New London, Connecticut.

Later life

After the war Arnold lived in London and New Brunswick, Canada, pursuing mercantile ventures with mixed success and never fully accepted by British society. He died in London in 1801. In American memory his early heroism was eclipsed by his betrayal: the Boot Monument at Saratoga honours his wounded leg without naming him, while his name became synonymous with "traitor".
