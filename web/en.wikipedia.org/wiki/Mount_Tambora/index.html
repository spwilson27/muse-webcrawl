Mount Tambora (Indonesian: Gunung Tambora) is an active stratovolcano on the Sanggar Peninsula of Sumbawa Island, Indonesia. Its eruption of April 1815 is the largest volcanic eruption in recorded history, rated VEI-7, and its global climatic effects made 1816 the "Year Without a Summer."

1815 eruption

After several years of increasing activity beginning in 1812, Tambora entered a climactic phase on 5 April 1815, heard as distant cannon fire as far away as Sumatra. The eruption column reached the stratosphere, and on 10–11 April the eruption climaxed in a colossal explosion that ejected an estimated 150–180 cubic kilometres of material. Pyroclastic flows swept the flanks and reached the sea, generating tsunamis. The summit — previously over 4,300 m high — was reduced to about 2,850 m, leaving a caldera roughly 6–7 km across and over 1 km deep.

Ash fell over a vast area of the Indonesian archipelago, blotting out the sun for days within a radius of hundreds of kilometres. The kingdoms of Tambora and Pekat on the peninsula were destroyed; direct deaths from the eruption, tsunamis and immediate effects are estimated at 10,000–11,000, with total deaths including subsequent famine and disease on Sumbawa and neighbouring islands commonly put at 71,000 or more — the deadliest eruption in recorded history.

Global consequences

The stratospheric sulphate veil cooled the Northern Hemisphere by roughly 0.4–0.7 °C on average. In 1816, killing frosts struck New England and eastern Canada through the summer, crops failed across Europe in the last great subsistence crisis of the Western world, and famine conditions spread. The gloomy weather of 1816 in Switzerland famously confined Mary Shelley and her companions indoors at the Villa Diodati, where she began Frankenstein; the same summer's spectacular sunsets influenced the skies in the paintings of J. M. W. Turner.

Archaeology

In 2004 a team of archaeologists uncovered the remains of a settlement buried under some 3 m of pyroclastic deposits on the volcano's flank — houses, pottery, bronze bowls and human remains preserved in place — leading to its description in the media as the "Pompeii of the East." Radiocarbon dating confirmed the village was destroyed in 1815; the finds preserve evidence of the Tambora culture and language, which was wiped out by the eruption.

Present day

Tambora remains an active volcano, with a small lava dome (Doro Api Toi) that grew in the caldera in the 19th–20th centuries and a minor eruption in 1967. Its alert level was raised in 2011 amid increased seismicity but later lowered. The mountain and its caldera are protected within Mount Tambora National Park, established in 2015, and the area is part of the Samota Biosphere Reserve recognised by UNESCO in 2019.
