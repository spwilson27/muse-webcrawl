Urban geography

Urban geography is the subdiscipline of geography that derives from the study of cities and urban processes. Urban geographers and urban geographers-sociologists examine the spatial structure and organization of cities — where things are within them and why — and the systems of cities within regions and the world economy. It is closely related to urban studies, urban planning, and urban sociology.

Scope and themes

The field addresses two broad scales. Within cities, it studies the distribution of land uses, housing, transport, segregation, gentrification, and public space. Between cities, it studies urban systems: the size distribution of settlements (rank-size rule, primate cities), central place theory developed by Walter Christaller, and hierarchies of global and world cities connected by flows of capital, information, and migration.

Approaches

Early 20th-century work produced classic models of internal urban structure — Ernest Burgess's concentric zone model (1925), Homer Hoyt's sector model, and Harris and Ullman's multiple nuclei model — associated with the Chicago School of sociology. The "quantitative revolution" of the 1950s–60s brought spatial analysis and location theory. From the 1970s, Marxist geographers such as David Harvey re-framed the city through political economy, analyzing capital accumulation, rent, and uneven development; later feminist, postcolonial, and poststructural approaches examined everyday life, difference, and the right to the city.

Contemporary topics

Current research includes suburbanization and edge cities, global-city networks, informal settlements and urbanization in the Global South, smart cities and platform urbanism, housing affordability crises, and the spatial effects of climate change and pandemics. More than half of humanity now lives in urban areas, a share projected by the United Nations to approach two-thirds by 2050.
