Operation Torch was the Allied invasion of French North Africa during the Second World War. Beginning on 8 November 1942, American and British forces landed in Vichy French-controlled Morocco and Algeria with the aims of reducing Axis pressure on Allied forces in Egypt, securing the southern shore of the Mediterranean, and preparing the way for an eventual advance into southern Europe. It was the first major American ground offensive against the Axis in the European-African theatre and the largest amphibious operation attempted up to that time.

Background

President Franklin D. Roosevelt favoured an early American landing in north-west Africa (originally planned as Operation Gymnast) over a cross-Channel invasion in 1942, which British planners considered premature. The compromise plan adopted in July 1942 called for landings at Casablanca in Morocco and at Oran and Algiers in Algeria. A central uncertainty was how the Vichy French forces in North Africa would react: the Allies courted French commanders in advance, notably through diplomat Robert Murphy and a clandestine meeting with General Charles Mast near Cherchell, hoping resistance would be token.

The landings

The operation was organised in three task forces. The Western Task Force under Major General George S. Patton sailed directly from the United States and landed near Casablanca, where French naval and ground forces resisted in earnest, including a naval action between French warships and the U.S. Navy. The Center Task Force landed at Oran, meeting stiff resistance — two British-manned sloops attempting to enter the harbour directly were sunk with heavy loss. The Eastern Task Force, landing at Algiers, benefited from a coup by French Resistance fighters in the city and took Algiers on the first day. Supreme Allied commander was Lieutenant General Dwight D. Eisenhower, with his headquarters at Gibraltar.

The Darlan deal and consequences

Admiral François Darlan, commander-in-chief of Vichy armed forces, happened to be in Algiers and, after negotiations, ordered a general ceasefire on 10 November in exchange for Allied recognition of him as head of French administration in North Africa. The "Darlan deal" secured the ceasefire and saved Allied casualties but caused political embarrassment in London and Washington, as Darlan had been a leading collaborator. He was assassinated on 24 December 1942. Germany responded to Torch by occupying the unoccupied zone of France (Case Anton) and rushing troops into Tunisia, leading to the Tunisia Campaign of 1942–43, in which the green American forces were bloodied at Kasserine Pass before the Axis forces in Africa surrendered in May 1943.

Significance

Torch marked the beginning of large-scale American involvement in the Mediterranean theatre, gave U.S. forces their first combat experience against European Axis opponents, strained Vichy–Allied relations to breaking point, and set in motion the sequence that led to the invasions of Sicily and Italy in 1943.
