Herd immunity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Herd immunity (also called community immunity) is the indirect protection from an infectious disease that occurs when a large share of a population has become immune — through vaccination or prior infection — so that chains of transmission are broken and even non-immune individuals are less likely to be exposed.

Thresholds

The proportion needed depends on how contagious the disease is: for a pathogen with basic reproduction number R₀, the threshold is approximately 1 − 1/R₀. Highly contagious measles (R₀ often estimated at 12–18) requires roughly 92–95% immunity, which is why the WHO and health agencies target 95% two-dose measles vaccination coverage; less contagious diseases need far less. The threshold is a guide, not a guarantee: immunity that wanes, uneven coverage between communities, and new variants (as with SARS-CoV-2) can allow outbreaks in pockets even when overall coverage seems adequate.

Importance

Herd immunity protects people who cannot be vaccinated — newborns, chemotherapy patients and others with weakened immune systems. It is the mechanism by which vaccination programmes have eliminated diseases regionally and eradicated smallpox globally (see disease eradication), and its erosion through falling coverage or hesitancy explains resurgences of measles in several countries in recent years.
