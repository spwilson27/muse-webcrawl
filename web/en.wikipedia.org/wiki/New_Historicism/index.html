New Historicism

New Historicism is a school of literary theory that first developed in the 1980s, primarily through the work of the critic Stephen Greenblatt, and gained widespread influence in the 1990s. New Historicists aim to understand a literary work through its historical context, and, reciprocally, to understand intellectual history through literature.

Principles

New Historicism rejects the distinction between a literary "foreground" and a historical "background." Instead, literary and non-literary texts are seen to circulate together within a culture's systems of power and representation. Characteristic essays open with an anecdote drawn from an obscure historical document—a travel account, a medical treatise, a legal record—and juxtapose it with a canonical play or poem, arguing for a shared "social energy." Key influences include Michel Foucault's accounts of power and discourse and Clifford Geertz's "thick description" in cultural anthropology.

Criticism

Critics have charged New Historicism with arbitrary selection of anecdotes, political bias, and dissolving literature into mere documentation of power relations. In Britain, the related movement of cultural materialism, associated with critics such as Jonathan Dollimore and Alan Sinfield, has placed greater emphasis on present political commitment.
