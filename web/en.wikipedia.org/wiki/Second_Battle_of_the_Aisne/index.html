Second Battle of the Aisne

Date16 April – 9 May 1917 (main offensive; operations continued to late May)
LocationAisne valley, between Soissons and Reims, France
ResultGerman defensive victory; French offensive failure
BelligerentsFrance (with Russian and British support) vs. German Empire

The Second Battle of the Aisne (16 April – 9 May 1917), also known as the Nivelle Offensive, was the main part of a French-led Allied offensive on the Western Front in the spring of 1917 during the First World War. French commander-in-chief Robert Nivelle promised a decisive breakthrough within 48 hours along the Chemin des Dames ridge above the Aisne River, using massive artillery preparation and "creeping barrage" tactics that had appeared successful at Verdun.

The offensive

Some 1.2 million French troops, over 7,000 guns, and the first large deployment of French Schneider and Saint-Chamond tanks attacked on 16 April 1917. The Germans, forewarned — plans had been lost and the offensive widely discussed — had withdrawn to prepared positions and deployed defense-in-depth. French infantry advanced into machine-gun and artillery fire on slopes swept from concealed reverse positions; gains were limited to a few kilometers at costs exceeding 100,000 casualties in the first days and some 187,000 overall by early May, against roughly 163,000 German casualties.

Consequences

The failure shattered French army morale. Beginning in late April, collective indiscipline escalated into the French Army mutinies of 1917, affecting dozens of divisions; soldiers refused orders to attack while generally continuing to hold defensive positions. Nivelle was replaced by Philippe Pétain, who combined limited repression with improvements in leave, rest, and food, and avoided major offensives until 1918. The disaster also intensified the political crisis in France and confirmed British skepticism, hastening greater coordination under a unified Allied command the following year.
