Germans

Native name: Deutsche
Total population: c. 100–150 million (including diaspora)
Languages: German
Religion: Christianity (Roman Catholicism, Protestantism), irreligion
Regions: Germany, Austria, Switzerland; diaspora in the United States, Brazil, and elsewhere

The Germans (German: Deutsche) are a Germanic ethnic group native to Central Europe, sharing a common culture, history, and the German language. Estimates of the number of Germans worldwide range from 100 to 150 million, the majority living in Germany, with significant populations in Austria and German-speaking Switzerland, and large diaspora communities formed by emigration since the 19th century.

History

The concept of a German people emerged from the Germanic tribes of antiquity and took clearer shape in the medieval East Frankish kingdom and the Holy Roman Empire, where theodiscus — "of the people" — gave rise to the word Deutsch. Political fragmentation into many states persisted until German unification in 1871. The catastrophes of the two world wars, the Holocaust perpetrated by Nazi Germany, the flight and expulsion of Germans from Central and Eastern Europe after 1945, and the division and reunification of Germany (1949–1990) profoundly shaped modern German identity.

Language and culture

Standard German (Hochdeutsch) is the common written and official language, while regional dialects (Low German, Bavarian, Alemannic, and others) continue to be spoken. German culture has contributed centrally to European philosophy, classical music, science, and literature, through figures such as Kant, Goethe, Bach, Beethoven, and Einstein.

Citizenship

German nationality law historically emphasised descent (jus sanguinis); ethnic German resettlers (Aussiedler) from Eastern Europe received citizenship upon arrival. Reforms in 2000 and 2024 introduced elements of birthright citizenship and broader acceptance of dual citizenship.
