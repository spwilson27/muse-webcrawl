Circumstellar habitable zone

In astronomy and astrobiology, the circumstellar habitable zone (CHZ) — often called the "Goldilocks zone" — is the range of orbits around a star within which a planetary surface can support liquid water, given sufficient atmospheric pressure. It is the most commonly used first criterion for planetary habitability.

Definition

The concept was developed in the 1950s–70s, notably by Hubertus Strughold, Stephen H. Dole and Michael H. Hart. In the Solar System the habitable zone roughly spans from just outside Venus's orbit to beyond Earth's orbit; Earth lies near its inner edge. The boundaries depend on stellar luminosity and spectrum, planetary mass, atmospheric composition and the greenhouse effect.

Other stellar types

Around cool red dwarfs (M-type stars), the habitable zone is much closer to the star, so habitable planets are often tidally locked; Proxima Centauri b and the TRAPPIST-1 planets e, f and g lie in or near their stars' habitable zones. Around hotter F-type stars, the zone is wider and farther out but stellar lifetimes are shorter.

Limitations

Water alone does not guarantee habitability: stellar flares, atmospheric erosion, lack of a magnetic field, and the planet's geochemistry all matter. Icy moons such as Europa and Enceladus host subsurface oceans far outside the Sun's habitable zone, heated by tidal forces.
