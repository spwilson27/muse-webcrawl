Antonymy

In lexical semantics, antonymy is a sense relation between words (lexemes) that have opposite meanings, such as hot and cold, rise and fall, or buy and sell. Pairs of words standing in this relation are called antonyms. Antonymy is one of the classical lexical relations studied in semantics, alongside synonymy, hyponymy, and meronymy.

Nature of the relation

Antonymy is a relation between word forms rather than strictly between concepts: a polysemous word may have different antonyms for different senses — old contrasts with young for people but with new for objects. Antonymous pairs tend to co-occur frequently in text (as in "hot and cold", "from cradle to grave"), and canonical pairs such as good/bad and big/small are learned early by children and are highly entrenched in the lexicon.

Classification

Following classifications such as those of John Lyons and D. A. Cruse, antonyms are often divided into gradable antonyms, which denote degrees along a dimension and admit a middle ground (e.g. hot/cold); complementary or binary antonyms, which exhaustively divide their domain (e.g. dead/alive); and converses, which express reciprocal perspectives on a single relation (e.g. lend/borrow, husband/wife). Some semanticists restrict the term antonymy proper to the gradable type and treat the others as distinct relations.

Antonymy across languages

Most languages possess antonym pairs concentrated in adjectives of dimension, temperature, speed, value, and emotion. The relation is exploited in rhetoric and literature through antithesis and oxymoron, and in lexicography antonyms are routinely supplied in dictionary and thesaurus entries.
