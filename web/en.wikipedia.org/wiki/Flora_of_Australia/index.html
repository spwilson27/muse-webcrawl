Flora of Australia

The flora of Australia comprises a vast assemblage of plant taxa estimated to include more than 20,000 vascular and 14,000 non-vascular plants, 250,000 species of fungi, and over 3,000 lichens. The flora has strong affinities with the flora of Gondwana, and below the family level has a highly endemic angiosperm flora whose diversity was shaped by the continent's long isolation, drifting north, and progressive aridification after its separation from Antarctica.

Origins and evolution

Australia's flora descends in large part from the rainforest flora of Gondwana, shared with South America, Africa, and Antarctica. As Australia drifted north and dried, sclerophyll vegetation adapted to fire, poor soils, and drought expanded, while Gondwanan rainforest retreated to the moist east coast. Genera such as Nothofagus (southern beech) preserve the Gondwanan connection. Later, interchange with Asia brought new elements, especially in the tropical north.

Dominant groups

The most characteristic Australian plants are the eucalypts (over 800 species of Eucalyptus, Corymbia, and Angophora) and the wattles (Acacia, with around 1,000 species, Australia's largest plant genus). Other prominent families and genera include the Proteaceae (banksias, grevilleas, waratah), the Myrtaceae (paperbarks, bottlebrushes), the pea family, the heath family (Epacris), kangaroo paws, and the grass trees (Xanthorrhoea). Roughly 85–90 percent of Australia's flowering plant species are endemic.

Vegetation types

Major vegetation communities include tropical and temperate rainforest, tall eucalypt forests (mountain ash is among the world's tallest flowering plants), eucalypt woodlands, acacia shrublands (mulga and brigalow), mallee, spinifex grasslands covering the arid interior, heathlands such as the species-rich kwongan of southwestern Australia — a global biodiversity hotspot — and mangroves and saltmarshes along the coast. Many communities are fire-adapted, with serotinous cones, lignotubers, epicormic buds, and smoke-triggered germination.

Conservation

Threats include land clearing, altered fire regimes, invasive species, the root-rot pathogen Phytophthora cinnamomi, and climate change. Large areas are protected in national parks and the World Heritage-listed regions such as the Gondwana Rainforests and the Greater Blue Mountains.
