History of computing hardware

This article covers the development of calculating and computing machines from antiquity to the present.


ENIAC, one of the first general-purpose electronic computers (1945).

The history of computing hardware spans developments from simple devices for counting — including the abacus and the Antikythera mechanism — through mechanical calculators, electromechanical tabulators and vacuum-tube machines, to transistorised computers, integrated circuits, microprocessors and today's ubiquitous devices.

Early devices

The abacus, used for millennia, was among the first calculating tools. The Antikythera mechanism (c. 100 BC) was an analogue computer predicting astronomical positions. In the seventeenth century, Wilhelm Schickard, Blaise Pascal and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz built mechanical calculators capable of arithmetic operations. Joseph-Marie Jacquard's loom (1804) used punched cards to control patterns — an idea later central to computing.

The analytical engine and tabulators

Charles Babbage designed the Difference Engine and then the programmable Analytical Engine, for which Ada Lovelace wrote what is regarded as the first algorithm. Herman Hollerith's punched-card tabulator, used in the 1890 US census, led to the company that became IBM.

Electromechanical and electronic computers

Konrad Zuse's Z3 (1941) was an early programmable electromechanical computer. The Atanasoff–Berry Computer introduced electronic digital computation, while Britain's Colossus machines (1943–44) broke German codes. ENIAC (1945), built by Eckert and Mauchly, was a general-purpose electronic computer using some 18,000 vacuum tubes. The Manchester Baby (1948) was the first stored-program computer to run a program.

Transistors and integrated circuits

The transistor (1947, Bell Labs) replaced tubes in machines such as the IBM 7090, shrinking size and cost. Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce independently developed the integrated circuit in 1958–59. IBM's System/360 family (1964) standardised a compatible product line, and minicomputers like DEC's PDP-8 brought computing to laboratories and businesses.

The microprocessor and personal computing

Intel's 4004 (1971) placed a CPU on a single chip. Microcomputers — the Altair 8800, Apple II, Commodore PET and later the IBM PC (1981) — made computing personal. GUIs popularised by the Apple Macintosh (1984), ever-denser chips following Moore's law, laptops, smartphones and cloud data centres followed, making computing hardware a global mass industry.
