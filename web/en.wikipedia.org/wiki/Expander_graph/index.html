Expander graph

An expander graph: sparse, yet every small set of vertices has many neighbours.

In combinatorics, an expander graph is a sparse graph that has strong connectivity properties. Intuitively, every subset of vertices that is not too large has a large "boundary" — many edges leaving the set. Different formalisations of this idea give rise to vertex expanders, edge expanders, and spectral expanders.

Expander graphs have found extensive applications in computer science, including the construction of error-correcting codes, derandomization of algorithms, sorting networks, cryptography, and the theory of communication networks. They are also central to pure mathematics, connecting graph theory with group theory and number theory.

Definitions

An edge expander is a finite undirected graph such that every subset of vertices of at most half the total has an edge boundary of size at least a constant fraction of the subset. A spectral expander is a regular graph whose second-largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix (in absolute value) is bounded away from the degree; by Cheeger-type inequalities (the "expander mixing lemma" and its converses), spectral expansion and edge expansion are closely related.

Examples and constructions

Random regular graphs are, with high probability, good expanders, but explicit constructions were long sought. Grigory Margulis gave the first explicit family in 1973 using Cayley graphs of the groups SL(2, Zp) with properties from representation theory. Later, Lubotzky, Phillips, and Sarnak constructed Ramanujan graphs — optimal spectral expanders whose nontrivial eigenvalues meet the Alon–Boppana bound — using deep results from number theory. The zig-zag product of Reingold, Vadhan, and Wigderson (2002) gave a purely combinatorial construction and led to Reingold's theorem that undirected connectivity is in L.

Applications

  • Robust communication networks that tolerate node failures.
  • Expander codes: low-density parity-check codes with efficient decoding.
  • Derandomization: expanders reduce the randomness needed by randomized algorithms.
  • Error-correcting codes and probabilistically checkable proofs.
  • Hash functions and extractors in cryptography and pseudorandomness.
