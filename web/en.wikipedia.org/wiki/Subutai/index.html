Subutai (c. 1175–1248), also spelled Subedei, was the primary military strategist and chief general of Genghis Khan and Ögedei Khan. Of humble origin — tradition makes him the son of a blacksmith of the Uriankhai — he rose purely by talent to direct more campaigns and conquer more territory than perhaps any general in history, subjugating peoples from China to Hungary.

Career

Subutai joined Genghis Khan's camp as a youth, famously offering to shield his lord "like felt cloth from wind and rain." He distinguished himself in the conquests of the Jin dynasty and the Khwarezmian Empire (1219–21), including the relentless pursuit of Shah Muhammad II. With Jebe he then led the great reconnaissance-in-force of 1220–24 through Georgia, the Caucasus and the steppe, crushing a Rus'–Cuman coalition at the Battle of the Kalka River (1223) and riding around the Caspian — a raid of some 8,000 km.

Conquest of Rus' and Hungary

As field commander (under the nominal leadership of Batu Khan) of the western campaign of 1236–42, Subutai planned the near-simultaneous invasions of the Rus' principalities, Poland and Hungary, coordinating widely separated armies across winter steppe and frozen rivers. His victory over King Béla IV at Mohi (11 April 1241), two days after the northern army's win at Legnica, opened central Europe — which was spared only by the Mongol withdrawal to elect a new khan. Subutai also planned the final conquest of the Song dynasty but died in Mongolia in 1248, aged about 73.

Legacy

Subutai pioneered deep operational manoeuvre, systematic intelligence gathering, integrated siege engineers from China and Persia, and the coordinated use of feigned retreats over distances of hundreds of kilometres. Military historians rank him among the greatest commanders of all time; his campaigns allegedly brought more than twenty nations under Mongol rule.
