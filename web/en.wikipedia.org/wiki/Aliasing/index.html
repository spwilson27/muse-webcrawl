Aliasing

In signal processing and related disciplines, aliasing is an effect that causes different signals to become indistinguishable (or "aliases" of one another) when sampled. It most commonly refers to the distortion that occurs when a continuous signal is sampled at too low a rate: frequency components above half the sampling rate (the Nyquist frequency) are folded back into lower frequencies and misrepresented.

Sampling theory

The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem states that a band-limited signal can be perfectly reconstructed from its samples if the sampling rate exceeds twice the highest frequency present (the Nyquist rate). When the input is not sufficiently band-limited, high frequencies alias into the passband. In practice, an anti-aliasing filter limits the input bandwidth before sampling.

Manifestations

Aliasing is familiar in many forms: wagon wheels appearing to spin backwards on film (temporal aliasing, an undersampling in time), jagged stair-step edges and moiré patterns in digital images (spatial aliasing), and false tones in undersampled audio. In computer graphics, techniques such as supersampling, multisample anti-aliasing (MSAA), and mipmapping reduce image aliasing. In audio production, converter design and oversampling suppress folded-back ultrasonic content.

A related usage in computing describes "aliasing" of memory locations and identifiers — for example, two pointers referring to the same object, or names in a namespace bound to the same entity — but in signal processing the sampling sense is standard.
