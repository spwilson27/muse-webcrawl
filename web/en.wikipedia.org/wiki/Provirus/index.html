Provirus

Provirus
DefinitionViral genome integrated into the DNA of a host cell
Formed byRetroviruses (via reverse transcriptase and integrase); some other viruses by recombination

A provirus is a virus genome that is integrated into the DNA of a host cell. Provirus formation is the defining step in the life cycle of the retroviruses: after the virus enters the cell, its enzyme reverse transcriptase copies its RNA genome into double-stranded DNA, and a second viral enzyme, integrase, inserts that DNA into a host chromosome. The integrated provirus is then replicated passively by the cell's own machinery every time the cell divides, so it is inherited by all daughter cells. A provirus may remain transcriptionally silent (latent) for years or be activated to produce new virus particles. The analogous integrated state of a bacterial virus is called a prophage.

Formation and structure

The provirus is bounded by two identical sequences called long terminal repeats (LTRs), generated during reverse transcription; the LTRs contain the promoter and enhancer elements that drive expression of the viral genes (gag, pol and env in a typical retrovirus). Integration is not random but is guided by integrase and host factors toward particular regions of chromatin—HIV, for example, favours active genes, while other retroviruses favour promoters or heterochromatin. Unlike the episomal DNA of latent herpesviruses, a true provirus cannot be removed by cell division, and the cell has no general mechanism to excise it; this permanence is why proviral latency is the central obstacle to curing HIV infection.

Latency and activation

In HIV infection, a small population of long-lived resting memory CD4+ T cells carries replication-competent proviruses that are transcriptionally silent and therefore invisible to the immune system and unaffected by antiretroviral drugs, which block active replication steps only. This latent reservoir decays extremely slowly (a half-life of years to decades), so lifelong therapy is required to prevent viral rebound. Strategies to cure HIV include "shock and kill" (pharmacologically reactivating proviruses so infected cells can be destroyed) and "block and lock" (deepening latency permanently), neither yet clinically established. Proviruses can also accumulate mutations and deletions; most integrated HIV genomes in treated patients are defective, and only a minority are intact and capable of rebound.

Endogenous retroviruses

When a retrovirus infects a germ-line cell, the resulting provirus can be passed to offspring as a Mendelian trait, becoming an endogenous retrovirus (ERV). Over evolutionary time such insertions accumulate: about 8 percent of the human genome derives from retroviral-like sequences, mostly degraded "fossil" proviruses that have lost the ability to replicate, though the recently endogenised koala retrovirus (KoRV) still produces infectious virus. ERV sequences are not inert: their LTRs can act as promoters and enhancers for host genes, some ERV proteins have been co-opted for essential functions—the syncytins that build the placenta are captured retroviral envelope proteins—and aberrant ERV expression is implicated in some cancers and autoimmune and neurological diseases. In livestock and laboratory animals, endogenous proviruses complicate xenotransplantation and vaccine production; pigs intended as organ donors have been gene-edited to inactivate all copies of porcine endogenous retrovirus (PERV).

History of the concept

The provirus hypothesis was proposed by Howard Temin in the 1960s to explain how RNA tumour viruses could be inherited by daughter cells; it required information to flow from RNA back to DNA, contradicting the then-standard reading of the central dogma, and was vindicated when Temin and David Baltimore independently discovered reverse transcriptase in 1970 (Nobel Prize, 1975). André Lwoff's earlier work on lysogeny and prophages in bacteria provided the conceptual model.
