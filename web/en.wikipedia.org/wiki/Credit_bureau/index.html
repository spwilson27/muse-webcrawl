Credit bureau

A credit bureau compiles consumer credit information into credit reports

A credit bureau is a data broker and agency that collects and sells information on the creditworthiness of individuals and businesses, in the form of credit reports, to lenders, employers, landlords, and others. In the United States they are commonly called consumer reporting agencies. The information collected includes borrowing and repayment history, credit inquiries, and public records such as bankruptcies.

How credit bureaus work

Lenders, card issuers, and other creditors voluntarily report account information — balances, credit limits, payment timeliness — to the bureaus, typically monthly. The bureaus assemble this into individual credit files, which are sold to subscribers evaluating applications for credit, insurance, employment, or housing. Many bureaus also compute or distribute credit scores, such as the FICO score and VantageScore, derived from the report data.

Major bureaus

In the United States, three nationwide bureaus dominate: Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion, each holding files on more than 200 million consumers. Other countries have their own bureaus or public credit registers; in the United Kingdom the main agencies are Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion (formerly Callcredit), while Germany relies largely on SCHUFA.

Regulation and consumer rights

In the US, credit bureaus are regulated under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), which requires accuracy, gives consumers the right to obtain their reports and dispute errors, and limits who may access reports and for what purposes. Consumers are entitled to free reports from each major bureau. The industry has faced criticism over errors, opaque scoring, and data security — most prominently the 2017 Equifax data breach, which exposed the personal information of roughly 147 million people.
