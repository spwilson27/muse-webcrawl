Natural horsemanship

Natural horsemanship is a collective term for a variety of horse training techniques that emphasise working with the horse's natural behaviour and instincts, communicating through body language and the timely application and release of pressure, rather than through force or mechanical devices. Practitioners often describe the approach as building a partnership based on how horses interact with one another in a herd.

History

Gentle, psychology-based methods have roots in classical horsemanship — Xenophon advised kindness in antiquity — and in the work of nineteenth- and twentieth-century horsemen such as the Dorrance brothers, Bill and Tom Dorrance, and Ray Hunt in the American West. From the 1980s the movement was popularised internationally by clinicians and authors including Monty Roberts, Pat Parelli (who coined much of the modern marketing of the term), Buck Brannaman and John Lyons. Techniques popularised under the banner include round-pen work, "join-up", desensitisation exercises and liberty work.

Methods

Common elements include groundwork before riding, pressure-and-release (negative reinforcement) applied with precise timing, reading the horse's body language, and establishing the handler as a calm, consistent leader. Training often progresses through structured levels or games, and equipment is typically simple: a rope halter, a long lead rope and a stick used as an extension of the arm.

Criticism

Some scientists and trainers caution that certain claims made for natural horsemanship — especially those based on simplified notions of herd dominance — are not well supported by equine ethology, and that techniques are only as humane as the skill of the person applying them. Nevertheless, many of its groundwork principles have been absorbed into mainstream horse training.
