The World Heritage Convention, formally the Convention Concerning the Protection of the World Cultural and Natural Heritage, is an international treaty adopted by UNESCO on 16 November 1972. It is the most widely ratified environmental and heritage agreement in the world, and the principal legal instrument behind the World Heritage List of cultural and natural sites considered to be of "outstanding universal value".

Origins

The Convention grew out of the international campaign of the 1960s to save the temples of Abu Simbel in Egypt from flooding by the Aswan High Dam, and from concern over threats such as the flooding of Venice. It uniquely joined the protection of cultural heritage and natural heritage in a single instrument, recognising that both face accelerating destruction.

Operation

States Parties nominate sites within their territory; the intergovernmental World Heritage Committee, advised by ICOMOS (for cultural sites) and the IUCN (for natural sites), decides on inscription. Sites facing serious threats may be placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger, and three sites have been delisted entirely. Inscription brings international recognition, access to the World Heritage Fund, and obligations of protection and reporting. Well over 1,200 sites in more than 160 countries are now listed, from the Great Wall of China and the Historic Sanctuary of Machu Picchu to Auschwitz-Birkenau, inscribed in 1979 under special criteria as a symbol of the Holocaust.
