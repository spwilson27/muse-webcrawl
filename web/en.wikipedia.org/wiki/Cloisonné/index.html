Cloisonné

Cloisonné (French: [klwazɔne]) is an ancient technique for decorating metalwork objects with coloured vitreous enamel. Thin metal strips or wires — the cloisons ("partitions", from French cloison) — are soldered edge-on to the object's surface to form compartments, and each compartment is filled with enamel paste of a chosen colour. The object is then fired in a kiln, the enamel fusing and shrinking; the process of filling and firing is repeated several times, after which the surface is ground smooth and polished so that enamel and metal wires lie flush.

History

Cloisonné enamel was developed in the ancient Near East and reached high refinement in the Byzantine Empire, where it was used for icons, crosses, and imperial jewellery such as the pieces in the Pala d'Oro in Venice. The technique spread along trade routes to China by the fourteenth century, where it flourished during the Ming dynasty — the Chinese name jǐngtàilán ("Jingtai blue") recalls the Jingtai Emperor (r. 1449–1457) and the prized blue enamel ground. Chinese cloisonné reached monumental scale in vases, incense burners, and throne furniture, and large quantities were exported to Europe. In Japan, shippō enamel reached great technical brilliance in the nineteenth century, with wireless and partly wireless variants developed by artists such as Namikawa Yasuyuki.

Technique and related forms

Traditional cloisons were made by soldering flat strips on edge; later and cheaper versions may use wires or stamped cells. Related enamel techniques include champlevé, in which the cells are carved or cast into the metal itself rather than applied, plique-à-jour, in which the enamel cells have no backing so that light shines through, and basse-taille, worked over engraved low relief. Cloisonné remains a living craft in China, Japan, and in the enamel art of the West.
