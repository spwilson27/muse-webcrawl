Nucleobase

Nucleobases (nitrogenous bases, or simply bases) are nitrogen-containing biological compounds that form nucleosides, which in turn are components of nucleotides, the monomers that make up nucleic acids. The ability of nucleobases to form base pairs and to stack one upon another leads directly to long-chain helical structures such as DNA and RNA.

The purines adenine and guanine are double-ring structures; the pyrimidines cytosine, thymine and uracil are single rings. In DNA, adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds and guanine with cytosine through three; in RNA, uracil replaces thymine.

Modified and non-canonical bases

Cells chemically modify bases after synthesis: DNA is methylated at cytosine (5-methylcytosine) in epigenetic regulation, and transfer RNAs contain many unusual bases such as inosine, pseudouridine (itself a modified nucleoside) and hypoxanthine. Hypoxanthine and xanthine arise from deamination and oxidation. Synthetic biologists have created unnatural base pairs that expand the genetic alphabet beyond the two natural pairs.

Metabolism

Bases are made by de novo synthesis or salvaged and recycled; in humans, purine degradation ends at uric acid, whose overproduction causes gout.
