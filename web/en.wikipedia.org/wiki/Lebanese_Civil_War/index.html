Lebanese Civil War
Date: 13 April 1975 – 13 October 1990
Location: Lebanon
Result: Taif Agreement; Syrian occupation until 2005
Deaths: an estimated 120,000–150,000

The Lebanese Civil War (Arabic: الحرب الأهلية اللبنانية) was a multifaceted armed conflict that took place from 1975 to 1990. It resulted in an estimated 150,000 civilian deaths and the exodus of almost one million people from Lebanon.

Background and outbreak

Lebanon's confessional political system, established by the 1943 National Pact, allocated power among Maronite Christians, Sunni Muslims, Shia Muslims, and Druze, but demographic change and the arrival of armed Palestinian factions after 1948 and especially after Black September in Jordan (1970) destabilised the balance. On 13 April 1975, fighting erupted in Beirut after an attack on a church and the ambush of a bus carrying Palestinians, marking the conventional start of the war.

Course of the war

The conflict pitted the mainly Christian Lebanese Front against the Lebanese National Movement, an alliance of leftist, Muslim, and Druze militias allied with the PLO. Syria intervened militarily in 1976; Israel invaded in 1978 and again in 1982, besieging Beirut and expelling the PLO, and Israeli forces and their allies were implicated in the Sabra and Shatila massacre of Palestinian refugees. A Multinational Force deployed in 1982–84 was targeted by suicide bombings, including the 1983 Beirut barracks bombings. New actors emerged, notably the Shia movements Amal and Hezbollah, and fighting continued through the "War of the Camps" and intra-Christian conflicts of the late 1980s.

End and aftermath

The Taif Agreement of 1989 rebalanced confessional power, and the war ended in October 1990 with the defeat of General Michel Aoun's forces by Syrian and Lebanese troops. Militias were largely disbanded—except Hezbollah—and a general amnesty was passed in 1991. Syrian forces remained in Lebanon until the Cedar Revolution forced their withdrawal in 2005. Reconstruction under Rafic Hariri transformed downtown Beirut, but sectarian divisions and unaddressed wartime grievances persisted.
