History of the periodic table

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of the periodic table traces the century-long effort to order the chemical elements into a system revealing their relationships. Its roots lie in the recognition of triads of similar elements (Döbereiner, 1820s), the first lists by atomic weight, and the Karlsruhe Congress of 1860, which standardised atomic weights and made ordering possible.

In the 1860s several chemists converged on periodicity: John Newlands proposed a "law of octaves" (1864) and was mocked; Lothar Meyer published tables in 1864 and 1870; and Dmitri Mendeleev published his table in 1869, leaving gaps and predicting undiscovered elements — gallium, scandium and germanium — whose discovery on cue made his system canonical.

The twentieth century explained and corrected it: the noble gases added a column in the 1890s; Henry Moseley showed in 1913 that atomic number, not weight, is the true ordering (resolving Mendeleev's anomalies); quantum mechanics explained the periods' lengths (2, 8, 8, 18…) through electron shells (see Schrödinger equation); and Glenn Seaborg's actinide concept (1944) and the synthesis of superheavy elements — including mendelevium (element 101, 1955) — extended the table to today's 118 elements.
