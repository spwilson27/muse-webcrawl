The Krupp trial (United States of America vs. Alfried Krupp, et al.) was the tenth of twelve trials for war crimes that United States authorities held in occupied Germany at Nuremberg after the end of World War II. It was held from 8 December 1947 to 31 July 1948 before a US military tribunal with judges Hu C. Anderson (presiding), Edward J. Daly and William J. Wilkins.

Background

The defendants were senior figures of the Krupp steel and armaments conglomerate, Germany's largest weapons manufacturer. Alfried Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach, the firm's sole owner, headed the list. His father Gustav Krupp had been named in the indictment of the first International Military Tribunal trial, but was found medically unfit to stand trial; the prosecution's attempt to substitute Alfried in that trial was refused, so the younger Krupp and eleven co-defendants were tried separately.

Indictment and verdict

The indictment centred on the Krupp firm's participation in preparing aggressive war, its plunder of occupied territories (including the seizure of factories in France and the Netherlands), and above all its mass use of slave labour — prisoners of war, foreign forced labourers and concentration camp inmates — under brutal conditions. All twelve defendants were acquitted on the crimes-against-peace and conspiracy counts. Eleven were convicted of war crimes and crimes against humanity relating to slave labour and spoliation; one, Karl Pfirsch, was acquitted on all counts.

Alfried Krupp was sentenced to 12 years' imprisonment and the confiscation of all his property. Sentences for the others ranged from about 6 to 12 years. In 1951, US High Commissioner John J. McCloy granted clemency: Alfried Krupp was released from Landsberg Prison and his property — including control of the Krupp firm — was restored, a decision that proved controversial amid the onset of the Cold War.

Significance

Together with the Flick and IG Farben trials, the Krupp trial was one of the three "industrialist trials" of the Subsequent Nuremberg Proceedings, addressing the complicity of private industry in Nazi crimes.
