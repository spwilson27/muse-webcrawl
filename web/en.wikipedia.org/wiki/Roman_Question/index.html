The Roman Question (Italian: Questione romana) was a dispute over the temporal power of the popes and the status of Rome between the Kingdom of Italy and the Holy See. It arose from the Italian unification: the annexation of most of the Papal States in 1860 and the capture of Rome itself on 20 September 1870 ended the pope's thousand-year temporal rule. Pope Pius IX refused to recognise the Italian state, declared himself a "prisoner in the Vatican", and forbade Italian Catholics from participating in national politics. The question was finally settled by the Lateran Treaty of 1929, which created the sovereign Vatican City State.

Background

The Risorgimento progressively stripped the Papal States away: the Romagna, Marche and Umbria were annexed after plebiscites in 1860, leaving the pope only Rome and its surrounding Lazio, protected by French troops. Italy proclaimed Rome its capital in 1861 but could not take it; attempts by Garibaldi were defeated at Aspromonte (1862) and Mentana (1867). When France withdrew its garrison during the Franco-Prussian War, Italian forces breached the walls at Porta Pia on 20 September 1870, and a plebiscite voted overwhelmingly for annexation. Rome became Italy's capital in July 1871.

The stand-off

Italy's unilateral Law of Guarantees (1871) offered the pope personal inviolability, sovereign honours and an annual indemnity, but Pius IX rejected it as revocable by the parliament that granted it. He and his successors refused to leave the Vatican or recognise Italy, and the Non Expedit policy discouraged Catholic voting in Italian elections — weakening mass politics in the new state, though the ban was gradually relaxed and formally lifted in 1919. Annual protests, diplomatic pressure for an international settlement, and the awkward status of Catholic Italy's capital persisted for six decades.

Resolution

Negotiations between Mussolini's government and the Holy See led to the Lateran Pacts of 11 February 1929: a treaty recognising Vatican City as a sovereign state under the pope, with Italy acknowledging the Holy See's ownership and the loss of the former Papal States as definitive; a concordat regulating Church–state relations in Italy; and a financial convention compensating the Holy See with cash and bonds. Pius XI called Mussolini "the man of Providence"; the settlement removed a central grievance from Italian public life and remains the basis of the Vatican's sovereignty today.
