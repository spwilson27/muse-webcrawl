Wins Above Replacement

Wins Above Replacement or Wins Above Replacement Player, commonly abbreviated to WAR, is a non-standardized sabermetric baseball statistic developed to sum up a player's total contributions to their team. A player's WAR value is claimed to be the number of additional wins their team has achieved compared to the number of expected team wins if that player were replaced by a replacement-level player: a player who may be added to the team for minimal cost and effort.

Individual WAR values are calculated from the number and success rate of on-field actions by a player (in batting, baserunning, fielding, and pitching), with higher values reflecting greater contributions to a team's success. WAR value also depends on what position a player plays, with more value going to key defensive positions such as catcher and shortstop than to positions with less defensive importance such as first base.

Calculation

There is no clearly established formula for WAR. Sources such as Baseball-Reference.com and FanGraphs use different versions, but all of them share the basic structure of combining batting runs, baserunning runs, fielding runs, positional adjustment, and a replacement-level adjustment, then converting runs to wins at roughly ten runs per win. Pitcher WAR is computed separately and differs between implementations, particularly over the use of fielding independent pitching versus runs allowed.

Usage

WAR is used extensively in player evaluation, Most Valuable Player voting discussions, and Hall of Fame debates, because it attempts to place players from different positions, teams, and eras on a single scale. As a rough guide, a WAR of about 2 is considered a solid starter, 5 an All-Star level season, and 8 or more an MVP-caliber season.

Criticism

Critics note that the different public frameworks can disagree meaningfully on a player's value, that defensive metrics feeding into WAR are noisy, and that WAR is a descriptive estimate rather than a precise measurement. Supporters respond that its transparency and all-in-one structure make it the most useful single summary statistic available.
