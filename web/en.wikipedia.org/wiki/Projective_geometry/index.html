Projective geometry

Projective geometry is a type of geometry that studies geometric properties that are invariant under projective transformations. This means that, compared to elementary Euclidean geometry, projective geometry has a different setting and a selective set of basic geometric notions. The basic intuitions are that projective space has more points than Euclidean space, in a given dimension, and that geometric transformations are permitted that transform the extra points (called "points at infinity") into Euclidean points, and vice versa.

Overview

In projective geometry, parallel lines meet at a point at infinity, so there is a single point at infinity for each direction and no distinction between intersecting and parallel lines. Properties involving measurement — lengths, angles — are not preserved; instead, incidence (which points lie on which lines) and the cross ratio are fundamental. Points and lines enter the theory symmetrically, giving rise to the principle of duality: any true statement yields another true statement when "point" and "line" (in the plane) are interchanged.

History

The subject grew out of Renaissance artists' study of perspective, formalized by Filippo Brunelleschi and Leon Battista Alberti. Girard Desargues laid mathematical foundations in the 17th century (Desargues's theorem), and Blaise Pascal proved his hexagon theorem as a teenager. After falling into neglect under the dominance of analytic geometry, the field was revived in the 19th century by Jean-Victor Poncelet, August Möbius (who introduced homogeneous coordinates), Karl von Staudt and others, and was systematized within Felix Klein's Erlangen program as the geometry of the projective group.

Projective spaces

Formally, a projective space can be defined as the set of lines through the origin of a vector space of one higher dimension. The real projective plane consists of the Euclidean plane plus a line at infinity; the real projective line is a circle-like one-dimensional space. Projective geometry underlies computer vision and graphics, where perspective projection is naturally modelled, and has deep connections with algebraic geometry.
