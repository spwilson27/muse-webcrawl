The Indiana General Assembly is the state legislature of the U.S. state of Indiana. It is a bicameral body consisting of a lower house, the Indiana House of Representatives, and an upper house, the Indiana Senate. The assembly meets annually at the Indiana Statehouse in Indianapolis. Under the Indiana Constitution of 1851, regular sessions are limited in length: in odd-numbered years (the "long session," when the biennial budget is written) the assembly may meet for up to 61 session days, and in even-numbered years (the "short session") for up to 31 session days.

Composition and terms

The Senate has 50 members elected from single-member districts for four-year terms, staggered so that roughly half are elected every two years. The House has 100 members elected for two-year terms. Senators must be at least 25 years old and representatives at least 21; members must be citizens of the United States, have lived in Indiana for at least two years, and in their district for at least one year before election. Indiana's is a part-time, citizen legislature; members typically hold outside employment.

Powers and procedure

The General Assembly enacts the laws of Indiana, levies taxes, appropriates funds — most importantly the state's biennial budget, adopted in the long session — and may propose amendments to the state constitution, which take effect upon voter approval after passage by two separately elected assemblies. Bills require passage by both houses and the signature of the governor, who may veto legislation; a veto can be overridden by a simple majority of both houses, a lower threshold than in most states. The assembly also draws its own and the state's congressional district boundaries following each decennial census, subject to gubernatorial veto.

History

The first General Assembly met in 1816 at Corydon, the territorial and first state capital, in the same period that Indiana entered the Union. The capital moved to Indianapolis in 1825, and the current Statehouse, completed in 1888, has housed the assembly since. The Constitution of 1851, still in force with amendments, reshaped the legislature's sessions and finances after the collapse of the state's ambitious internal-improvements program in the 1840s.

Leadership

The House is led by the Speaker of the House; the Senate is formally presided over by the lieutenant governor, who may vote only to break ties, while day-to-day leadership rests with the Senate President Pro Tempore. Each chamber also has majority and minority leaders and caucus structures organized by party.
