Logistics

Logistics is the part of supply chain management that deals with the efficient forward and reverse flow of goods, services, and related information from the point of origin to the point of consumption according to the needs of customers. It involves the integration of information, transportation, inventory, warehousing, material handling, packaging, and sometimes security.

Etymology and history

The word derives from the French logistique, associated with the movement and supply of armies; military logistics — the art of moving armies and supplying them — was systematised in the 19th century, notably by Antoine-Henri Jomini. After the Second World War, military techniques of operations research and distribution were adapted to business, giving rise to modern commercial logistics and, later, supply chain management.

Areas of logistics

  • Procurement logistics — market research and purchasing of raw materials and parts
  • Production logistics — flow of materials within factories
  • Distribution logistics — delivery of finished products to customers
  • Reverse logistics — returns, recycling and disposal
  • Third-party logistics (3PL) — outsourcing logistics functions to specialist providers such as DHL, FedEx and UPS networks

Key concepts

Modern logistics relies on containerisation (standardised by Malcom McLean's shipping container in 1956), just-in-time inventory, warehouse management systems, barcoding and RFID tracking, and route optimisation software. Performance is commonly measured by the "seven rights": getting the right product, in the right quantity and condition, to the right place, at the right time, for the right customer, at the right cost.

Military logistics

Military logistics remains a discipline in its own right, covering the supply of fuel, ammunition, food and spare parts to forces in the field; the saying "amateurs talk strategy, professionals talk logistics" is attributed to US generals of the Second World War era.
