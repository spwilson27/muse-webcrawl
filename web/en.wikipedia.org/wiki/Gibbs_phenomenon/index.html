Gibbs phenomenon

In mathematics, the Gibbs phenomenon is the peculiar manner in which the Fourier series of a piecewise continuously differentiable periodic function behaves at a jump discontinuity. The nth partial sum of the Fourier series has large oscillations near the jump, which might increase the maximum of the partial sum above that of the function itself. The overshoot does not die out as the frequency increases, but approaches a finite limit.

Description

As the number of terms in the partial sum increases, the oscillations become narrower and are compressed toward the discontinuity, but their peak height converges to a constant: the partial sums overshoot the function value by about 8.95% of the size of the jump on each side. This limiting constant is expressed in terms of the sine integral Si(π); the overshoot fraction is (Si(π)/π − 1/2) ≈ 0.08949 of the jump magnitude. The effect is named after the American physicist Josiah Willard Gibbs, who explained it in 1899; it had earlier been discussed by the English mathematician Henry Wilbraham in 1848, and is sometimes called the Wilbraham–Gibbs phenomenon.

History

The phenomenon was first noticed in experiments with Albert Michelson's harmonic analyser, a mechanical device for computing Fourier series. When the machine produced unexpected overshoots near the discontinuities of a square wave, Michelson queried whether the effect was a defect of the machine or a genuine mathematical fact. Gibbs's letters to Nature in 1898–1899 established that the overshoot is real: the sequence of partial sums converges pointwise to the function (and to the midpoint at the jump), but not uniformly, and the maximum of the partial sums converges to a value exceeding the function's by the fixed percentage of the jump.

Practical implications

In signal processing, the Gibbs phenomenon is responsible for ringing artefacts near sharp edges when signals are filtered or truncated in the frequency domain. It appears in image compression (such as JPEG artefacts near edges), in Fourier-transform spectroscopy, in MRI reconstruction and in numerical solutions of partial differential equations with shocks. It can be mitigated by smoothing methods such as σ-approximation (Lanczos sigma factors), Fejér summation, or by using window functions and filters designed to trade oscillation against resolution.
