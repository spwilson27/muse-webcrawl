Spatial planning

Spatial planning refers to the methods used by the public sector to influence the distribution of people and activities in spaces of various scales. It encompasses urban planning, regional planning, transport planning, environmental planning and land use planning, and aims to mediate between the competing demands of development, infrastructure, housing and environmental protection.

A comprehensive spatial plan coordinating land use across a region.

Definition and scope

The Royal Town Planning Institute defines spatial planning as going beyond traditional land-use planning by bringing together and integrating policies for the development and use of land with other policies that influence the nature of places and how they function. Spatial planning operates at local, regional, national and transnational levels and typically has a longer-term, strategic focus than day-to-day development control.

European tradition

In continental Europe, spatial planning has deep roots: the German Raumordnung and Raumplanung, the French aménagement du territoire, and Dutch planning, famous for the Randstad and the Green Heart concept. The European Spatial Development Perspective (1999) promoted polycentric development across the EU. The 2007 Leipzig Charter and the Territorial Agenda of the European Union continue this tradition.

United Kingdom

The Planning and Compulsory Purchase Act 2004 replaced the term "town and country planning" with "spatial planning" in official UK usage, introducing Regional Spatial Strategies and Local Development Frameworks. Later reforms, including the Localism Act 2011 and the National Planning Policy Framework (2012), restructured the system toward local and neighbourhood plans.

Marine spatial planning

Marine spatial planning extends the approach to oceans and coastal zones, allocating marine space among fishing, shipping, conservation, and offshore energy; UNESCO and the EU have promoted it widely since the 2000s.

Criticism

Critics argue that spatial planning can be technocratic, slow to respond to market signals, and — where plans restrict housing supply — a contributor to high housing costs. Advocates reply that coordination failures in land markets make some form of planning indispensable.
