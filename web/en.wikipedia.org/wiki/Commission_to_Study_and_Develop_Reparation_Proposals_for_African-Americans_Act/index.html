The Commission to Study and Develop Reparation Proposals for African-Americans Act, commonly known by its recurring bill number H.R. 40, is proposed United States federal legislation that would establish a commission to study the history of slavery in the United States, its lasting effects on African Americans, and to develop proposals for reparations. The bill takes its number from the promise of "forty acres and a mule" made to freed slaves after the American Civil War under Special Field Orders No. 15.

History

The bill was first introduced by Representative John Conyers of Michigan in 1989, and Conyers reintroduced it in every Congress until his retirement in 2017. After Conyers's departure, Representative Sheila Jackson Lee of Texas became the bill's lead sponsor. Hearings on the legislation were held by the House Judiciary Committee, including a notable hearing on Juneteenth in 2019. In April 2021, the House Judiciary Committee voted to advance H.R. 40 to the full House of Representatives, the first time the bill had been reported out of committee, though it did not receive a floor vote.

Provisions

The bill would create a 13-member commission charged with examining slavery and discrimination against African Americans from 1619 to the present, documenting the federal and state governments' role in supporting slavery, analyzing lingering negative effects, and recommending appropriate remedies, including a formal apology and proposals for compensation. The commission would be authorized to hold hearings, subpoena witnesses and documents, and report its findings to Congress.

Debate

Supporters argue that a federal study is a necessary first step toward addressing the wealth gap and systemic disadvantages rooted in slavery and subsequent racial discrimination, pointing to precedents such as reparations paid to Japanese Americans interned during World War II under the Civil Liberties Act of 1988. Critics argue that reparations are impractical, that present-day taxpayers should not bear responsibility for historical injustices, or that other policy approaches would be more effective. The bill has been endorsed by a number of civil rights organizations and was cited in Ta-Nehisi Coates's widely discussed 2014 essay "The Case for Reparations."
