Diophantus

Diophantus of Alexandria (Ancient Greek: Διόφαντος ὁ Ἀλεξανδρεύς; born probably sometime between AD 200 and 214; died around 284, aged about 84) was a Greek mathematician who was the author of a series of books called Arithmetica, many of which are now lost. His texts deal with solving algebraic equations. Diophantus was the first Greek mathematician to recognise fractions as numbers, and he introduced early algebraic symbolism, which is why he is sometimes called "the father of algebra". Equations whose solutions are sought in integers or rational numbers are today called Diophantine equations in his honour.

Life

Almost nothing is known of Diophantus's life beyond his association with Alexandria in Roman Egypt. A puzzle in the Greek Anthology, attributed to Metrodorus, states his lifespan in the form of a riddle: his boyhood lasted a sixth of his life, youth a twelfth, he married after a further seventh, his son was born five years later, the son died at half his father's age, and Diophantus died four years afterwards — giving an age of 84. His dates are inferred from internal references and from the works he cites, notably Hypsicles, and from authors who cite him, such as Theon of Alexandria.

Arithmetica

The Arithmetica originally comprised thirteen books, of which six survive in Greek and four more in a medieval Arabic translation attributed to Qusta ibn Luqa. The work is a collection of some 130 problems giving numerical solutions to determinate and indeterminate equations. Diophantus sought positive rational solutions rather than integers alone, and he developed a "syncopated" notation: abbreviations for the unknown (ς), its powers, and operations, an intermediate step between rhetorical and fully symbolic algebra. One problem, splitting a square into two rational squares, famously inspired Fermat's Last Theorem: Pierre de Fermat wrote in the margin of his copy of Bachet's 1621 Latin edition that he had a "truly marvellous proof" that no such splitting is possible into cubes or higher powers.

Other works and influence

Diophantus also wrote a treatise on polygonal numbers, surviving only in part, and a work called the Porisms, which is lost. The Arithmetica shaped both Islamic and European algebra: al-Karaji and others extended its methods, and its Renaissance recovery — through Regiomontanus, Bombelli, and Viète — helped give algebra its modern form. The adjective "Diophantine" is applied to equations, approximation, and geometry throughout number theory.
