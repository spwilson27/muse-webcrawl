Genocide

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genocide is violence that targets individuals because of their membership of a group and aims at the destruction of a people. The term was coined in 1944 by the Polish-Jewish lawyer Raphael Lemkin, combining Greek genos (race, tribe) with Latin -cide (killing), and was made a crime under international law by the UN Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (1948), which defines it as specified acts "committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such" — including killing, causing serious harm, inflicting destructive conditions of life, preventing births and forcibly transferring children.

History and cases

The Holocaust — the Nazi murder of six million Jews and of Roma and others — was the paradigm case in view at Nuremberg and in the Convention's drafting. Recognised and adjudicated cases since include the Rwandan genocide (1994, about 800,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu killed in roughly 100 days — figures vary by source), the Bosnian genocide centred on Srebrenica (1995, adjudicated by the ICTY and ICJ), Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge (whose classification remains legally debated), and the Armenian genocide of 1915–17 (recognised by many states, disputed by Turkey). Prevention duties and "responsibility to protect" doctrine followed from the failures of the 1990s.

Debates

Scholars debate the Convention's exclusion of political groups (a Soviet-era compromise), the evidentiary difficulty of "specific intent", whether colonial and cultural destruction should count (Lemkin's own wider conception), and the politics of recognition — labels trigger legal duties, so governments often avoid the word while events unfold. Genocide studies as a field compares cases to identify warning signs and prevention strategies.
