Ragged school

Ragged schools were charitable schools in 19th-century Britain that provided free elementary education, and often food, clothing and lodging, to destitute children. The name referred to the ragged clothing of the pupils. They were a product of Victorian philanthropy and an important forerunner of universal state education.

Origins

The movement is usually traced to John Pounds, a Portsmouth cobbler who from about 1818 taught poor children in his workshop without charge. In London, the Field Lane Ragged School, supported by Charles Dickens, opened in the 1840s. In 1844 the Ragged School Union was founded under the presidency of Lord Shaftesbury, and it eventually supported nearly 200 schools with around 300,000 pupils in London alone. In Scotland, Thomas Guthrie championed ragged schools in Edinburgh, emphasising feeding and industrial training alongside literacy and scripture.

Role

Ragged schools targeted children excluded from other provision — street children, orphans and the children of prisoners — many of whom worked or begged during the day, so classes were often held in the evening or on Sundays. Reformers argued they reduced juvenile crime by rescuing children from the streets.

Decline

After the Elementary Education Act 1870 created state-funded board schools, and education became compulsory, the ragged schools gradually closed or were absorbed into the state system, though the Ragged School Union's successor organisations continued charitable work.
