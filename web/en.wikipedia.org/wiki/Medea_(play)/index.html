Medea (play)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Medea in her dragon-drawn chariot, escaping Corinth in a classical theatre production]
Medea in her dragon-drawn chariot, escaping Corinth in a classical theatre production

Medea is an ancient Greek tragedy written by Euripides, first performed in 431 BC at the City Dionysia. It dramatises the myth of Medea, the foreign wife of Jason, who — abandoned for a Corinthian princess — murders the bride, her father and her own two sons to annihilate Jason's house, then escapes in the chariot of her grandfather the Sun. Though it placed third (last) in its competition, it became one of the most influential and frequently performed of all Greek tragedies.

Plot

At Corinth, Jason discards Medea to marry Glauce, daughter of King Creon, who banishes Medea. Feigning reconciliation, she sends the bride a poisoned robe and crown that consume Glauce and Creon in flame; she then kills her sons — in Euripides' version (probably his innovation) deliberately, as revenge rather than accident or mob violence — and, refusing Jason even their bodies, departs for Athens in Helios's dragon chariot, a deus ex machina ending that ancient critics already debated.

Themes

The play is a study of passion versus reason (Medea's great monologue: 'I know what evils I am about to do, but thumos is master of my counsels'), of the foreigner's vulnerability, and of women's condition — her speech on the lot of wives ('we women are the most miserable of creatures') is the sharpest in surviving tragedy. Euripides makes the barbarian sorceress the play's intelligence and its only victorious figure; Jason dwindles into shabby opportunism.

Reception

Third prize in 431 BC did not prevent canonisation: Seneca's Medea hardened her into a witch-avenger; Jean Anouilh, Robinson Jeffers, feminist directors and postcolonial readings (as the subaltern's revenge) have kept the play central. Medea's name has become the type-case — in law, psychology and journalism — for maternal filicide, a reading the play itself deliberately torments.
