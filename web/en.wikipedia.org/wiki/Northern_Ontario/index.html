Northern Ontario

Northern Ontario is a primary geographic and quasi-administrative region of the Canadian province of Ontario, the province's vast northern expanse lying north of Lake Huron and the French River. Although it makes up roughly 87 percent of Ontario's land area — some 800,000 square kilometres — it contains only about 6 percent of the province's population, around 800,000 people.

Geography and communities

The region is centred on the Canadian Shield, a terrain of boreal forest, rock, and tens of thousands of lakes, rising to the Hudson Bay Lowlands in the far north. Its largest cities are Greater Sudbury, Thunder Bay, Sault Ste. Marie, North Bay, and Timmins, all of which developed around mining, forestry, and transportation. The Trans-Canada Highway and the Canadian Pacific and Canadian National railways cross the region, linking the resource towns. Numerous First Nations communities, including remote fly-in settlements in the far north, are part of the region; significant Franco-Ontarian populations live in the northeast.

Economy

Mining is the historic backbone of the economy: Sudbury's nickel and copper deposits are world-famous, and gold, iron, and other metals are mined around Timmins, Red Lake, and the emerging Ring of Fire chromite district in the James Bay Lowlands. Forestry and pulp and paper, hydroelectric power, tourism, and public services are also important. The region has long debated its political status, with periodic proposals for it to become a separate province or to receive greater autonomy, reflecting the economic and cultural distance from Toronto and Southern Ontario.

Boundaries

The region's southern boundary is conventionally drawn at the French River, Lake Nipissing, and the Mattawa River, though some definitions based on administrative districts include the districts of Parry Sound and Muskoka, which are usually counted with Southern Ontario. For statistical purposes Statistics Canada divides the region into Northeastern and Northwestern Ontario.
