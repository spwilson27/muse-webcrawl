Supercell

EffectLarge hail, damaging winds, tornadoes, flash flooding
Key featureMesocyclone (rotating updraft)

A classic supercell thunderstorm over the Great Plains.

A supercell is a type of thunderstorm characterized by the presence of a mesocyclone, a deep, persistently rotating updraft. Supercells are the least common type of thunderstorm but have a high propensity to produce severe weather, including damaging winds, very large hail, and sometimes strong to violent tornadoes.

Characteristics

Supercells can occur anywhere in the world under the right atmospheric conditions, but they are most common in the Great Plains of the United States, in the region known as Tornado Alley, and in the Pampas of Argentina. They can last for hours and travel hundreds of kilometres, largely independent of the environmental wind field because of their internal dynamics. Visually, supercells often show a sculpted, striated appearance with a rain-free base and a wall cloud lowering beneath the rotating updraft.

Types

Meteorologists classify supercells along a spectrum defined by precipitation: classic supercells, high-precipitation (HP) supercells, in which heavy rain wraps around the mesocyclone, and low-precipitation (LP) supercells, which produce little rain at the surface but can generate very large hail. Miniature supercells occur in cooler-season environments with shallower instability.

Hazards

Although only a minority of supercells produce tornadoes, those that do account for a disproportionate share of strong and violent tornadoes. Other hazards include hailstones larger than baseballs, flash flooding, and destructive straight-line winds.
