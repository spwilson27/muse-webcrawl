Metcalfe's law

The number of possible connections in a network grows proportionally to the square of the number of nodes

Metcalfe's law states that the financial value or impact of a telecommunications network is proportional to the square of the number of connected users of the system (n²). The law was formulated by Robert Metcalfe, co-inventor of Ethernet, in the context of selling network equipment in about 1980, and popularized by George Gilder in 1993.

Explanation

The reasoning is that the number of unique possible connections in a network of n nodes is n(n−1)/2, which grows proportionally to n² as n becomes large. A fax machine alone is useless; two permit one connection; each additional user increases the value of the network to all other users because it increases the number of possible connections. The law became a touchstone of the dot-com era as an explanation for the value of internet businesses and network effects.

Criticism and alternatives

Critics, including Andrew Odlyzko, have argued that Metcalfe's law overstates value, since not all connections are equally valuable; Odlyzko proposed n·log(n) as a more realistic heuristic. Others, such as Reed's law, argue for even faster growth in networks that support group formation (proportional to 2ⁿ subsets). Empirical studies of networks such as Facebook and Tencent have found value growth consistent with n·log(n) or between that and n². Despite criticism, Metcalfe's law remains an influential qualitative statement of network effects.
