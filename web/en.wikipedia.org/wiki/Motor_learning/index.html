Motor learning

Motor learning is a relatively permanent change in the capability for skilled movement that develops as a result of practice or experience. It involves complex processes in the brain that occur in response to practising or experiencing a certain skill, and it underlies everything from learning to walk and write to acquiring elite sporting, musical, and surgical expertise. Motor learning is measured by analysing performance in a specific task, with retention and transfer tests used to distinguish genuine learning from temporary performance effects.

Stages of learning

Fitts and Posner (1967) proposed three stages:

  1. Cognitive stage — the learner consciously works out what to do; performance is variable and error-prone and demands attention.
  2. Associative stage — movements become more consistent and refined; errors are detected and corrected using feedback.
  3. Autonomous stage — the skill is performed automatically with little conscious attention, freeing resources for strategy and anticipation.

Neural basis

Motor learning recruits interacting systems: the primary motor cortex and premotor areas, the basal ganglia (important for sequencing and habit formation), the cerebellum (critical for error-based adaptation and timing), and the spinal cord. Different paradigms isolate different mechanisms — error-based adaptation (e.g., adjusting to prism goggles or force fields), reinforcement learning (reward-driven selection of successful actions), use-dependent learning, and observational learning. Sleep contributes to consolidation of motor memories.

Key principles

Research has established robust phenomena including the power law of practice, the benefits of distributed over massed practice for retention, variable practice and the contextual-interference effect (blocked practice aids acquisition, random practice aids retention), the guidance hypothesis (frequent augmented feedback helps performance but can hinder learning), and specificity of practice. These principles inform coaching, music pedagogy, and rehabilitation after stroke or injury, where relearning movement is the central therapeutic goal.

Theories

Adams's closed-loop theory and Schmidt's schema theory (1975) were foundational cognitive accounts; ecological and dynamical-systems approaches instead emphasise the self-organisation of coordination under constraints of the organism, task, and environment. Contemporary computational accounts model motor learning as Bayesian state estimation and optimal feedback control.
