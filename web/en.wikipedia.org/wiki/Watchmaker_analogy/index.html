Watchmaker analogy

A pocket watch: finding one, Paley argued, compels the inference of a watchmaker.

The watchmaker analogy or watchmaker argument is a teleological argument used to support the existence of God. By way of an analogy, the argument states that design implies a designer, especially intelligent design by a creator. If a person walking across a heath found a stone, they might suppose it had always been there; but if they found a watch — an object with parts fitted together for a purpose — they would inevitably infer that it had a maker.

Paley's formulation

The analogy is most closely associated with the Anglican clergyman and philosopher William Paley, whose Natural Theology (1802) opened with the watch on the heath. Paley argued that, just as the intricate mechanism of a watch implies an intelligent watchmaker, the complexity and apparent purposefulness of living organisms and of nature imply an intelligent creator. Versions of the reasoning predate Paley — it appears in Cicero and in early modern writers — but his presentation became canonical in the 19th century.

Criticism

David Hume, writing before Paley in the Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (1779), had already challenged design arguments: the analogy between artifacts and the universe is weak, order can arise from other principles, and an imperfect world sits oddly with a perfect designer. Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection (1859) supplied a naturalistic mechanism that produces the appearance of design in organisms without a designer. Richard Dawkins's book The Blind Watchmaker (1986) takes its title from the analogy, arguing that natural selection is a "blind watchmaker" that builds complexity cumulatively. The analogy nonetheless remains influential in debates over intelligent design.
