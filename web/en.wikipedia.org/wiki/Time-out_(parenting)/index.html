Time-out (parenting)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Time-out is a form of behavioural discipline used by parents and teachers, most often with young children, in which a child is removed from an environment where misbehaviour has occurred and placed in a quiet, unstimulating area for a brief period. The term derives from the behaviourist concept of "time out from positive reinforcement": the procedure is intended to reduce undesirable behaviour by withdrawing access to attention, activities, and other reinforcers, rather than by physical punishment.

Procedure

In the standard procedure recommended by paediatric and behavioural guidelines, the parent calmly names the unacceptable behaviour, gives one warning, and if it continues, seats the child in a designated spot (a chair, step, or corner) for a short period — commonly about one minute per year of age — without interaction. The time-out ends once the child is calm; the parent then briefly restates the rule and the child returns to the activity. Proponents emphasize consistency, brevity, calm delivery, and pairing time-out with praise and "time-in" (positive attention) for good behaviour.

History and evidence

The technique was formalized in behavioural psychology in the 1960s, drawing on operant-conditioning research by B. F. Skinner and others, and was popularized as a non-violent alternative to corporal punishment. Reviews by bodies including the American Academy of Pediatrics consider time-out effective for reducing noncompliance and aggression in children roughly aged two to eight when implemented correctly, and it is a component of evidence-based programmes such as Parent–Child Interaction Therapy and the Incredible Years.

Criticism

Critics, including some attachment-oriented psychologists, argue that time-out can feel like rejection or withdrawal of love, may escalate distressed children rather than teach self-regulation, and is ineffective when the misbehaviour stems from unmet needs or overwhelming emotion. Defenders reply that properly used time-out is brief, predictable, and non-punitive, and distinguish it from punitive isolation or prolonged confinement, which is not recommended. Alternatives promoted by critics include emotion coaching and collaborative problem-solving.
