Cissoid
TypeAlgebraic curve (cubic)
Named fromGreek kissoeidēs, "ivy-shaped"
Most famous exampleCissoid of Diocles

In geometry, a cissoid is a curve generated from two given curves C1 and C2 and a point O, the pole. Given a line through O intersecting C1 at P1 and C2 at P2, the point P on the line satisfying OP = |P2P1| traces the cissoid of the two curves with respect to O. The construction generalizes many classical cubic curves.

Cissoid of Diocles

The best-known example is the cissoid of Diocles, attributed to the Greek geometer Diocles (c. 240 – c. 180 BC), formed from a circle and its tangent line. In Cartesian coordinates, with a circle of radius a, its equation is y² = x³ / (2a − x). It has a cusp at the origin and a vertical asymptote at x = 2a.

Diocles devised the curve to solve the problem of doubling the cube — finding the edge of a cube with twice the volume of a given cube — one of the three classical construction problems of antiquity, which cannot be solved with compass and straightedge alone. Newton later gave a mechanical construction of the curve using a sliding right angle.

General cissoids

When one of the generating curves is a line, the cissoid family includes the strophoid and the trisectrix of Maclaurin as special cases. Cissoids of conics with respect to a point on them generate rational cubic curves, a connection explored in classical algebraic geometry.
