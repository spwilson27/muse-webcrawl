Pages of the Madrid Codex, the longest surviving Maya book.

The Madrid Codex, also known as the Troano Codex or by its combined name Tro-Cortesianus Codex, is one of only a handful of surviving books of the pre-Columbian Maya civilization. At 112 pages it is the longest of the surviving Maya codices, which also include the Dresden, Paris and Grolier codices.

History

The manuscript reached Spain in the colonial period and eventually surfaced in two separate fragments. The "Troano" portion, named after its owner Juan Tro y Ortolano, was published in the 1860s; a second fragment, the "Cortesianus", appeared in 1875. The scholar Léon de Rosny recognised that the two pieces belonged to a single screenfold book, and they were reunited under the name Tro-Cortesianus. The codex is now held by the Museo de América in Madrid.

Contents

Like the other Maya codices, the Madrid Codex is a divinatory and ritual almanac rather than a narrative or historical text. Its pages contain calendrical tables keyed to the 260-day tzolk'in cycle, astronomical references, and illustrated ceremonies associated with rain, agriculture, beekeeping, hunting, weaving and the New Year rituals. Deities such as Itzamna and Chaac appear throughout, accompanied by hieroglyphic captions.

Physical description

The codex is painted on amate bark paper coated with a fine gesso, folded accordion-style. It is generally dated to the Late Postclassic period and is thought to originate from Yucatán, possibly the region of Tayasal. Although its draftsmanship is cruder than that of the Dresden Codex, its breadth of almanacs makes it a central source for Maya religion and everyday ritual practice.
