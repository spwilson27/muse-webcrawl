Silver Age of Latin literature

The Silver Age of Latin literature is the period of Latin writing that followed the so-called Golden Age, conventionally dated from the death of Augustus in AD 14 (or from about AD 18) to roughly AD 200, covering the literature of the early and middle Roman Empire.

Characteristics

Silver Age writing was shaped by the rhetoric schools (declamation), producing a pointed, epigrammatic style rich in sententiae — sharp, quotable aphorisms. Literature of the period often turned toward satire, epic on contemporary or mythological themes, technical prose, and encyclopaedic compilation, in an imperial setting where political outspokenness could be dangerous.

Principal authors

  • Poetry: Lucan (Pharsalia), Statius, Valerius Flaccus, Silius Italicus, Martial (epigrams), Juvenal and Persius (satire), Phaedrus (fables).
  • Prose: Seneca the Younger, Pliny the Elder (Natural History), Pliny the Younger, Tacitus, Suetonius, Quintilian, Petronius (Satyricon), Apuleius (The Golden Ass), Aulus Gellius.

Assessment

The label "Silver" reflects an older scholarly judgement that imperial literature was inferior to that of the late Republic and Augustan age; modern criticism has substantially rehabilitated many Silver Age authors, valuing their stylistic brilliance and their testimony to imperial society.
