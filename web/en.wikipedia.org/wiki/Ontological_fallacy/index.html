Ontological fallacy

The ontological fallacy confuses our concepts with mind-independent reality

The ontological fallacy is the error of treating concepts, categories, or descriptions as if they were mind-independent realities — that is, assuming that because a distinction exists in our language or thought, a corresponding distinction must exist in the world itself. It is closely related to reification (treating an abstraction as a concrete thing) and to Alfred North Whitehead's "fallacy of misplaced concreteness."

Discussion

The fallacy arises when reasoning moves uncritically from "we think of the world as divided into X and Y" to "the world is divided into X and Y." Philosophers point to examples such as assuming that because grammar distinguishes nouns and verbs, reality must be composed of things and processes as fundamentally separate kinds, or that because a model in science uses a mathematical entity, that entity must physically exist exactly as modelled.

The concept has been discussed in metaphysics and the philosophy of science, and related ideas appear in the philosophy of education and in phenomenology, where the ontological fallacy can describe the mistake of projecting the structure of one's own modes of experience or cognition onto being itself. Critics of a discipline sometimes charge it with the ontological fallacy when it mistakes its classificatory schemes — for instance, diagnostic categories or economic indicators — for natural, self-evident divisions of reality.

Related ideas

The ontological fallacy overlaps with several classic warnings in philosophy: Whitehead's fallacy of misplaced concreteness; the map–territory relation emphasised by Alfred Korzybski ("the map is not the territory"); the reification fallacy; and critiques of naive realism. It differs from the ontological argument for the existence of God, which concerns inferring existence from a concept — a different, though neighbouring, charge of reasoning illegitimately from thought to being, one Kant pressed when he argued that existence is not a real predicate.
