Hepburn Act of 1906
Enacted: June 29, 1906
Sponsor: Rep. William P. Hepburn (R-IA)
Effect: Empowered the Interstate Commerce Commission to set maximum railroad rates

Hepburn Act

The Hepburn Act is a 1906 United States federal law that greatly strengthened the Interstate Commerce Commission (ICC). Named for its sponsor, Representative William Peters Hepburn of Iowa, and championed by President Theodore Roosevelt, it authorised the ICC to set maximum railroad freight rates that were "just and reasonable", replacing the largely ineffective regulatory regime of the Interstate Commerce Act of 1887.

Provisions

The Act extended the ICC's jurisdiction to express companies, sleeping-car companies, oil pipelines, bridges, and ferry services; required standardised railroad accounting and reports; prohibited free passes except to limited classes of persons; and made ICC orders binding without prior court approval, shifting the burden of appeal to the railroads. The commission was enlarged from five to seven members.

Significance

The Hepburn Act was a landmark of Progressive Era regulation: for the first time a federal agency could actually control the prices charged by the railroads, curbing discriminatory rates and rebates that had favoured large shippers such as Standard Oil. Further authority followed in the Mann–Elkins Act of 1910.
