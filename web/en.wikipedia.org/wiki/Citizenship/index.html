Citizenship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Citizenship is a legal and social relationship between an individual and a state, in which the state grants the citizen rights and protections in exchange for allegiance and the fulfilment of duties. Citizenship typically confers the right to live and work in the state, to vote and stand for office, to consular protection abroad, and to enter the country freely; duties may include taxation, jury service and military service.

Acquisition and loss

States allocate citizenship mainly by descent (jus sanguinis) or by birth on the territory (jus soli), in varying mixtures; most also provide naturalisation for immigrants, usually requiring residence, language and civic knowledge, and good character. Multiple citizenship is increasingly tolerated, while deprivation of citizenship (for fraud or, controversially, for terrorism offences) and statelessness — lacking any nationality — are recurring legal and humanitarian issues, addressed partly by the 1954 and 1961 UN conventions.

Development

From the exclusive citizen-body of the Greek polis and Roman citizenship's expansion across an empire, through subjecthood under monarchies, citizenship became a universal status with the democratic revolutions. T. H. Marshall's classic account traced the sequential growth of civil rights (18th century), political rights (19th) and social rights (20th) in Britain. Contemporary debates concern "citizenship by investment" (golden passports), EU citizenship as a supranational layer, denizenship for long-term residents, and whether digital and climate-age mobility requires new forms of membership.
