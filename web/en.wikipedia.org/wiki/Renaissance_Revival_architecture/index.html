Renaissance Revival architecture

Renaissance Revival architecture (sometimes referred to as "Neo-Renaissance") is a group of 19th-century architectural revival styles which were neither Greek Revival nor Gothic Revival but instead drew inspiration from a wide range of classicising Italian modes. It emerged in the early 19th century and flourished in the second half of the century for public buildings, railway stations, banks, clubs and grand houses.

Characteristics

Typical features include rusticated ground floors, quoins, regularly spaced windows with pediments or architraves, cornices, balustrades, and plans organised around courtyards on the model of the Florentine and Roman palazzo. Related variants include the Italianate style, the Rundbogenstil in Germany, and the "palazzo" mode popularised in Britain by Charles Barry's clubs, such as the Reform Club in London.

Development

The style's flexibility made it suitable for the expanding institutions of the industrial era: town halls, museums, universities and government offices across Europe and the Americas adopted Neo-Renaissance forms to project civic dignity and historical continuity. By the early 20th century it was largely supplanted by other revival styles and early modernism.
