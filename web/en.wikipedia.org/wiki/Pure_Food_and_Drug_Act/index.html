Pure Food and Drug Act


Harvey W. Wiley, the chief advocate of the 1906 Act.

The Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906 was the first of a series of significant consumer protection laws enacted by the United States Congress in the 20th century and led to the creation of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Its main purpose was to ban foreign and interstate traffic in adulterated or mislabeled food and drug products, and it directed the U.S. Bureau of Chemistry to inspect products and refer offenders to prosecutors.

Background

The Act followed a decades-long campaign by Dr. Harvey Washington Wiley, chief chemist of the Department of Agriculture, whose "Poison Squad" experiments tested food preservatives on volunteers. Public outrage crystallized in 1906 with Upton Sinclair's novel The Jungle, which exposed unsanitary conditions in the Chicago meatpacking industry; the same year, Samuel Hopkins Adams's articles exposed dangerous patent medicines. President Theodore Roosevelt supported the legislation, which passed alongside the Meat Inspection Act on June 30, 1906.

Provisions

The Act prohibited the manufacture, sale, or transportation of adulterated, misbranded, poisonous, or deleterious foods, drugs, medicines, and liquors in interstate commerce. It required that certain ingredients—including alcohol, morphine, opium, cocaine, and cannabis—be disclosed on labels if present. However, it did not require proof of safety or efficacy, and a 1911 Supreme Court decision (United States v. Johnson) held that it did not prohibit false therapeutic claims, a loophole partially closed by the Sherley Amendment of 1912.

Legacy

The Act laid the foundation of American food and drug regulation. Enforcement passed from the Bureau of Chemistry to the Food, Drug, and Insecticide Administration in 1927, renamed the Food and Drug Administration in 1930. The 1906 Act was superseded by the much stronger Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938.
