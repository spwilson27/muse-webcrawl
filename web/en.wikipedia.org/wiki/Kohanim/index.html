Kohanim

Hebrewכֹּהֲנִים (kohanim), singular kohen (כֹּהֵן)
DescentPatrilineal, from Aaron
RolesTemple service (historically); priestly blessing today

Kohanim (Hebrew: כֹּהֲנִים, romanized: kohanim; singular kohen, "priest") are the hereditary male descendants of Aaron, the elder brother of Moses, who served as priests in the Tabernacle and the Temple in Jerusalem. Together with the Levites, they belong to the tribe of Levi, but hold a distinct, elevated status within it.

Roles

In the era of the Temple, kohanim performed the daily and festival sacrifices, burned incense, and pronounced the Priestly Blessing over the congregation. Following the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, sacrificial duties ceased, but kohanim retain special roles in Jewish religious life: they receive the first aliyah (call to the Torah reading) in synagogue, perform the Priestly Blessing (Birkat Kohanim) during services, and officiate at the redemption of the firstborn (pidyon haben).

Restrictions and status

Traditional Jewish law imposes special rules on kohanim, including restrictions on marriage and on contact with the dead or entry into cemeteries. Kohen status is transmitted from father to son and is often reflected in surnames such as Cohen, Katz and Azoulay. Genetic studies of the Y-chromosome "Cohen modal haplotype" have been used to investigate the claimed common ancestry of kohanim.
