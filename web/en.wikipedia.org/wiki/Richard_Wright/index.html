Richard Nathaniel Wright (September 4, 1908 – November 28, 1960) was an American author of novels, short stories, poems and non-fiction. Much of his literature concerns racial themes, especially related to the plight of African Americans during the late 19th to mid-20th centuries suffering discrimination and violence. His work helped redefine discussions of race in America in the mid-20th century.

Early life

Wright was born on a plantation near Natchez, Mississippi, the son of a sharecropper. After a childhood of poverty, hunger and movement between Mississippi, Tennessee and Arkansas, he moved to Chicago in 1927 during the Great Migration, working menial jobs while reading voraciously — famously obtaining library books with a borrowed card and a forged note. He joined the Communist Party and the Federal Writers' Project, publishing the story collection Uncle Tom's Children (1938).

Native Son and Black Boy

His novel Native Son (1940), the story of Bigger Thomas, a young Black man in Chicago driven to murder, was a Book-of-the-Month Club selection and an enormous bestseller, making Wright the most famous Black writer in America. His memoir Black Boy (1945), about his Southern childhood, is now a classic of American autobiography.

Later years

Disillusioned with the Communist Party — described in his essay in The God That Failed (1949) — Wright moved to Paris in 1946, where he befriended Jean-Paul Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir and became a leading figure among expatriate writers. Later books included the novel The Outsider (1953) and travel and political works on Africa, Spain and Asia. He died of a heart attack in Paris in 1960. His uncompromising naturalism influenced — and was challenged by — the next generation, including Ralph Ellison and James Baldwin, whose essay "Everybody's Protest Novel" criticised Native Son.
