Martial law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Martial law is the replacement of civilian government by military rule and the suspension of ordinary civilian legal processes — or their extension to civilians — usually declared by a head of state during war, rebellion, occupation or major disaster. Under martial law, military commanders may legislate by decree, military tribunals may try civilians, and rights such as habeas corpus may be suspended.

Practice and examples

Historic instances include Lincoln's and US state-level declarations during the Civil War era (qualified by the Supreme Court in Ex parte Milligan, 1866: military trial of civilians is illegitimate where civil courts function), British martial law in Ireland (1916–21), and much longer episodes in authoritarian states — the Philippines under Marcos (1972–81), Thailand's repeated declarations, Pakistan's several military regimes, and South Korea's 1979–81 period and its brief December 2024 declaration, rescinded within hours after the National Assembly voted to lift it, an episode that led to the president's impeachment and removal. Occupied territory governed by an occupier's military law under the Hague and Geneva frameworks is a related but distinct regime.

Modern frameworks

Most democracies now prefer statutory states of emergency with time limits and legislative oversight over open-ended martial law, and international human-rights law (ICCPR Article 4) permits only declared, necessary and non-discriminatory derogations, with some rights non-derogable — standards against which declarations are litigated and judged.
