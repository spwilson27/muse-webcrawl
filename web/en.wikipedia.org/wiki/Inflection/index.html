Inflection

In linguistic morphology, inflection (or inflexion) is a process of word formation in which a word is modified to express different grammatical categories such as tense, case, voice, aspect, person, number, gender and mood. Inflection is distinct from derivation, which forms new words (lexemes) with different meanings; inflectional forms are regarded as variants of the same lexeme. The term also covers the corresponding set of forms, the word's paradigm.

Inflection in different parts of speech

Inflection of nouns and pronouns is called declension — typically for case and number (and gender class), as in Latin, German, Russian and Sanskrit. Inflection of verbs is called conjugation — for tense, aspect, mood, person and number. Adjectives may inflect for degree (comparative and superlative: big, bigger, biggest) and, in many languages, agree with the nouns they modify in case, number and gender.

Mechanisms

The commonest mechanism is affixation: adding prefixes, suffixes, infixes or circumfixes (English plural -s, past tense -ed). Other mechanisms include internal modification such as vowel change (Germanic ablaut: sing–sang–sung; umlaut), consonant mutation, suppletion (using unrelated forms, as go–went or good–better), reduplication, and changes of stress or tone.

Inflection across languages

Languages vary enormously in how much inflection they use. Highly inflected fusional languages (Latin, Russian, Sanskrit) pack several categories into single endings, while agglutinative languages (Turkish, Finnish, Japanese) string together separate, regular morphemes. Isolating languages such as Mandarin and Vietnamese use little inflection at all, relying on word order and particles. Modern English is weakly inflected — retaining plural -s, possessive -'s, third-person -s, past -ed and a handful of irregular paradigms — having shed most of the rich inflection of Old English.
