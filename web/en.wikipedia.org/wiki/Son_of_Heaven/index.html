Son of Heaven (Chinese: 天子, Tiānzǐ) is a traditional imperial title of the Chinese emperor, originating with the Zhou dynasty. It expresses the doctrine that the emperor rules by the Mandate of Heaven: he is Heaven's chosen representative on Earth, responsible for maintaining harmony between the cosmic and human orders through correct ritual.

Origins

The Zhou kings adopted the title after overthrowing the Shang around 1046 BCE, justifying their rule with the idea that Heaven grants authority to the virtuous and withdraws it from the corrupt. When the emperor lost virtue, natural disasters and rebellions signaled the mandate's loss, enabling the dynastic cycle by which one ruling house replaced another.

Duties and rituals

The Son of Heaven performed state rites at the Temple of Heaven in Beijing—praying for good harvests at the winter solstice—and at the altars of soil and grain. He alone could offer the highest sacrifices to Heaven (Tian), while nobles and commoners worshipped lesser spirits and ancestors.

Spread of the concept

The title and its ideology spread through the Sinosphere: Japanese emperors were styled tenshi, Korean rulers used the concept while kings styled themselves more modestly under Chinese suzerainty, and Vietnamese emperors adopted the title domestically. In diplomacy, foreign rulers were expected to acknowledge the Chinese emperor's unique status as Son of Heaven within the tributary system.

End of the title

The last emperor to bear the title was Puyi, whose abdication in 1912 ended over two thousand years of imperial rule. The phrase survives in historical and cultural references.
