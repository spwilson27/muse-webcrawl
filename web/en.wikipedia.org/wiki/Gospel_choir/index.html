Gospel choir

A gospel choir is a choir that performs gospel music, the sacred music tradition that emerged from African American churches in the United States in the early 20th century. Gospel choirs are typically anchored in church worship services, particularly in Black Protestant traditions such as Baptist, Methodist, and Pentecostal churches, but they also perform in concerts, competitions, recordings and community events. Characterized by rich vocal harmonies, call-and-response patterns, rhythmic hand-clapping, and expressive soloists, the gospel choir became one of the central institutions of gospel music and a training ground for many of America's most influential singers.

History

Origins

The modern gospel choir developed in Chicago in the 1930s. Thomas A. Dorsey, the "father of gospel music," organized some of the first gospel choirs at Pilgrim Baptist Church and through the National Convention of Gospel Choirs and Choruses, which he co-founded with Sallie Martin in 1932. Dorsey's compositions — blending sacred lyrics with blues and jazz idioms — gave choirs a distinctive repertoire distinct from traditional hymns and spirituals. Sallie Martin was instrumental in organizing choirs across the country and promoting gospel choruses as a movement.

Golden age and the "choir sound"

After World War II, gospel quartets and soloists dominated commercial gospel, but choirs remained central to church life. In the 1950s and 1960s, figures such as Professor J. Earle Hines in Los Angeles and Alex Bradford in New York helped popularize large "mass choirs." The Reverend James Cleveland, founder of the Gospel Music Workshop of America (1967), became the most influential choir leader of the era, recording with the Southern California Community Choir and others. In 1969, the Edwin Hawkins Singers' recording of "Oh Happy Day" became an international pop hit, introducing the gospel choir sound to mainstream audiences worldwide.

Contemporary era

From the 1980s onward, choir directors such as Hezekiah Walker, Kirk Franklin (with God's Property), Donald Lawrence, and Richard Smallwood blended gospel choir traditions with contemporary R&B, hip hop and praise-and-worship styles. College and university gospel choirs — such as the Howard Gospel Choir (founded 1968) — became prominent, and gospel choirs spread internationally, with active scenes in Europe, Japan and elsewhere. Choir competitions, festivals and television performances have sustained the format, while many megachurches maintain large volunteer choirs as a centerpiece of worship.

Musical characteristics

Gospel choirs typically sing in three- or four-part harmony (soprano, alto, tenor, and sometimes bass), directed by a conductor who cues dynamics, entrances and choreography-like movement. Performance practice draws on:

  • Call and response between soloist ("lead") and choir
  • Vamps — repeated closing sections that build in intensity and invite improvisation
  • Modulation ("lifting" the song by semitone steps) for dramatic effect
  • Rhythmic accompaniment from piano or Hammond organ, drums, bass guitar and hand-clapping
  • Vocal techniques including melisma, growls, shouts and "squalling"

Rehearsal culture in gospel choirs traditionally emphasizes learning by ear (rote) rather than from sheet music, though many modern choirs use both approaches.
