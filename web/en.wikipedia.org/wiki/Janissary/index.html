The Janissaries (Ottoman Turkish: يڭيچرى, yeñiçeri, "new soldier") were an elite infantry corps that formed the Ottoman sultan's household troops and bodyguards. Founded in the 14th century, traditionally under Sultan Murad I, they were recruited through the devshirme system, in which Christian boys — mainly from the Balkans — were levied, converted to Islam, and trained as soldiers personally loyal to the sultan.

Organization and training

Recruits passed through the acemi oğlan (novice) stage, working on farms or in palace service before entering Janissary regiments (orta). Training was strict: discipline, celibacy in the early centuries, and devotion to the corps, whose patron was the Sufi saint Haji Bektash Veli. Janissaries were among the first standing armies in Europe and early adopters of firearms, marching to the distinctive music of the mehter band.

Role and power

At their peak in the 16th century, under sultans such as Selim I and Suleiman the Magnificent, the Janissaries were the decisive arm of Ottoman warfare, also garrisoning fortresses throughout the empire. Over time their privileges hardened into political power: they mutinied over pay debasement, deposed and killed sultans (including Osman II in 1622 and Selim III in 1807), and resisted military reform, while their numbers swelled and military effectiveness declined as membership became hereditary and entangled with trade.

Destruction

Sultan Mahmud II, having secured religious and popular support, provoked and crushed a Janissary revolt in June 1826 — the "Auspicious Incident" — bombarding their barracks in Constantinople and killing thousands; the corps was abolished and replaced by a modern-style army.
