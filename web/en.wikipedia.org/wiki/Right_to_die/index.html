Right to die

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The right to die is a concept based on the opinion that human beings are entitled to end their lives or undergo voluntary euthanasia. Possession of this right is variously understood as the right of a person who is terminally ill, in great pain, or without hope of recovery, to choose to end their life — either by their own hand, with medical assistance, or by refusing life-sustaining treatment.

Forms

  • Refusal of treatment: competent patients' right to decline medical interventions, including life-sustaining ones, is widely recognized in law and medical ethics, and may be recorded in advance directives and living wills.
  • Assisted dying / assisted suicide: a physician or another person provides the means (such as a lethal prescription) for a person to end their own life. Legal under safeguards in jurisdictions including several US states (beginning with Oregon's Death with Dignity Act, effective 1997), Canada, Spain, New Zealand and others.
  • Voluntary euthanasia: a clinician actively administers the means of death at the patient's request; legal in the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Colombia, Spain, Canada and parts of Australia, among others.

Arguments

Supporters rest the claim on autonomy, bodily integrity and relief of suffering, arguing that a competent person's choice about their own dying should be respected. Opponents cite the sanctity of life, risks of coercion or error ("slippery slope" concerns), the vulnerability of elderly and disabled people, and the availability of palliative care, arguing instead for improved end-of-life care.

Landmark cases and organizations

Cases such as those of Karen Ann Quinlan, Nancy Cruzan, Terri Schiavo, Diane Pretty, Tony Nicklinson and Brittany Maynard shaped public and legal debate. Advocacy organizations include Dignitas (Switzerland), Exit International and Compassion & Choices (US); opponents include many medical associations, disability-rights groups and religious bodies.
