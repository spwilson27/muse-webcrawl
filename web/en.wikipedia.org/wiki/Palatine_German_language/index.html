Palatine German (endonym: Pälzisch; Standard German: Pfälzisch) is a group of Rhine Franconian dialects of West Central German spoken in the Palatinate region of southwestern Germany and in neighbouring areas. It is historically important far beyond the region because it is the principal parent dialect of Pennsylvania Dutch in North America and closely related to Hunsrik in Brazil.

Distribution and varieties

The dialect area covers the Palatinate proper — the Vorderpfalz along the Rhine, the Westpfalz around Kaiserslautern and Pirmasens — and extends into the southern Rhineland, the Saarland border country, South Hesse, and the Kraichgau in northern Baden-Württemberg. Linguists distinguish West Palatine and East (Frontier) Palatine varieties, separated roughly by the dat/das isogloss among other features. Within the region there is no single standard form; pronunciation and vocabulary vary noticeably from village to village, and standard German is used in writing and formal speech.

Features

Like other High German dialects, Palatine German participated in the High German consonant shift, though in less complete form than Upper German: for example, it retains p in words such as Pund where standard German has Pfund. Characteristic features include distinctive vowel developments (diphthongisations differing between West and East Palatine), frequent loss of unstressed endings, and a vocabulary enriched by French loanwords reflecting centuries of border history with France.

Emigrant descendants

Large-scale emigration from the Palatinate in the 18th century carried the dialect overseas. Pennsylvania Dutch (Deitsch), spoken by Old Order Amish and some Mennonite communities in Pennsylvania, Ohio, Indiana, and Ontario, derives mainly from the Palatine dialects of the emigrants' homelands. Hunsrik, spoken in southern Brazil, descends primarily from the related Hunsrück dialects of the same Rhine-Franconian family.

Use today

Palatine German remains a vigorous spoken vernacular in much of its home area, strongly associated with regional identity, carnival (Fasenacht) performances, dialect poetry and theatre, and everyday informal speech, though like other German dialects it is receding among younger speakers in favour of regionally coloured standard German.
