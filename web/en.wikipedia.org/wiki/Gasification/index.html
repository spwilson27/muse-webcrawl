Gasification

Gasification is a process that converts biomass- or fossil fuel-based carbonaceous materials into gases, including as the largest fractions: nitrogen (N2), carbon monoxide (CO), hydrogen (H2), and carbon dioxide (CO2).

Gasification
Feedstock: Coal, biomass, waste
Products: Syngas (CO + H2), heat, power
Temperature: Typically >700 °C

Process

Gasification is achieved by reacting the feedstock at high temperatures (typically >700 °C), without combustion, via controlling the amount of oxygen and/or steam present. The resulting mixture is called syngas (synthesis gas) or producer gas and is itself a fuel. Key reactions include partial oxidation, the water-gas reaction, the Boudouard reaction and methanation.

Types of gasifier

Designs include fixed-bed (updraft and downdraft), fluidised-bed, and entrained-flow gasifiers, differing in how feedstock and gasifying agent contact each other.

Applications

Syngas can be burned for electricity — notably in integrated gasification combined cycle (IGCC) plants — converted to hydrogen, or used as feedstock for Fischer–Tropsch fuels, methanol, ammonia and other chemicals. Historically, town gas from coal gasification lit cities in the 19th and early 20th centuries, and wood gasifiers powered vehicles during World War II fuel shortages.

Advantages and issues

Gasification can handle diverse feedstocks including waste, and facilitates carbon capture because CO2 is concentrated before combustion. Challenges include tar formation, feedstock handling, cost, and overall efficiency compared with direct combustion or pyrolysis.
