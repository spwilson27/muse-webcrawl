The common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR) of a differential amplifier (or other device) is a metric used to quantify the ability of the device to reject common-mode signals — identical signals that appear simultaneously and in phase on both inputs. It is defined as the ratio of differential-mode gain to common-mode gain:

CMRR = Ad / Acm, usually expressed in decibels as 20·log10(Ad/Acm).

Significance

An ideal differential amplifier amplifies only the difference between its two inputs and outputs zero for a purely common-mode signal, giving an infinite CMRR. In practice, mismatches between components produce a small common-mode gain. High CMRR is essential in measurement and audio applications where a small differential signal rides on a large interfering signal, such as mains hum picked up equally by both leads of a sensor cable.

Values and frequency dependence

Typical operational amplifiers specify CMRR between about 60 dB and 120 dB at DC. CMRR generally falls with increasing frequency as internal capacitances and asymmetries dominate. Instrumentation amplifiers are designed and laser-trimmed for very high CMRR, and techniques such as guarding, shielding, and balanced (differential) cabling further reduce common-mode interference in complete systems.
