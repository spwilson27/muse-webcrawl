Lake Agassiz

Lake Agassiz was a vast glacial lake in central North America in the late Pleistocene, fed by glacial meltwater at the end of the last glacial period. At its maximum, it may have covered as much as 440,000 square kilometres, larger than any lake in the world today, including the Caspian Sea.

Geology and history

The lake formed as the Laurentide Ice Sheet retreated northward and meltwater was ponded against the ice and higher ground to the south. Its successive phases — Lockington, Moorhead, Emerson and Nipigon — are recorded in beach ridges and lake sediments across Manitoba, Ontario, Saskatchewan, Minnesota and North Dakota.

Drainage changed repeatedly. Southern outflow through the Minnesota River carved the valley at Big Stone Lake; later outlets opened eastward toward Lake Superior and northwest toward the Mackenzie River. A catastrophic final drainage into Hudson Bay around 8,200 years ago is thought to have raised global sea level and disrupted ocean circulation, contributing to the 8.2-kiloyear cold event.

Legacy

The flat clay plain left by Lake Agassiz forms the fertile Red River Valley, one of North America's most productive agricultural regions. Modern remnants include Lakes Winnipeg, Manitoba, Winnipegosis and Lake of the Woods. The lake was named in 1879 by Warren Upham for Swiss-American naturalist Louis Agassiz, an early proponent of ice-age theory. See also Last Glacial Period.
