Precision rectifier

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The precision rectifier, also known as a super diode, is a configuration obtained with an operational amplifier in order to have a circuit behave like an ideal diode and rectifier. It is very useful for high-precision signal processing. Unlike a simple diode rectifier, whose forward voltage drop (about 0.7 V for silicon) prevents it from rectifying small signals, the precision rectifier places the diode inside the op-amp's feedback loop, so the op-amp compensates for the diode's voltage drop; signals of only a few millivolts can be rectified accurately.

Basic circuit

In the basic circuit, a diode is connected between the op-amp output and the circuit output, which is fed back to the inverting input. When the input is positive the op-amp drives the diode on and the output follows the input; when the input is negative the diode is off, the feedback path is broken, and the output sits at zero — a half-wave rectifier. A drawback is op-amp saturation during the off half-cycle, which slows recovery; improved designs add a second diode and resistor network to keep the amplifier in its linear region. Full-wave versions combine a half-wave precision rectifier with a summing amplifier.

Applications

Precision rectifiers are used in AC measurement and instrumentation, audio peak detectors, AM demodulation of low-level signals, and in building blocks of analogue computing and RMS-to-DC conversion circuits.
