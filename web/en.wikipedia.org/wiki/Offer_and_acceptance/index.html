Offer and acceptance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Offer and acceptance are the traditional approach to the analysis of contract formation in contract law. An offer is an expression of willingness to contract on specified terms, made with the intention that it is to become binding as soon as it is accepted by the person to whom it is addressed. Acceptance is the final and unqualified expression of assent to those terms; together they create the agreement that, with consideration and intention, forms a contract.

Offers must be distinguished from invitations to treat — advertisements, displays of goods and requests for tenders are usually invitations, so that the customer or bidder makes the offer (the pharmacist's display in Pharmaceutical Society v Boots, 1953; the shop window in Fisher v Bell, 1961). An offer may be revoked before acceptance but not afterwards; a purported acceptance that varies the terms is a counter-offer, which destroys the original offer (Hyde v Wrench, 1840). Acceptance generally must be communicated, though unilateral offers (rewards — the smoke-ball company's in Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co, 1893) can be accepted by performance.

Postal rule and modern communications

Under the postal acceptance rule (Adams v Lindsell, 1818), an acceptance by post is effective on posting, provided post was contemplated. Instantaneous communications (telephone, email in most analyses) follow the receipt rule. Modern contract formation by website clicks and automated systems has been accommodated by statutes on electronic commerce, generally treating the customer's order as the offer and the seller's confirmation or dispatch as acceptance.
