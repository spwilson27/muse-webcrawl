Node of Ranvier

Nodes of Ranvier (also known as myelin-sheath gaps) are small gaps in the myelin sheath insulating an axon, occurring at regular intervals of roughly one micrometre in length between successive Schwann cells (in the peripheral nervous system) or oligodendrocyte processes (in the central nervous system). They are named after the French anatomist Louis-Antoine Ranvier, who described them in 1854.

Function

Because the fatty myelin sheath insulates the axon, action potentials cannot be regenerated along the myelinated internode segments. Instead, the electrical signal travels passively and rapidly beneath the sheath and is regenerated at each node, where voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels are densely concentrated. The action potential thus appears to "jump" from node to node — a process called saltatory conduction (from Latin saltare, to leap) — which greatly increases conduction velocity compared with unmyelinated axons of the same diameter and reduces energy expenditure.

Structure

At the node, the axolemma is exposed to the extracellular fluid. Voltage-gated Nav1.6 sodium channels cluster at high density in the nodal membrane, anchored by proteins such as ankyrin G and neurofascin-186. Flanking the node are the paranodal junctions, where myelin loops attach to the axon via contactin/Caspr complexes, and beyond them the juxtaparanodes, rich in Kv1 potassium channels. This molecular organisation keeps the nodal channels localised and maintains fast conduction.

Clinical significance

Demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis and Guillain–Barré syndrome disrupt nodal and paranodal architecture, slowing or blocking conduction. Autoimmune nodopathies, in which antibodies target nodal proteins such as neurofascin-155 or contactin-1, are now recognised as distinct causes of peripheral neuropathy.
