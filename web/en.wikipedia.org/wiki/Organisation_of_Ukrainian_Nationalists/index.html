Organisation of Ukrainian Nationalists

The Organisation of Ukrainian Nationalists (OUN; Ukrainian: Організація українських націоналістів, Orhanizatsiia ukrainskykh natsionalistiv) was a Ukrainian nationalist political organisation established in 1929 in Vienna, uniting the Ukrainian Military Organisation (UVO) with radical nationalist student groups. It sought an independent, ethnically Ukrainian state, and engaged in assassinations and sabotage against the Polish authorities who ruled western Ukraine in the interwar period.

The OUN's armed wing, the UPA, fought in western Ukraine into the early 1950s.

Interwar period

Led by Yevhen Konovalets until his assassination by an NKVD agent in Rotterdam in 1938, the OUN conducted a campaign of violence against the Polish state, including the 1934 assassination of Polish interior minister Bronisław Pieracki, which brought mass arrests and the Bereza Kartuska detention camp. Dmytro Dontsov's doctrine of integral nationalism strongly influenced its ideology.

Split and the Second World War

After Konovalets's death, leadership passed to Andriy Melnyk, but in 1940 the organisation split into the moderate OUN-M and the radical OUN-B led by Stepan Bandera. Following the German invasion of the USSR, the OUN-B proclaimed Ukrainian independence in Lviv on 30 June 1941; the Germans responded by arresting Bandera and other leaders. In 1942–43 the OUN-B built the Ukrainian Insurgent Army (UPA), which fought both Nazi Germany and the USSR and carried out the ethnic cleansing of Poles in Volhynia and Eastern Galicia in 1943–44, in which tens of thousands of Polish civilians were killed. Historians continue to debate the OUN's collaboration with Nazi Germany, its role in pogroms and the Holocaust, and the character of its nationalism.

Cold War and legacy

The UPA's insurgency against Soviet rule continued into the early 1950s. Bandera was assassinated by a KGB agent in Munich in 1959. In post-1991 Ukraine the OUN and UPA have been officially honoured as independence fighters — a position contested by Polish and Russian narratives — and the legacy of the organisation remains a charged subject in Ukrainian memory politics.
