The East Roman army was the military establishment of the Eastern Roman Empire, formed from the division of the Roman army when the empire was split into eastern and western halves at the end of the fourth century AD. It is conventionally traced from the final partition of the empire in AD 395 until the fall of Constantinople in 1453, after which point it is more usually described as the Byzantine army; the "East Roman" phase proper covers roughly the period from 395 to the crisis of the seventh century.

Origins

The army inherited the late Roman structure created by the reforms of Diocletian and Constantine I: mobile field armies (comitatenses) held in reserve under senior commanders, and frontier troops (limitanei) stationed in fortifications along the Danube and the Persian frontier. In the East there were eventually five field armies — two stationed near Constantinople (in praesenti) and one each in Illyricum, Thrace and Oriens (the eastern provinces).

Organisation

By the sixth century, the period documented by Procopius and the military manual known as the Strategikon, cavalry had become the decisive arm: horse-archers and armoured lancers (kataphraktoi) fought alongside infantry who retained the large shield, spear and sword of the late Roman tradition. Elite palace and expeditionary units included the household cavalry recruited from federate peoples (foederati), the emperor's guards, and the bucellarii — private retinues maintained by great generals such as Belisarius, whose largely cavalry armies reconquered North Africa (533–534) and much of Italy for Justinian I.

Strength

Modern estimates of the eastern army's paper strength in the later fourth century, based on the Notitia Dignitatum, range between about 350,000 and 600,000 men, though effective strengths were certainly lower. Justinian's expeditionary forces were strikingly small — Belisarius invaded Africa with perhaps 15,000–18,000 men — reflecting the empire's simultaneous commitments against Persia and on the Danube.

Transformation

The exhaustion of the last Roman–Persian war (602–628) and the Arab conquests of the 630s–640s stripped the empire of Syria, Egypt and Africa, destroying the old fiscal and recruiting base. In response the surviving forces were settled in military districts — the themata (themes) of Anatolia — supported by grants of land, while professional regiments (the tagmata) guarded the capital. This thematic system defines the middle Byzantine army and marks the end of the late-antique East Roman army in its classical form.
