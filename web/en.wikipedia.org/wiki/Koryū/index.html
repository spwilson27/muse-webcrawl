Koryū

Koryū (Japanese: 古流, "old school" or "old stream") are the traditional schools of Japanese martial arts that originated before the Meiji Restoration (1868), in contrast to the modern gendai budō (such as judo, kendo, karate-do, and aikido) created afterward. The term is used most strictly for schools founded during or before the Edo period whose lineages continue to the present day.

Characteristics

Koryū were originally systems of battlefield training (koryū bujutsu) for the samurai class, often teaching a curriculum of several weapons and grappling: swordsmanship (kenjutsu), drawing the sword (iaijutsu), staff, spear, halberd (naginata), and unarmed combat (jūjutsu). Training centres on pre-arranged paired forms (kata) transmitted within the school, on etiquette and mental discipline, and on documentary transmission — licences and scrolls (densho) issued through a graded system culminating in menkyo kaiden, full transmission.

Schools are headed by a sōke (headmaster) or chief instructor, and membership traditionally involves a formal oath (keppan). Because each ryūha guards its own methods, public information about techniques is often limited by design.

Surviving schools

A few dozen koryū lineages survive in Japan, with others practised worldwide. Among the best known are Tenshin Shōden Katori Shintō-ryū (founded in the 15th century and designated an intangible cultural asset of Chiba Prefecture), Kashima Shin-ryū, Musō Jikiden Eishin-ryū (iai), Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu (root of aikido), and the spear and naginata art of Tendō-ryū. Umbrella bodies such as the Nihon Kobudo Kyokai (founded 1979) hold public demonstrations of the classical schools at venues like the Meiji Shrine in Tokyo.
