Euonymus alatus

Euonymus alatus, known variously as winged spindle, winged euonymus, or burning bush, is a species of flowering plant in the family Celastraceae, native to central and northern China, Japan, and Korea. The common names refer to its two most striking features: the corky, wing-like ridges along its stems (Latin alatus, "winged"), and its brilliant scarlet autumn foliage.

Description

It is a deciduous shrub growing slowly to about 2.5–3 m (8–10 ft) tall and wide, with a dense, flat-topped, horizontally layered habit. The opposite, finely toothed leaves turn an intense fluorescent pink-to-red in autumn. Inconspicuous yellow-green flowers in late spring are followed by small orange-red fruit that split to reveal orange seeds, eaten and dispersed by birds.

Cultivation and invasiveness

Introduced to the West around 1860, burning bush became one of the most popular ornamental shrubs in North America for hedges and foundation planting, tolerant of shade, drought and urban conditions once established. The compact cultivar 'Compactus' is widely planted. However, the species has naturalised aggressively in the forests of eastern North America, where it forms dense thickets that displace native understorey plants; it is now listed as invasive in many US states and its sale is banned in some, including Massachusetts and New Hampshire. Sterile cultivars have been developed in response.

Uses

In traditional Chinese medicine the winged twigs (gui jian yu) have been used for various complaints; the plant contains alkaloids and is considered toxic if ingested by people or livestock.
