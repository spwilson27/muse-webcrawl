Burnout (psychology)

FieldPsychology, occupational health
SymptomsExhaustion, cynicism, reduced professional efficacy
CausesChronic workplace stress
ICD-11QD85 (occupational phenomenon)

Burnout results from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed.

Burnout is a psychological syndrome emerging as a prolonged response to chronic interpersonal and organisational stressors at work. It is conventionally characterised by three dimensions: overwhelming exhaustion, feelings of cynicism and detachment from the job (depersonalisation), and a sense of ineffectiveness and lack of accomplishment. The term was popularised in the 1970s by the psychologist Herbert Freudenberger, who described the state among staff working in free clinics, and was later operationalised by Christina Maslach through the Maslach Burnout Inventory.

Classification

The World Health Organization's ICD-11 classifies burnout as an "occupational phenomenon" rather than a medical condition, defining it as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It is explicitly limited to the occupational context and should not be applied to experiences in other areas of life.

Causes

Research associated with Maslach and Michael Leiter identifies six areas of worklife whose chronic mismatch with the worker predicts burnout: workload, control, reward, community, fairness, and values. Burnout is especially studied in human-service professions such as healthcare, teaching, and social work, where emotional labour is high.

Effects

Burnout is associated with reduced job performance, absenteeism, staff turnover, and adverse health outcomes including cardiovascular disease, insomnia, and depression. Organisational interventions — adjusting workload, increasing autonomy and fairness — are generally considered more effective than purely individual coping strategies, though rest, recovery, and psychological treatment can help affected individuals.
