Litmus

Litmus is a water-soluble mixture of dyes extracted from lichens, especially species of Roccella. It is one of the oldest and most common pH indicators: in acidic solutions it turns red, in alkaline (basic) solutions it turns blue, and it is purple near neutral pH. The colour change occurs roughly over the pH range 4.5–8.3.

History and production

The word "litmus" comes from an Old Norse word meaning "moss used for dyeing". Lichens of the genera Roccella, Lecanora and Ochrolechia are collected (historically around the Mediterranean and in the Netherlands) and processed by fermentation with ammonia and potash, yielding the dye mixture. Litmus was first used as an acid–alkali indicator around 1300 by the physician Arnaldus de Villa Nova.

Litmus paper

Filter paper impregnated with litmus is ubiquitous in school and laboratory chemistry:

  • Blue litmus paper turns red under acidic conditions (pH < ~4.5).
  • Red litmus paper turns blue under alkaline conditions (pH > ~8.3).
  • Neutral litmus paper is purple and turns red with acid, blue with base.

Litmus gives only a qualitative indication of acidity or alkalinity, not a precise pH value; for that, a universal indicator or pH meter is used.

Chemistry

Litmus is a mixture of some ten or more compounds; the principal active component is often called azolitmin, a polymeric substance related to orcein dyes. The colour change results from a weak-acid chromophore whose protonated and deprotonated forms absorb light differently.

Figurative use

The term litmus test has passed into general usage: in politics and debate, a question or criterion that decisively reveals a person's or policy's true character or position.
