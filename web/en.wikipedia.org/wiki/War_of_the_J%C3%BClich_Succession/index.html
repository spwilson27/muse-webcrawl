The War of the Jülich Succession (German: Jülich-Klevischer Erbfolgestreit) was a conflict over the succession to the United Duchies of Jülich-Cleves-Berg in northwestern Germany. It began in March 1609, when Duke John William died childless and mentally ill, leaving one of the richest and most strategically placed territories of the Holy Roman Empire in dispute between Protestant and Catholic claimants. The war is often regarded as a precursor to the Thirty Years' War.

Background

The United Duchies combined Jülich, Cleves, and Berg with the counties of Mark and Ravensberg, straddling the lower Rhine near the border of the Spanish Netherlands. Several princely houses had claims through female lines, but the two strongest were John Sigismund, Elector of Brandenburg, and Wolfgang Wilhelm, Count Palatine of Neuburg, both married to daughters of Duke John William's eldest sister. Emperor Rudolf II claimed the right to decide the succession and attempted to place an imperial commissioner in the duchies.

Course of the conflict

Brandenburg and Neuburg initially agreed, in the Treaty of Dortmund (1609), to rule the duchies jointly as "possessing princes" and to resist imperial interference. They received support from France under Henry IV, the Dutch Republic, and England, while Spain and the Emperor backed Catholic interests. In 1610 imperial and Spanish-backed forces seized the fortress of Jülich, but after the assassination of Henry IV in May 1610 the wider European war many feared did not materialise, and the fortress surrendered to the allied princes that September.

The joint rule broke down when Wolfgang Wilhelm converted to Catholicism and married a Bavarian princess, while John Sigismund converted to Calvinism. Rivalry turned to fighting in 1614: Spanish troops under Ambrogio Spinola seized towns including Wesel, while Dutch forces under Maurice of Nassau occupied other strongpoints.

Treaty of Xanten

The Treaty of Xanten, signed on 12 November 1614, partitioned the inheritance. Brandenburg received Cleves, Mark, and Ravensberg in the north; Neuburg received Jülich and Berg in the south. The division endured in essence until the Napoleonic era and shaped the future Prussian presence on the Rhine, while the presence of Spanish and Dutch garrisons in the duchies helped entangle the region in the Eighty Years' War and later the Thirty Years' War.
