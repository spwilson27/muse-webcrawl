Classification

A hierarchical classification: items are grouped into classes nested within broader classes.

Classification is the activity of assigning objects, concepts or data to categories (classes) according to shared characteristics, and the resulting scheme itself. Classification may be understood as "the process in which ideas and objects are recognized, differentiated, and understood," and in which objects are sorted into groups whose members share similar features.

The word derives from the French term classification, popularised in the 18th and 19th centuries by naturalists who sought to order the living world. Carl Linnaeus's Systema Naturae (1735) established the binomial nomenclature and hierarchical ranks — kingdom, class, order, genus, species — that made "classification" a central scientific practice.

Forms of classification

  • Biological classification (taxonomy) — the classification of organisms into taxa, now largely based on evolutionary relationships (phylogenetics).
  • Library classification — schemes such as the Dewey Decimal Classification and the Library of Congress Classification used to organise collections.
  • Statistical classification — in machine learning and statistics, algorithms (classifiers) that assign observations to categories, such as naive Bayes classifiers, decision trees and support vector machines.
  • Document and data classification — the labelling of documents and information, including for security (e.g. "confidential", "secret").
  • Medical classification — systems such as the ICD for diseases and causes of death.

Properties

A formal classification scheme typically aims for classes that are mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive at each level, arranged in a hierarchy (taxonomy) or, in more flexible systems, a faceted or network structure. Classification differs from mere grouping or clustering in that the categories are defined in advance by explicit criteria, which makes classification a form of supervised categorisation, in contrast to unsupervised cluster analysis.

Evaluation

In statistical classification, performance is measured with confusion matrices and metrics such as accuracy, precision and recall. In knowledge organisation, schemes are judged by their hospitality to new subjects, their consistency, and their usefulness to the communities that use them — raising the point, stressed by scholars such as Geoffrey Bowker and Susan Leigh Star, that classifications are consequential social and technical artefacts, not neutral mirrors of nature.
