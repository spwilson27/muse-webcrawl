Environmental sociology

Environmental sociology is the study of interactions between societies and their natural environments. The field emphasises the social factors that influence environmental resource management and cause environmental problems, the societal processes by which environmental conditions become defined as social problems, and societal responses to those problems.

History

Although classical sociologists such as Marx, Weber and Durkheim touched on society–environment relations, sociology in the mid-20th century generally neglected them — a stance later criticised as the "human exemptionalism paradigm", the assumption that humans are exempt from ecological constraints. Environmental sociology crystallised in the 1970s, following the rise of the environmental movement; the work of Riley Dunlap and William Catton, including their proposed "New Ecological Paradigm", and Allan Schnaiberg's theory of the treadmill of production were foundational.

Themes

  • The social construction of environmental problems
  • Environmental movements and their mobilisation
  • Environmental justice — the unequal distribution of environmental benefits and harms by race, class and geography
  • Risk, hazard, and the "risk society" (associated with Ulrich Beck)
  • Political economy of the environment, including metabolic rift and ecologically unequal exchange
  • Public attitudes, values, and behaviour concerning the environment
