Richard Burbage (c. 1567 – 13 March 1619) was an English actor, theatre manager and painter, the leading tragedian of the company for which William Shakespeare wrote most of his plays. As the star of the Lord Chamberlain's Men (from 1603 the King's Men), Burbage created the title roles in Hamlet, Othello, King Lear, Macbeth (probably), Richard III and many others, as well as leading parts in plays by Ben Jonson, John Webster, and Beaumont and Fletcher. With his brother Cuthbert he was co-owner of the Globe Theatre and the Blackfriars Theatre, and he was one of the "sharers" who published Shakespeare's plays in the First Folio of 1623 (his name heads the list of principal actors).

Life

Burbage was the son of James Burbage, a joiner-turned-actor who built The Theatre in Shoreditch in 1576, England's first permanent playhouse. Richard was acting by his early twenties and was famous by 1590. In 1594 he became a founding member of the Lord Chamberlain's Men alongside Shakespeare. When the lease on The Theatre failed, the company dismantled the building in December 1598 and reused its timbers across the Thames to build the Globe (1599), in which Burbage held a half share with Cuthbert. In 1608 the company also acquired the indoor Blackfriars, giving it summer and winter playhouses.

Reputation

Contemporaries regarded Burbage as the foremost actor of the age. An elegy on his death (1619) lists his signature roles—Hamlet, Othello, Lear, Richard III, Henry V, Brutus and others—and famously grieves that "he's gone, and with him what a world are dead." A short poem attributed to the period imagines audiences weeping at his performances. Records also show he was a competent painter, and a portrait long called the Chandos portrait of Shakespeare has sometimes been attributed to his hand.

Death and legacy

Burbage died in March 1619 and was buried at St Leonard's Church, Shoreditch. His death occasioned an outpouring of tributes said to eclipse even the response to Shakespeare's death three years earlier. With Edward Alleyn of the rival Admiral's Men, he defines the first great age of English professional acting, and Shakespeare's longest, most demanding tragic roles were shaped to his talents.
