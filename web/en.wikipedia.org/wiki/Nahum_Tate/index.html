Nahum Tate (1652 – 30 July 1715) was an Anglo-Irish poet, hymnist and lyricist, who became Poet Laureate of England in 1692. He is best known today for his adaptation of Shakespeare's King Lear with a happy ending, and for writing the libretto of Henry Purcell's opera Dido and Aeneas.

Life

Tate was born in Dublin, the son of Faithful Teate, a Puritan clergyman. The family name was changed from Teate to Tate. He graduated from Trinity College Dublin in 1672 and moved to London, where he published a volume of poems in 1677 and began writing for the stage.

Works

Tate produced numerous adaptations of earlier plays, often reshaping them to contemporary taste and political caution. His The History of King Lear (1681) omitted the Fool, gave the play a happy ending in which Lear is restored and Cordelia marries Edgar; this version held the English stage for over 150 years, until William Macready restored Shakespeare's tragic ending in the 1830s. He also adapted Richard II (as The Sicilian Usurper), Coriolanus, and plays by Fletcher and others.

With Nicholas Brady, Tate published A New Version of the Psalms of David (1696), which long remained in use in Anglican worship; some of its settings, such as "While shepherds watched their flocks", remain familiar Christmas carols. He wrote the words to Purcell's Dido and Aeneas (c. 1688), including Dido's lament "When I am laid in earth". His annual birthday odes and New Year odes as Poet Laureate, to which he succeeded Thomas Shadwell in 1692, drew some ridicule from satirists but secured his reputation in his lifetime.

Death

Tate died in 1715 within the precincts of the Mint, Southwark, where he had taken refuge from his creditors, and was buried at St George's, Southwark.
