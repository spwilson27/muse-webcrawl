Diedc. 1221, Nishapur

Abū Ḥāmid bin Abū Bakr Ibrāhīm (c. 1145 – c. 1221), better known by his pen names Farīd ud-Dīn and ʿAṭṭār of Nishapur, was a Persian Muslim poet, theoretician of Sufism, and hagiographer from Nishapur who had an immense and lasting influence on Persian poetry and Sufism. His name ʿAṭṭār means "apothecary" or "perfumer," the trade he practiced in his native city.

Life

Details of Attar's life are sparse and heavily overlaid with legend. He was born in Nishapur in northeastern Iran, inherited his father's pharmacy, and traveled in his youth. According to tradition he was converted to the Sufi path by an encounter with a wandering dervish. He is said to have died during the Mongol sack of Nishapur around 1221. His tomb in Nishapur is a major pilgrimage site. He is counted, with Sanai and Rumi — who acknowledged Attar as a master and whose father is said to have received a copy of Attar's work — among the greatest poets of Sufi literature.

Works

Attar's most famous work is the Manṭiq-uṭ-Ṭayr (The Conference of the Birds, c. 1177), a long allegorical poem in which the birds of the world, led by the hoopoe, journey through seven valleys in search of their king, the Simurgh; of the thousands who set out, only thirty birds survive to discover that they themselves are the Simurgh (in Persian, sī morgh, "thirty birds").

His prose Tazkirat al-Awliyā (Memorial of the Saints) is a celebrated collection of biographies and sayings of early Sufi saints. Other works attributed to him include the Ilāhī-nāma (Book of God), the Muṣībat-nāma (Book of Affliction), the Asrār-nāma (Book of Mysteries), and a divan of lyric poems; many works circulating under his name are considered spurious.

Legacy

Attar's poetry shaped the Sufi literary tradition in Persian, Turkish, and Urdu. The Conference of the Birds has been translated into many languages and adapted for the stage, notably by Peter Brook in 1979.
