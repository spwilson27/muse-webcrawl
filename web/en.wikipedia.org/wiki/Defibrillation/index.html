Defibrillation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A paramedic applying defibrillator paddles to a patient's chest in an ambulance]
A paramedic applying defibrillator paddles to a patient's chest in an ambulance

Defibrillation is a treatment for life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias — specifically Ventricular fibrillation and pulseless Ventricular tachycardia — in which an electrical current is passed through the heart to depolarise the myocardium and allow the natural pacemaker to restore an organised rhythm. It is delivered by manual defibrillators, automated external defibrillators (AEDs), or implanted devices (ICDs).

Principle and practice

In fibrillation the heart quivers chaotically and pumps no blood; a defibrillatory shock (typically 120–200 joules biphasic in adults) simultaneously depolarises a critical mass of heart muscle, after which the sinoatrial node may resume control. Success falls roughly 7–10% per minute without CPR, which is why guidelines demand shock within minutes and immediate resumption of compressions (Cardiopulmonary resuscitation) after each attempt.

History

Experiments on animals date to 1899 (Prévost and Battelli); Claude Beck achieved the first successful internal defibrillation in a human in 1947, and Paul Zoll external defibrillation in 1956. Portable and then automated devices followed — the AED's algorithms now let laypeople deliver shocks safely, and public-access defibrillation programmes are a cornerstone of cardiac-arrest survival.

Related devices

ICDs, implanted since 1980, monitor rhythm continuously and shock from within; wearable defibrillator vests bridge high-risk periods. Defibrillation does not restart a heart in asystole ('flatline') — a common film error — which is treated with CPR and adrenaline instead.
