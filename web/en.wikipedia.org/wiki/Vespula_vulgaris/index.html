Vespula vulgaris

Common nameCommon wasp
OrderHymenoptera
FamilyVespidae
Binomial nameVespula vulgaris (Linnaeus, 1758)

Vespula vulgaris, known as the common wasp, is a species of wasp found in various regions, including parts of Europe and Asia, and has been introduced into Australia and New Zealand, where it is an invasive pest. It is a eusocial vespid that builds papery nests, most often underground in disused mammal burrows, but also in wall cavities and hollow trees.

Description

Workers are 12–17 mm long with the black-and-yellow banded abdomen typical of yellowjackets; the pattern of black anchor-shaped markings on the abdominal segments helps distinguish it from the German wasp, Vespula germanica. Queens are larger, up to about 20 mm. Like other social wasps, females can sting repeatedly.

Life cycle

A single fertilised queen emerges from hibernation in spring and founds the nest, rearing the first workers herself. The colony expands through the summer and may contain 5,000–10,000 workers at its peak. Workers hunt insects to feed the larvae and gather sugary foods; in late summer they increasingly scavenge around human food. New queens and males are produced in autumn; the old queen and workers die as cold weather arrives.

As an invasive species

In New Zealand, V. vulgaris reaches exceptionally high densities in South Island beech forests, where it competes with native birds and insects for honeydew and prey, and it is regarded as a significant pest.
