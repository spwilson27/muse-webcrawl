Moose

The moose (North America) or elk (Eurasia), Alces alces, is the largest species in the deer family (Cervidae) and the tallest, longest, and heaviest. It inhabits circumpolar boreal forests and temperate broadleaf and mixed forests of the Northern Hemisphere, from Scandinavia and Russia across Canada to Alaska and New England.

Description

Bulls stand up to about 2.1 metres at the shoulder and can weigh more than 700 kg. Males are distinguished by broad, palmate ("open-hand shaped") antlers, shed each winter, and by a flap of skin called a bell hanging from the throat. Moose have long legs for wading through snow and wetlands, a drooping, flexible upper lip for stripping leaves, and a short tail. Their dark coat consists of hollow guard hairs that insulate against cold.

Ecology and behaviour

Moose are solitary browsers, feeding on leaves, bark, twigs, and aquatic vegetation; in summer they wade into lakes and ponds, sometimes diving for plants. The autumn rut brings bulls into competition for cows. Calves are born in spring. Predators include wolves, bears, and orcas (which occasionally take swimming moose). Moose are generally not aggressive but can be dangerous if provoked, especially cows with calves; collisions with vehicles are a serious hazard in moose country because of the animals' great height and mass.
