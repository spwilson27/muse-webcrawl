Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Acronym: ERISA

Enacted: 2 September 1974

Signed by: President Gerald Ford

Codified: 29 U.S.C. ch. 18

The Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA) is a United States federal law that sets minimum standards for most voluntarily established retirement and health plans in private industry to provide protection for individuals in these plans.

Provisions

ERISA requires plans to provide participants with information about plan features and funding, sets minimum standards for participation, vesting, benefit accrual and funding, requires accountability of plan fiduciaries, and gives participants the right to sue for benefits and breaches of fiduciary duty. It established the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC) to insure defined-benefit pension plans. ERISA does not require employers to offer plans, nor does it set benefit amounts; plans of governments and churches are generally exempt.

History

The law was prompted by well-publicised pension failures, most famously the 1963 shutdown of the Studebaker-Packard plant, whose underfunded pension left many workers without promised benefits. Signed by President Gerald Ford on Labor Day 1974, it has since been amended many times, notably by COBRA (continuation of health coverage), HIPAA (health coverage portability) and the Pension Protection Act of 2006.

Preemption and criticism

ERISA's broad preemption of state laws relating to employee benefit plans has generated extensive litigation, including Supreme Court cases on whether state insurance and healthcare laws survive. Critics argue preemption leaves some claimants — particularly those denied health benefits — without adequate remedies, while supporters say uniform national rules are essential for multi-state employers.
