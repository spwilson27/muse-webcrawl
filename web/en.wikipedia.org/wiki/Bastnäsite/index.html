Bastnäsite (also spelled bastnaesite or bastnasite) is a group of three carbonate-fluoride minerals rich in rare-earth elements: bastnäsite-(Ce), bastnäsite-(La) and bastnäsite-(Y). Together with monazite, bastnäsite is one of the two principal ores of the rare-earth elements, and it is the dominant ore mineral in several of the world's largest rare-earth deposits.

History

The mineral was first described in 1838 from the Bastnäs mine near Riddarhyttan in Västmanland, Sweden, from which it takes its name. The Bastnäs deposit, worked for copper and iron, was a prolific source of new minerals in the nineteenth century, and it was here that cerium was first identified in cerite.

Chemistry and structure

Bastnäsite has the general formula (REE)CO3F, where REE stands for a rare-earth element. The three recognised species are distinguished by the dominant rare earth: cerium in bastnäsite-(Ce), lanthanum in bastnäsite-(La), and yttrium in bastnäsite-(Y). Bastnäsite-(Ce) is by far the most common. The mineral crystallises in the hexagonal system, forming tabular to equant crystals, though massive granular material is more typical in ore deposits.

Occurrence

Bastnäsite occurs chiefly in carbonatites and related alkaline rocks. Major deposits include the Mountain Pass mine in California, United States — for many years the world's largest rare-earth mine — and the giant Bayan Obo deposit in Inner Mongolia, China, where bastnäsite occurs together with monazite. Other notable localities include the Karonge (Gakara) deposit in Burundi, Kangankunde in Malawi, and Wigu Hill in Tanzania.

Processing

Because bastnäsite is a fluorocarbonate, it can be processed by roasting with sulphuric acid or by hydrochloric acid leaching after an oxidative roast. The fluorine content complicates processing and waste management. Individual rare-earth elements are separated from the mixed concentrate by solvent extraction.
