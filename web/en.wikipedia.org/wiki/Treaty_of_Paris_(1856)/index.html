Treaty of Paris (1856)

The Treaty of Paris of 1856 brought an end to the Crimean War between the Russian Empire and an alliance of the Ottoman Empire, the United Kingdom, France, and the Kingdom of Sardinia. Signed on 30 March 1856 in Paris, it made the Black Sea neutral territory, closed it to all warships, and forbade fortifications and armaments on its shores — a severe check on Russian influence in the region.

Terms

Russia returned Southern Bessarabia to Moldavia and the city of Kars to the Ottomans, and agreed to the demilitarization of the Åland Islands. The treaty guaranteed the independence and territorial integrity of the Ottoman Empire and admitted it to the European Concert. The Danubian Principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia remained under nominal Ottoman suzerainty but gained practical autonomy under the guarantee of the great powers; free navigation of the Danube was assured under an international commission. Christians within the Ottoman Empire were promised protection, though the signatories disclaimed any right of intervention.

Significance and aftermath

The settlement marked the high point of the post-1815 concert diplomacy and was accompanied by the Paris Declaration Respecting Maritime Law. Its Black Sea clauses humiliated Russia; during the Franco-Prussian War in 1870, Prince Gorchakov unilaterally repudiated them, and the Treaty of London (1871) accepted the change, though reaffirming that treaties could not be altered unilaterally.
