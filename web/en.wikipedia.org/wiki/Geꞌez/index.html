Geꞌez (ግእዝ, Gəʾəz) is an ancient South Semitic language that originated in the region of present-day Ethiopia and Eritrea. It was the official language of the Kingdom of Aksum and the later Ethiopian Empire, and although it ceased to be spoken as a vernacular probably by the early medieval period, it survives as the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church, the Ethiopian Catholic Church, and the Beta Israel Jewish community. A rich body of literature — scripture translations, royal chronicles, hagiographies, and theological works — was composed in Geꞌez, and it remains the classical language in which traditional Ethiopian scholarship and church poetry (qene) are cultivated.

History

The earliest inscriptions in Geꞌez date from the pre-Aksumite period, written in the South Arabian musnad script. By the reign of Ezana of Aksum in the 4th century CE, inscriptions were being written in the unvocalized Geꞌez script, and the king's conversion to Christianity was accompanied by the translation of the Bible into Geꞌez — one of the earliest and most complete early Bible translations, of enduring importance for preserving texts such as the Book of Enoch in full.

After the decline of Aksum, Geꞌez gradually gave way in speech to regional languages such as Amharic, Tigrinya, and Tigre — all of which descend from, or were deeply influenced by, forms of Ethiopic speech — but it remained the sole language of writing in Ethiopia for centuries. Under the Zagwe and restored Solomonic dynasties a flourishing literature developed, including the Kebra Nagast ("Glory of Kings"), royal chronicles, and the massive hagiographic collection Gadla Samaitat.

Grammar

Geꞌez is a typical Semitic language: its morphology is built on consonantal roots, most of them triconsonantal, fitted into vowel patterns to form words. It distinguishes two genders (masculine and feminine), two numbers with a sound and a "broken" plural system, and a rich verbal system of stems (basic, causative, passive/reflexive, and others) conjugated for person, gender, and number in perfect and imperfect aspects. Nouns mark the accusative with the suffix -a, and possession with pronominal suffixes. Word order in classical prose is usually verb–subject–object.

Its phonology originally included emphatic consonants and pharyngeal sounds typical of Semitic, though several consonants merged over time; in modern liturgical pronunciation the reading traditions of different schools preserve the distinctions to varying degrees.

Literature and legacy

Geꞌez literature spans more than a millennium: biblical and apocryphal translations, patristic works translated from Greek and Arabic, lives of saints, royal chronicles, legal codes such as the Fetha Nagast, treatises on computus and astronomy, and magical and medical texts. The script devised for Geꞌez — an abugida in which each consonant sign is modified for seven vowel orders — was later adapted to write Amharic, Tigrinya, Tigre, and other Ethiopian languages, and remains in daily use by over one hundred million people.
