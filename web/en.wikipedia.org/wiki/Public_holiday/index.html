A public holiday is a day established by law or custom on which normal working activities are suspended: government offices, schools and most businesses close, and employees are generally entitled to the day off with pay or to premium pay if they work. Public holidays mark events and values a society chooses to honour collectively—national independence and founding, religious feasts, the labour movement, remembrance of war dead, and seasonal festivals such as New Year. The number and identity of public holidays vary greatly by country, from around eleven federal holidays in the United States to more than twenty public holidays in parts of India (where national, state and religious calendars overlap) and a similarly large tally in Sri Lanka and Nepal; Cambodia and Iran have often topped comparative counts. Countries also differ in whether a holiday falling on a weekend is "moved" (substituted) to a weekday, as with British bank holidays, or simply lost, and in whether private employers are obliged to observe them.

Types

  • National days — independence days (4 July in the United States; 15 August in India; 14 July Bastille Day in France), unification and republic days, monarchs' official birthdays, and constitution days.
  • Religious holidays — Christmas and Easter Monday in historically Christian countries; Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha in Muslim-majority states and as gazetted holidays elsewhere; Diwali, Vesak and Thaipusam in South and Southeast Asia; Jewish holidays in Israel. Secular states vary in how many religious days they retain.
  • Labour holidays — International Workers' Day (1 May) across much of the world; Labor Day (first Monday of September) in the United States and Canada.
  • Remembrance days — Armistice/Remembrance Day (11 November) in Commonwealth countries; Memorial Day and Veterans Day in the United States; ANZAC Day (25 April) in Australia and New Zealand.
  • Seasonal and traditional festivals — New Year (1 January, and Lunar New Year across East and Southeast Asia), midsummer in Scandinavia, harvest festivals, and Japan's Golden Week cluster of holidays in late April and early May.
  • Civic and regional days — state, provincial and city foundation days, saints' days (St Patrick's Day in Ireland and Northern Ireland; St Andrew's Day in Scotland), and election days in some jurisdictions.

Legal status and substitution

The legal machinery differs by country. In the United Kingdom, bank holidays are declared under the Banking and Financial Dealings Act 1971 and by royal proclamation (for special occasions such as jubilees and royal funerals); there is no statutory right to time off or extra pay, which depends on contract. In the United States, the eleven federal holidays bind federal institutions; states and private employers largely decide for themselves, though most follow the federal calendar. Many countries shift holidays adjacent to weekends to create long weekends—Argentina's feriados puente and Japan's "Happy Monday" system are deliberate examples—and China's State Council famously rearranges working weekends around the Spring Festival and National Day "golden weeks" to concentrate leisure for domestic tourism. Some holidays are movable feasts tied to lunar or liturgical calendars (Easter, Eid, Passover), complicating calendars years ahead.

History

Public holidays descend from the religious festivals and saints' days of pre-modern calendars—medieval England observed dozens of holy days ("holidays" in the literal sense)—pruned at the Reformation and again by industrial discipline, which preferred predictable working weeks; Britain's Bank Holidays Act 1871 (championed by Sir John Lubbock, and long nicknamed "St Lubbock's Days") reduced and standardised the list while giving bank holidays their name. The modern additions reflect the nation-state and the labour movement: national days spread with independence movements in the 19th and 20th centuries, and May Day was adopted as workers won recognition after 1889. States continue to create and abolish holidays as political acts—Juneteenth became a U.S. federal holiday in 2021; several post-Soviet and post-colonial states replaced imperial or communist anniversaries with new national days—while tourism and retail interests lobby both for more long weekends and against closures.

Effects

Economists debate the net effect of public holidays: closures cost output in some sectors (estimates of roughly 0.1–0.2 percent of annual GDP per holiday are commonly cited for the UK) but stimulate tourism, hospitality and retail, coordinate leisure so families and friends can meet (a coordination benefit that scattered individual leave cannot replicate), and may improve wellbeing and productivity. Public holidays also structure the civic year: parades, ceremonies, sales, sporting fixtures (Boxing Day football, Thanksgiving NFL games) and travel peaks are all anchored to them.
