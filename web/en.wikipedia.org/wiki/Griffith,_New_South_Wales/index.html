Griffith is a city in the Riverina region of south-western New South Wales, Australia, about 570 km west of Sydney. It was founded in 1916 as the administrative and commercial centre of the Murrumbidgee Irrigation Area and named after Arthur Frederick Griffith, the New South Wales minister for public works who championed the irrigation scheme. The city's street plan, like that of nearby Leeton, was designed by Walter Burley Griffin, the architect of Canberra.

History

Griffith grew with the soldier-settler and migrant farms of the irrigation area. After both world wars, large numbers of Italian migrants settled in the district, transforming its agriculture and culture; Italian-Australians remain a defining part of the city's identity, celebrated in its food, wine and festivals. The city gained national notoriety in 1977 with the disappearance of anti-drugs campaigner Donald Mackay, a crime widely attributed to organised crime figures involved in the local marijuana trade.

Economy

Griffith is one of Australia's most productive agricultural centres. The surrounding irrigation farms produce wine grapes — the Riverina is among the country's largest wine regions, home to major producers — as well as citrus, rice, wheat, cotton, nuts and poultry. Large food and wine processing businesses anchor the local economy, supported by road and rail links to Sydney, Melbourne and Adelaide.

Culture

The city has a regional art gallery, theatre and museum, including the Griffith Pioneer Park Museum. Annual events include the Festival of Gardens and food and wine celebrations reflecting the area's Italian heritage. Griffith is also known for its wide main street, Banna Avenue, and its collection of buildings reflecting its planned origins.
