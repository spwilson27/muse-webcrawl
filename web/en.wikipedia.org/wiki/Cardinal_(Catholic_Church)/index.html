A cardinal (Latin: Sanctae Romanae Ecclesiae cardinalis, "cardinal of the Holy Roman Church") is a senior member of the clergy of the Catholic Church. Cardinals are created by the pope in consistory and are known as "princes of the Church."

Duties

Collectively the cardinals form the College of Cardinals. Their most important duty is to elect a new pope in a conclave when the Holy See is vacant. Individually they advise the pope, head Vatican dicasteries (curial cardinals), or serve as archbishops of major sees worldwide.

Orders

Since the reforms of John XXIII and Paul VI, cardinals are divided into three orders: cardinal bishops (including the Eastern Catholic patriarchs and the suburbicarian bishops), cardinal priests (the bulk of the college, mostly diocesan archbishops), and cardinal deacons (mostly curial officials). Since 1962 all cardinals have normally been ordained bishops.

Vesture and privileges

Cardinals wear scarlet — symbolizing willingness to shed blood for the faith — including a zucchetto, biretta, mozzetta, and a ring presented by the pope. They enjoy the privilege of a personal coat of arms with a red galero (wide-brimmed hat) with fifteen tassels on each side.

History

The title derives from the Latin cardo (hinge), originally denoting clergy incardinated in Rome's principal churches. In 1059 Pope Nicholas II reserved papal election to the cardinal bishops, extended to the whole college in 1179. Paul VI limited electors to those under 80 years of age and capped their number at 120.
