Adam and Eve

Adam and Eve, according to the creation myth of the Abrahamic religions, were the first man and first woman. They are central figures in the Book of Genesis, chapters 1–5, and their story — creation in the Garden of Eden, the temptation by the serpent, the eating of the forbidden fruit, and the expulsion known as the Fall of man — has shaped Jewish, Christian and Islamic theology, as well as Western art and literature.

The Genesis narrative

Genesis 1 describes God creating humankind, male and female, in his image. Genesis 2 gives a second account: God forms Adam (from Hebrew adamah, "ground") from dust and breathes life into him, places him in Eden, and fashions Eve from Adam's rib as his companion. Forbidden to eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, the pair are persuaded by the serpent that the fruit will make them like God. They eat, become aware of their nakedness, and are expelled from Eden, guarded by cherubim, to a life of labour, pain in childbirth and mortality. Their sons include Cain, Abel and Seth.

In Judaism, Christianity and Islam

In Judaism, Adam (אָדָם) and Eve (חַוָּה, Chavah) are the ancestors of all humanity; the Fall is not framed as inherited guilt in the Christian sense. Christianity developed the doctrine of original sin, in which Adam's disobedience corrupts human nature, contrasted with Christ as the "second Adam"; Eve is correspondingly paired with Mary. In Islam, Adam (Ādam) is the first prophet, and he and Hawwa (Eve) are tempted by Satan, repent, and are forgiven; there is no doctrine of original sin.

In art and culture

Adam and Eve are among the most depicted figures in Western art, including famous treatments by Masaccio, Michelangelo (on the Sistine Chapel ceiling), Dürer, Cranach and Rubens, and in literature from Milton's Paradise Lost to Mark Twain's The Diaries of Adam and Eve.
