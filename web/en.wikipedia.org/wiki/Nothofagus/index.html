Nothofagus

Nothofagus, also known as the southern beeches, is a genus of about 40 species of trees and shrubs native to the temperate and tropical highlands of the Southern Hemisphere — southern South America, Australia, New Zealand, New Guinea and New Caledonia. It is the sole genus of the family Nothofagaceae. The name derives from Greek words meaning "false beech", because the trees were once classified with the northern beeches (Fagus).

Distribution and significance

The disjunct distribution of Nothofagus across southern continents separated by oceans made it a classic example in biogeography: its pattern, together with fossils found in Antarctica, is strong evidence that the genus originated on the supercontinent Gondwana and spread before the continents drifted apart. Fossil pollen (known as Nothofagidites) is important in Southern Hemisphere palaeobotany.

Description and ecology

Southern beeches include both evergreen and deciduous species. They often dominate cool temperate rainforests — for example, the Valdivian forests of Chile and Argentina, the beech forests of New Zealand (where species include red beech, silver beech and black beech), and Tasmanian forests with myrtle beech (Nothofagus cunninghamii). Many species are important timber trees. Periodically, forests produce heavy seed crops ("masting"), which trigger population explosions of rodents and their predators such as stoats in New Zealand, with serious conservation consequences for native birds.
