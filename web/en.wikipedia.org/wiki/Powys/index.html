Powys (Welsh: [ˈpowɨs]) is a county and preserved county in Wales, in the United Kingdom. It is the largest county in Wales by area — covering roughly a quarter of the country — and the most sparsely populated, with a population of around 133,000. Created in 1974 from the historic counties of Montgomeryshire, Radnorshire, and most of Brecknockshire, it takes its name from the medieval Welsh Kingdom of Powys. Its administrative headquarters are at Llandrindod Wells, and its main towns include Welshpool, Newtown, Brecon, Builth Wells, Machynlleth, and Ystradgynlais.

Geography

Powys is a largely mountainous and rural county encompassing the Cambrian Mountains, the Brecon Beacons (Bannau Brycheiniog) in the south, and the headwaters of the rivers Severn, Wye, and Usk. Agriculture, especially sheep farming, and tourism dominate the economy. The county contains the Elan Valley reservoirs that supply Birmingham, much of the Brecon Beacons National Park, and long-distance trails including Offa's Dyke Path along the English border and Glyndŵr's Way.

History and culture

The medieval kingdom of Powys occupied much of east-central Wales and resisted both English and rival Welsh powers; its rulers included the princes of the House of Mathrafal. The county retains a notable Welsh-language presence, strongest in the northwest around Machynlleth, the ancient seat of Owain Glyndŵr's parliament of 1404. The Royal Welsh Show, Britain's largest agricultural show, is held each July at Llanelwedd near Builth Wells, and the Hay Festival of literature takes place in the Powys border town of Hay-on-Wye.
