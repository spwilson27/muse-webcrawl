Town privileges

Town privileges or borough rights were important concepts in medieval and early modern Europe, referring to the set of legal rights and privileges granted to a town or borough by a monarch, feudal lord, or other sovereign authority through a charter. Such privileges distinguished towns from villages and rural settlements and formed the legal foundation of urban self-government.

Contents of town privileges

The specific privileges varied by place and period, but commonly included:

  • Market rights — the right to hold regular markets and fairs, often with a monopoly on trade within a surrounding area.
  • Self-government — the right of burghers to elect a mayor and town council and to administer local affairs.
  • An own court — the right to hold a town court applying town law (ius civitatis) to citizens, rather than being subject solely to the lord's court.
  • Defensive rights — permission to build town walls and fortifications.
  • Tolls and minting — in some cases, rights to levy tolls or mint coins.
  • Personal freedoms — personal freedom for burghers; under the principle Stadtluft macht frei ("town air makes one free"), a serf who lived in a town for a year and a day could become free.

Grant and development

Privileges were typically granted by charter when a settlement was formally founded or elevated to town status. Examples of influential bodies of town law include Magdeburg law and Lübeck law in northern and eastern Europe, which were copied by many newly founded towns. Lords granted privileges to attract settlers, merchants, and craftsmen, thereby increasing the economic value of their lands. Over time, many privileges were curtailed or abolished as states centralised administration in the 19th century, though town charters and municipal rights remain their modern legacy.
