Sheila Greibach

Born: 1939
Alma mater: Radcliffe College; Harvard University
Known for: Greibach normal form
Fields: Theoretical computer science

Sheila Adele Greibach (born 1939) is an American computer scientist known for foundational contributions to formal language theory, automata theory and computational complexity. She is a professor emerita of computer science at the University of California, Los Angeles.

Career and contributions

Greibach earned her bachelor's degree in linguistics and applied mathematics from Radcliffe College in 1960 and her PhD from Harvard University in 1963, with a dissertation on formal languages written under Anthony Oettinger; she was among the first women to receive a doctorate in computer science in the United States. After appointments at Harvard, Purdue and Penn State, she joined UCLA in 1970.

Her best-known result is the Greibach normal form (1965): every context-free grammar can be transformed into an equivalent grammar whose productions all begin with a terminal symbol (of the form A → aα, where α is a string of nonterminals) — a normal form especially useful in parsing and automata constructions. She also proved the undecidability of equivalence and related problems for deterministic pushdown automata, and in 1973 proved the "hardest context-free language" theorem: there exists a fixed context-free language to which every context-free language can be reduced by inverse homomorphism, so that deciding membership in it is as hard as for any CFL. With Seymour Ginsburg and Michael Harrison she developed the AFL (abstract family of languages) theory.

Recognition

Greibach's work, alongside that of contemporaries such as Seymour Ginsburg, helped establish formal language theory as a core discipline of computer science. Her 1978 textbook Theory of Program Structures was widely used.
