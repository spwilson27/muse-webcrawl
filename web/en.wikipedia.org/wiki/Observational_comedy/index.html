Observational comedy

Observational comedy is a form of humour based on making comic remarks about the ordinary aspects of everyday life. It is one of the dominant styles of modern stand-up comedy. Observational routines build jokes from shared, mundane experiences — air travel, dating, shopping, technology, family habits — inviting the audience to recognise their own lives in the material. The stereotypical set-up is the question "Did you ever notice...?" or "What's the deal with...?", the latter inseparably associated with Jerry Seinfeld, whose act and sitcom Seinfeld made the style ubiquitous in the 1980s and 1990s.

Characteristics and practitioners

Rather than inventing absurd fictional premises, the observational comic acts as a commentator on common behaviour, exaggerating small frustrations and unspoken social rules. The humour depends on precision of detail and the audience's sense of "that's so true". Prominent practitioners include Seinfeld, Ellen DeGeneres, Billy Connolly, Eddie Izzard, Michael McIntyre, and many of the comics who filled the comedy-club boom of the 1980s; earlier roots can be found in the routines of Robert Klein, David Brenner, and George Carlin's wordplay about everyday things.

Criticism

Because the form is so closely associated with the comedy boom, "observational" is sometimes used pejoratively by critics to describe safe, formulaic, or hack material — a stereotype parodied by comics and sitcoms that mimic the "What's the deal with airline food?" formula. Defenders note that the style's apparent simplicity conceals demanding craftsmanship in wording and structure.
