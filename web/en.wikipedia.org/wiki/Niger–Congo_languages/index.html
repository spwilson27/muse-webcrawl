Niger–Congo is a major language family of Africa, with roughly 1,500–1,600 languages and over 700 million speakers. Major branches include Atlantic, Mande, Gur, Kwa, Benue–Congo (including the Bantu languages), and Adamawa–Ubangi.

Niger–Congo languages

The Niger–Congo languages constitute one of the world's largest language families and the principal family of sub-Saharan Africa in terms of numbers of languages and speakers. By most counts it includes some 1,500 or more distinct languages — about a fifth of all languages on Earth — spoken by more than 700 million people. It was established as a family by Joseph Greenberg in his classification of African languages (1949–1963).

Characteristics

Many Niger–Congo languages share a system of noun classes — prefixes marking singular/plural pairings and triggering agreement throughout the sentence — and many are tonal. Characteristic word order is subject–verb–object. The family's internal classification remains debated; branches commonly recognised include Atlantic, Mande, Dogon, Ijoid, Gur, Kru, Kwa, Benue–Congo, Adamawa and Ubangian, though some linguists question whether Mande and certain other branches belong to the family at all, and whether "Niger–Congo" in its widest form is a valid genetic unit rather than a convenience grouping.

Bantu

The largest and best-known subgroup is Bantu, within Benue–Congo, whose roughly 500 languages — including Swahili, Zulu, Xhosa, Shona and Kikongo — spread from a homeland near the Nigeria–Cameroon border across central, eastern and southern Africa in the Bantu expansion of the last few thousand years. Other major Niger–Congo languages by speakers include Yoruba, Igbo, Fula and Akan.

Note on the name

The family takes its name from the Niger and Congo rivers, the two great river systems of the region where most of its languages are spoken. It is also frequently written with a hyphen, "Niger-Congo", a variant title used by some reference works.
