Pan-Slavism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pan-Slavism is a political and cultural movement that emerged in the nineteenth century advocating the unity and solidarity of the Slavic peoples — Russians, Poles, Czechs, Slovaks, Serbs, Croats, Slovenes, Bulgarians, Ukrainians, Belarusians, and others — on the basis of their shared linguistic and cultural heritage.

Origins

The idea drew on the philological work of scholars such as Josef Dobrovský and Ján Kollár, whose 1824 poem Slávy dcera ("Slava's Daughter") celebrated Slavic unity, and the writings of Ľudovít Štúr. The movement's first political manifestation was the Prague Slav Congress of June 1848, convened amid the Revolutions of 1848, which called for equality of Slavs within the Austrian Empire. Pavel Jozef Šafárik and František Palacký were among its leading figures; Palacký favoured "Austro-Slavism" — reform of the Habsburg monarchy rather than union with Russia.

Russian Pan-Slavism

In the Russian Empire, writers such as Mikhail Pogodin and later Nikolai Danilevsky recast Pan-Slavism as a doctrine of Russian leadership over all Slavs, closely linked to Orthodox Christianity and suspicion of the Catholic West — sentiments sharpened by Polish uprisings against Russian rule, which starkly divided the movement. The Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78, fought partly under the banner of liberating Balkan Slavs, marked the high point of popular Pan-Slavic enthusiasm in Russia.

Later history

Pan-Slavic sentiment influenced Balkan politics before the First World War, the creation of Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia (both unions of Slavic peoples), and Slav committees sponsored by the Soviet Union during the Second World War. After 1945 the ideology was largely subsumed into communist internationalism. The pan-Slavic colours — white, blue, and red, adopted at Prague in 1848 — still appear in the flags of Russia, Serbia, Croatia, Slovenia, Slovakia, and the Czech Republic.

Criticism

Critics, including Karl Marx and many Polish and Habsburg liberals, viewed Pan-Slavism as a vehicle for Russian imperial expansion. The movement also struggled with deep internal divisions of religion (Orthodox, Catholic, Muslim), alphabet (Cyrillic and Latin), and competing national programmes.
