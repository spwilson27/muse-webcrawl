Cyclooxygenase


COX-1 enzyme structure (placeholder image)

Abbreviation: COX
Also called: Prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase (PTGS)

Cyclooxygenase (COX), officially known as prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase, is an enzyme family responsible for the formation of prostaglandins, thromboxane and prostacyclin from arachidonic acid. It is the principal target of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).

Isoforms and function

The two main isoforms are COX-1, expressed constitutively in most tissues, where its products protect the gastric mucosa and support platelet and kidney function, and COX-2, largely induced at sites of inflammation. A third variant, COX-3, is a splice variant of COX-1 found mainly in the central nervous system. The enzyme is bifunctional, with both cyclooxygenase activity (forming PGG2) and peroxidase activity (reducing it to PGH2).

Pharmacology

Aspirin irreversibly acetylates COX; most other NSAIDs inhibit it reversibly. Selective COX-2 inhibitors ("coxibs" such as celecoxib) were developed to reduce gastrointestinal side effects, though some were withdrawn over cardiovascular risks. The elucidation of this pathway earned John Vane a share of the 1982 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
