Haute couture (French for "high dressmaking") is the creation of exclusive, custom-fitted clothing made by hand from start to finish, constructed from high-quality, often unusual fabric and sewn with extreme attention to detail by experienced artisans. In France the term is legally protected: only fashion houses meeting the criteria of the Chambre Syndicale de la Haute Couture — including maintaining a Paris atelier with a minimum number of full-time staff, presenting collections of a set number of original designs each season, and making garments to private clients' individual measurements — may call themselves haute couture houses.

History

The English dressmaker Charles Frederick Worth, working in Paris from the 1850s, is regarded as the father of haute couture: he pioneered the designer's label, seasonal collections shown on live models, and the couturier as artist. The system was formalised by the Chambre Syndicale's founding in 1868 and given legal definition in 1945. Post-war couturiers included Christian Dior, whose 1947 "New Look" revived Paris, Cristóbal Balenciaga, Coco Chanel, and Hubert de Givenchy.

The modern industry

Today the official calendar counts only about fifteen member houses — among them Chanel, Dior, Givenchy, Schiaparelli, and Jean Paul Gaultier — plus correspondent (foreign) and guest members. Couture is rarely profitable in itself: a single gown may take hundreds of hours and sell to a global clientele of only a few thousand women, but it generates prestige and publicity that support the houses' far larger ready-to-wear, fragrance, and accessories businesses.
