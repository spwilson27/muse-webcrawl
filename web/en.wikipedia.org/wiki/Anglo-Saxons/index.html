The Anglo-Saxons were a cultural group that inhabited England in the Early Middle Ages. They traced their origins to Germanic settlers who began arriving in Britain from the mid-5th century AD, following the end of Roman rule — principally Angles, Saxons and Jutes from what is now northern Germany and Denmark. Over the following centuries their language, Old English, their laws and their kingdoms laid the foundations of England, until the Norman Conquest of 1066 ended Anglo-Saxon political rule.

Settlement and kingdoms

Tradition recorded by Bede and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle describes an Adventus Saxonum in which settlers founded kingdoms that coalesced into the so-called Heptarchy: Northumbria, Mercia, East Anglia, Kent, Essex, Sussex and Wessex. Modern archaeology and genetic studies show a substantial migration mixed with considerable continuity of the native British population, and the scale and violence of the takeover remain debated.

Society and culture

Initially pagan — worshipping gods such as Woden and Thunor — the Anglo-Saxons were converted to Christianity from 597 by the Gregorian mission to Kent and by Irish missionaries from Iona, producing a flourishing monastic culture of learning: Bede, Alcuin and the great illuminated manuscripts, and vernacular literature including the epic Beowulf. Their craftsmanship is displayed by the treasures of Sutton Hoo and the Staffordshire Hoard. From the 790s Viking raids, and later Danish settlement in the Danelaw, reshaped the political map, until Alfred the Great of Wessex and his descendants forged a single Kingdom of England in the 10th century.

End and legacy

The last Anglo-Saxon king, Harold Godwinson, was killed at the Battle of Hastings in 1066, and William the Conqueror replaced the English aristocracy with Normans. Yet much endured: the English language descends from Old English, and shires, common-law traditions and many place-names are Anglo-Saxon inheritances.
