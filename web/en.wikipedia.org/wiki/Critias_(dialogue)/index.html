Critias (dialogue)

The Critias is an unfinished dialogue by the ancient Greek philosopher Plato, written around 360 BC. Together with the Timaeus, it contains the story of the mighty island empire of Atlantis and its war against a prehistoric Athens — the source of the Atlantis legend that has fascinated readers ever since.

Content

The dialogue continues a conversation begun in the Timaeus. Critias — named for its principal speaker, Plato's relative — recounts a story he claims Solon heard from Egyptian priests at Saïs: nine thousand years earlier, Athens, then an idealised virtuous city, defeated the invading forces of Atlantis, a rich and powerful island civilisation beyond the Pillars of Hercules, before both were destroyed by earthquakes and floods "in a single day and night of misfortune".

Critias describes Atlantis in elaborate detail: its concentric rings of water and land around the royal island, its harbours, temples, palaces, elephants, and the mysterious metal orichalcum. He also describes primeval Athens, its guardian class, and the geography of Attica in the distant past. The dialogue breaks off abruptly, mid-sentence, just as Zeus rises to pronounce judgment on the corrupt Atlanteans; a projected third dialogue, the Hermocrates, was apparently never written.

Interpretation

Most scholars regard Atlantis as Plato's philosophical invention — a cautionary tale about imperial wealth and moral decline — rather than a genuine historical tradition, though a minority literature has searched for real prototypes such as the Minoan eruption of Thera. The unfinished state of the Critias has prompted speculation that Plato abandoned the trilogy to write the Laws.
