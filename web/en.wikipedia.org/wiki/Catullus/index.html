Catullus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A Roman marble portrait bust of a young man of the late Republic, evocative of Catullus's era]
A Roman marble portrait bust of a young man of the late Republic, evocative of Catullus's era

Gaius Valerius Catullus (c. 84 – c. 54 BC) was a Latin poet of the late Roman Republic who wrote in the neoteric style of the 'new poets', influencing Virgil and the elegists. His surviving corpus of 116 poems ranges from exquisite love lyrics to the woman he calls Lesbia — among them some of the most passionate verse in Latin — to invective of legendary obscenity, polymetric songs and a miniature epic, the Peleus and Thetis.

Life and circle

Catullus came from a wealthy Verona family (his father hosted Julius Caesar), moved in Rome's fashionable literary set — Cinna, Calvus, Asinius Pollio — served on the staff of the governor of Bithynia (57–56 BC), and mourned his brother, who died in the Troad. He died around age thirty, traditionally at Rome in 54 BC. Lesbia — his married mistress — is generally identified (following Apuleius) with Clodia, sister of the tribune Clodius.

Poems

The collection as transmitted divides into the short polymetrics (1–60) — sparrow poems, kisses counted in thousands ('da mi basia mille'), the farewell to his brother ('ave atque vale'); the long poems (61–68) — wedding hymns, the Attis (63) and the epyllion on Peleus and Thetis (64); and the elegiac epigrams (69–116), including the devastating 'Odi et amo' ('I hate and I love', poem 85). His speakers veer between tenderness and filth, sometimes in adjacent poems — a range that scandalised Victorian editors and electrified modern ones.

Survival and influence

Catullus's book barely survived: a single lost manuscript rediscovered at Verona around 1300 fathered all copies. His compression, wit and emotional nakedness shaped Ovid, Martial and the whole tradition of Latin love elegy, and he is routinely ranked with the greatest lyric poets of antiquity.
