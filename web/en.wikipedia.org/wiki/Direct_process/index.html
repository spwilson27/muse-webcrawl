Direct process

The direct process, also called the Rochow process or Rochow–Müller process, is the most common technology for preparing organosilicon compounds on an industrial scale. It was first reported independently in the 1940s by Eugene G. Rochow in the United States and Richard Müller in Germany. The process is used to produce methylchlorosilanes, which are the key intermediates in the manufacture of silicones.

Reaction

In the direct process, elemental silicon (typically metallurgical-grade silicon) reacts directly with chloromethane (methyl chloride) in the presence of a copper catalyst at temperatures of roughly 250–350 °C. The overall reaction is commonly written:

Si + 2 CH3Cl → (CH3)2SiCl2

The main product is dimethyldichlorosilane, (CH3)2SiCl2, accompanied by smaller amounts of methyltrichlorosilane, trimethylchlorosilane, methyldichlorosilane and other by-products. The product mixture is separated by fractional distillation; the boiling points of the principal methylchlorosilanes lie close together, so efficient fractionation columns are required.

Importance

Dimethyldichlorosilane is hydrolysed to give silanols that condense to form polydimethylsiloxane, the backbone polymer of most commercial silicone fluids, elastomers and resins. Because virtually all silicone production passes through the direct process, it is one of the foundational reactions of organosilicon chemistry.

Mechanism

The mechanism is thought to involve the formation of a copper–silicon alloy (an intermetallic phase, often formulated as Cu3Si) at the silicon surface, which activates the silicon toward attack by chloromethane. Promoters such as zinc, tin, aluminium and antimony may be added in small amounts to improve selectivity and reaction rate.
