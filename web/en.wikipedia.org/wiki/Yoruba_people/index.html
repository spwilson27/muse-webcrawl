The Yoruba people (Yoruba: Àwọn ọmọ Yorùbá) are a West African ethnic group who inhabit parts of Nigeria, Benin and Togo, in the region collectively known as Yorubaland. Numbering more than 45 million, they are among the largest ethnic groups in Africa. Most Yoruba live in southwestern Nigeria, where they form the majority in states such as Lagos, Oyo, Ogun, Ondo, Osun and Ekiti, with significant populations elsewhere in Nigeria and a worldwide diaspora.

History

Yoruba tradition centres on the sacred city of Ilé-Ifẹ̀, regarded in mythology as the place where the world was created and from which the culture-hero Oduduwa founded the ruling dynasties of the Yoruba kingdoms. City-states including Oyo, Ife, Ijebu, Ketu and the Egba towns developed over centuries; the Oyo Empire became the dominant power in the region between the 17th and early 19th centuries before collapsing amid civil wars. The 19th century saw the founding of Ibadan and Abeokuta and the arrival of Christian missions and British colonial rule. Yorubaland has long been notable for dense urbanisation, with a tradition of large towns ruled by kings (oba) and councils.

Culture

The Yoruba speak a tonal language of the Benue-Congo family, written in a Latin-based orthography. Their traditional religion venerates a pantheon of deities (orisha) under the supreme god Olodumare and centres on Ifá divination, which UNESCO has recognised as intangible heritage; related religions such as Santería, Candomblé and Lucumí flourished in the Americas through the diaspora. Today most Yoruba are Christians or Muslims, while traditional practice persists alongside both.

Yoruba culture is renowned for its art — including the naturalistic bronze and terracotta heads of Ife, palace carvings, and Gelede and Egungun masquerades — its music, from talking-drum (dundun) ensembles to modern genres such as jùjú, fuji and Afrobeat, and a rich literary tradition in both Yoruba and English.
