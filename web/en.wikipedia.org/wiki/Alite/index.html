Alite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chemical formulaCa3SiO5 (3CaO·SiO2)
Crystal systemMonoclinic / triclinic / rhombohedral (polymorphs)
RolePrincipal strength-giving phase of Portland cement clinker

Alite is an impure form of tricalcium silicate, Ca3SiO5, sometimes formulated as 3CaO·SiO2 (C3S in cement chemist notation). It is the most important constituent of Portland cement clinker, typically making up 50–70% of its mass, and is chiefly responsible for the early strength development of concrete.

Composition and structure

Pure tricalcium silicate occurs in several polymorphs — triclinic, monoclinic and rhombohedral — which transform on heating and cooling. In industrial clinker the high-temperature monoclinic form is stabilised at room temperature by foreign ions such as Mg2+, Al3+ and Fe3+ incorporated into the crystal lattice; this substituted material is called alite. The name was given by Törnebohm in 1897, from the Greek letter alpha, as it was the first phase he identified in clinker under the microscope.

Formation

Alite forms in the cement kiln above about 1338 °C, when belite (Ca2SiO4) reacts with free lime (CaO) in the presence of a liquid melt. Below that temperature it is thermodynamically unstable and decomposes very slowly back to belite and lime; the rapid cooling of clinker preserves it.

Hydration

When cement is mixed with water, alite reacts relatively quickly to form calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel and calcium hydroxide, releasing heat. This reaction dominates the setting of cement and most strength gain in the first 28 days of curing. Its control — through fineness, temperature and admixtures — is central to concrete technology and to efforts to reduce the large carbon footprint of cement production.
