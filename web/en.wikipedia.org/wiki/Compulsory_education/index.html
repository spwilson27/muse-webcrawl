Compulsory education

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Compulsory education refers to a period of education that is required of all children by law, typically from about age 5 or 6 until the mid-to-late teens. The obligation usually falls on parents or guardians to ensure the child receives an education meeting state standards — which in many jurisdictions may be satisfied by public schools, private schools, or approved home schooling. Compulsory schooling is a near-universal feature of modern states and underpins child labour law and mass literacy.

History

Early mandates appeared in a few pre-modern states — for example in the Aztec Triple Alliance and in some Protestant territories after the Reformation. Prussia's Generallandschulreglement of 1763 is often cited as a landmark of state-compelled attendance. Massachusetts enacted the first US compulsory attendance law in 1852, and by 1918 every US state had one. England and Wales introduced compulsory schooling with the Elementary Education Act 1880. Over the 20th century, leaving ages rose steadily: many countries now require education or training to 18.

Rationale

Arguments for compulsion include the child's own interest in education as a right, the public benefits of an educated citizenry and workforce, the protection of children from labour exploitation, and the mitigation of inequality of opportunity. The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child obliges states to make primary education compulsory and free.

Criticism and alternatives

Critics — ranging from Ivan Illich's "deschooling" movement to home-education advocates and unschoolers — argue that compulsory attendance institutionalises children, privileges one model of learning, and infringes parental rights. Legal systems have struck balances: the US Supreme Court protected religious exemptions for the Amish after eighth grade in Wisconsin v. Yoder (1972), while several European states, such as Germany, largely prohibit homeschooling.

By country

Starting ages range from 4 to 7 and leaving ages from 14 to 19. Enforcement ranges from nominal to strict truancy regimes with fines or, rarely, imprisonment of parents. Globally, UNESCO estimates that despite compulsion laws, over 200 million children and youth remain out of school, concentrated in low-income countries and conflict zones.
