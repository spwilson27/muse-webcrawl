Parmigiano-Reggiano

Parmigiano-Reggiano is an Italian hard, granular cheese made from cow's milk and aged at least 12 months. It is named after the producing areas — the provinces of Parma, Reggio Emilia, Modena, Bologna (west of the Reno River), and Mantua (on the right bank of the Po) — and is protected by a PDO (Denominazione di Origine Protetta) designation in the European Union, where only cheese made in this region by the prescribed method may bear the name. In English it is widely known as Parmesan, though outside the EU "parmesan" may also denote imitations.

Production

Parmigiano-Reggiano is made from a mixture of whole morning milk and naturally skimmed evening milk, heated in copper vats with calf rennet. The curd is broken into tiny granules (hence "grana"), cooked, settled, and pressed into wheels weighing about 38–40 kg. Wheels are brined in saturated salt solution for about 20 days, then aged on wooden shelves for a minimum of 12 months, with common commercial ages of 24 or 36 months. Each wheel requires roughly 550 litres of milk.

During production, the name, month and year of manufacture, and the dairy's registration number are repeated in pin-dot stenciling around the rind, making imitation difficult. At 12 months, consortium inspectors tap each wheel with a small hammer and listen for defects; approved wheels receive the official oval brand, and defective ones have their markings struck through (the lowest grade is stripped of the name entirely).

History

The cheese has been made in essentially its modern form since the Middle Ages; Benedictine and Cistercian monks in the Po plain are generally credited with developing it around the 12th–13th centuries, draining marshland and producing a long-keeping cheese from extra milk. Boccaccio mentions it in the Decameron (14th century), describing a mountain of grated Parmesan down which macaroni and ravioli are rolled. Samuel Pepys buried his Parmesan cheese to protect it during the Great Fire of London in 1666.

Uses and imitation

Often called the "King of Cheeses," it is grated over pasta, shaved into salads, eaten in chunks with balsamic vinegar or wine, and used in risottos; the crunchy crystals within the paste are calcium lactate and amino acid (tyrosine) deposits formed during aging. Outside Europe the name "parmesan" is generic in many countries — a long-standing trade dispute: the EU and the Parmigiano-Reggiano Consortium argue the term should refer only to the PDO product, while producers in countries such as the United States sell domestic "parmesan" legally.
