Rigid designator

Rigid designator
FieldPhilosophy of language; modal logic
Introduced bySaul Kripke, Naming and Necessity (lectures 1970; book 1972)
Contrasted withNon-rigid (flaccid) designator; definite description

In the philosophy of language and modal logic, a term is said to be a rigid designator when it designates (picks out, denotes, refers to) the same object in all possible worlds in which that object exists, or, in a strengthened version, in every possible world whatsoever. The notion is most commonly applied to names and natural kind terms, and stands in contrast with the non-rigid designator, also called a flaccid designator, which may pick out different objects in different possible worlds — as definite descriptions typically do. The terminology was introduced by Saul Kripke in his celebrated Princeton lectures of 1970, published as Naming and Necessity (1972).

Examples

The proper name "Richard Nixon" is a rigid designator: the man picked out by it in the actual world is the referent in every world where he exists, including worlds where he never became president. By contrast, the description "the 37th President of the United States" is non-rigid, because in some possible world someone else won the 1968 election. Mathematical terms like "the smallest prime number" are rigid because they denote the same object (2) in every possible world. Kripke argued that natural kind terms such as "water," "gold," and "tiger" also function as rigid designators, referring to the same substance or species across worlds by virtue of its underlying essence (for instance, H2O) — a view that implies the existence of necessary truths known a posteriori, since "water is H2O," if true, is true in every possible world.

Role in Kripke's theory

Rigid designation underpins Kripke's causal-historical theory of reference, on which a name's reference is fixed by an initial "baptism" and passed along a causal chain of speakers, rather than by a cluster of descriptions in speakers' minds. It also supports his arguments against the identity theory of mind in its contingent form, his account of necessary a posteriori truths (e.g., "Hesperus is Phosphorus"), and metaphysical discussions of de re modality. Variants include obstinate designators (same referent even in worlds where the object does not exist) and persistent designators.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether ordinary names are genuinely rigid, worrying about empty names, fictional names, and worlds in which the referent never exists. Others, such as Nathan Salmon, have developed "mildly rigid" treatments, and two-dimensional semantics attempts to reconcile rigidity with descriptive content via primary and secondary intensions. The notion nonetheless remains central to contemporary analytic philosophy.
