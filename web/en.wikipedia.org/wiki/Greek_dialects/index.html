Greek dialects are the regional varieties of the Greek language, attested from the Mycenaean period to the present day. In antiquity the language was divided into a number of clearly distinguished dialects — principally Ionic (including Attic), Doric, Aeolic, Arcadocypriot and Northwest Greek — which were only gradually levelled by the Koine, the common Hellenistic standard based largely on Attic. In the Byzantine and modern periods new regional varieties arose, some of which, such as Tsakonian and Griko, survive today in endangered form.

Ancient dialects

The earliest attested form of Greek is Mycenaean, written in the Linear B syllabary in the palaces of Bronze Age Greece. After the Bronze Age collapse, the classical dialect map emerged:

  • Attic–Ionic — spoken in Attica, Euboea, the Cyclades and the central Aegean coast of Asia Minor. Attic became the language of classical Athenian literature and the basis of the Koine.
  • Doric — spoken in the Peloponnese, Crete, Rhodes and the southern Aegean, and in colonies such as Syracuse. Known for retaining long ā where Attic–Ionic shifted it to ē.
  • Aeolic — spoken in Boeotia, Thessaly and Lesbos; the language of Sappho and Alcaeus.
  • Arcadocypriot — spoken in Arcadia and Cyprus, most closely related to Mycenaean.
  • Northwest Greek — spoken in Phocis, Locris, Elis and Epirus, closely related to Doric.

Koine and Byzantine Greek

From the conquests of Alexander the Great, the Koine ("common") Greek spread across the eastern Mediterranean and became the administrative and literary standard, largely suppressing the old dialects. Medieval Greek developed out of the Koine, with increasing divergence between the learned written language and spoken vernaculars.

Modern dialects

Modern Greek dialects include Cappadocian, Pontic and Mariupolitan (heavily influenced by Turkish and other contact languages), Cretan, Cypriot, the northern dialects of mainland Greece, and the southern varieties of the Aegean islands. Two are particularly notable: Tsakonian, spoken in the eastern Peloponnese and descended from ancient Doric rather than the Koine, and Griko, spoken in enclaves in Calabria and Apulia in southern Italy. The modern standard, Demotic, became the official language of Greece in 1976, ending the long diglossia with the archaizing Katharevousa.
