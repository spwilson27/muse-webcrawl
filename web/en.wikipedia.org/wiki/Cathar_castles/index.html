Cathar castles

Cathar castles (in French, Châteaux cathares) is a modern term used by the tourism industry of southern France for a group of medieval castles in the Languedoc region associated — sometimes loosely — with the Cathars and with the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229) and the subsequent French conquest of the region. Many of the dramatic hilltop fortresses were in fact built or rebuilt by the French crown after the crusade to guard the new border with the Crown of Aragon.

Major sites

The most famous include Montségur, the mountain refuge where more than 200 Cathars were burned after the castle's surrender in 1244; Quéribus, the last Cathar stronghold, which held out until 1255; Peyrepertuse, an enormous fortress sometimes called the "Céleste citadel"; Lastours, a group of four towers above the Orbiel valley; Termes, Aguilar, Puivert, and Puilaurens. The city of Carcassonne, with its restored double walls, was the seat of the Trencavel viscounts, dispossessed during the crusade.

History

During the Albigensian Crusade, northern French crusaders and the French crown seized the lands of southern lords who protected Cathars. After the Treaty of Paris (1229), royal engineers constructed or modernised a line of border fortresses — the so-called "five sons of Carcassonne" — facing Aragon. When the Treaty of the Pyrenees (1659) moved the border south, the castles lost their military role, decayed into picturesque ruins, and were later romanticised as symbols of Occitan identity and resistance.
