Carina Nebula

The Carina Nebula (catalogued as NGC 3372, also called the Eta Carinae Nebula) is a vast complex of bright and dark nebulae in the constellation Carina, about 7,500–8,500 light-years from Earth. One of the largest diffuse nebulae in the sky — spanning some 300 light-years — it is a giant star-forming region containing several open clusters and some of the most massive and luminous stars known, including the unstable luminous blue variable Eta Carinae.

Contents

The nebula hosts the open clusters Trumpler 14 and Trumpler 16, home to WR 25 and HD 93129A, among the most massive and luminous stars in the Milky Way. Eta Carinae, within Trumpler 16, brightened during its "Great Eruption" of the 1840s to become the second-brightest star in the sky; the expelled material forms the bipolar Homunculus Nebula around it. A dark patch of obscuring dust near Eta Carinae is nicknamed the Keyhole Nebula. Towering dust pillars such as the "Mystic Mountain", imaged by the Hubble Space Telescope for its 20th anniversary, are nurseries where jets from newborn stars punch through the gas.

Observation

Catalogued by Lacaille from the Cape of Good Hope in 1751–52, the nebula is a showpiece of the southern sky, four times as large and far brighter than the Orion Nebula, though less famous because it is invisible from most of the northern hemisphere. John Herschel observed it extensively in the 1830s and witnessed the beginning of Eta Carinae's great eruption. Modern infrared images from Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope reveal thousands of previously hidden young stars within its dust.

Star formation

The Carina complex contains over a million solar masses of gas and dust and scores of O-type stars whose ultraviolet radiation and winds sculpt the nebula and trigger further star formation in compressed pillar tips. Because of its size, proximity and rich massive-star population, it serves as a nearby analogue of the giant starburst regions seen in other galaxies.
