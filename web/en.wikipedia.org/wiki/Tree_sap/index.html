Tree sap

Sap is a fluid transported in the xylem cells or phloem sieve tube elements of a plant, including trees. These cells carry water and nutrients throughout the plant. Sap is distinct from latex, resin, and other plant secretions, though the terms are often confused in everyday usage.

Types

Xylem sap consists mainly of water with dissolved mineral nutrients drawn up from the roots, moving upward under tension generated by transpiration. Phloem sap is rich in sugars produced by photosynthesis, along with amino acids and hormones, and moves from source tissues (usually leaves) to sink tissues such as roots, fruits, and growing shoots.

Human uses

Sap has long been harvested from trees. Maple sap is boiled down to make maple syrup, and birch sap is drunk as a beverage in northern Europe and Asia. Pine trees are tapped for resin (which yields turpentine and rosin), and the sap of the rubber tree provides natural latex. Palm sap is fermented into palm wine or boiled into palm sugar in many tropical regions.

Ecology

Many insects feed on sap, including aphids, cicadas, and spittlebugs; they in turn excrete sugary honeydew. Some birds, such as sapsuckers, drill rows of holes in bark to feed on flowing sap and the insects it attracts.
