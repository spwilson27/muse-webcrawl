Price–earnings ratio

The price–earnings ratio (P/E ratio) is a valuation metric for a company, defined as its share price divided by its earnings per share (EPS). It indicates how much investors are willing to pay per unit of current (or expected) earnings, and is one of the most widely used tools for comparing the relative value of stocks.

Variants

The trailing P/E uses earnings from the most recent 12 months, while the forward P/E uses analysts' earnings estimates for the coming year. Robert Shiller's cyclically adjusted P/E (CAPE, or Shiller P/E) divides price by the average of ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings, and is used to assess long-run market valuation.

Interpretation

A high P/E may mean investors expect strong future growth — or that a stock is overvalued; a low P/E may signal undervaluation or weak prospects. Ratios vary greatly by industry and interest-rate environment, and the metric is meaningless for companies with negative earnings. Its reciprocal, the earnings yield, is sometimes compared with bond yields.

History

Value investors such as Benjamin Graham popularised earnings-based valuation in the 1930s, suggesting rules of thumb for acceptable P/E ranges that later investors have debated and refined.
