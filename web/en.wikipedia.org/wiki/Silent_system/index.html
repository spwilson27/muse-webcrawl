Silent system

The silent system was a form of prison management in which inmates were forbidden to speak to one another. It was one of the two principal systems of nineteenth-century prison discipline, the other being the separate system, in which prisoners were kept in complete isolation from one another.

History

Under the silent system, prisoners worked together in association during the day — typically at productive labour in workshops — but were required to maintain absolute silence at all times. They slept in individual cells at night. Conversation, gestures, and even glances between prisoners were punished, often severely, on the theory that communication between offenders led to moral contamination and the planning of further crimes, while enforced silence encouraged reflection and reformation.

The system was closely associated with Auburn Prison in New York, where it was developed under wardens such as Elam Lynds in the 1820s, and it is therefore also known as the Auburn system. It became the dominant model for prisons in the United States during the nineteenth century, in part because congregate labour under silence was more economical than the solitary labour of the Pennsylvania (separate) system.

Enforcement and decline

Silence was enforced by strict surveillance and corporal punishments such as flogging for infractions. Critics charged that the regime was unnatural and psychologically harmful. By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries the silent system was gradually abandoned in favour of more rehabilitative approaches, though rules of silence survived in some institutions for much longer.
