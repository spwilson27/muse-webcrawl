Catechol (also known as pyrocatechol or 1,2-dihydroxybenzene) is an organic compound with the molecular formula C6H4(OH)2. It is the ortho isomer of the three isomeric benzenediols. This colourless compound occurs naturally in trace amounts; it was first discovered in 1839 by Edgar Hugo Emil Reinsch during the destructive distillation of the plant extract catechin, from which it takes its name.

Production and synthesis

Catechol is produced industrially by the hydroxylation of phenol using hydrogen peroxide, a process developed by Rhône-Poulenc. It can also be prepared in the laboratory by demethylation of guaiacol (2-methoxyphenol) with hydrobromic acid, or by hydrolysis of 2-chlorophenol with aqueous base at elevated temperature.

Reactions

Catechol is an important chelating agent: its two oxygen atoms bind strongly to metal ions such as Fe3+, forming intensely coloured complexes. It is readily oxidised, first to the semiquinone and then to ortho-benzoquinone. The catechol moiety appears in many natural products and biomolecules, including the catecholamines (dopamine, adrenaline and noradrenaline), urushiol (the irritant in poison ivy), and numerous flavonoids.

Uses

About half of synthetic catechol is consumed in the production of pesticides such as carbofuran and propoxur. It is also used as a photographic developer, in fur dyeing, and as a precursor to flavours and fragrances such as piperonal, guaiacol derivatives and vanillin-related compounds. The strong adhesion of catechol groups to surfaces inspired the development of mussel-inspired adhesives based on dopamine chemistry.
