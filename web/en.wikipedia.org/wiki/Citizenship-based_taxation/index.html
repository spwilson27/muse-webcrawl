Citizenship-based taxation

Citizenship-based taxation (CBT) is a tax regime in which a country taxes its citizens on their worldwide income regardless of where they live or where the income is earned. It contrasts with residence-based taxation, used by most countries, in which tax liability generally depends on living (or being tax resident) in the country. The United States is the most prominent country applying citizenship-based taxation, and Eritrea applies a diaspora tax to its citizens abroad; most other countries tax primarily on the basis of residence or source of income.

Almost all countries tax by residence; the United States and Eritrea tax citizens abroad.

United States

The United States taxes its citizens and resident aliens on worldwide income wherever they reside. Americans living abroad must file annual US tax returns and report foreign bank accounts (FBAR and FATCA reporting), though foreign-earned income exclusions and foreign tax credits reduce or eliminate double taxation for many. The system dates to the Civil War era and was upheld by the Supreme Court in Cook v. Tait (1924), which held that the benefits of citizenship justify taxing citizens resident abroad. Critics, including expatriate advocacy groups, argue the regime is burdensome, causes "accidental Americans" difficulties with banking, and drives record numbers of citizenship renunciations. Defenders argue it prevents tax evasion through emigration and treats citizens equally.

Eritrea

Eritrea levies a flat 2 percent "diaspora tax" on the income of its citizens living abroad, officially for national recovery and development. Enforcement through consulates has been controversial; several governments and a UN Security Council resolution condemned coercive collection practices.

History and comparisons

Historically, a few other countries, including Mexico, the Philippines and Vietnam, at times taxed citizens abroad, but most abandoned the practice. The Philippines moved to taxing only residents on worldwide income in 1997. Some countries apply "extended residence" rules taxing recent emigrants for a limited period, which differs from full citizenship-based taxation.
