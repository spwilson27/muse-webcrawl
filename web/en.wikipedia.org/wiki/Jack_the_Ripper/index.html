Jack the Ripper is the best-known name for an unidentified serial killer active in and around the impoverished Whitechapel district of London, England, in 1888. Attacks ascribed to the Ripper typically involved female prostitutes whose throats were cut and bodies mutilated. The name originated in a letter, the "Dear Boss" letter, purportedly written by the killer and widely disseminated in the media.

The canonical five

Five victims are generally accepted as the "canonical five": Mary Ann Nichols (31 August 1888), Annie Chapman (8 September), Elizabeth Stride and Catherine Eddowes (both 30 September, the "double event"), and Mary Jane Kelly (9 November). All were killed in the early morning hours, mostly outdoors, in or near Whitechapel and Spitalfields. The removal of internal organs from at least three victims led contemporaries to suggest the murderer had anatomical or surgical knowledge.

Investigation

The Metropolitan Police, led at various points by officers including Inspector Frederick Abberline, investigated extensively but never charged anyone with the murders. The case coincided with intense press coverage, public panic, and scrutiny of police methods; Sir Charles Warren, the Commissioner of the Metropolitan Police, resigned shortly after Kelly's murder. The Whitechapel Vigilance Committee, chaired by George Lusk, received the "From Hell" letter, accompanied by part of a human kidney.

Suspects and theories

Over a hundred suspects have been proposed over the years, including Montague Druitt, Aaron Kosminski, Severin Klosowski (George Chapman), Walter Sickert, and various conspiracy theories involving the royal family and Freemasons. The study of the case, known as "Ripperology," remains an active field of amateur and professional research. DNA claims published in 2019 regarding a shawl said to be linked to Eddowes were met with significant scientific criticism.

Legacy

The Ripper murders are a landmark in the history of crime reporting, forensic science, and public perceptions of the Victorian East End. The unidentified killer has become a fixture of folklore, fiction, film, and tourism in London.
