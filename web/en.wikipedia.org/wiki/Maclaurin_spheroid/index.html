Maclaurin spheroid

A Maclaurin spheroid is an oblate spheroid which arises when a self-gravitating fluid body of uniform density rotates with constant angular velocity. It is named after the Scottish mathematician Colin Maclaurin, who derived the formula for its shape in 1742 in the course of his work on the figure of the Earth and the attraction of ellipsoids.

Theory

Maclaurin showed that for a given angular momentum, a rotating homogeneous fluid mass can rest in equilibrium in the form of an oblate spheroid — a sphere flattened at the poles. For slow rotation the eccentricity is small, but as angular velocity increases the spheroid becomes progressively flatter.

Instability and Jacobi ellipsoids

Above a critical eccentricity (about 0.8127), the Maclaurin spheroid becomes unstable and the equilibrium figure bifurcates to a triaxial Jacobi ellipsoid, which rotates about its shortest axis. This result, developed later by Jacobi and others, is a classic problem in the theory of rotating fluids and relates to the shapes of planets, stars and rotating galaxies.
