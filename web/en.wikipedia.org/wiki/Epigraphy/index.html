SubjectStudy of inscriptions on durable materials
MaterialsStone, metal, pottery, wood, wax

Epigraphy (from Ancient Greek epigraphḗ, "inscription") is the study of inscriptions, or epigraphs, as writing; it is the science of identifying graphemes, clarifying their meanings, and classifying their uses according to dates and cultural contexts. Inscriptions are typically engraved or painted on durable materials such as stone and metal, and range from monumental public decrees and laws to funerary epitaphs, milestones, coins, graffiti, and makers' marks on pottery.

Methods and significance

Epigraphers record inscriptions through photography, squeezes (paper impressions), and increasingly digital imaging such as reflectance transformation imaging, then edit them with standard conventions recording restorations and uncertain letters. Because inscriptions are primary documents produced at the time of the events they describe — unlike literary texts transmitted through centuries of copying — they are a cornerstone source for ancient history, providing evidence on law, religion, administration, prosopography, and everyday life that literary sources omit.

Major traditions

Greek and Latin epigraphy are the most developed branches, supported by vast published corpora such as the Inscriptiones Graecae and Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum. Other major traditions study Egyptian hieroglyphs and demotic texts, cuneiform tablets and inscriptions, Maya glyphs, Chinese bronze and oracle-bone inscriptions, and runic inscriptions. Decipherments — most famously of Egyptian hieroglyphs using the Rosetta Stone — are among the discipline's most celebrated achievements.
