The Mental Capacity Act 2005 (c. 9) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom applying to England and Wales. Its primary purpose is to provide a legal framework for acting and making decisions on behalf of adults who lack the mental capacity to make particular decisions for themselves.

Key principles

Section 1 of the Act sets out five statutory principles:

  1. A person must be assumed to have capacity unless it is established that they lack capacity.
  2. A person is not to be treated as unable to make a decision unless all practicable steps to help them to do so have been taken without success.
  3. A person is not to be treated as unable to make a decision merely because they make an unwise decision.
  4. An act done, or decision made, under this Act for or on behalf of a person who lacks capacity must be done, or made, in their best interests.
  5. Before the act is done, or the decision is made, regard must be had to whether the purpose for which it is needed can be as effectively achieved in a way that is less restrictive of the person's rights and freedom of action.

Assessing capacity

Under sections 2 and 3, a person lacks capacity in relation to a matter if, at the material time, they are unable to make a decision for themselves in relation to the matter because of an impairment of, or a disturbance in the functioning of, the mind or brain. A person is unable to make a decision if they cannot understand the information relevant to the decision, retain that information, use or weigh it as part of the process of making the decision, or communicate their decision.

Institutions created

The Act established the Court of Protection as a superior court of record, together with the office of the Public Guardian and the Office of the Public Guardian. It also created the role of the Independent Mental Capacity Advocate (IMCA) and introduced lasting powers of attorney, replacing enduring powers of attorney under the Enduring Powers of Attorney Act 1985.

Deprivation of liberty

Following the European Court of Human Rights judgment in HL v United Kingdom (the "Bournewood" case), the Act was amended by the Mental Health Act 2007 to introduce the Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards (DoLS), which came into force in April 2009. The Supreme Court's 2014 judgment in Cheshire West significantly widened the scope of what counts as a deprivation of liberty. The Mental Capacity (Amendment) Act 2019 introduced the Liberty Protection Safeguards intended to replace DoLS, although their implementation was repeatedly delayed and ultimately shelved by the government in 2023.
