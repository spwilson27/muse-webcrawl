Shmuel Yosef Agnon

Shmuel Yosef Agnon (Hebrew: שמואל יוסף עגנון; July 17, 1888 – February 17, 1970) was an Israeli novelist, poet, and short-story writer, and one of the central figures of modern Hebrew literature. In 1966 he shared the Nobel Prize in Literature with Nelly Sachs, honoured "for his profoundly characteristic narrative art with motifs from the life of the Jewish people." He was the first Hebrew-language writer to receive the prize.

Life

Born Shmuel Yosef Czaczkes in Buczacz, Galicia (now Buchach, Ukraine), he was educated in traditional Jewish texts and began publishing poetry and stories in Hebrew and Yiddish as a teenager. He immigrated to Ottoman Palestine in 1908, taking the pen name "Agnon" from his story "Agunot" ("Forsaken Wives"). After years in Germany (1913–1924), where his house and manuscripts burned in a fire, he settled permanently in the Talpiot neighbourhood of Jerusalem. His home is now a museum, Beit Agnon.

Works

Agnon's major works include the novels The Bridal Canopy (1931), A Simple Story (1935), A Guest for the Night (1938), and Only Yesterday (1945), as well as story collections such as A Book That Was Lost. His dense, allusive Hebrew layers biblical, rabbinic, and Hasidic sources, and his tales often move between the traditional world of Eastern European Jewry and the dislocations of modern life.

Recognition

Agnon twice received the Israel Prize for literature (1954, 1958) and the Bialik Prize. His portrait appears on the Israeli fifty-shekel banknote.
