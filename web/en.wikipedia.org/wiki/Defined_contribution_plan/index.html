Defined contribution plan

A defined contribution (DC) plan is a type of retirement plan in which the employer, the employee, or both make contributions on a regular basis, and the final benefit received by the employee depends on the amounts contributed and the investment returns earned on those contributions. Individual accounts are set up for each participant, and the benefits are based on the amounts credited to the account plus any investment earnings. Unlike a defined benefit plan, a defined contribution plan does not promise a specific benefit at retirement, so the investment risk is borne by the employee.

Examples

In the United States, the best-known defined contribution plans are the 401(k) plan for private-sector employees, the 403(b) plan for employees of non-profit organisations and schools, and the federal government's Thrift Savings Plan. Many take the form of profit-sharing, money purchase, or employee stock ownership plans (ESOPs). In other countries, comparable arrangements include Australia's superannuation guarantee system, Chile's AFP individual accounts, and auto-enrolment workplace pensions in the United Kingdom.

Characteristics

Contributions are commonly a percentage of salary, frequently with the employer matching part of the employee's contribution. Accounts are typically invested in a menu of mutual funds or in default options such as target-date funds, and are portable between employers. Plan assets are usually tax-advantaged: contributions may be tax-deferred (traditional) or made after tax with tax-free withdrawals (Roth). Withdrawals before retirement age are generally restricted and penalised.

Shift from defined benefit

Since the late 20th century, defined contribution plans have largely displaced defined benefit plans in the private sector of the United States and elsewhere, shifting longevity and investment risk from employers to employees. Supporters emphasise portability, transparency of account balances, and individual control; critics note that many workers contribute too little, invest poorly, pay high fees, or fail to convert their balances into secure retirement income, making outcomes far less predictable than under traditional pensions.
