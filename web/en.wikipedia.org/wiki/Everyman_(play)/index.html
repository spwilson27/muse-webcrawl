Everyman (play)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Somonyng of Everyman (The Summoning of Everyman), usually known simply as Everyman, is a late 15th-century English morality play. Like John Bunyan's 1678 Christian novel The Pilgrim's Progress, Everyman uses allegorical characters to examine the question of Christian salvation and what people must do to attain it. The play survives in four early printed editions dating from about 1510–1535; its author is unknown, and it may be a translation or adaptation of the Dutch play Elckerlijc (c. 1495).

Plot

God, seeing that humanity has become absorbed in worldly wealth, sends Death to summon Everyman to give an account of his life before his Maker. Everyman begs for more time and seeks companions for the journey. Fellowship, Kindred and Cousin each promise loyalty but refuse to accompany him into death. Goods — his worldly possessions — likewise cannot go with him and declares that accumulated wealth only damns the soul.

At last Everyman turns to Good Deeds, who is weak because of his sins but agrees to go with him. Guided by Knowledge, Everyman makes confession and receives penance, and Good Deeds grows strong. Beauty, Strength, Discretion and the Five Wits advise him but abandon him at the grave's edge; only Good Deeds descends with him. An Angel announces that his soul is received into heaven.

Significance

Everyman is generally considered the finest of the surviving English morality plays and a touchstone of early Tudor drama. Its stark structure — a single figure successively stripped of everything except his good deeds — has made it a perennial choice for revival, adaptation and study, and its protagonist's name has passed into the language as the type of the ordinary human being.
