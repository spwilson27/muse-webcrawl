Tibetanབོཙ་འྀ་ (sprul sku)
Literal meaning"Emanation body" (Sanskrit: nirmāṇakāya)
TraditionTibetan Buddhism
Notable lineagesDalai Lamas, Panchen Lamas, Karmapas

A tulku (Tibetan: བོཙ་འྀ་, Wylie: sprul sku) is a person recognised in Tibetan Buddhism as the reincarnation of a deceased master, who is believed to have consciously chosen to be reborn in order to continue teaching the Dharma. The tulku system is a distinctive feature of Tibetan Buddhism; the person is often identified in childhood through dreams, omens, oracular consultation, and tests in which the child is asked to recognise possessions of the predecessor.

History

The first recognised tulku lineage was that of the Karmapas, beginning with Karma Pakshi (1204–1283), the second Karmapa, who was identified as the reincarnation of Düsum Khyenpa. The institution subsequently spread through all schools of Tibetan Buddhism, most famously in the lineage of the Dalai Lamas, which dates from Gendun Drup (1391–1474). Recognised tulkus traditionally head monasteries and hold their predecessor's estates and titles, receiving intensive monastic education from a young age.

Recognition and controversy

Recognition processes have sometimes produced rival candidates — most prominently in the cases of the 17th Karmapa and the 11th Panchen Lama — and the Chinese government asserts regulatory authority over tulku recognition within China, a position contested by the Tibetan exile community and the 14th Dalai Lama, who has stated that his own succession will be decided by the Tibetan Buddhist community.
