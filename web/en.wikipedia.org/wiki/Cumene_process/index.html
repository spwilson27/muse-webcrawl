Cumene process

Placeholder image: Cumene process

The cumene process (also called the cumene–phenol process or Hock process) is an industrial method for synthesizing phenol and acetone from benzene and propylene. It accounts for the overwhelming majority of world phenol production. The process was developed in the 1940s, with key work by Heinrich Hock and colleagues in Germany.

Process steps

The process proceeds in three main stages:

  1. Alkylation: Benzene is alkylated with propylene, typically over an acid catalyst (originally solid phosphoric acid; modern plants use zeolite catalysts), to form cumene (isopropylbenzene).
  2. Oxidation: Cumene is oxidized with air at around 100–130 °C to form cumene hydroperoxide. The reaction proceeds by a radical mechanism and is run to partial conversion for safety and selectivity.
  3. Cleavage: Cumene hydroperoxide is treated with dilute sulfuric acid, undergoing the Hock rearrangement to yield phenol and acetone.

Overall reaction

The net transformation converts benzene, propylene, and oxygen into phenol and acetone, with water as the only stoichiometric byproduct. Because acetone is produced in a fixed ratio to phenol, the economics of the process depend partly on the market balance between the two products; excess acetone can be hydrogenated to isopropanol or recycled to propylene.

Byproducts and improvements

The oxidation step also produces smaller amounts of acetophenone and 2-phenyl-2-propanol (dimethylphenylcarbinol), the latter of which can be dehydrated to alpha-methylstyrene, a saleable monomer or intermediate that can be hydrogenated back to cumene. Various process variants and catalyst systems have been developed to improve yield, reduce waste, and enhance safety, since cumene hydroperoxide is a potentially hazardous peroxide.

Significance

Phenol produced by the cumene process is used in phenolic resins, bisphenol A (for polycarbonates and epoxy resins), caprolactam, and other chemicals. The process displaced older methods such as the sulfonation of benzene and chlorobenzene hydrolysis, which generated large amounts of waste salts.
