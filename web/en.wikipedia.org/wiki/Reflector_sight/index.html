Reflector sight

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A reflector sight, or reflex sight, is an optical sight that allows the user to look through a partially reflecting glass element and see an illuminated projection of an aiming point (reticle) superimposed on the field of view. Because the reticle is collimated to infinity, it appears to stay on the target regardless of the eye's position behind the sight, largely eliminating parallax at typical ranges.

History

The reflector sight was patented by Irish optical designer Howard Grubb in 1900. It was widely adopted for aircraft gunsights in both World Wars — the German Revi and British GM Mk II sights are examples — and later for anti-aircraft weapons. Miniaturised, battery-powered red dot sights for small arms appeared in the 1970s, notably the Aimpoint Electronic, and became standard military equipment from the 1990s.

Operation

A light source (today usually an LED) illuminates a reticle, which is reflected by a curved, dichroic-coated mirror or lens into the shooter's eye. The optics collimate the reflected image so the reticle appears focused at infinity, aligned with the weapon's bore axis once zeroed. Modern variants include open (heads-up) sights, compact tube sights, and holographic weapon sights, which use a laser and holographic grating instead of a reflected LED reticle.
