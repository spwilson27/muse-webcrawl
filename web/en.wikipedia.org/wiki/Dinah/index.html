Dinah

In the Book of Genesis, Dinah was the daughter of Jacob and Leah, and the full sister of Simeon and Levi. She is mentioned in Genesis 30:21 as the couple's seventh child and only named daughter. Dinah is principally known from the account in Genesis 34, often called "the rape of Dinah", in which she is taken by Shechem, son of Hamor the Hivite, the prince of the region, and her brothers Simeon and Levi avenge her by slaughtering the men of Shechem's city.

Biblical narrative

According to Genesis 34, Dinah went out to visit the women of the region near Shechem, where Jacob had encamped. Shechem saw her, seized her and lay with her by force. He then fell in love with her and asked his father Hamor to obtain her as his wife. Hamor proposed intermarriage between his people and Jacob's family. Jacob's sons answered deceitfully that their sister could marry Shechem only if all the males of the city were circumcised. On the third day after the circumcisions, while the men were still in pain, Simeon and Levi entered the city, killed every male, including Hamor and Shechem, and took Dinah from Shechem's house. Jacob rebuked his sons for bringing danger upon his household, but they answered: "Should he treat our sister like a prostitute?"

Interpretation

The meaning of the episode and the nature of the offence against Dinah have been debated for centuries. The Hebrew text says Shechem "humbled" or "violated" her, and traditional interpretation understood the act as rape, a reading reflected in most translations. The narrative is unusual in that Dinah herself never speaks. Later Jewish tradition, in works such as the Book of Jubilees and rabbinic midrash, expanded the story, defending the brothers' vengeance and praising Dinah; other traditions proposed that Dinah bore Shechem a daughter. In modern feminist scholarship the chapter is read as a story about honour, intermarriage and the silencing of women, and it has inspired reinterpretations such as Anita Diamant's novel The Red Tent (1997), which gives Dinah a voice.
