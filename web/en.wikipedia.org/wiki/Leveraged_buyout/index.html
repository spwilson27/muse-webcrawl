Leveraged buyout

A leveraged buyout (LBO) is the acquisition of a company using a significant amount of borrowed money (bonds or loans) to meet the cost of acquisition. The assets of the company being acquired are often used as collateral for the loans, along with the assets of the acquiring company. The purpose of leveraged buyouts is to allow companies to make large acquisitions without having to commit a lot of capital.

Structure

In a typical LBO, a private equity firm forms an acquisition vehicle that raises debt — bank loans, high-yield ("junk") bonds and mezzanine finance — amounting to 60–90% of the purchase price, with the fund contributing the remainder as equity. After the acquisition, the target's cash flows are used to pay interest and repay principal. The sponsor typically plans to exit in four to seven years through a sale, recapitalisation or initial public offering. Because the equity cheque is small relative to the enterprise value, successful LBOs can generate very high returns on equity; conversely, leverage magnifies losses and raises the risk of bankruptcy.

History

LBOs emerged in the 1960s and 1970s through firms such as Kohlberg Kravis Roberts (KKR) and Thomas H. Lee Partners. The market boomed in the 1980s, financed by the high-yield bond market associated with Michael Milken and Drexel Burnham Lambert. The decade culminated in KKR's 1989 buyout of RJR Nabisco for about $25 billion (roughly $31 billion including assumed debt), then the largest LBO in history and the subject of the book and film Barbarians at the Gate.

A second, larger boom in 2005–2007 produced mega-buyouts such as TXU (Energy Future Holdings, ~$45 billion), Equity Office Properties (~$39 billion), HCA (~$33 billion) and First Data (~$29 billion). The 2007–2008 financial crisis sharply reduced deal activity and several highly leveraged deals failed — TXU's successor entered bankruptcy in 2014, as did Toys "R" Us in 2017, a case often cited in debates about private equity's effects on employment and investment.

Characteristics and criticism

Attractive LBO targets typically have stable, predictable cash flows, low existing leverage, strong market positions and assets that can secure borrowing. Proponents argue that LBOs impose financial discipline, align management incentives through equity ownership, and improve operations. Critics argue that high leverage burdens companies with debt service, encourages asset sales, layoffs and underinvestment, and transfers value from employees, creditors and taxpayers (through interest tax deductibility) to sponsors.

Related strategies

Related transaction types include the management buyout (MBO), in which the target's own managers lead the acquisition; the management buy-in (MBI); growth-equity investments; and leveraged recapitalisations, in which a company borrows to pay a special dividend without changing ownership.
