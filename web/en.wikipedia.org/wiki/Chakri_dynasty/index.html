Chakri dynasty

The Chakri dynasty (Thai: ราชวงศ์จักรี) is the current reigning royal house of the Kingdom of Thailand, founded in 1782 by King Rama I. Its kings have reigned from Bangkok for more than two centuries, guiding Siam — renamed Thailand in 1939 — through the colonial era (as Southeast Asia's only uncolonised state), the end of absolute monarchy in 1932, and into the modern constitutional era.

History

The dynasty was founded by the general Chao Phraya Chakri, who ascended the throne as Rama I in 1782 after the fall of the Thonburi kingdom of King Taksin, and moved the capital across the Chao Phraya River to Bangkok. The early Chakri kings consolidated the kingdom and codified law and literature, including the Thai version of the Ramakien epic. Rama IV (Mongkut) and Rama V (Chulalongkorn) cautiously modernised administration, abolished slavery and corvée, and played European powers against one another to preserve independence. The revolution of 1932 ended absolute monarchy; Rama VII abdicated in 1935. Rama IX, Bhumibol Adulyadej, reigned from 1946 until his death in 2016 — seventy years, making him one of the longest-reigning monarchs in history — and became a unifying national figure. His son Maha Vajiralongkorn succeeded him as Rama X.

Regnal naming

All Chakri kings are styled "Rama" followed by a regnal number, a convention popularised by Rama VI, partly for the convenience of foreigners. In Thai, each king is known by a lengthy ceremonial name, and Bangkok's formal name enumerates the dynasty's founding. Chakri Day, 6 April, is a public holiday in Thailand commemorating the dynasty's founding.
