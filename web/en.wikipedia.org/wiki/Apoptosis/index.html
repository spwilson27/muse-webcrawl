Apoptosis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Apoptosis is a form of programmed cell death that occurs in multicellular organisms: an orderly, controlled process in which a cell dismantles itself and is absorbed by neighbours or phagocytes without causing Inflammation. It sculpts tissues during development, removes superfluous or damaged cells throughout life, and balances cell division to maintain tissue size.

Mechanism

Apoptosis is executed by protease enzymes called caspases. It can be triggered from outside the cell by death-receptor signals (the extrinsic pathway) or from inside by stress, DNA damage or withdrawal of growth signals (the intrinsic pathway), which acts through the mitochondria and the Bcl-2 protein family. The dying cell shrinks, its chromatin condenses, its membrane blebs, and it breaks into membrane-wrapped apoptotic bodies displaying 'eat-me' signals.

Importance

Apoptosis separates the fingers in the developing hand, deletes self-reactive immune cells, and sheds the uterine lining. Too little apoptosis lets damaged cells survive and contributes to cancer and autoimmune disease; too much contributes to neurodegeneration and tissue loss after stroke. Many cancer drugs aim to restore apoptotic pathways. The process was characterised in 1972 by Kerr, Wyllie and Currie, and its genetic control in the worm Caenorhabditis elegans earned the 2002 Nobel Prize for Brenner, Horvitz and Sulston.
