The Wayuu (also spelled Wayu, Wayúu, Guajiro, or Wahiro) are an Indigenous American ethnic group of the La Guajira Peninsula in northeastern Colombia and northwestern Venezuela. They are the largest Indigenous group in both countries, numbering several hundred thousand. The Wayuu speak Wayuunaiki, a language of the Arawakan family, and many also speak Spanish.

Society and culture

Wayuu society is organised into matrilineal clans, known as eiruku, each associated with a territory, an animal symbol, and a cemetery. Children belong to their mother's clan, and inheritance and names pass through the maternal line; the maternal uncle traditionally holds authority over his sisters' children. Disputes are settled by a pütchipü'ü, a palabrero or "word-carrier," who mediates compensation according to customary law — a practice recognised by UNESCO in 2010 as Intangible Cultural Heritage.

Wayuu women are renowned for vivid crocheted bags called mochilas, woven hammocks (chinchorros), and brightly coloured dresses (mantas). Traditional livelihoods include goat herding, fishing, salt extraction at Manaure, and trade — including a long history of commerce that long predates the modern border.

History and challenges

The Wayuu famously resisted Spanish colonisation, retaining substantial autonomy in the arid Guajira desert. In recent decades they have faced severe pressures: coal mining at Cerrejón, one of the world's largest open-pit mines, drought, water scarcity, malnutrition among children, and cross-border smuggling economies. Wayuu political movements have pressed for water rights, territorial protection, and recognition of their binational status.
