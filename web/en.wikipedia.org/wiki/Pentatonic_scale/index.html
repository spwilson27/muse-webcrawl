A pentatonic scale is a musical scale with five notes per octave, in contrast to the seven-note heptatonic scales (such as the major scale) dominant in Western classical music. Pentatonic scales occur independently in musical traditions all over the world and are among the oldest scale types known.

Types

Anhemitonic pentatonic scales contain no semitones. The major pentatonic scale consists of the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 5th, and 6th degrees of the major scale (C–D–E–G–A in C major), while its relative minor, the minor pentatonic, uses the 1st, flattened 3rd, 4th, 5th, and flattened 7th degrees (A–C–D–E–G in A minor). Hemitonic pentatonic scales include semitones; examples include the Japanese insen and yo scales and the scales of Indonesian gamelan music, notably pélog and sléndro.

Distribution

Pentatonicism is characteristic of much East and Southeast Asian music, West African music and its diasporic descendants, Andean music, Celtic folk music, Hungarian folk music, and Native American traditions. In Western popular music, the minor pentatonic scale is the backbone of blues, rock, and heavy metal improvisation, while the major pentatonic is pervasive in country and folk melodies — "Amazing Grace" and "Auld Lang Syne" are standard examples of pentatonic tunes.

Because anhemitonic pentatonic scales avoid semitone clashes, nearly any combination of their notes sounds consonant, which makes them a common first tool in music education — Carl Orff's Schulwerk instruments are tuned pentatonically for this reason — and in improvisation pedagogy.
