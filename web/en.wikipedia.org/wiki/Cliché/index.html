Cliché

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cliché is an element of an artistic work, saying, or idea that has become overused to the point of losing its original meaning or effect, even to the point of being trite or irritating, especially when at some earlier time it was considered meaningful or novel.

Etymology

The word is borrowed from French, where it was originally the past participle of clicher, "to click." In nineteenth-century printing, a cliché was a stereotype plate — a block cast from movable type so that a phrase or image could be reproduced endlessly; the word is thought to imitate the sound the matrix made striking the molten metal. By extension, any phrase "cast" ready-made for repeated use became a cliché.

Use

In language, clichés include stock phrases such as "at the end of the day," "think outside the box," and "avoid it like the plague." In literature and film, the term extends to overused plot devices, character types, and settings. Writers' guides from George Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" onward have counselled against clichés on the grounds that they substitute ready-made phrases for fresh thought — though commentators also note that clichés persist because they are memorable, rhythmical, and instantly understood, and skilled writers sometimes revive them ironically or literally.
