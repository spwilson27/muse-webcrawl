Latin square

In combinatorics and in experimental design, a Latin square is an n × n array filled with n different symbols, each occurring exactly once in each row and exactly once in each column. The name derives from the papers of Leonhard Euler, who used Latin characters as the symbols. An example of a 3 × 3 Latin square is:

ABC
CAB
BCA

History

Latin squares predate Euler: arrangements equivalent to them appeared in Korean mathematician Choi Seok-jeong's work (1700) and in earlier Islamic and medieval sources, and were used as amulets and in card arrangements. Euler systematically studied them in the 18th century and posed the famous thirty-six officers problem — asking whether two orthogonal Latin squares of order 6 exist — which was shown to be impossible by Gaston Tarry in 1900. Euler's conjecture that no orthogonal pair exists for any order 4k + 2 was disproved by Bose, Shrikhande, and Parker in 1959–1960 for all larger orders.

Reduced form and enumeration

A Latin square is said to be reduced (or normalised) if both its first row and first column are in their natural order. Any Latin square can be reduced by permuting rows and columns. The numbers of Latin squares of orders 1 through 11 are known exactly; the count grows extremely fast — there are 61,479,419,904,000 reduced squares of order 8 alone (and vastly more unreduced). No closed formula for the number of Latin squares is known.

Orthogonal Latin squares

Two Latin squares of the same order are orthogonal if, when superimposed, every ordered pair of symbols occurs exactly once. A set of pairwise orthogonal Latin squares (MOLS) of order n has at most n − 1 members; a complete set exists whenever n is a prime power, and is equivalent to a finite projective plane of order n. Sudoku puzzles are Latin squares of order 9 (on digits) with an additional constraint on the 3 × 3 blocks; the completed grid of a Sudoku is a Latin square.

Applications

Latin squares are used in the design of experiments to control for two sources of nuisance variation (rows and columns) while testing treatments, notably in agricultural trials. They also appear in algebra — the multiplication table (Cayley table) of a quasigroup with the Latin square property is a Latin square — in error-correcting codes, in scheduling, and in recreational mathematics.
