Mawlid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Mawlid celebrations include processions, recitation and charitable giving.

Mawlid (Arabic: مَوْلِد, romanized: mawlid), also called Mawlid al-Nabi or Milad, is the observance of the birthday of the Islamic prophet Muhammad, which according to tradition occurred on 12 Rabi' al-awwal, the third month of the Islamic calendar. The word mawlid is Arabic for "birth" or "birthday".

History

The celebration developed gradually in the early centuries of Islam. The Fatimid caliphs in Egypt held court celebrations of the Prophet's birthday in the 10th–11th centuries, and public festivities spread under the Ayyubids and Mamluks; the ruler of Erbil, Muzaffar al-Din Gökböri, sponsored elaborate public celebrations in the early 13th century. Sufi orders played a central role in popularising the festival, composing poems in praise of Muhammad such as Süleyman Çelebi's Turkish Mevlid and the Arabic Mawlid al-Barzanji. Under the Ottoman Empire the Mawlid became an official state celebration.

Observance

Mawlid is a public holiday in most Muslim-majority countries, with notable exceptions such as Saudi Arabia and Qatar, where Salafi and Wahhabi scholars regard the celebration as an unlawful innovation (bid'ah). Observances typically include sermons and recitations of the Prophet's biography, poetry in his praise, processions, feasting and charity. In some countries the festivities extend over much of the month of Rabi' al-awwal.
