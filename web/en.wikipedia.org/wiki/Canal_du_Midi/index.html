The Canal du Midi (Occitan: Canal del Miègjorn) is a 240 km (150 mi) long canal in southern France. Originally named the Canal royal en Languedoc, it was renamed by French revolutionaries in 1789. It connects Toulouse to the Mediterranean Sea at the Étang de Thau, and together with the later Canal de Garonne forms the Canal des Deux Mers, joining the Atlantic to the Mediterranean.

Considered one of the greatest construction works of the 17th century, the canal was built under the direction of Pierre-Paul Riquet, a salt-tax farmer from Béziers, who persuaded King Louis XIV's finance minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert of the project. Work began in 1666 and the canal opened in May 1681, a few months after Riquet's death. Riquet solved the key problem of feeding water to the summit level by building the Bassin de Saint-Ferréol and a system of feeder channels from the Montagne Noire.

Engineering

The canal climbs 57 m from Toulouse to the summit at the Seuil de Naurouze and descends 189 m to the Mediterranean. It contains 63 locks, along with aqueducts, tunnels (including the Malpas Tunnel, the first canal tunnel), the Fonserannes Lock staircase near Béziers, and the Orb Aqueduct. Rows of plane trees, planted to stabilise the banks, give the canal its characteristic appearance.

History and use

Built to carry wheat, wine and textiles while avoiding the long sea voyage around Spain, the canal prospered until the coming of the railways in the 19th century. Commercial traffic declined and ended in the 1970s; today the canal is used chiefly by leisure boats and barges, with its towpaths popular with cyclists and walkers. It was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1996.
