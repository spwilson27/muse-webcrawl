FODMAP

Common foods rich in fermentable short-chain carbohydrates

FODMAPs are short-chain carbohydrates that are poorly absorbed in the small intestine and are prone to absorbing water and being fermented in the colon. The acronym stands for fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols. The term was coined by a research team at Monash University in Melbourne, Australia, in the early 2000s.

FODMAPs include the sugars lactose and fructose when present in excess of glucose, the sugar alcohols sorbitol and mannitol, the fructans found in wheat, onions and garlic, and the galacto-oligosaccharides found in legumes. Because they are osmotically active and rapidly fermented by gut bacteria, FODMAPs can draw water into the bowel and generate gas, producing symptoms in susceptible people.

Low-FODMAP diet

A low-FODMAP diet restricts these carbohydrates and is used in the management of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and other functional gastrointestinal disorders. Clinical trials have found that a substantial proportion of people with IBS report symptom improvement on the diet, although long-term adherence is difficult and the diet is usually implemented in phases: an elimination phase of roughly two to six weeks, a systematic reintroduction phase to identify individual triggers, and a personalised maintenance phase.

Critics note that the diet can reduce intake of prebiotic fibres and may alter the gut microbiota, so it is generally recommended under the supervision of a dietitian rather than as a permanent eating pattern.

Mechanism

FODMAPs are small, osmotically active molecules. In the small intestine they draw water into the lumen; in the large intestine they are rapidly fermented by bacteria, producing hydrogen, carbon dioxide and methane. In people with visceral hypersensitivity, the resulting distension of the gut wall triggers pain, bloating, flatulence and altered bowel habits.
