Kamoro people

The Kamoro people are an ethnic group of approximately 18,000 people living in the lowland coastal region of Mimika Regency, Central Papua, Indonesia, along the northern shore of the Arafura Sea. They speak the Kamoro language, a member of the Asmat–Kamoro family of Trans–New Guinea languages. Neighbouring peoples include the Asmat to the east and the Amungme in the highlands to the north.

Culture

Traditional Kamoro life centred on semi-nomadic villages and subsistence fishing, hunting and gathering, especially of sago and river and coastal fish. The Kamoro are renowned woodcarvers, producing ritual objects such as mbis poles, wurramon shields and drums connected with feasts and funerary ceremonies. Their carving tradition is celebrated annually at the Kamoro Kakuru Festival, established in the 1990s to promote Kamoro art and culture.

Modern context

Kamoro lands lie adjacent to the Grasberg mine, one of the world's largest gold and copper mines, operated by Freeport Indonesia. The Kamoro, together with the Amungme, are recognised as the customary landowners of the area and receive a share of mining-related funds and development programmes, though disputes over land, environmental damage and benefit distribution have continued.
