Two-streams hypothesis

The two-streams hypothesis is a model of the neural processing of vision, first proposed in influential form by David Milner and Melvyn Goodale in 1992. It holds that visual information leaving the occipital lobe follows two functional pathways: a ventral stream (the "what" pathway) projecting to the temporal lobe, concerned with object identification and recognition, and a dorsal stream (the "where/how" pathway) projecting to the parietal lobe, concerned with spatial awareness and the visual guidance of action.

Evidence

The model grew out of lesion studies in monkeys by Ungerleider and Mishkin, and was reframed by Milner and Goodale as a distinction between vision-for-perception and vision-for-action. Key evidence comes from neuropsychological double dissociations: the patient D.F., with ventral-stream damage causing visual form agnosia, could not describe the orientation of a slot yet could post a card through it accurately, while patients with dorsal-stream damage (optic ataxia) can recognise objects but cannot act on them under visual guidance.

Extensions and criticism

An analogous two-stream division has been proposed for hearing (a ventral "what" and dorsal "where/how" auditory pathway) and forms part of the dual-stream model of language processing. Later work emphasises that the two streams interact extensively and that the division is graded rather than absolute, but the hypothesis remains a foundational framework in visual neuroscience.
