The Elementary Education Act 1870 (33 & 34 Vict. c. 75), commonly known as Forster's Education Act, was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that laid the foundations of primary education in England and Wales. Drafted by William Edward Forster and passed by William Gladstone's Liberal government, it was the first national framework for schooling provided by the state.

Provisions

The Act divided England and Wales into school districts and required an assessment of the elementary education available in each. Where existing provision — chiefly the voluntary schools run by religious bodies — was insufficient, elected school boards were to be established with power to build and maintain new schools, funded by local rates. Such board schools were to be non-denominational in their religious teaching. School boards could make attendance compulsory through local byelaws, though the Act itself did not impose national compulsion; attendance only became compulsory nationwide under the Act of 1880.

Religious settlement

The Act embodied a compromise between Anglican, Nonconformist and secular interests. Under the Cowper-Temple clause, no religious catechism or formulary distinctive of any particular denomination could be taught in board schools. Parents could withdraw their children from any religious instruction, and public inspection of religious teaching was excluded.

Legacy

The Act did not make education free — fees were still charged in most board schools until 1891 — nor immediately universal, but it created the machinery of a national system. School boards built thousands of new schools, and the Act is generally regarded as the starting point of state elementary education in England and Wales. Later reforms, especially the Education Act 1902, replaced the school boards with local education authorities.
