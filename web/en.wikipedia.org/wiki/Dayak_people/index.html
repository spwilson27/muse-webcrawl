Dayak people

The Dayak (older spelling Dyak) are the diverse indigenous peoples of Borneo, the island shared by Malaysia (Sarawak and Sabah), Indonesia (Kalimantan) and Brunei. "Dayak" is a collective term for more than 200 ethnic subgroups, each with its own language, customs and laws, traditionally living in the island's interior along its great rivers.

Groups and languages

Major groupings include the Iban (Sea Dayaks), Bidayuh (Land Dayaks), Kayan, Kenyah, Penan, Ngaju, Barito and many others. Their languages belong to several branches of the Austronesian family. Population estimates for Dayak peoples overall run to several million.

Culture

Traditional Dayak society centred on the longhouse — a communal dwelling raised on stilts that housed many families. Wet-rice and swidden farming, river trade and forest products sustained the economy. Historically, some groups practised headhunting, connected with ritual and status, though this ended under colonial rule in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Dayak art is noted for elaborate wood carving, beadwork, woven textiles and tattooing.

Religion and modern life

Many Dayaks today are Christians or Muslims, while the Ngaju and others maintain the indigenous religion Kaharingan, officially recognised in Indonesia under the Hindu umbrella. Since the 20th century, logging, oil palm plantations and transmigration have put pressure on Dayak land rights, and indigenous land tenure (adat) remains a central political issue in both Malaysian and Indonesian Borneo.
