A fungus gnat (family Sciaridae), the group most often seen around houseplants.

Fungus gnats are small, dark, delicate flies of the infraorder Bibionomorpha, comprising several families, principally the Sciaridae (dark-winged fungus gnats) and the Mycetophilidae. Adults are typically 2–8 mm long, mosquito-like but weak fliers, and are found in moist environments where fungi grow.

Life cycle

Females lay eggs in damp soil, leaf litter, decaying wood or fungal fruiting bodies. The legless larvae — translucent with shiny black heads in the Sciaridae — feed on fungi, decaying organic matter, and sometimes fine roots and root hairs, passing through four instars before pupating in the substrate. The whole cycle can complete in three to four weeks at room temperature, allowing rapid population growth indoors.

As pests

Fungus gnats are among the most familiar houseplant pests: clouds of adults rise from overwatered potting compost. Adults are harmless and short-lived, but heavy larval infestations can damage seedlings and cuttings by feeding on roots, and larvae can vector fungal pathogens such as Pythium and Fusarium. In commercial mushroom production, sciarid and phorid "mushroom flies" are serious pests, reducing yield and spreading disease.

Control

Cultural control relies on letting the growing medium dry between waterings and removing decaying matter. Yellow sticky traps catch adults for monitoring and reduction. Biological control uses the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis subsp. israelensis (Bti), predatory mites such as Stratiolaelaps, rove beetles, and entomopathogenic nematodes (Steinernema feltiae), which attack the larvae in the soil.

Notable species

The glow-worm Arachnocampa luminosa of New Zealand caves is a fungus gnat (Keroplatidae) whose larvae are bioluminescent, using sticky, glowing threads to snare prey in cave ceilings. Some Mycetophila species construct webs for the same purpose.
