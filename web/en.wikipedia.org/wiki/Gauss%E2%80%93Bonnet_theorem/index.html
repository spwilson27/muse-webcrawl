Gauss–Bonnet theorem

The Gauss–Bonnet theorem, or Gauss–Bonnet formula, is a fundamental result in differential geometry that relates the geometry of a surface (its curvature) to its topology (its Euler characteristic). It states that the integral of the Gaussian curvature over a compact two-dimensional Riemannian manifold equals 2π times the Euler characteristic of the manifold.

Statement

For a compact two-dimensional Riemannian manifold M without boundary, the theorem reads ∫M K dA = 2πχ(M), where K is the Gaussian curvature, dA the area element, and χ(M) the Euler characteristic. For a sphere of radius r, K = 1/r² and the area is 4πr², giving 4π = 2πχ, hence χ = 2.

History

Versions of the result were known to Carl Friedrich Gauss, who published work on the curvature of surfaces in 1827, and it was later extended by Pierre Ossian Bonnet in 1848 to surfaces with boundary. A far-reaching generalization is the Atiyah–Singer index theorem; the Chern–Gauss–Bonnet theorem extends the formula to even-dimensional manifolds.

Interpretation and examples

The theorem implies that the total curvature of a closed surface depends only on its genus: a torus has χ = 0 and therefore total curvature zero, while surfaces of higher genus have negative total curvature. It is a striking example of a local geometric quantity determining a global topological invariant.
