Attachment in children concerns the emotional bond that develops between an infant and their primary caregivers, and the patterns of behaviour that result. According to attachment theory, first formulated by John Bowlby, infants are biologically predisposed to seek proximity to caregivers, especially under stress, and the quality of early attachment shapes later social and emotional development.

Strange Situation and attachment styles

Mary Ainsworth's Strange Situation procedure (1970s) observed toddlers' responses to separation from and reunion with their caregiver in an unfamiliar room. It identified three organised patterns: secure attachment (distress at separation, comfort and recovery on reunion — about 60–65% in typical US samples), insecure-avoidant (apparent indifference masking stress), and insecure-ambivalent/resistant (intense distress and difficulty settling). Mary Main later added disorganised attachment, associated with frightened or frightening caregiving and with maltreatment, in which the child shows contradictory, apprehensive behaviour.

Development and outcomes

Clear-cut attachment behaviour typically emerges between about 6–8 months and 2 years, accompanied by separation anxiety and stranger wariness. Secure attachment is associated, on average, with later social competence, emotion regulation and school readiness, though outcomes are probabilistic and influenced by later experience and temperament. Severe early deprivation can produce attachment disorders such as reactive attachment disorder.

Criticism and culture

Researchers have debated how far the Strange Situation categories generalise across cultures with different caregiving norms (such as multiple caregiving or near-constant physical contact), and whether categories or continuous dimensions better describe attachment security.
