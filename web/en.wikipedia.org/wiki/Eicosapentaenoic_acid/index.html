Eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA; also icosapentaenoic acid) is an omega-3 fatty acid. In physiological literature, it is given the name 20:5(n-3). It also has the trivial name timnodonic acid. In chemical structure, EPA is a carboxylic acid with a 20-carbon chain and five cis double bonds; the first double bond is located at the third carbon from the omega end.

EPA, together with docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), is found principally in fatty fish and fish oil. It is a precursor for prostaglandin-3 (which inhibits platelet aggregation), thromboxane-3, and leukotriene-5 eicosanoids, which are generally less potent inflammatory mediators than the corresponding compounds derived from arachidonic acid.

Sources

EPA is obtained in the human diet by eating oily fish such as cod liver, herring, mackerel, salmon, menhaden, and sardines, or by taking fish oil supplements. It is also found in human breast milk. Fish do not themselves produce EPA in large quantities but obtain it from the algae they consume; commercially, EPA is also produced from microalgae such as Nannochloropsis and the diatom Phaeodactylum tricornutum. Humans can convert some dietary alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) to EPA, but the conversion efficiency is low (commonly estimated at under 10 percent).

Clinical significance

EPA has been studied for effects on inflammation, triglyceride levels, and cardiovascular outcomes. Icosapent ethyl, a purified ethyl ester of EPA, is approved as an adjunct to statin therapy to reduce cardiovascular risk in certain patients with elevated triglycerides, based on the REDUCE-IT trial, which reported a significant reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events. Evidence for benefits in depression and other conditions is mixed and remains under investigation.

Biosynthesis

In humans, EPA can be synthesized from ALA via desaturase and elongase enzymes, or retroconverted from DHA. In marine food webs, EPA originates mainly from photosynthetic microalgae.
