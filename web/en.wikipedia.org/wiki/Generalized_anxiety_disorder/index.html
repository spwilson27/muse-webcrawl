Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) is a mental disorder characterized by excessive, uncontrollable, and often irrational worry about everyday events and activities. The worry occurs more days than not for at least six months and is accompanied by symptoms such as restlessness, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, and disturbed sleep, causing significant distress or impairment in functioning.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on criteria in the DSM-5 or ICD-11 and typically involves a clinical interview; screening instruments such as the GAD-7 questionnaire are commonly used. Other medical conditions (for example, thyroid disorders) and other mental disorders must be ruled out. GAD frequently co-occurs with major depressive disorder, other anxiety disorders, and substance use disorders.

Causes and risk factors

The exact cause is not fully understood. A combination of genetic vulnerability, neurobiological factors involving neurotransmitter systems, personality traits such as neuroticism, and environmental stressors is thought to contribute. Lifetime prevalence is estimated at roughly 3–6% of adults in many Western countries, with women affected about twice as often as men.

Treatment

First-line treatments include cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and pharmacotherapy with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) or serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs). Other options include pregabalin, buspirone, and, generally for short-term use, benzodiazepines. Lifestyle measures, exercise, and stress-management techniques may also help. With treatment, many people achieve substantial improvement, though the course of GAD is often chronic with fluctuating symptoms.
