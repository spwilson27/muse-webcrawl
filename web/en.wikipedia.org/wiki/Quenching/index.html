Quenching

In materials science, quenching is the rapid cooling of a workpiece in water, gas, oil, polymer, air, or other fluids to obtain certain material properties. It is a type of heat treating that prevents undesired low-temperature processes, such as phase transformations, from occurring: by rapidly reducing the temperature, quenching "freezes" a high-temperature structure in place, practically reducing the window of time in which equilibrium transformations can happen.

Quenching a heated steel workpiece

Quench hardening of steel

In metallurgy, quenching is most commonly used to harden steel. Austenitized steel cooled quickly enough avoids the formation of soft pearlite and instead transforms to martensite, a very hard but brittle microstructure. The quenched part is usually then tempered — reheated to a lower temperature — to trade some hardness for toughness.

The cooling medium determines the quench severity: brine and water are the most severe, followed by oils, molten salts, and gas quenching in vacuum furnaces. Uneven cooling can cause distortion or cracking, so quench practice balances cooling rate against these risks. Related heat treatments include annealing, normalizing, and case hardening (such as carburizing followed by quenching).

Other uses

Rapid cooling is also used to produce metallic glasses, to retain solute in supersaturated solid solutions in aluminium alloys (solution heat treating), and in superconductivity, where a "quench" denotes the sudden loss of the superconducting state in a magnet.
