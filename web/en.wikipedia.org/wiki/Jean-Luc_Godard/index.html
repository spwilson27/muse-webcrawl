Jean-Luc Godard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Jean-Luc Godard behind dark glasses with a cigarette in the 1960s, on the set of one of the New Wave films that remade cinema]

Jean-Luc Godard (3 December 1930 – 13 September 2022) was a French-Swiss film director, screenwriter and critic, the most influential filmmaker of the French New Wave and a permanent revolutionary of film form. His first feature Breathless (1960) — jump cuts, location shooting, a Bogart-haunted petty criminal (Jean-Paul Belmondo) and Jean Seberg's gamine — detonated the rules of mainstream cinema.

Through the sixties he made a film a season that redefined its genre: Vivre sa vie (1962), Contempt (1963), Band of Outsiders (1964), Alphaville (1965), Pierrot le Fou (1965), Weekend (1967). After 1968 he dissolved into Maoist collectives (the Dziga Vertov Group), then returned with video experiments, the monumental essay Histoire(s) du cinéma (1988–98) and late features (Film Socialisme, 2010; Goodbye to Language, 2014, in 3D) made from Rolle, Switzerland.

The New Wave critic-director

Godard wrote for Cahiers du Cinéma under André Bazin, theorising with Truffaut and Rivette that directors are authors. His dictum, in its usual paraphrase — that a film needs a beginning, a middle and an end, though not necessarily in that order — is the New Wave's ars poetica. Breathless's techniques (handheld camera, direct address, quoting trash and classics in one breath) became the common language of modern film from Scorsese to Tarantino.

Later life and death

Godard remained cinema's licensed heretic into his nineties, spurning honours (an honorary Oscar in 2010 went uncollected) and festivals that begged for him. Suffering from multiple illnesses, he died by assisted suicide at Rolle in September 2022, aged 91 — his death front-page news across France, where presidents mourned the man who had called cinema, in a famous aphorism, truth at twenty-four frames a second (rendered here in close paraphrase).
