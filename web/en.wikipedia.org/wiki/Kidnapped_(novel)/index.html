Kidnapped (novel)

David Balfour and Alan Breck in flight across the Highlands
AuthorRobert Louis Stevenson
LanguageEnglish, Lowland Scots
GenreHistorical fiction, adventure novel
Set inScotland, 1751
Published1886 (serialised in Young Folks; book by Cassell)
SequelCatriona (1893)

Kidnapped is a historical fiction adventure novel by the Scottish author Robert Louis Stevenson, first published in 1886. Written as a boys' novel and serialised in the magazine Young Folks from May to July 1886, it tells of young David Balfour's adventures in the aftermath of the Jacobite rising of 1745, and weaves real events — above all the Appin murder of 1752 — into its fictional plot. The novel is regarded as a classic of Scottish literature and of the adventure genre.

Plot summary

In 1751, seventeen-year-old David Balfour, newly orphaned, travels to the House of Shaws near Edinburgh to claim his inheritance from his miserly uncle Ebenezer, who instead has him kidnapped and put aboard the brig Covenant, bound for the Carolinas where he is to be sold into servitude. At sea the ship runs down a boat and takes aboard its sole survivor, Alan Breck Stewart, a flamboyant Jacobite agent carrying rent money for his exiled clan chief. When the captain plots to murder Alan, the two defend the roundhouse together; soon after, the Covenant is wrecked on the Torran Rocks off Mull.

David struggles ashore in the Highlands and, after witnessing the political tensions of the region, is reunited with Alan. In the novel's famous central episode, Colin Roy Campbell, the government factor known as the Red Fox, is shot by an unseen assassin — the historical Appin murder. David and Alan, prime suspects, flee across the heather, hiding by day and moving by night, in the episode remembered as "the Flight in the Heather". Reaching the Lowlands exhausted, David confronts his uncle with the help of the lawyer Mr Rankeillor, forcing Ebenezer to settle a fair share of the estate on him.

Historical basis

The Appin murder — the 1752 shooting of Colin Campbell of Glenure and the subsequent trial and hanging of James Stewart of the Glens, whom many considered innocent — fascinated Stevenson. Alan Breck Stewart was a real person, though Stevenson reshaped him freely. The depiction of the post-'45 Highlands, disarmed, garrisoned and administered by Campbell agents, draws on Stevenson's research and family travels.

Reception and legacy

Kidnapped was praised on publication and never went out of print; Jorge Luis Borges and other writers admired its pace and economy. Stevenson published a sequel, Catriona (also called David Balfour), in 1893. The story has been adapted many times for film and television, including versions in 1960 (Disney) and 1971, and a statue of David and Alan stands in Edinburgh. Memorials at Corstorphine and along the "Kidnapped Trail" in Mull and Appin mark the route of the fictional flight.
