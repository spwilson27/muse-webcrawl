Zonal architecture

Zonal architecture is an approach to the electrical and electronic (E/E) architecture of modern vehicles in which functions are grouped by physical location ("zones") rather than by functional domain. A small number of powerful central computers provide the main computing, while zonal controllers near the vehicle's corners gather sensor and actuator connections and communicate with the central computers over high-speed links such as automotive Ethernet.

Background

Traditional vehicle architectures used one electronic control unit (ECU) per function, leading to over a hundred ECUs and very long, heavy wiring harnesses in premium cars. Domain architectures consolidated functions into a handful of domain controllers. Zonal architecture goes further, organizing by position in the vehicle to shorten wiring runs, simplify the harness, and support software-defined vehicle concepts with over-the-air updates.

Advantages and challenges

Advantages claimed for zonal designs include reduced wiring weight and cost, easier scalability across models, and cleaner separation between central software and local I/O. Challenges include the need for robust high-bandwidth networks, power distribution redesign, functional-safety partitioning, and reliable communication between zonal gateways and the central compute platform. The approach has been adopted, in varying degrees, in recent electric vehicles and is a major direction of development across the automotive industry.
