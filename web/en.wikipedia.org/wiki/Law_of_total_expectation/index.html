Law of total expectation

The law of total expectation, also known as the law of iterated expectations or the tower property, is a fundamental identity in probability theory. It states that the expected value of a random variable equals the expected value of its conditional expectation given another random variable:

E[X] = E[E[X | Y]]

Conditioning partitions the sample space; averaging the conditional means recovers the overall mean.

Statement

If X and Y are random variables on the same probability space with E|X| finite, then E[X] = E[E[X|Y]]. In the discrete case this becomes a weighted sum: E[X] = Σy E[X | Y = y] · P(Y = y), analogous to the law of total probability.

Uses

The identity underlies recursive expectation arguments throughout probability and statistics — for example in analysing random sums, branching processes, and Wald's equation — and, together with the law of total variance, forms a standard tool for computing moments by conditioning.
