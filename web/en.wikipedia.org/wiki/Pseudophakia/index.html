Pseudophakia

Placeholder image — a posterior chamber intraocular lens

Pseudophakia is the condition of the eye in which the natural crystalline lens has been replaced by an artificial intraocular lens (IOL). An eye with an implanted IOL is described as pseudophakic. The term derives from Greek pseudo- ("false") and phakos ("lens"). Pseudophakia is the usual outcome of modern cataract surgery, in which the clouded natural lens is removed, most commonly by phacoemulsification, and a synthetic lens is placed in the capsular bag.

Types of intraocular lens

IOLs are classified by their position in the eye — posterior chamber (in the capsular bag, the most common), anterior chamber, or iris- or scleral-fixated — and by their optical design. Monofocal lenses correct vision at a single distance; multifocal and extended-depth-of-focus lenses aim to provide both distance and near vision; toric lenses correct corneal astigmatism; and accommodating designs attempt to mimic the natural lens's change of focus.

Comparison with aphakia

Pseudophakia contrasts with aphakia, the absence of a lens without an implant, which requires correction with thick spectacles or a contact lens and was the standard result of older intracapsular cataract extraction. The first IOL was implanted by Sir Harold Ridley in London in 1949, inspired by the observation that fragments of acrylic canopy material were tolerated in the eyes of injured wartime pilots.

Complications

Possible issues include posterior capsule opacification (a secondary "cataract" treatable with Nd:YAG laser capsulotomy), lens dislocation or decentration, refractive surprise, glare and dysphotopsias, and, rarely, endophthalmitis.
