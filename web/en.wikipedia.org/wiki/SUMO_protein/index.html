SUMO proteins (Small Ubiquitin-like Modifiers) are a family of small proteins that are covalently attached to, and detached from, other proteins in cells to modify their function. This post-translational modification, called sumoylation, is analogous to ubiquitination but, unlike ubiquitin, SUMO usually does not mark proteins for degradation. Instead it alters a target protein's activity, localisation, stability or interactions.

Structure and family

SUMO proteins are about 100 amino acids long and share the β-grasp fold of ubiquitin, though their sequence identity with ubiquitin is low (around 20%) and their surface charge distribution is quite different. Humans have five paralogues: SUMO1, SUMO2, SUMO3, SUMO4 and SUMO5. SUMO2 and SUMO3 are nearly identical to each other and can form polymeric chains; SUMO1 usually caps or forms monomeric attachments. Budding yeast has a single SUMO, called Smt3.

The sumoylation cycle

SUMO is synthesised as a precursor and matured by SUMO-specific proteases (SENPs in humans, Ulp1/Ulp2 in yeast) to expose a C-terminal glycine-glycine motif. It is then activated by the E1 enzyme (the SAE1/SAE2 heterodimer) in an ATP-dependent step, passed to the sole E2 conjugating enzyme, Ubc9, and ligated — often with the help of E3 ligases such as the PIAS family — to lysine residues of target proteins, typically within the consensus motif ΨKxE (a bulky hydrophobic residue, the target lysine, any residue, and glutamate). SENP proteases also remove SUMO, making the modification dynamic and reversible.

Functions

Sumoylation regulates a wide range of cellular processes, most prominently in the nucleus: transcription, DNA replication and repair, chromosome segregation, nuclear transport through the nuclear pore complex (the first identified SUMO substrate was the pore component RanGAP1), and the cellular response to stress — global sumoylation rises sharply after heat shock or oxidative stress. Dysregulation of sumoylation is implicated in cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, heart disease and viral infection, and the pathway is being explored as a drug target.
