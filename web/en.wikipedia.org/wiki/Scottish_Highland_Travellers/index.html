Scottish Highland Travellers

Scottish Highland Travellers, known in Scottish Gaelic as the Ceàrdannan ("the craftsmen") and in Scots as Nawken, are an indigenous ethnic group of itinerant people native to the Highlands of Scotland. They are distinct from Irish Travellers and from Romani groups, though there has been intermarriage and cultural exchange over centuries. Highland Travellers traditionally worked as tinsmiths, pedlars, pearl-fishers, horse dealers, and seasonal farm labourers, travelling in extended family groups.

Culture and language

Highland Travellers speak varieties of Scots and Gaelic, and a cant known as Beurla-reagaird, a Gaelic-based argot used within the community. Their oral tradition is exceptionally rich: Traveller storytellers and singers were among the most important sources for folklorists, preserving Gaelic wonder tales, ballads, and songs that had faded elsewhere. Notable tradition-bearers include storyteller Duncan Williamson and the Stewart family of Blairgowrie — ballad singer Belle Stewart and her daughter, piper and singer Sheila Stewart.

History

The group's origins are debated; theories link them to displaced craftspeople and metalworkers of early medieval Scotland, to clan followers displaced by social change, and to families forced onto the road by the Highland Clearances and famine. For much of the 20th century they lived in "bow tents" and later caravans, camping on traditional stopping places, many of which were progressively closed off. Like other Traveller groups they faced discrimination, including the notorious mid-20th-century efforts by authorities to settle Traveller children and break up the travelling life.

Recognition

Highland Travellers are recognised as a distinct ethnic group in Scotland, and Scottish Travellers (including Lowland Scottish Gypsy/Travellers) are protected under equality legislation. Campaigners continue to press for preservation of stopping places, language, and cultural heritage.
