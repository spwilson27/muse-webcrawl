Rosh Hashanah

The shofar, apples and honey are symbols of the festival

Rosh Hashanah (Hebrew: רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה, literally "head of the year") is the Jewish New Year. It is observed on the first two days of the month of Tishrei in the Hebrew calendar (usually in September or early October) and marks the beginning of the High Holy Days, a ten-day period of repentance culminating in Yom Kippur.

Meaning and themes

According to tradition, on Rosh Hashanah God inscribes each person's fate for the coming year in the Book of Life, to be sealed on Yom Kippur; the intervening Days of Awe (Yamim Noraim) are given to repentance (teshuvah), prayer, and charity. The day is also regarded as the anniversary of the creation of Adam and Eve.

Customs

The central ritual is hearing the sounding of the shofar, a ram's horn blown in prescribed series of calls (tekiah, shevarim, teruah). Other customs include eating apples dipped in honey, pomegranates, and other symbolic foods (simanim) for a sweet year; round challah bread; the tashlich ceremony, in which sins are symbolically cast into flowing water; and the greeting "Shanah Tovah" ("a good year") or "L'shanah tovah tikatevu" ("may you be inscribed for a good year").

Origins

The Bible calls the day a "memorial of trumpet blasts" (Leviticus 23) and a day of sacred assembly on the first of the seventh month; its identification as the New Year is set out in the Mishnah, which lists four new years for different purposes.
