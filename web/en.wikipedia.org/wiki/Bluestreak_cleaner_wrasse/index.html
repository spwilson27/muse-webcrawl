The bluestreak cleaner wrasse (Labroides dimidiatus) is a species of wrasse widely distributed in the tropical and subtropical waters of the Indo-Pacific and Red Sea. It is one of the best-known cleaner fish, feeding on parasites and dead tissue removed from the skin, gills and mouths of larger fish at specialised "cleaning stations" on coral reefs.

Behaviour

Cleaner wrasses establish territories at prominent reef locations where client fish queue to be cleaned. Clients include groupers, moray eels and sometimes sea turtles and sharks. The wrasse advertises its service with a distinctive up-and-down "dance" and its bold black-and-blue striping. The relationship is a classic example of mutualism, though cleaners occasionally "cheat" by biting healthy mucus instead of parasites, and adjust their behaviour when clients can observe them.

Mimicry

The false cleanerfish (Aspidontus taeniatus), a blenny, mimics the cleaner's appearance and dance, then bites chunks of fin from unsuspecting clients — an example of aggressive mimicry.

Cognition studies

The species has been used in experiments on animal cognition, including mirror self-recognition tests, which some researchers interpret as suggesting a degree of self-awareness.
