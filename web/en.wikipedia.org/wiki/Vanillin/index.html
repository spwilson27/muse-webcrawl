Vanillin is an organic compound, a phenolic aldehyde with the molecular formula C8H8O3. It is the principal flavour and aroma compound of the vanilla bean, constituting roughly 2% of the dry weight of cured pods of Vanilla planifolia, and is one of the most widely used flavouring agents in the world.

Occurrence and extraction

Vanillin occurs in vanilla pods as its glucoside, glucovanillin, which is hydrolysed during curing to free the aromatic aldehyde. It also appears in small quantities in other materials, including aged oak wood (contributing to the flavour of barrel-aged spirits), roasted coffee and some resins. The compound was first isolated from vanilla pods in 1858 by Nicolas-Théodore Gobley, and its structure was established in 1874 by Ferdinand Tiemann and Wilhelm Haarmann, who also achieved its synthesis from coniferin.

Production

Demand for vanillin far exceeds the supply of natural vanilla. Most vanillin is synthesised: historically from eugenol and from lignin sulfonates in paper-mill waste liquor, and today predominantly from petrochemical guaiacol. "Bio-based" vanillin is also produced by microbial fermentation of ferulic acid from rice bran, which can be marketed as a natural flavour in some jurisdictions. Synthetic vanillin and its stronger relative ethyl vanillin flavour the bulk of the world's ice cream, chocolate, baked goods and soft drinks.

Uses

Beyond food flavouring, vanillin is used in perfumery, as an intermediate in pharmaceutical synthesis, and as a staining agent in thin-layer chromatography. It has mild antimicrobial and antioxidant properties under study, and is a precursor in laboratory syntheses of compounds such as L-DOPA.
