Parasitic worm

Groups includedNematoda, Trematoda, Cestoda, Acanthocephala
Common nameHelminths

Parasitic worms, also known as helminths, are a polyphyletic group of macroparasites that live in and feed on living hosts. They receive nourishment and protection from their hosts while potentially causing disease. The major groups are the roundworms (nematodes), the flukes (trematodes), the tapeworms (cestodes), and the thorny-headed worms (acanthocephalans).

Helminth infections are among the most common infections in humans, affecting more than a billion people worldwide, predominantly in tropical and subtropical regions with poor sanitation. Many infections are chronic and cause relatively mild symptoms, but heavy burdens can lead to malnutrition, anemia, impaired growth and cognitive development, and organ damage.

Life cycles

Parasitic worms have varied life cycles, often involving one or more intermediate hosts. Eggs are typically shed in feces; larvae may develop in soil (soil-transmitted helminths such as Ascaris lumbricoides, hookworms and whipworm), in snails (schistosomes and liver flukes), or in arthropod vectors (filarial worms). Humans become infected by ingesting eggs or larvae, eating undercooked meat or fish, or through skin penetration by larvae.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually by microscopic examination of stool for eggs, though blood tests, imaging and antigen detection are used for tissue-dwelling species. Treatment relies on anthelmintic drugs such as albendazole, mebendazole, praziquantel and ivermectin. Mass drug administration campaigns are a mainstay of control programs for neglected tropical diseases.

Prevention

Improved sanitation, clean water, hand washing, wearing shoes, and thorough cooking of meat and fish reduce transmission. The World Health Organization coordinates preventive chemotherapy and WASH (water, sanitation and hygiene) programs in endemic countries.
