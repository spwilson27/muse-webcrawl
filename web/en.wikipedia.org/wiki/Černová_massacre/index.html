The Černová massacre (Slovak: Černovská tragédia; Hungarian: Csernovai tragédia) took place on 27 October 1907 in the village of Černová in the Kingdom of Hungary, then part of Austria-Hungary (today part of Ružomberok, Slovakia), when Hungarian gendarmes opened fire on a crowd of Slovak villagers, killing 15 people and wounding many more. The shooting became an international scandal and a symbol of the Hungarian government's policy of forced Magyarization of the Slovaks.

Background

The villagers of Černová had built a new Roman Catholic church, largely through the efforts and fundraising of their native son, the priest and Slovak national activist Andrej Hlinka. Hlinka had been suspended from his priesthood and barred from consecrating the church by the Hungarian church authorities amid political conflict. The bishop instead appointed a Hungarian-aligned priest to perform the consecration, and the villagers, who wanted the ceremony postponed until Hlinka's appeal to Rome was decided, gathered to block the officials' carriages from entering the village.

The shooting

When the procession arrived under gendarme escort on 27 October, the crowd obstructed the narrow road and some threw stones. The gendarmes fired into the crowd: nine people died on the spot and six more later succumbed to their wounds; 52 were injured, 12 seriously. In the trials that followed, it was villagers, not gendarmes, who were prosecuted — 40 Slovaks received prison sentences totalling over 30 years.

Reaction and legacy

The massacre drew condemnation across Europe and America: the Norwegian writer Bjørnstjerne Bjørnson, the British historian R. W. Seton-Watson (who publicised it in his book Racial Problems in Hungary), and Czech and Austrian politicians denounced Magyarization. The affair accelerated Slovak political organisation and Hlinka's rise as the leading figure of the Slovak national movement; a memorial at Černová commemorates the victims, who are remembered in Slovakia as martyrs of the national cause.
