Millennium Drought

The Millennium Drought (also known as the Big Dry) was a prolonged period of dry weather in southern and eastern Australia, generally dated from the late 1990s to 2009–2010. It was among the most severe droughts in Australia's recorded history, profoundly affecting agriculture, water supplies, and the ecology of the Murray–Darling Basin.

Development

Rainfall deficiencies began in parts of south-eastern Australia in 1996–1997 and intensified from 2001 onward. The drought is often dated from 1997 or 2001 depending on the region and the measure used. A series of El Niño events, together with long-term shifts in atmospheric circulation linked to climate change, contributed to persistently below-average rainfall and record-high temperatures.

Impacts

Water storages in major cities fell to critically low levels; in several capitals, including Melbourne, Perth, Brisbane and Adelaide, storage levels dropped below 30 percent, prompting severe water restrictions. In the Murray–Darling Basin, reduced flows devastated irrigated agriculture, wetlands and native fish populations. Rural communities experienced economic hardship and population decline, and the drought was associated with elevated rates of mental-health problems in farming communities.

Responses

Governments responded with major investments in water infrastructure, including desalination plants in Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Adelaide and Perth, water recycling schemes, and inter-basin pipelines. Water policy reform accelerated, including the Commonwealth Water Act 2007 and the development of the Murray–Darling Basin Plan, which sought to recover water for the environment through buybacks of irrigation entitlements.

Breaking of the drought

The drought broke with the strong La Niña events of 2010–2012, which brought widespread flooding to eastern Australia, including the devastating 2010–11 Queensland floods. The Millennium Drought remains a benchmark event in Australian climate and water-policy discussions.
