Daytona International Speedway is a race track in Daytona Beach, Florida, United States. Since its opening in 1959 it has been the home of the Daytona 500, the most prestigious race in NASCAR, and it also hosts endurance sports-car racing, motorcycle racing and other events. The track is owned by NASCAR through its International Speedway Corporation subsidiary and bills itself the "World Center of Racing".

History

Daytona Beach was a cradle of American speed: land-speed records were set on its hard-packed sand from 1902, and organised racing used a beach-and-road course from 1936. NASCAR founder William "Big Bill" France Sr., seeking a permanent venue to replace the beach course, broke ground in 1957 on a 2.5-mile paved superspeedway just west of the beach. Its distinguishing feature was its steep banking — 31 degrees in the turns — allowing high speeds and letting spectators see the whole circuit, an innovation adapted from test-track practice and executed with the help of engineer Charles Moneypenny. The first Daytona 500 was held on 22 February 1959 before some 41,000 fans.

Layout

The main circuit is a 2.5-mile (4.0 km) tri-oval superspeedway: two long straights, a slightly curved frontstretch forming the "tri-oval", and steeply banked turns. Inside is a 3.56-mile (5.73 km) road course used by the Rolex 24 Hours and motorcycle events, incorporating most of the oval with an infield section, plus shorter road and flat-track configurations. Lake Lloyd, a 29-acre lake dug during construction to provide fill for the banking, occupies part of the infield.

Events

The speedway's February Speedweeks culminate in the Daytona 500; the calendar also includes the Rolex 24 at Daytona (IMSA's 24-hour endurance classic, first held in 1962 as a shorter race), the summer Coke Zero Sugar 400 for the Cup Series, the Daytona 200 motorcycle race during Bike Week, and the infield's karting and other club events. In 2016 the $400 million "Daytona Rising" redevelopment rebuilt the frontstretch grandstands into a modern stadium with escalators, wide concourses and about 101,500 seats, winning major sports-business awards.

Safety and tragedy

The track's high speeds have produced fatal accidents, most famously the death of Dale Earnhardt in the 2001 Daytona 500, which catalysed sweeping safety reforms across NASCAR. The circuit has repeatedly been reconfigured, slowed and protected with SAFER barriers and catch fencing — the latter tested in 2013 when debris from a Nationwide Series crash injured spectators, prompting further fence and crossover-gate improvements.
