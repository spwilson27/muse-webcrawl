Spinodal

A spinodal is the boundary in a phase diagram separating the region in which a homogeneous mixture is metastable from the region in which it is absolutely unstable against infinitesimal composition fluctuations. Inside the spinodal, a mixture undergoes spinodal decomposition: it separates spontaneously into two phases without any nucleation barrier.

Thermodynamic definition

For a binary mixture with Gibbs free energy of mixing G(x), the spinodal is defined by the condition that the second derivative of free energy with respect to composition vanishes:

∂2G/∂x2 = 0.

Where this second derivative is negative, any small local fluctuation in composition lowers the free energy and therefore grows; the mixture is unstable. Between the spinodal and the binodal (coexistence curve), the second derivative is positive but the homogeneous phase is still metastable: phase separation there requires nucleation of droplets that exceed a critical size.

Spinodal decomposition

Spinodal decomposition produces a characteristic interconnected, quasi-periodic microstructure whose length scale coarsens over time, described by the Cahn–Hilliard equation. It is important in polymer blends, alloys (such as Alnico magnets), glass phase separation (as in Vycor glass), and in theories of pattern formation. The concept was developed by John W. Cahn and co-workers in the 1960s.
