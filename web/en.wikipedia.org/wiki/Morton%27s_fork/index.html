Morton's fork

TypeRhetorical figure / dilemma
OriginAttributed to John Morton (c. 1420–1500)
Related conceptsFalse dilemma, double bind, Catch-22

Morton's fork is a rhetorical figure in which two contradictory observations lead to the same unpleasant conclusion, leaving the victim no escape. The expression derives from the tax-raising logic attributed to John Morton, Archbishop of Canterbury and Lord Chancellor under Henry VII of England: taxpayers who lived extravagantly must have plenty of money and could pay, while those who lived frugally must have saved plenty and could equally pay. Either way, the conclusion — payment — is the same.

Origin

The argument is associated with Morton's role in raising funds for Henry VII, including the so-called benevolences — nominally voluntary contributions demanded of wealthy subjects. The story of the fork was recorded by later writers, including Francis Bacon in his history of Henry VII's reign, and the phrase passed into general use for any dilemma constructed so that every branch yields the same adverse outcome.

Morton himself rose from bishop of Ely to archbishop of Canterbury in 1486 and became one of the king's most trusted administrators.

Modern usage

In modern English, 'Morton's fork' is used more broadly for a false dilemma in which the available choices are engineered to produce an identical result, or for any argument whose premises are manipulated so the conclusion follows no matter which premise is accepted. It is related to but distinct from a simple dilemma, in which the horns lead to different but equally bad outcomes.
