Referendums in Australia are national votes used to alter the Constitution of Australia, as required by section 128. A proposed amendment must first pass the federal Parliament and then win a double majority at the referendum: a majority of voters nationally and majorities in a majority of the six states. Since Federation in 1901, 45 referendums (putting 45 questions) have been held, of which only eight have carried — making constitutional change famously difficult.

Mechanism

Voting is compulsory, as in Australian elections. If the double majority is achieved the amendment receives royal assent and the Constitution is altered. Proposals that gain a national majority but fail in enough states fail overall, which has happened on several occasions (for example the 1937 aviation question and parts of the 1977 package that won nationally but not the state majority). Territory voters count toward the national total but not the state count.

Notable referendums

The most celebrated success is the 1967 referendum on Aboriginal affairs, carried with 90.77 percent "Yes" — the highest yes vote ever — which empowered the Commonwealth to legislate for Aboriginal people and removed the exclusion of Aboriginal people from the census reckoning. Other successes include the 1946 social services question and three of the four 1977 questions (on Senate casual vacancies, territory voting rights and judicial retirement ages). Famous failures include the 1951 attempt to give the Commonwealth power to ban the Communist Party (defeated narrowly during the early Cold War), the 1988 package (all four questions lost heavily), the 1999 republic referendum (defeated with 45.13 percent support for a parliamentary-appointed president model) and the 2023 referendum on an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Voice to Parliament, which was defeated nationally and in every state.

State and other votes

The states hold their own referendums on state constitutional and other issues, and non-binding national votes called plebiscites have occasionally been used (the conscription plebiscites of 1916 and 1917, both lost, and the 2017 postal survey on same-sex marriage, which returned 61.6 percent "Yes" and led to legislation).

Reasons for failure

Political scientists attribute the low success rate to the double-majority hurdle, bipartisan support being almost essential (most successful questions enjoyed it), voter caution about unfamiliar change, and the requirement that proposals first pass a Parliament that may itself be divided.
