The Corded Ware culture comprises a broad archaeological horizon of Europe between c. 3000 BC and 2350 BC, thus from the late Neolithic, through the Copper Age, and ending in the early Bronze Age. It is named after its characteristic pottery, decorated with impressions of twisted cord.

Corded Ware encompasses a vast area, from the Rhine in the west to the Volga in the east, including much of continental northern and central Europe. It is closely related to the Battle Axe culture of Scandinavia and the Single Grave culture of northern Germany and Denmark.

Origins and genetics

Ancient DNA studies published in 2015 showed that Corded Ware populations carried a large component of ancestry from the Yamnaya culture of the Pontic–Caspian steppe, indicating a major migration of steppe pastoralists into Europe around 3000 BC. This finding revived and substantially confirmed the "steppe hypothesis" for the spread of Indo-European languages, which many scholars now associate with the Corded Ware expansion.

Material culture and society

Typical finds include cord-decorated beakers, amphorae, and polished stone battle axes. Burials were usually single inhumations under barrows (tumuli), with men typically laid on their right side and women on their left, accompanied by grave goods such as battle axes for men and ornaments for women. The economy rested on stockbreeding and agriculture; wheeled vehicles are attested, and the culture is often associated with some of the earliest evidence for horse domestication in Europe.
