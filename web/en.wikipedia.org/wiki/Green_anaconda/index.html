Green anaconda

The green anaconda (Eunectes murinus), also called the giant anaconda or common anaconda, is a boa species found in South America. It is the heaviest and one of the longest snakes in the world, with reliably measured individuals exceeding 5 metres (16 ft) and 97 kg (214 lb); females are far larger than males.

Description

The green anaconda is olive-green with large black blotches along the back and oval spots with yellow centres along the sides. Its eyes and nostrils sit on top of the head, allowing it to breathe and watch for prey while almost fully submerged. Reports of anacondas 6–9 m long are common in folklore but remain unverified by measured specimens.

Distribution and habitat

The species inhabits swamps, marshes, and slow-moving streams of the Amazon and Orinoco basins in Brazil, Venezuela, Colombia, Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, the Guianas, and Trinidad. It is strongly aquatic and is an apex predator of wetlands.

Behaviour and reproduction

Green anacondas are non-venomous constrictors that ambush prey — fish, birds, mammals such as capybara and deer, caimans, and occasionally jaguars — at the water's edge, killing by constriction and swallowing prey whole. They are largely solitary except in the dry-season breeding aggregations, when several males cluster around a female in a "breeding ball". Females are ovoviviparous, giving birth to litters of 20–40 live young. Sexual cannibalism by females on smaller males has been documented.

Conservation

The IUCN lists the green anaconda as Least Concern, though it is persecuted locally and affected by wetland loss. In 2024 researchers proposed that the northern population represented a distinct species, Eunectes akayima, a claim that has prompted taxonomic debate.
