Electrocyclic reaction

In organic chemistry, an electrocyclic reaction is a type of pericyclic rearrangement in which the net result is the conversion of one pi bond into a sigma bond, or the reverse, forming or breaking a ring. In a ring-closing electrocyclisation, the two ends of a conjugated π system join to create a new C–C σ bond; in the reverse reaction, a σ bond in a ring breaks to give an open-chain conjugated system.

Characteristics

Electrocyclic reactions are concerted and stereospecific: the geometry of the product is determined by the manner in which the terminal p orbitals of the conjugated system rotate. The terminal groups may rotate in the same direction (conrotatory) or in opposite directions (disrotatory). Which motion occurs is governed by the Woodward–Hoffmann rules, which depend on the number of π electrons in the system and on whether the reaction is driven by heat or by light.

Woodward–Hoffmann selection rules

For a system with 4n π electrons (n an integer), the thermal reaction is conrotatory and the photochemical reaction is disrotatory. For a system with 4n + 2 π electrons, the pattern reverses: thermal reactions are disrotatory, and photochemical reactions are conrotatory. These rules, published by R. B. Woodward and Roald Hoffmann in 1965, are explained by the conservation of orbital symmetry and are conveniently analysed using frontier molecular orbital theory or correlation diagrams.

Examples

The interconversion of 1,3-butadiene and cyclobutene is the classic 4π example: heating (3E)-1,3-pentadiene gives trans-3,4-dimethylcyclobutene by a conrotatory pathway. A 6π example is the interconversion of 1,3,5-hexatriene and 1,3-cyclohexadiene, which proceeds thermally in a disrotatory sense. A biologically vital electrocyclic reaction occurs in the skin, where ultraviolet light opens the B ring of 7-dehydrocholesterol to give previtamin D3, the precursor of vitamin D. The Nazarov cyclisation, a 4π conrotatory closure of divinyl ketones, is widely used in synthesis.
