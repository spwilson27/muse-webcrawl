OpenFlow

OpenFlow is a communications protocol that gives access to the forwarding plane of a network switch or router over the network. It is the enabling technology of software-defined networking (SDN): it allows a remote controller to determine the path of network packets through a network by programming the flow tables of switches directly, separating the network's control plane from its data plane.

History

OpenFlow originated in research at Stanford University, described in a 2008 paper by Nick McKeown and colleagues, "OpenFlow: Enabling Innovation in Campus Networks". The Open Networking Foundation (ONF), founded in 2011 by companies including Google, Facebook, Microsoft and Deutsche Telekom, took over stewardship of the standard. Google's announcement in 2012 that its internal B4 wide-area network ran entirely on OpenFlow was a major early demonstration at scale.

How it works

An OpenFlow switch maintains one or more flow tables. Each flow-table entry consists of match fields (such as ingress port, Ethernet and IP addresses, and transport ports), counters, and a set of instructions or actions to apply to matching packets — forward to a port, encapsulate and send to the controller, drop, or modify fields. A logically centralised controller communicates with switches over a secure channel, typically over TCP with TLS, adding and removing flow entries. When a packet matches no entry, the switch may forward it to the controller, which decides how the flow should be handled. Major protocol versions include 1.0 (2009), 1.3 (2012), widely implemented by vendors, and 1.5.

Applications and criticism

OpenFlow is used in campus and data-centre networks, traffic engineering, network virtualization and experimental testbeds. Critics have noted scalability and complexity concerns, and the industry has since also adopted alternative approaches such as programmable data planes (P4) and model-driven management interfaces; nevertheless, OpenFlow remains the canonical example of the SDN southbound API.
