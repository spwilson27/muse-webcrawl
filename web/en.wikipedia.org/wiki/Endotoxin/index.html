Endotoxin

In bacteriology, the term endotoxin is used to refer to any cell-associated bacterial toxin; in practice it most often denotes the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) complexes found in the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. Endotoxins are released when bacteria die and their cell walls disintegrate. They are potent activators of the immune system and are pyrogenic, causing fever.

Structure

Lipopolysaccharide consists of three parts: a lipid component called lipid A, which anchors the molecule in the membrane and is responsible for most of its toxicity; a core oligosaccharide; and an outer O-antigen polysaccharide chain, whose composition varies between strains and is used in serotyping. When LPS enters the bloodstream it is bound by LPS-binding protein and transferred to the CD14/TLR4/MD2 receptor complex on immune cells.

Biological effects

Activation of macrophages and other immune cells by endotoxin triggers the release of inflammatory mediators such as tumour necrosis factor alpha, interleukin-1 and interleukin-6. In small amounts this produces fever and malaise; massive release, as in Gram-negative sepsis, can cause septic shock with hypotension, disseminated intravascular coagulation and multi-organ failure. Endotoxins are heat-stable, so they can persist even after sterilisation, and their detection in pharmaceutical products is a major concern.

Detection

The standard assay for endotoxin contamination is the Limulus amoebocyte lysate (LAL) test, based on the clotting reaction of blood cells from the Atlantic horseshoe crab (Limulus polyphemus) in the presence of LPS. Recombinant factor C assays are increasingly used as an animal-free alternative.
