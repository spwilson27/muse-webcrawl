First law of thermodynamics

The first law of thermodynamics is a formulation of the law of conservation of energy adapted for thermodynamic processes. It distinguishes two kinds of energy transfer — as heat and as thermodynamic work — and relates them to a function of a body's state, its internal energy. In essence: energy can be transformed from one form to another, but it can be neither created nor destroyed. A perpetual motion machine of the first kind, which would produce work without consuming energy, is therefore impossible.

Statement

For a closed system undergoing a cycle, the net heat supplied equals the net work done by the system. For a general change of state, the law reads:

ΔU = Q − W

where ΔU is the change in internal energy of the system, Q is the net heat added to the system, and W is the net work done by the system. (In chemistry and some engineering conventions work done on the system is taken as positive, and the law is written ΔU = Q + W.) Because Q and W depend on the path of the process, the law is often written in differential form with inexact differentials: dU = δQ − δW; internal energy U, by contrast, is a state function — its change depends only on the initial and final states.

History

The law crystallised in the 1840s–1850s from the work of Julius Robert von Mayer, who in 1842 asserted the equivalence and conservation of "force" (energy); James Prescott Joule, whose paddle-wheel experiments (1843–1849) measured the mechanical equivalent of heat (about 4.186 joules per calorie); and Hermann von Helmholtz, who gave a general statement of conservation of energy in 1847. Rudolf Clausius published the first clear formulation of the first law in 1850, and its mature statements appear in the thermodynamics texts of Clausius and William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) in the 1850s.

Extensions

For open systems, the first law is written as a rate balance accounting for energy carried in and out by mass flows (through enthalpy), together with shaft work and heat transfer — the basis of engineering analysis of turbines, compressors, and nozzles. Changes in kinetic and potential energy of the system can be added when significant. At a deeper level, Einstein's mass–energy equivalence (1905) extends the conservation principle: mass itself is a form of energy, so the strict law is conservation of mass-energy.

Applications

The first law underlies the analysis of heat engines and refrigerators, calorimetry, atmospheric and ocean energetics, and the energy budgets of living organisms. Together with the second law it fixes the maximum possible efficiency of a heat engine operating between two temperatures.
