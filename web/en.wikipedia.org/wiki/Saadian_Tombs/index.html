The Saadian Tombs are a royal necropolis in Marrakech, Morocco, dating to the reign of the Saadian sultan Ahmad al-Mansur (1578–1603). Located just south of the Kasbah Mosque, they contain the tombs of about sixty members of the Saadian dynasty, which ruled Morocco from 1549 to 1659, and are among the most visited monuments in the city — renowned as a supreme example of Moroccan and Islamic decorative art at its height.

History

The site served as a burial ground from the early Saadian period — the dynasty's founder, Mohammed ash-Sheikh, was buried here in 1557 — but its celebrated buildings were created by Ahmad al-Mansur, whose victories and the ransoms and sugar wealth of his reign financed a lavish mausoleum complex. After the fall of the Saadians, the Alaouite sultan Moulay Ismail, who demolished much of Saadian Marrakech (including the El Badi Palace), chose instead to seal the tombs off, walling up their entrance so that the necropolis was largely forgotten. It was re-identified from the air in 1917 during the French protectorate, a passage was opened from the Kasbah Mosque, and the site was restored.

Description

The complex consists of two main mausoleum groups set in a walled garden where lesser graves, many tiled in bright colours, fill the ground between them. The first and grandest mausoleum contains the famous Chamber of the Twelve Columns, where Ahmad al-Mansur and his closest family lie beneath carved cedar-and-stucco domes supported by twelve white Carrara marble columns. Interiors are decorated with zellige tilework, carved plaster, muqarnas vaulting and Arabic calligraphy of exceptional fineness. The second mausoleum, built over the grave of the sultan's mother Lalla Masuda, is slightly earlier and somewhat less ornate. A second enclosure across the garden contains the tombs of earlier Saadian princes.
