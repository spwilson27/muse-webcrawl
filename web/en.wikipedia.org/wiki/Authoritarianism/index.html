Authoritarianism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Authoritarianism is a political system characterised by the rejection of political plurality, the use of strong central power to preserve the political status quo, and reductions in the rule of law, separation of powers and democratic voting. In Juan Linz's classic typology, authoritarian regimes differ from totalitarian ones by allowing limited, depoliticised pluralism — some autonomous social and economic life — while denying genuinely competitive politics.

Characteristics

Authoritarian governments concentrate power in a leader or small group not constitutionally accountable to the people; elections, if held, are not freely contested; opposition parties, independent media and civil-society organisations are restricted, co-opted or suppressed; and repression is selective rather than total. Regimes may rest on militaries, parties, monarchies or personalist rule, and legitimise themselves by nationalism, religion, development goals or fear of disorder.

History and scholarship

Most states in history have been authoritarian in this sense. Modern scholarship distinguishes military regimes, one-party states, monarchies and personalist regimes, and studies "competitive authoritarianism" and hybrid regimes where democratic institutions exist but the playing field is systematically tilted. Since the 2000s, researchers have tracked gradual democratic backsliding in several countries as a pathway into authoritarianism, often executed through legal means by elected incumbents.
