Constitutional tort

A constitutional tort is a claim for damages arising from the violation of a person's constitutional rights by a government official. In United States law, such claims are typically brought under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 against state and local officials, or as Bivens actions against federal officials. The term is comparative as well: scholars use it to describe damages remedies for constitutional violations in other legal systems, such as the "constitutional tort" developed by the Supreme Court of India.

United States

Section 1983

Section 1983, originally section 1 of the Civil Rights Act of 1871 (the Ku Klux Klan Act), provides a cause of action against any person who, under colour of state law, deprives another of rights secured by the U.S. Constitution or federal law. In Monroe v. Pape (1961), the Supreme Court held that the statute could be used against police officers who acted unlawfully, even where their conduct violated state law. In Monell v. Department of Social Services (1978), the Court held that municipalities are "persons" subject to suit, though not on a respondeat superior theory: liability attaches only where the violation results from an official policy or custom.

Bivens actions

Because Section 1983 does not reach federal officials, in Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents (1971) the Supreme Court implied a damages action directly under the Constitution for a Fourth Amendment violation by federal narcotics agents. The Court later extended Bivens to Fifth Amendment due process/equal protection (Davis v. Passman, 1979) and Eighth Amendment claims (Carlson v. Green, 1980). In recent decades the Court has declined to extend Bivens to new contexts, notably in Ziglar v. Abbasi (2017), Hernández v. Mesa (2020) and Egbert v. Boule (2022), reasoning that creating damages remedies is generally Congress's role.

Defences and limits

  • Qualified immunity: officials are shielded from damages unless they violated "clearly established" law.
  • Absolute immunity: judges, prosecutors and legislators enjoy absolute immunity for acts within their functions.
  • State action: the Fourteenth Amendment and Section 1983 reach only conduct attributable to the state.

Comparative law

The Supreme Court of India has awarded compensation for violations of Article 21 (right to life) in cases such as Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar (1983) and Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa (1993), a public-law remedy described as constitutional tort and distinct from private tort claims against the state.
