Station wagon

A station wagon combines a passenger car cabin with an extended, full-height cargo area.

A station wagon (US and Canada) or estate car (UK), often shortened to estate, is an automotive body style variant of a sedan with its roof extended rearward over a shared passenger-and-cargo volume, with access at the back via a single flip-up window and/or a sideways- or bottom-hinged door. Unlike a hatchback, a station wagon typically has a longer, less sloped rear overhang and often additional D-pillars.

History

The name originated with depot hacks — vehicles that carried passengers and luggage between railway stations ("stations") and hotels. Early wagons of the 1910s–1930s had coachbuilt wooden bodywork, giving rise to the "woodie". After the Second World War, all-steel wagons became mass-market family cars in the United States, often with three rows of seats; famous models include the Chevrolet Nomad, Ford Country Squire, and the Volvo and Mercedes-Benz estates long popular in Europe.

Decline and persistence

From the 1980s onward, the minivan and then the SUV displaced station wagons in North America, and later in much of Europe. Wagons nonetheless remain a significant segment in European markets and persist elsewhere as niche and performance models (such as the Audi RS 6 Avant and BMW M3 Touring), and the body style is sometimes marketed with names like "Touring", "Avant", "Sportwagen", "Combi", or "Variant".

Characteristics

Station wagons share platforms and running gear with their sedan equivalents but offer greater load capacity with a lower centre of gravity and better fuel economy than a comparable SUV. Rear seats fold flat in most modern designs, and the tailgate opens to a low load floor.
