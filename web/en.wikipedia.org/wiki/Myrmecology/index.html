Myrmecology

Myrmecology (/mɜːrmɪˈkɒlədʒi/; from Greek myrmex, "ant", and -λογία, -logia) is a branch of entomology focusing on ants. Ants are among the most ecologically dominant animals on Earth — an estimated 20 quadrillion individuals, with a combined biomass exceeding that of wild birds and mammals — and their complex eusocial colonies make them important model systems in behavioural ecology, evolutionary biology, and the study of collective behaviour.

History

Observation of ants is ancient — ant proverbs appear in the Bible and Aesop — but scientific myrmecology began in the 18th and 19th centuries with naturalists such as Pierre André Latreille, who erected the family name Formicidae, and Pierre Huber, whose 1810 Recherches sur les mœurs des fourmis indigènes pioneered experimental study of ant behaviour. Auguste Forel and Carlo Emery built the taxonomy of the group in the late 19th century, while William Morton Wheeler's Ants: Their Structure, Development and Behavior (1910) founded modern myrmecology in the United States. In the 20th century, E. O. Wilson and Bert Hölldobler transformed the field; their monograph The Ants (1990) won the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction.

Scope

Myrmecologists study ant taxonomy and phylogeny, caste systems and division of labour, chemical communication by pheromones, foraging and navigation, social parasitism and slave-making, mutualisms with plants and aphids, fungus-growing (attine ants), and the ecology of invasive species such as the red imported fire ant and Argentine ant. The discipline overlaps with sociobiology, of which ants — together with other social Hymenoptera — were a central inspiration, particularly for the theory of kin selection.

Applications and keeping

Ant research informs pest management, ecosystem monitoring (ants are widely used as bioindicators), swarm robotics, and optimization algorithms (ant colony optimization). Ant-keeping as a hobby, using artificial nests called formicaria, is popular, and amateur myrmecologists contribute distribution records and natural-history observations.
