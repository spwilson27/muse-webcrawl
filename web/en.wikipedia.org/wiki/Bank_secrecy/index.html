Bank secrecy

Bank secrecy, or banking confidentiality, is the principle and, in some jurisdictions, the legal duty that banks must not disclose information about their customers and their accounts to third parties, including governments, except under defined conditions. Like the confidentiality owed by doctors or lawyers to clients, secrecy has been a cornerstone of banking in countries such as Switzerland and Lebanon, and underpinned the use of numbered bank accounts.

Switzerland

Swiss banking secrecy was codified in the Federal Banking Act of 1934, which made it a criminal offence for bankers to reveal client information. Combined with political stability and the Swiss franc, secrecy attracted large amounts of foreign capital, but also persistent allegations that the system sheltered tax evasion, organised crime and the assets of dictators. Pressure from the United States and the European Union in the 2000s and 2010s — including the UBS case of 2008–2009 and the US Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) — led Switzerland to adopt the automatic exchange of information under the OECD Common Reporting Standard from 2018, effectively ending secrecy toward foreign tax authorities while retaining it as a matter of privacy law.

Other jurisdictions

Many offshore financial centres historically offered strong secrecy, while in other countries confidentiality is balanced by anti-money-laundering obligations, suspicious-transaction reporting and court-ordered disclosure. Critics argue secrecy facilitates corruption and tax avoidance; defenders argue that financial privacy protects legitimate clients from crime and oppressive governments.
