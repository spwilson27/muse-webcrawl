Kraft process

The kraft process (also known as kraft pulping or the sulfate process) is a process for conversion of wood into wood pulp, which consists of nearly pure cellulose fibres, the main component of paper. The kraft process entails the treatment of wood chips with a hot mixture of water, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), and sodium sulfide (Na2S), known as white liquor, that breaks the bonds that link lignin, hemicellulose, and cellulose. The technology entails several steps, both mechanical and chemical. It is the dominant method for producing paper. In some situations, the process has been controversial because kraft plants can release malodorous products and in some cases produce substantial liquid wastes.

History

The kraft process was developed by the German chemist Carl F. Dahl in Danzig (now Gdańsk, Poland) in 1879 and patented in 1884. The name derives from the German word Kraft for "strength", a reference to the strength of the paper produced. The process grew rapidly in importance after the invention of the recovery boiler by G. H. Tomlinson in the early 1930s made it economical to recover and reuse the inorganic pulping chemicals.

Process description

Wood chips are fed into a pressure vessel called a digester, where they are impregnated with white liquor and cooked at elevated temperature (roughly 170 °C) and pressure for several hours. The sulfide and hydroxide ions dissolve the lignin and much of the hemicellulose, freeing the cellulose fibres. After cooking, the contents are discharged into a blow tank; the spent liquor, called black liquor, contains the dissolved lignin fragments and the inorganic chemicals.

The pulp is washed and screened, and may be bleached to produce white grades. The black liquor is concentrated in evaporators and burned in a recovery boiler, generating steam and electricity for the mill while the inorganic chemicals are recovered as a molten smelt of sodium carbonate and sodium sulfide. The smelt is dissolved to form green liquor, which is then causticized with calcium oxide (quicklime) to regenerate white liquor; the lime is recovered in a lime kiln. This closed chemical recovery cycle is the key to the process's economics.

By-products and emissions

Important by-products include crude tall oil and turpentine, recovered from softwood feedstocks. The process is notorious for reduced sulfur gases (such as methyl mercaptan and hydrogen sulfide) that give kraft mills a characteristic odour, though modern mills have greatly reduced emissions. Kraft pulp produces stronger paper than the older sulfite process and can be applied to a wider range of wood species, including resinous pines.
