A digital computer is a computer that represents and manipulates data using discrete values, in practice binary digits (bits), as opposed to an analog computer, which uses continuous physical quantities. Because digital representations can be made arbitrarily precise by adding more bits, and because digital circuits can correct small errors at each stage, digital computers came to dominate computing in the second half of the 20th century.

History

Mechanical digital aids such as the abacus and Pascal's calculator are centuries old, and Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine (designed in the 1830s) embodied the key ideas of a programmable digital machine. Electromechanical devices followed (Konrad Zuse's Z3, 1941), then electronic machines during the Second World War: the Atanasoff–Berry computer, the British Colossus codebreaking machines (1943), and ENIAC (1945). The stored-program concept, embodied in the EDVAC design and first run on the Manchester Baby (1948), made the digital computer a universal machine whose behaviour is fixed by software.

Architecture

A digital computer combines a processing unit that executes instructions, memory that stores both instructions and data, storage, and input/output devices. Transistors replaced vacuum tubes in the 1950s and integrated circuits followed in the 1960s, driving exponential improvements in cost, size and speed summarised by Moore's law. Modern digital computers range from embedded microcontrollers to supercomputers and cloud data centres.
