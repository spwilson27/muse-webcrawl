Christian pacifism

Christian pacifism is the theological and ethical position according to which Christianity requires a rejection of violence and war. Christian pacifists ground their stance in the teachings of Jesus, particularly the Sermon on the Mount, with its injunctions to love one's enemies and turn the other cheek.

History

Many early Christians refused military service, interpreting the commandments against killing as absolute. After Christianity became the Roman state religion, just war theory came to dominate, but a pacifist witness persisted in movements such as the Waldensians and, after the Reformation, in the historic peace churches: the Anabaptists (including Mennonites and the Amish), Quakers, and the Church of the Brethren.

Modern expressions

Christian pacifists have been prominent conscientious objectors in modern wars, and organizations such as the Fellowship of Reconciliation (founded 1914) have linked Christian nonviolence to wider movements for civil rights and disarmament. Figures associated with Christian pacifism include Leo Tolstoy, Dorothy Day, and Martin Luther King Jr., whose nonviolence was shaped by both Christian teaching and Gandhian practice.

Criticism

Theologians such as Reinhold Niebuhr criticized pacifism as an irresponsible refusal to resist evil, arguing that justice sometimes requires coercive force — a debate central to 20th-century Christian ethics.
