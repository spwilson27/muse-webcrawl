Fountain is a readymade sculpture by Marcel Duchamp consisting of a porcelain urinal signed "R. Mutt" and dated 1917. Submitted anonymously to the exhibition of the Society of Independent Artists in New York — whose rules promised to show any work for a fee — it was rejected (hidden from view), provoking a scandal that made it one of the most influential artworks of the 20th century and a founding gesture of conceptual art.

The R. Mutt case

Duchamp, himself a board member of the Society, resigned in protest at the rejection. An editorial in the journal The Blind Man, "The Richard Mutt Case," defended the work, arguing that whether Mr Mutt made the fountain with his own hands was unimportant — he chose it, thereby creating a new thought for the object. The original was lost, but Alfred Stieglitz's photograph of it survives; Duchamp later authorised replicas, now held by institutions including the Tate and the Philadelphia Museum of Art.

Legacy

In 2004 Fountain was voted the most influential artwork of the modern era in a poll of 500 art experts. It challenged definitions of art, authorship and taste, and anticipated conceptual art, Dada's anti-art stance and later debates about institutional critique. Some scholars have debated the authorship of the idea, with Duchamp himself hinting in a letter that a female friend had sent the urinal under the Mutt pseudonym.
