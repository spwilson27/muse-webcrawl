Internet governance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Photograph of a UN Internet Governance Forum plenary hall with rows of delegates and flags]

Internet governance is the development and application by governments, the private sector and civil society, in their respective roles, of shared principles, norms, rules, decision-making procedures, and programmes that shape the evolution and use of the Internet. That famously convoluted definition, from the UN's 2005 Tunis Agenda, reflects the field's central fact: no one is in charge of the Internet as a whole, yet it must somehow be governed.

In practice, governance is layered and fragmented. Technical coordination — names, numbers and protocol standards — is done by the multistakeholder bodies: ICANN, the regional address registries and the Internet Engineering Task Force, their legitimacy resting on openness and on the 2016 transition that ended formal US stewardship of the DNS root. Policy issues — cybercrime, content, privacy, trade, human rights — spill into national law and international forums: the UN's Internet Governance Forum (a talk-shop without decision power), the ITU, trade negotiations and courts.

The standing argument is between the multistakeholder model, defended by most Western governments, engineers and much of civil society, and state-sovereignty models advanced by Russia, China and others, in which governments control their national Internet segments (the 'splinternet' fear). Every few years the argument re-ignites — over encryption, platform power, AI — because the Internet's governance is, in effect, the constitution-writing of a borderless polity that never quite agrees to have one.
