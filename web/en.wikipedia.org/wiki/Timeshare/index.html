Timeshare

A timeshare (sometimes called vacation ownership) is a property arrangement in which several joint owners hold rights to use the property, with each owner allotted a period of time — typically one week per year — in which they may stay there. Most timeshares are condominiums in resort destinations, sold by developers and increasingly administered by major hospitality brands such as Marriott Vacations, Hilton Grand Vacations, and Wyndham Destinations.

History

The concept began in Europe in the early 1960s. A ski resort developer in the French Alps, the Société des Grands Travaux de Marseille, marketed its SuperDévoluy resort with the slogan "No need to rent the room; buy the hotel, it's cheaper!" In Britain, shared holiday-home ownership schemes appeared at the same period. The model spread to the United States in the 1970s — first in Hawaii and Florida — where developers converted struggling condominiums into timeshares during a real-estate downturn. By the 1990s the industry was worth billions of dollars annually.

Types

In a deeded fixed-week arrangement, the buyer owns a specific unit for a specific week in perpetuity. Floating-week systems let owners reserve any week within a season. Right-to-use contracts grant occupancy for a set number of years without transferring title. Modern points-based systems award owners an annual allocation of points, spendable across a portfolio of resorts, unit sizes, and dates. Exchange companies such as RCI and Interval International let owners trade their weeks for stays at thousands of affiliated resorts worldwide. Owners pay annual maintenance fees in addition to the purchase price.

Criticism

Timeshares are notorious for high-pressure sales presentations, steep mark-ups, and maintenance fees that rise faster than inflation. The resale market is weak — units commonly resell for a fraction of the developer price, and sometimes for nothing at all — which has spawned a secondary industry of "timeshare exit" firms, some of which regulators have prosecuted as scams. Many jurisdictions therefore mandate cooling-off periods during which buyers may rescind their contracts.
