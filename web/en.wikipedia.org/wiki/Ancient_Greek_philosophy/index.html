Ancient Greek philosophy

Period: 6th century BC – 6th century AD
Beginnings: Miletus, Ionia
Key figures: Thales, Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Epicurus, Zeno

Ancient Greek philosophy arose in the Greek-speaking world of the eastern Mediterranean in the 6th century BC and continued through the Hellenistic and Roman periods until the early centuries AD. It marks the beginning of Western philosophy: a shift from mythological explanations of the world toward inquiry based on reason and argument, addressing the nature of reality, knowledge, ethics, politics and the soul.

Presocratics

The first philosophers, the so-called Presocratics, sought the archē or first principle of things: Thales of Miletus proposed water, Anaximenes air, Heraclitus fire and ceaseless change, while Parmenides argued that reality is one and unchanging, and Democritus developed atomism. Pythagoras fused mathematics, mysticism and a way of life, and the Sophists of 5th-century Athens turned attention to rhetoric, relativism and human affairs.

The classical age

Socrates redirected philosophy to ethics and definition through questioning; his pupil Plato founded the Academy and set out the theory of Forms in dialogues that remain foundational. Plato's student Aristotle founded the Lyceum, systematised logic, and wrote on nearly every subject from metaphysics and biology to poetics and politics. Together they defined the agenda of Western thought.

Hellenistic schools

After Alexander the Great, the dominant schools — Stoicism, Epicureanism, Skepticism and Cynicism — concentrated on how to live and attain tranquillity. Philosophy continued in the Roman Empire with figures such as Plotinus, whose Neoplatonism shaped Christian and Islamic thought; the closing of the Academy in Athens in AD 529 is conventionally taken as its end, though Greek philosophy passed into Byzantine, Arabic and Latin traditions that preserved it.
