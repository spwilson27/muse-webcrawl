Pterosaurs (/ˈtɛrəsɔːrz/; from Greek pteron "wing" and sauros "lizard") were flying reptiles of the extinct order Pterosauria. They existed from the late Triassic to the end of the Cretaceous (228 to 66 million years ago) and were the earliest vertebrates known to have evolved powered flight. Often popularly called "pterodactyls"—a name properly belonging to the genus Pterodactylus—pterosaurs were close relatives of the dinosaurs within the group Avemetatarsalia, but were not themselves dinosaurs. Their wings were membranes of skin, muscle and fibre stiffened by actinofibrils, supported mainly by a greatly elongated fourth finger. Pterosaurs ranged from sparrow-sized forms such as Nemicolopterus (25 cm wingspan) to the azhdarchids Quetzalcoatlus and Hatzegopteryx, whose wingspans exceeded 10 metres and which rank among the largest flying animals of all time.

Anatomy and flight

The pterosaur wing joined a membrane (the brachiopatagium) running from the elongated fourth finger to the hindlimbs, a smaller forewing (propatagium) before the arm, supported by a unique wrist bone, the pteroid, and a tail membrane in early forms. Hollow, thin-walled, air-sac-pneumatised bones kept the skeleton light; a keeled sternum anchored flight muscles. Most researchers hold that large pterosaurs launched quadrupedally—vaulting into the air using their powerful forelimbs—which decoupled launch ability from hindlimb size and permitted their gigantic proportions. On the ground, pterodactyloids walked quadrupedally, folding their wings, and fossil trackways show competent terrestrial locomotion; azhdarchids appear adapted to stalking prey on foot like giant storks. Pterosaurs were covered in hair-like filaments called pycnofibres, evidence of insulation and probably warm-bloodedness; some preserved filaments are branched and have been interpreted as protofeathers, suggesting that filamentous coverings predate flight in the dinosaur–pterosaur lineage. Many species bore elaborate head crests of bone and soft tissue, used in display.

Classification and evolution

The earliest pterosaurs of the Late Triassic (such as Eudimorphodon from Italy) already flew, so their immediate ancestors—small bipedal archosaurs such as the lagerpetids, identified as the closest relatives of pterosaurs—are inferred rather than documented in transition. Jurassic seas were patrolled by long-tailed "rhamphorhynchoids" (e.g., Rhamphorhynchus, Dimorphodon) while the short-tailed pterodactyloids, including Pterodactylus from the Solnhofen limestones, radiated from the Late Jurassic onward. Cretaceous pterodactyloids achieved the greatest diversity and size: toothed fish-eaters (Ornithocheiridae, with wingspans to 8 m), the crested Pteranodon of the Western Interior Seaway, tapejarids with huge cranial crests, the filter-feeding Pterodaustro with a thousand bristle teeth, dsungaripterids that crushed shellfish, and the toothless, giraffe-sized azhdarchids. The discovery of Darwinopterus (2009), a "modular" intermediate between the two great body plans, refined understanding of the transition. Pterosaurs declined in diversity through the Late Cretaceous in step, perhaps, with the rise of birds, though they remained widespread until the end-Cretaceous mass extinction 66 million years ago ended the group along with the non-avian dinosaurs.

Palaeobiology

Diets inferred from teeth, jaws, gut contents and coprolites include fish and squid (many coastal forms), insects (the small anurognathids, wide-mouthed aerial hunters), crustaceans and molluscs, filter-feeding, fruit, and terrestrial vertebrate prey for azhdarchids. Eggs were soft-shelled and buried; embryos show well-developed wing bones, and juveniles ("flaplings") could probably fly soon after hatching, growing up independently—unlike birds, with evidence of rapid early growth and, in large species, many years to reach adult size. Bone histology indicates fast, sustained growth and high metabolic rates.

History of study

The first pterosaur fossil, from Solnhofen, was described by Cosimo Collini in 1784 as an unknown marine animal; Georges Cuvier recognised it in 1809 as a flying reptile and named it Ptéro-Dactyle. Through the 19th century, finds such as Mary Anning's Dimorphodon (1828) and the great Kansas chalk discoveries of Marsh's Pteranodon (1870s) revealed the group's scale, though early restorations wrongly depicted pterosaurs as bat-like gliders or marsupial swimmers. The 20th-century finds of crested Brazilian forms in the Santana Formation, the recognition of quadrupedal launch, and computed tomography of braincases (showing bird-like balance organs and large flocculi for flight control) have made pterosaurs a central case study in the evolution of flight. More than 200 species are now known, and new taxa are described each year.

In culture

Pterosaurs—above all the crested Pteranodon and Pterodactylus—are staples of popular palaeontology, featuring in The Lost World, the Jurassic Park films and countless restorations, usually (inaccurately) as scaly, bat-winged contemporaries snatching human-sized prey, and often wrongly labelled dinosaurs.
