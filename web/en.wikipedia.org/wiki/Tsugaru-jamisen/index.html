Tsugaru-jamisen

Tsugaru-jamisen
Origin:Tsugaru region, Aomori Prefecture, Japan
Instrument:Shamisen (futozao, "thick neck")
Founding figure:Nitabō (Akimoto Nitarō, 1857–1928)

Tsugaru-jamisen (Japanese: 津軽三味線) is a virtuosic, percussive style of shamisen music originating in the Tsugaru region of Aomori Prefecture in northern Japan. It grew out of the performances of itinerant blind musicians known as bosama, who travelled from door to door in the harsh Tsugaru winters playing for food and lodging. The genre's foundational figure is Nitabō (Akimoto Nitarō, 1857–1928), who developed the thick-necked instrument and its forceful technique.

Instrument and technique

The Tsugaru shamisen (Tsugaru-jamisen refers to both the music and its instrument) is larger than other shamisen, with a thick neck (futozao), thick silk or synthetic strings, and a body traditionally covered with dog skin. It is played with a large, heavy plectrum (bachi), striking the strings and the skin of the body to produce a powerful, snapping, almost drum-like sound. Techniques include rapid repeated strokes, percussive slaps, and long improvised flights building from slow folk melodies to dazzling climaxes.

Repertoire and revival

The core repertoire derives from Tsugaru folk songs (min'yō) such as "Tsugaru Jongara Bushi", "Tsugaru Ohara Bushi", and "Tsugaru Yosare Bushi", which soloists expand into extended improvisations. In the 20th century the style was popularised by players such as Takahashi Chikuzan, whose autobiographical film and recordings made him its emblematic figure, and by national competitions held in Hirosaki. Since the late 20th century, performers such as the Yoshida Brothers have carried Tsugaru-jamisen to international and crossover audiences, blending it with rock, jazz and electronic music.
