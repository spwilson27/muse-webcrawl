Raoul Salan

Raoul Albin Louis Salan (10 June 1899 – 3 July 1984) was a French Army general. One of the most decorated soldiers in French history, he commanded French forces in Indochina and Algeria, but is chiefly remembered as the leader of the Organisation armée secrète (OAS), the clandestine organisation that waged a terrorist campaign against Algerian independence after the 1961 Generals' Putsch.

Early career

Salan entered the Saint-Cyr military academy during the First World War and fought at Verdun. He spent much of the interwar period as an intelligence officer in French Indochina, becoming an authority on the region. In the Second World War he served with the Free French and French Expeditionary forces, rising rapidly; he was wounded in Italy and ended the war a senior staff officer. He later commanded French forces in Indochina (1952–1953) between Jean de Lattre de Tassigny and Henri Navarre.

Algeria and the putsch

Appointed commander-in-chief in Algeria in 1956, Salan directed the French war against the FLN during the Battle of Algiers period. In May 1958, fearing the government in Paris would negotiate with the FLN, Salan publicly called for Charles de Gaulle's return to power — his cry of "Vive de Gaulle!" from the balcony of the Government General in Algiers helped precipitate the May 1958 crisis that brought de Gaulle back. Yet when de Gaulle moved toward Algerian self-determination, Salan turned against him. Retired to Spain, he returned secretly to Algiers in April 1961 to join the Generals' Putsch, which collapsed in four days.

OAS, trial and later life

Salan then led the OAS, whose campaign of bombings and assassinations in Algeria and France — including attempts on de Gaulle's life — killed hundreds. Captured in Algiers in April 1962, he was tried by a military court; controversially, he was sentenced to life imprisonment rather than death, a verdict de Gaulle considered scandalously lenient. Salan was amnestied in 1968, released, and had his rank and pension restored under a 1982 law reintegrating the putschist generals. He died in Paris in 1984, having published his memoirs, Fin d'un empire.
