Samuel Parris (1653 – February 27, 1720) was the Puritan minister of Salem Village (now Danvers, Massachusetts) during the Salem witch trials of 1692–93. The crisis began in his own household, where his nine-year-old daughter Betty and eleven-year-old niece Abigail Williams were the first girls to display the strange afflictions attributed to witchcraft, and Parris was a vigorous promoter of the prosecutions.

Early life and ministry

Born in London, Parris emigrated as a child with his family to Boston and briefly attended Harvard College before leaving to manage a sugar plantation in Barbados inherited from his father, where he married Elizabeth Eldridge. Returning to Boston in 1680 as a merchant of modest success, he sought ordination and in 1689 became the first settled minister of fractious Salem Village, after three predecessors had resigned amid disputes. His contract — salary, firewood, and ownership of the parsonage — became a bitter grievance, and the village was divided between his supporters, notably the Putnam family, and opponents who withheld his pay.

The witch trials

In the winter of 1691–92 Betty Parris and Abigail Williams began screaming, contorting, and complaining of being pinched and pricked. The enslaved woman in Parris's household, Tituba, was among the first three accused; Parris beat her before she gave a detailed confession that ignited the hunt. From his pulpit Parris preached that Satan was assaulting the village church, recorded the afflicted girls' testimony, and supported the special Court of Oyer and Terminer, which hanged nineteen people and pressed one man to death before it was dissolved; over two hundred were accused in all. The preacher's own role has made him, in much historical writing, a villain of the episode — though modern historians note the wider context of frontier war trauma, factionalism, and ergot and disease theories.

Aftermath

Parris's congregation turned against him; in 1694 he published an essay of "Meditations for Peace" conceding errors, and in 1697 the church formally forgave him, but withheld salary and lawsuits drove him out in 1696–97. He preached thereafter in Stow, Concord, and Dunstable, Massachusetts, trading and farming in his final years at Sudbury, where he died in 1720. He is a central figure — often depicted as rigid and self-righteous — in Arthur Miller's play The Crucible (1953).
