Oliver Ellsworth (April 29, 1745 – November 26, 1807) was an American lawyer, statesman, and Founding Father. A Connecticut delegate to the Constitutional Convention of 1787, he co-authored the Connecticut Compromise, served as one of the first United States Senators, drafted much of the Judiciary Act of 1789 that created the federal court system, and became the third Chief Justice of the United States.

Constitutional Convention and Senate

Educated at Yale and Princeton (then the College of New Jersey), Ellsworth built a thriving legal practice and served Connecticut in the Continental Congress during the Revolution. At Philadelphia in 1787 he and Roger Sherman proposed the Connecticut (Great) Compromise — representation by population in the House, equality in the Senate — that broke the Convention's deadlock, and Ellsworth served on the Committee of Detail that produced the Constitution's first draft. In the Senate he was the principal author of the Judiciary Act of 1789, establishing the federal district and circuit courts and the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction; he was also a leader in funding and assumption of state debts and in the creation of the First Bank of the United States.

Chief Justice and envoy

President George Washington appointed Ellsworth Chief Justice in 1796; he was unanimously confirmed. His tenure was brief and the Court's docket small, but he presided over cases such as Worrall and helped regularize circuit-riding practice. In 1799 President John Adams named him envoy to France; he and his fellow commissioners negotiated the Convention of 1800 (Mortefontaine), ending the Quasi-War and the Franco-American alliance of 1778. The mission ruined his health; he resigned the chief justiceship in 1800 and retired to Windsor, Connecticut, where he died in 1807.

Legacy

The town of Ellsworth, Maine, and Ellsworth avenues and schools nationwide are named for him. Historians rank his role in designing the federal judiciary as his most durable achievement.
