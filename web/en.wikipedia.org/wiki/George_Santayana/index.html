George Santayana

Born Jorge Agustín Nicolás Ruiz de Santayana y Borrás, 16 December 1863, Madrid, Spain
Died 26 September 1952 (aged 88), Rome, Italy
Occupation Philosopher, essayist, poet, novelist

George Santayana (born Jorge Agustín Nicolás Ruiz de Santayana y Borrás, December 16, 1863 – September 26, 1952) was a Spanish-American philosopher, essayist, poet, and novelist. Born in Spain, he was raised and educated in the United States from the age of nine and identified himself as an American, although he always kept a valid Spanish passport. He wrote in English and is generally considered an American philosopher.

Along with William James and Josiah Royce, he was a leading teacher of philosophy at Harvard University, where he taught for twenty-three years. His best-known works include The Life of Reason (1905–1906), The Sense of Beauty (1896), Scepticism and Animal Faith (1923), and The Realms of Being (1927–1940). He is best remembered by the general reader for the saying "Those who cannot remember the past are condemned to repeat it," from The Life of Reason, and for the aphorism "Fanaticism consists in redoubling your efforts when you have forgotten your aim."

Life

Santayana was born in Madrid and spent his early childhood in Ávila. His mother, Josefina Borrás, was the daughter of a Spanish official in the Philippines; his father, Agustín Ruiz de Santayana, was a lawyer and civil servant. In 1872 his mother moved to Boston with her children; Santayana followed in 1872, initially living with his maternal grandfather. He attended Boston Latin School and then Harvard College, where he graduated summa cum laude in 1886. After two years of study in Berlin, he returned to Harvard, completed his doctorate under William James in 1889 with a dissertation on Rudolf Hermann Lotze, and joined the philosophy faculty.

In 1912, having inherited money from his mother, Santayana resigned from Harvard and left the United States for good. He spent the remaining forty years of his life in Europe — in France, England, and finally Italy. During the Second World War he lived in a nursing home in Rome run by the Blue Nuns, where he wrote much of his later work. He died of stomach cancer in Rome in 1952 and was buried in the Spanish pantheon at the Cimitero del Verano.

Philosophy

Santayana's philosophy combined materialism about nature with a profound appreciation of spirit, religion, art, and myth, which he valued as expressions of human imagination rather than literal truths. His five-volume The Life of Reason (1905–1906) traced the development of human rationality through its phases in society, religion, art, and science. In Scepticism and Animal Faith (1923) he developed a form of critical realism, arguing that knowledge rests on "animal faith" — an instinctive trust in the existence of an external world. The four volumes of The Realms of Being (1927–1940) elaborated his ontology of essence, matter, truth, and spirit.

Santayana was also a noted literary critic and cultural commentator; his essay "The Genteel Tradition in American Philosophy" (1911) criticized the genteel tradition in American thought, and his novel The Last Puritan (1935) was a bestseller nominated for the Pulitzer Prize.
