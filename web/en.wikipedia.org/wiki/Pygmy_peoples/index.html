Pygmy peoples

In anthropology, pygmy peoples are ethnic groups whose average height is unusually short. The term pygmyism is used to describe the phenotype of endemic short stature (as opposed to disproportionate dwarfism) for populations in which adult men are on average less than 150 cm (4 ft 11 in) tall.

The best-known pygmy peoples are the hunter-gatherer groups of the Congo Basin in Central Africa, including the Aka, Efe, and Mbuti. Other groups described as pygmies include the Batek and other Negrito peoples of Southeast Asia, and various Indigenous peoples of New Guinea and the Andaman Islands. The term derives from the Greek pygmaios, used in ancient sources for a legendary people of short stature.

Origins

Genetic studies suggest that the short stature of Central African pygmy peoples evolved independently in western and eastern groups, possibly as an adaptation to life in dense rainforest: shorter stature may aid movement through undergrowth, thermoregulation in hot and humid climates, and earlier reproduction in conditions of high mortality. African pygmy populations are among the most genetically divergent human groups, with ancestors separating from other populations tens of thousands of years ago.

Culture and threats

Central African pygmy groups traditionally live as hunter-gatherers in close but often unequal exchange relationships with neighbouring farming peoples. Deforestation, mining, conservation enclosures, and discrimination have displaced many communities, and activists campaign for recognition of their land rights.
