Circumpolar vortex

The Northern Hemisphere circumpolar vortex.

The circumpolar vortex, or polar vortex, is the large-scale cyclonic circulation of winds that encircles each of Earth's poles in the middle and upper troposphere and stratosphere. The vortices are features of the planetary circulation driven by the rotation of the Earth and the temperature contrast between the polar regions and lower latitudes. In each hemisphere there are two nested systems: a stratospheric polar vortex, strongest in winter, and a tropospheric vortex that exists year-round.

Behaviour

The vortex is strongest in winter, when the pole-to-equator temperature gradient is greatest and the polar night leaves the air over the pole extremely cold, and weakens or breaks down in summer. The tropospheric vortex is associated with the polar-front jet stream; when the vortex is strong and compact, cold air is largely confined to high latitudes, whereas when it weakens, its circulation meanders in large Rossby waves and lobes of frigid polar air can plunge into mid-latitudes, producing severe cold outbreaks — the phenomenon behind many of the publicised "polar vortex" cold snaps in North America. Sudden stratospheric warming events can abruptly disrupt the stratospheric vortex, sometimes splitting it in two, with effects on surface weather in the following weeks.

Ozone and other planets

Over Antarctica, the strong, stable vortex isolates polar air in winter, promoting the formation of polar stratospheric clouds and the chemical reactions that cause the Antarctic ozone hole each spring. Circumpolar vortices also exist on other planets and moons with atmospheres, including Mars, Venus, Saturn, and Titan, where Saturn's south polar vortex and hexagonal north polar circulation are notable examples.
