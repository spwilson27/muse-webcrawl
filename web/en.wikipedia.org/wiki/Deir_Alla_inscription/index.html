Deir Alla inscription

Created: c. 9th–8th century BC

Discovered: 1967, Deir Alla, Jordan

Material: Ink on plaster

Current location: Jordan Museum, Amman

The Deir Alla inscription, also known as the Balaam inscription, is an ancient text written in ink on plaster, discovered during the 1967 excavations at Deir Alla in the Jordan Valley. Dated to roughly the ninth or eighth century BC, it is one of the earliest known examples of a literary text written in a West Semitic alphabetic script and is remarkable for featuring Balaam son of Beor, a seer also known from the Hebrew Bible.

Discovery

An earthquake destroyed the room in which the text had been written on a plastered wall, scattering hundreds of fragments across the floor. Excavators recovered the pieces and scholars painstakingly reassembled two main clusters of text, known as Combination I and Combination II, from many small joins.

Language and content

The language is debated: it shows features of both Aramaic and Canaanite dialects and may represent a local Gileadite idiom. Written in black ink with rubrics in red, the text describes the gods coming to Balaam at night and warning him of coming doom, after which Balaam fasts, weeps, and reports the vision of an assembly of gods — including a council mentioning Shaddai — decreeing darkness and chaos. Combination II is more fragmentary and harder to interpret. The reconstructed inscription is displayed in the Jordan Museum in Amman.

Significance

The inscription demonstrates the existence of a literary tradition about Balaam outside Israel and Judah and provides key evidence for early alphabetic writing, ancient prophecy, and the shared religious world of the Iron Age Levant.
