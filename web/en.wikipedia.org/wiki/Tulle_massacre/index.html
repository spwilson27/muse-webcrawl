The Tulle massacre was a war crime committed on 9 June 1944 by troops of the 2nd SS Panzer Division Das Reich in the town of Tulle, in the Corrèze department of central France, during the Second World War. In reprisal for an attack by the French Resistance (the Francs-Tireurs et Partisans) that had briefly liberated the town and killed German garrison troops, the SS rounded up the male population and hanged 99 men from balconies and lampposts around the town. A further 149 men were deported to concentration camps, where 101 of them died.

Background

Following the Normandy landings of 6 June 1944, the Das Reich division was ordered north from southern France toward the front, and was harassed by Resistance sabotage and ambushes along the way. At Tulle, Resistance fighters attacked the German garrison on 7–8 June; when the SS retook the town on 9 June, they carried out the hangings as a collective reprisal. The following day, troops of the same division committed the still larger massacre at Oradour-sur-Glane.

Aftermath

After the war, several participants were prosecuted in French courts, most notably at the Bordeaux trial of 1951, though sentences were soon reduced and the affair became entangled in controversy over amnesty laws. Tulle commemorates the victims annually; the sites of the hangings are marked with plaques and a memorial cemetery.
