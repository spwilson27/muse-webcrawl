Jacobs bogie

A Jacobs bogie (in German: Jakobs-Drehgestell) is a type of rail vehicle bogie commonly found on articulated railcars and trams. Instead of being under each carbody, the bogie is placed between two car sections, supporting the adjacent ends of both, so that the two carbodies share one bogie.

Design and history

The arrangement is named after the German railway engineer Wilhelm Jakobs (1858–1942), who patented his design in 1901. A conventional train of n cars has 2n bogies; a Jacobs-articulated train has only n+1. Fewer bogies mean less weight, less rolling resistance and lower construction and maintenance costs, while the overlapping car ends also give a smooth, continuous gangway and reduce the risk of cars jack-knifing or overriding each other in a derailment.

Advantages and disadvantages

Because the cars share bogies they cannot easily be separated; train length can only be changed in a workshop, and the axle load on each bogie is higher than on a conventional bogie carrying one car end. Articulated trains are therefore generally built as fixed consists.

Applications

Jacobs bogies are a signature feature of the French TGV trainsets, whose trailers share bogies — a design credited with contributing to their safety record at high speed — and of the AGV, many modern regional multiple units such as the Bombardier Talent and Alstom Lint and Coradia families, and numerous low-floor trams and light-rail vehicles, including North American articulated streetcars. Several early American streamliners, such as the Pioneer Zephyr, also used shared bogies between cars.
