Solidus (coin)


Composition: Gold
Weight: ~4.5 g (1/72 Roman pound)
Introduced: c. 301–312 AD

The solidus (plural solidi) was a gold coin introduced by the Roman emperors Diocletian and Constantine I in the early 4th century AD. Struck at 72 to the Roman pound (about 4.5 grams) of nearly pure gold, it remained the principal gold coin of the Roman and Byzantine world for more than seven centuries.

History

Constantine established the solidus at a stable weight and fineness, replacing the debased aureus. Its consistency made it the dominant trade coin of the Mediterranean; in the Byzantine Empire it was later known in Greek as the nomisma. Hoards of solidi have been found from Britain to India and China along trade routes.

Decline

From the 1030s the fineness of the solidus was progressively debased, and Alexios I's monetary reform of 1092 replaced it with the hyperpyron. The word survives in later coin names such as the Italian soldo and the French sou, and in the term "soldier's pay" etymology associated with solidus.
