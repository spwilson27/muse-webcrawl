Transposing instrument

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A transposing instrument is a musical instrument for which the written notes are read at a pitch different from the pitch actually produced when the player reads the music. The difference between the written pitch and the sounding pitch is the instrument's transposition interval. By convention, when a player performs a written C, the pitch designated by the instrument's name sounds: a B♭ clarinet sounds B♭, an E♭ alto saxophone sounds E♭, and an F horn sounds F.

Common examples

Clarinets are usually in B♭ or A; saxophones in B♭ and E♭; trumpets and cornets in B♭; horns in F (with older parts in many keys); the English horn in F; and some flutes, oboes d'amore, and soprano instruments also transpose. Non-transposing instruments, which sound as written, are said to be "in C". A special case is octave transposition: piccolo, double bass, guitar, and tenor voice sound an octave from where they are written to keep their parts within a readable staff.

Reasons

The practice arose historically from instruments built in different sizes and keys, and persists because it lets players switch between family members (for example B♭ and A clarinets) using identical fingerings, and keeps parts within practical key signatures. Composers and arrangers must transpose parts when preparing scores, while a "concert pitch" full score shows all instruments at sounding pitch.
