Lysenkoism

Lysenkoism was a political campaign led by the Soviet agronomist Trofim Lysenko against genetics and science-based agriculture in the Soviet Union from the late 1920s to the mid-1960s. Lysenko rejected Mendelian genetics and the theory of natural selection, claiming instead that characteristics acquired during an organism's lifetime could be inherited, and that techniques such as vernalisation (exposing seeds to cold and moisture) could permanently improve crops.

History

Lysenko rose to prominence by promising quick increases in crop yields during a period of famine and collectivisation. Backed by Joseph Stalin, he became president of the Lenin Academy of Agricultural Sciences. At the August 1948 session of the Academy, Lysenko's supporters outlawed the teaching and practice of genetics in the USSR. Geneticists lost their posts; some, including the distinguished botanist Nikolai Vavilov, who died in prison in 1943, were persecuted.

Consequences

Lysenkoist policies damaged Soviet agriculture and biology for decades: crop yields stagnated, genuine plant-breeding science was suppressed, and Soviet genetics fell far behind the West. Lysenko's influence survived Stalin and continued under Nikita Khrushchev, ending only after Khrushchev's fall in 1964. Lysenkoism is widely cited as a classic case of pseudoscience sustained by political power, and the term is used metaphorically for the subordination of science to ideology.
