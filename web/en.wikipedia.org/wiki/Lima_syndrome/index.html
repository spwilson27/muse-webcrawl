Lima syndrome

Lima syndrome is a psychological phenomenon in which hostage-takers or abductors develop sympathy, affection, or positive feelings toward their captives, sometimes to the point of releasing them or improving their treatment. It is generally regarded as the inverse of Stockholm syndrome, in which captives develop positive feelings toward their captors.

Origin

The term was coined after the Japanese embassy hostage crisis in Lima, Peru, in December 1996, when members of the Túpac Amaru Revolutionary Movement (MRTA) seized hundreds of guests at a celebration at the Japanese ambassador's residence. Within days — unexpectedly for seasoned militants — the captors released many hostages, including some of high value to their cause, apparently having developed sympathy for them. The crisis ended in April 1997 when Peruvian commandos stormed the residence.

Proposed factors

Commentators have suggested that the phenomenon may be more likely when captors are young, inexperienced, or personally exposed to captives over an extended period in relatively humane conditions, allowing individual relationships to form. It has been discussed alongside related concepts such as captor bonding and trauma responses, though, like Stockholm syndrome, it is not a formal psychiatric diagnosis and is supported mainly by case reports rather than systematic study.
