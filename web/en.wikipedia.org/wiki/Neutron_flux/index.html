Neutron flux

Neutron flux is a scalar quantity used in nuclear physics and reactor engineering, defined as the total distance travelled by all free neutrons per unit time and volume. Equivalently, it is the product of the neutron density and their average speed, expressed in neutrons per square centimetre per second (n·cm−2·s−1).

Definition and use

Reaction rates in a material are proportional to the neutron flux multiplied by the relevant cross-section and the number density of target nuclei, so flux determines the rate of fission in a reactor core, the rate of neutron activation of materials, and radiation dose. The time-integrated flux is called neutron fluence.

Typical values

Fluxes vary enormously: natural background flux is extremely low, research reactors operate around 1013–1014 n·cm−2·s−1, power reactors somewhat higher in the core, and the flux in a nuclear explosion is vastly greater for a brief instant. Flux is usually divided by energy into thermal, epithermal and fast components, since cross-sections depend strongly on neutron energy.

Measurement

Flux is measured with fission chambers, boron-lined proportional counters, self-powered neutron detectors, and by irradiation of activation foils whose induced activity reveals the fluence.
