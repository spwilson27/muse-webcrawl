Nine Herbs Charm

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Herbs of the charm: mugwort, plantain, chamomile and others

The Nine Herbs Charm is an Old English medical charm recorded in the 10th- or 11th-century manuscript collection known as the Lacnunga ("Remedies"), preserved in the British Library as Harley MS 585. Written partly in verse and partly in prose, the charm describes the healing powers of nine herbs and is intended to treat poisoning and infection, picturing the herbs as weapons against nine poisons and nine infectious "flying venoms".

The nine herbs

The charm names and praises the herbs in turn. They are usually identified as mugwort, plantain (described as the "mother of herbs"), watercress (or stime), betony (or "atterlothe" in some identifications), chamomile, nettle, crab apple, chervil, and fennel — though the Old English plant names stune, atterlothe, and wergulu have been variously identified by scholars. Each herb is addressed directly and credited with power against specific poisons, worms, and diseases.

Content and mythology

The charm is notable for its reference to the Germanic god Woden (Odin), who is said to have taken nine "glory-twigs" and struck a serpent, which broke into nine parts — a passage often compared with Norse mythological material and regarded as one of the few surviving references to Woden in Old English literature. The text also contains Christian elements, and the prose conclusion gives practical instructions: the herbs are to be pounded into a salve with soap and apple juice, sung over three times, and applied to the patient while the charm is recited over the wound, the mixture, and the sufferer's ears.

Significance

Together with the Bald's Leechbook and the Herbarium Apuleii, the Lacnunga charms are a principal source for Anglo-Saxon medicine, illustrating the blend of herbal practice, ritual performance, and pagan and Christian belief in early medieval healing. The Nine Herbs Charm has attracted particular scholarly attention for its mythology and for what it reveals about the oral, performative character of early English medicine.
