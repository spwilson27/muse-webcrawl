Adivasi is a collective term for the Indigenous peoples of the Indian subcontinent, regarded as the earliest inhabitants of the region. In India most Adivasi communities are officially recognised as Scheduled Tribes, a constitutional category carrying affirmative-action protections in education, employment and political representation. Adivasis number roughly 104 million people in India, with additional communities in Nepal, Bangladesh and elsewhere.

Communities and languages

Adivasi peoples comprise hundreds of distinct communities speaking languages of several families — Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, Austroasiatic (such as Santali and Mundari) and Tibeto-Burman. Large groups include the Gond and Bhil of central India, the Santhal, Munda and Oraon of the Chota Nagpur plateau, the Meena of Rajasthan, and the Naga, Khasi, Garo and Mizo peoples of the Northeast. Many communities have traditionally practised animist religions centred on nature spirits and sacred groves, though many Adivasis today follow Hinduism, Christianity, Islam, Buddhism, or distinct codified faiths such as Sarnaism and Donyi-Polo.

History and issues

Adivasi communities have historically lived in forested and hill regions, often outside the caste system, and suffered land alienation under colonial rule — sparking rebellions such as the Santhal hul of 1855 and Birsa Munda's ulgulan of 1899–1900. Modern India protects tribal land and self-governance through the Fifth and Sixth Schedules of the Constitution, the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act of 1996, and the Forest Rights Act of 2006. Nevertheless, displacement by dams, mining and industry, together with poverty and limited services, remain major concerns, and Adivasi identity and rights are central themes in the politics of states such as Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh.
