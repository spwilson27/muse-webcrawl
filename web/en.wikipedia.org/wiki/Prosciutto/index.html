Prosciutto (Italian: [proʃˈʃutto]) is an Italian dry-cured ham that is usually thinly sliced and served uncooked; this style is called prosciutto crudo in Italian (or simply crudo) and is distinguished from cooked ham, prosciutto cotto. The word derives from the Latin perexsuctum, "thoroughly dried". A cured leg of pork is made from either a pig's or a wild boar's hind leg.

Production

The process of making prosciutto can take from nine months to two years, depending on the size of the ham. The leg is cleaned, salted with sea salt, and left for about two months; it is then washed to remove the salt, sometimes covered with a protective mixture of lard and flour (sugna), and hung in a dark, well-ventilated environment to dry and mature. Enzymatic changes during ageing give the ham its characteristic sweet, delicate flavour and deep rose colour. Traditionally no nitrates are used for the protected Italian hams.

Protected varieties

The most famous crudos are Prosciutto di Parma (Parma ham) from Emilia-Romagna and Prosciutto di San Daniele from Friuli-Venezia Giulia, both protected by European Union PDO status; other protected types include those of Modena, Tuscany, the Veneto, and Norcia. Similar dry-cured hams are produced elsewhere, such as Spanish jamón, French jambon de Bayonne, and Croatian pršut.

Serving

Prosciutto crudo is served in paper-thin slices, classically as an antipasto, wrapped around grissini or melon, or as an ingredient in dishes such as saltimbocca. Prosciutto cotto is a rosy cooked ham used in sandwiches and cooking throughout Italy.
