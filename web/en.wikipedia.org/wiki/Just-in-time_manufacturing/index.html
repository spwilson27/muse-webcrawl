Just-in-time (JIT) manufacturing, also known as just-in-time production, is a methodology aimed primarily at reducing times within the production system as well as response times from suppliers and to customers. It originated in Japan as a core element of the Toyota Production System (TPS) and became a foundation of lean manufacturing worldwide.

Principles

Under JIT, parts and materials are produced or delivered only as they are needed, in the quantity needed, thereby minimising inventory, storage costs and waste (muda). Production is "pulled" by actual demand rather than "pushed" by forecasts. Key mechanisms include:

  • Kanban — visual signalling cards or bins that authorise production or replenishment of a specific part.
  • Takt time — pacing production to the rate of customer demand.
  • Small lot sizes and rapid changeovers (SMED), reducing work-in-progress.
  • Jidoka — building quality in at the source, with authority to stop the line on defects.
  • Continuous improvement (kaizen) and close, long-term supplier relationships.

History

Kiichiro Toyoda articulated the "just in time" concept in the 1930s, and engineer Taiichi Ohno developed and implemented the system at Toyota from the late 1940s through the 1970s, influenced by American supermarket replenishment and Ford's early flow production. Western manufacturers adopted JIT widely during the 1980s as Japanese competition intensified.

Advantages and risks

JIT reduces inventory carrying costs, frees working capital, shortens lead times and exposes quality problems quickly. However, it leaves supply chains vulnerable to disruption: supplier failures, transport delays, natural disasters and geopolitical shocks — highlighted by the semiconductor shortages of the early 2020s — can halt production rapidly because buffer stocks are minimal. Many firms consequently blend JIT with strategic safety stocks for critical components.
