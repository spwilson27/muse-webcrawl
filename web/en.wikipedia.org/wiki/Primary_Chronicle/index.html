The Primary Chronicle, shortened from the Russian Primary Chronicle (Old East Slavic: Повѣсть времѧньныхъ лѣтъ, Povest' vremennykh let, "Tale of Bygone Years"), is a chronicle of the Kievan Rus' from the legendary founding of its peoples to about 1110. It was compiled in Kiev around 1113 and is the principal native source for the early history of the East Slavs. Tradition attributes the original compilation to Nestor, a monk of the Kiev Caves Monastery, though modern scholarship treats the text as the work of several compilers and editors.

Contents

The chronicle opens with the biblical division of the earth among the sons of Noah and traces the origins of the Slavs, then narrates the founding of Kiev, the calling of the Varangians under Rurik (the famous invitation of 862, "Our land is great and rich, but there is no order in it — come and rule over us"), the reigns of Oleg, Igor, Olga — whose revenge on the Drevlians and baptism are recounted in detail — Sviatoslav, and Vladimir the Great, whose "testing of the faiths" precedes the Christianization of Rus' in 988. The narrative continues through the reign of Yaroslav the Wise and the struggles of his descendants, ending with the early 12th century. Interspersed are treaties with Byzantium, saints' lives, speeches, and folk tales that give the work great literary as well as historical value.

Manuscripts and editions

The original is lost. The two principal witnesses are the Laurentian Codex, copied in 1377 by the monk Laurentius for Prince Dmitry of Suzdal — the oldest dated East Slavic chronicle manuscript — and the Hypatian Codex (c. 1425), found in the Ipatiev Monastery at Kostroma. The standard critical reconstruction is that of Aleksey Shakhmatov, who identified earlier lost compilations behind the surviving text, including an "Initial Compilation" of the 1090s. The standard English translation is by Samuel Hazzard Cross and Olgerd P. Sherbowitz-Wetzor (1953).

Historical reliability

Scholars use the chronicle critically: its accounts of the 9th and 10th centuries were written down two centuries or more after the events and blend record with legend (as in the tale of Oleg's death prophesied by his horse), while its dating framework ("In the year 6360...") and verbatim treaties of 911 and 944 preserve genuinely early material. It remains indispensable for the history of Rus', its relations with Byzantium and the steppe, and the Christianization of Eastern Europe.
