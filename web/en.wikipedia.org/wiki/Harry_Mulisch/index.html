Harry Kurt Victor Mulisch (29 July 1927 – 30 October 2010) was a Dutch writer, widely regarded as one of the "Great Three" of post-war Dutch literature alongside Willem Frederik Hermans and Gerard Reve. His novels, essays and plays repeatedly explore the moral legacy of the Second World War, the problem of guilt and responsibility, and the interplay of myth, technology and fate.

Life

Mulisch was born in Haarlem. His father, an Austrian-Hungarian-born bank employee, collaborated with the German occupation authorities during the war, administering confiscated Jewish property, while his mother was Jewish; the paradox of being both "the son of the war" from both sides haunted his work. After the war his father was imprisoned as a collaborator. Mulisch published his first novel, Archibald Strohalm, in 1952.

Works

His reportage on the Eichmann trial, Criminal Case 40/61 (1961), brought him international attention. The Assault (De aanslag, 1982), the story of a Dutch boy whose family is murdered in reprisal for an assassination, became his most widely read novel and was filmed in 1986, winning the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film. The Discovery of Heaven (De ontdekking van de hemel, 1992), an epic philosophical novel about two friends and a divine plan to recover the stone tablets of the Ten Commandments, is considered his masterpiece and was voted the best Dutch novel ever in a 2007 readers' poll. Other major works include The Stone Bridal Bed (1959), Two Women (1975), The Procedure (1998) and Siegfried (2001).

Death and reputation

Mulisch died of cancer in Amsterdam in 2010. He received numerous honours, including the P. C. Hooft Award (1977) and the Dutch Literature Prize (1995), and was frequently mentioned as a candidate for the Nobel Prize in Literature.
