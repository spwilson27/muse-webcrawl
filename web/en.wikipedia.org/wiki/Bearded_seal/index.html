Bearded seal

Scientific name: Erignathus barbatus
Family: Phocidae (earless seals)
Conservation status: Least Concern (IUCN)

The bearded seal (Erignathus barbatus), also called the square flipper seal, is a medium-sized pinniped found in and near the Arctic Ocean and the northernmost parts of the Pacific and Atlantic. It gets its name from its very long, abundant whiskers (vibrissae), which curl when dry, and is the largest of the northern phocid seals.

Description

Adults are typically 2.1–2.7 m long and weigh 200–430 kg, with females larger than males. Distinctive features include four teats in females (unique among phocids), square-shaped fore-flippers with strong claws, a broad head, and a greyish-brown coat without patterning. The whiskers are highly sensitive and are used to locate prey on the seabed.

Behaviour and diet

Bearded seals are solitary, bottom-feeding seals. They maintain breathing holes in shore-fast ice in winter and rest on ice floes in summer. They dive to depths of up to 300 m but usually forage in water shallower than 100 m, eating crabs, shrimp, clams, whelks, squid, and fish such as Arctic cod and sculpins. Males produce long, eerie, descending trill songs underwater during the spring breeding season, audible over great distances.

Relation to humans

Bearded seals are an important subsistence resource for Arctic indigenous peoples; their tough skin (ugruk in Iñupiaq) is traditionally used for boat covers, lines, and boot soles. The species depends on sea ice for pupping and moulting and is considered vulnerable to climate-driven loss of Arctic ice.
