Unequal treaties

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The term unequal treaties refers to a class of treaties that were imposed by technologically and militarily stronger powers — principally the Western colonial powers and later Japan — on weaker states, mainly in East and Southeast Asia and the Ottoman sphere, during the 19th and early 20th centuries. The treaties typically granted the stronger party one-sided advantages: extraterritoriality for its nationals (exemption from local law), control over the weaker state's tariffs, territorial cessions or leases, indemnities, and access to opened "treaty ports" — without reciprocal concessions.

China

The archetypal series began with the Treaty of Nanking (1842), which ended the First Opium War between Britain and Qing China: Hong Kong Island was ceded, five ports were opened to British trade and residence, a large indemnity was imposed, and China's tariff autonomy was surrendered. The supplementary Treaty of the Bogue (1843) added extraterritoriality and a most-favoured-nation clause that automatically extended any later privilege to Britain. France and the United States obtained similar terms in 1844 (Treaties of Whampoa and Wanghia). A second round followed the Second Opium War — the Treaties of Tientsin (1858) and Convention of Peking (1860) opened more ports, legalised the opium trade, admitted missionaries and foreign envoys to Beijing, and ceded Kowloon. After China's defeat by Japan in 1895, the Treaty of Shimonoseki ceded Taiwan and recognised Korean independence; in 1901 the Boxer Protocol imposed a crushing indemnity. China also lost territory to Russia under the Treaties of Aigun (1858) and Peking (1860).

In Chinese historical memory the unequal treaties anchor the "century of humiliation" (c. 1839–1949), ending with the founding of the People's Republic; most extraterritorial rights were relinquished by the Western powers in 1943, and Hong Kong's retrocession in 1997 — after the 99-year lease of the New Territories expired — is often framed as the final closing of the era.

Japan and Korea

Japan itself was subjected to unequal arrangements after Commodore Perry's arrival: the Harris Treaty of 1858 imposed extraterritoriality and fixed tariffs. The Meiji government made treaty revision a central goal, regaining tariff autonomy by 1911 and ending extraterritoriality in 1899. Japan then imposed its own unequal treaty on Korea — the Treaty of Ganghwa (1876) — en route to annexation in 1910. The Ottoman Empire's "capitulations" to European powers are an older, parallel example of extraterritorial privilege, abolished by the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923.

Assessment

Historians note that the term is a later political and historiographical label — prominently used by Chinese nationalists from the 1920s — rather than a category of contemporary international law, and that the treaties' inequity lay less in their text than in the coercive circumstances of their imposition and their unilateral revision of sovereignty. The narrative remains politically potent, especially in China, where it is a cornerstone of patriotic education.
