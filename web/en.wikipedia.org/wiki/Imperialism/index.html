Imperialism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Imperialism is the practice, theory or attitude of a state extending its power and dominion, especially through territorial acquisition or by gaining political and economic control over other areas — often through formal empires, and by extension through informal mechanisms of influence. The word acquired its modern political charge in the 19th century (first about Napoleon III's France, then Britain's expansion), and its most influential theory in Lenin's Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism (1917), which grounded it in monopoly capital's need for outlets; Hobson's liberal version (1902) and Schumpeter's sociological one (atavistic warrior classes) frame the classic debate.

History and usage

Practice long predates the word — Assyrian, Roman, Ottoman, Spanish and Qing expansions — but the era named by historians "New Imperialism" (c. 1870–1914) saw European powers, the US and Japan partition most of Africa and Asia in the "Scramble", justified by civilising missions and racial ideology and resisted everywhere it landed. Decolonization formally ended the empires; the term then migrated to analyses of informal hierarchy — "neo-colonialism" (Nkrumah), dependency theory, American post-1945 primacy (debated as empire, hegemony or liberal order), and 21st-century resource and infrastructure politics. Whether "imperialism" remains a precise category or a polemical residue depends on the user's theory of power — the contest over the word is part of the politics it describes.
