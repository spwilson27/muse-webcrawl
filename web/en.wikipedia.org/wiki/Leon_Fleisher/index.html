Leon Fleisher (July 23, 1928 – August 2, 2020) was an American classical pianist, conductor, and teacher. A child prodigy who studied with Artur Schnabel, he won the Queen Elisabeth Competition in Brussels in 1952 and made a celebrated series of concerto recordings with George Szell and the Cleveland Orchestra. In the mid-1960s he lost the use of two fingers of his right hand to focal dystonia, and reinvented himself as a performer of the left-hand repertoire, a conductor, and one of the most influential piano teachers in the United States. Partial recovery late in life allowed him to return to two-handed playing and recording.

Early life and career

Fleisher was born in San Francisco to immigrant parents and began piano lessons at four. He gave his first public recital at eight and at nine became a pupil of the great Austrian pianist Artur Schnabel, with whom he studied for ten years, first in Italy and then in New York. At sixteen he performed with the New York Philharmonic under Pierre Monteux, who called him "the pianistic find of the century". After winning first prize at the Queen Elisabeth Competition in 1952 — the first American to do so — he embarked on a major international career. His recordings of the Beethoven and Brahms piano concertos, the Schumann and Grieg concertos, and Rachmaninoff's Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini with Szell and Cleveland, made for Epic/CBS in the late 1950s and early 1960s, remain reference recordings.

Focal dystonia and left-hand career

In 1964, on the eve of a tour of the Soviet Union, Fleisher began to lose control of the fourth and fifth fingers of his right hand; they curled involuntarily inward. The condition, eventually diagnosed as focal dystonia, forced him to cancel his two-handed career at its height. After years of searching for treatment, he built a second career performing the repertoire written for the left hand alone — works commissioned by the one-armed pianist Paul Wittgenstein, including Ravel's Piano Concerto for the Left Hand and concertos by Prokofiev, Britten, and Hindemith — and commissioned new left-hand works from contemporary composers. He also became music director of the Annapolis Symphony Orchestra and an associate conductor of the Baltimore Symphony, and co-founded the Theater Chamber Players in Washington, D.C.

Teaching and late career

Fleisher taught at the Peabody Institute in Baltimore from 1959 until his death, and also at the Curtis Institute of Music and the Royal Conservatory of Music in Toronto; his students include many leading pianists of the next generations. In the 1990s, a combination of Rolfing bodywork and Botox injections released his right hand enough for him to resume two-handed performance. His 2004 album Two Hands, his first two-handed recording in over forty years, was widely acclaimed, and Charles Michener's documentary of the same name was nominated for an Academy Award. Fleisher received the Kennedy Center Honors in 2007 and published a memoir, My Nine Lives (2010), with Anne Midgette. He died of cancer in Baltimore on 2 August 2020, aged 92.
