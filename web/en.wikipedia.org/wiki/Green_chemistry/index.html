Green chemistry

Green chemistry aims to design chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate hazardous substances.

Green chemistry, also called sustainable chemistry, is an area of chemistry and chemical engineering focused on the design of products and processes that minimise or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances. It emerged in the 1990s, influenced by pollution-prevention legislation in the United States, and was given a systematic framework by Paul Anastas and John Warner.

The twelve principles

Anastas and Warner's Green Chemistry: Theory and Practice (1998) set out twelve principles, including: waste prevention rather than clean-up; atom economy in synthesis; designing less hazardous chemical syntheses; designing safer chemicals; using safer solvents and auxiliaries; designing for energy efficiency; using renewable feedstocks; reducing unnecessary derivatisation; preferring catalysis over stoichiometric reagents; designing products to degrade after use; real-time analysis for pollution prevention; and inherently safer chemistry for accident prevention.

Metrics and practice

Practitioners assess processes using metrics such as the E-factor (mass of waste per mass of product) and process mass intensity, alongside atom economy. Green chemistry approaches include solvent-free reactions, reactions in water or supercritical carbon dioxide, biocatalysis with enzymes, catalysis with recyclable catalysts, and the use of biomass-derived starting materials. The pharmaceutical industry has been a prominent adopter, redesigning syntheses of major drugs to cut waste and solvent use dramatically.

Recognition

The Presidential Green Chemistry Challenge Awards, administered by the US Environmental Protection Agency since 1996, recognise industrial and academic innovations in the field. Dedicated journals, conferences and institutes — including the ACS Green Chemistry Institute — support a large international research community.
