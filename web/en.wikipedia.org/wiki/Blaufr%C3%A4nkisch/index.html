Blaufränkisch (German for "blue Frankish") is a dark-skinned variety of grape used for red wine. It is a late-ripening variety that gives deeply coloured wines with firm tannins, pronounced acidity and flavours of dark cherry, blackberry and spice. It is the second most widely planted red grape in Austria and is widespread across Central Europe.

History

The variety's exact origin is uncertain, but DNA profiling has shown it to be a cross of Gouais blanc (Heunisch) and an unknown, possibly extinct, variety related to Blaue Zimmettraube; it was long thought to descend from the Franconian wine country. The name "Fränkisch" distinguished higher-quality vines from the lesser "Heunisch" grapes in the medieval Holy Roman Empire. In 1862 the name Blaufränkisch appeared officially at an exhibition in Vienna, and in 1877 the grape was introduced to Germany in the Württemberg district of Weinsberg, where it became known as Lemberger.

Regional names and distribution

In Hungary the grape is called Kékfrankos and is a key component of Egri Bikavér ("Bull's Blood") as well as varietal wines from Sopron and Villány. In Germany, as Lemberger, it is grown mainly in Württemberg. In Slovakia it is Frankovka modrá, in the Czech Republic Frankovka, in Croatia Frankovka, and in Italy Franconia. Plantings exist in the Finger Lakes of New York and in Washington State, where it is usually labelled Lemberger.

Wine style

Blaufränkisch wines are typically medium- to full-bodied, with refreshing acidity, flavours of sour cherry, dark berries, black pepper and sometimes a mineral, earthy character. The best examples, notably from Burgenland appellations such as Mittelburgenland DAC and Eisenberg DAC, age well for a decade or more. It is also used for rosé and, in blends, contributes colour and structure.
