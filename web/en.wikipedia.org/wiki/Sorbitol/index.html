Sorbitol

Chemical formula: C6H14O6

Molar mass: 182.17 g/mol

Appearance: White crystalline powder

E number: E420

Also known as: Glucitol, D-sorbitol

Sorbitol (less commonly known as glucitol) is a sugar alcohol with a sweet taste that the human body metabolizes slowly. Most sorbitol is made from potato starch, but it is also found in nature, for example in apples, pears, peaches and prunes. It is converted to fructose by the enzyme sorbitol-6-phosphate 2-dehydrogenase. Sorbitol is an isomer of mannitol, another sugar alcohol; the two differ only in the orientation of the hydroxyl group on carbon 2.

Uses

Sorbitol is widely used as a sugar substitute in "sugar-free" and reduced-calorie foods, including mints, chewing gum, cough syrups and diet products. It provides about 2.6 kilocalories per gram, roughly two-thirds the energy of sucrose, with about 60% of its sweetness. As a food additive it has the E number E420 and also serves as a humectant, texturizer and softener.

Industrially, sorbitol is a precursor in the manufacture of vitamin C (ascorbic acid) via the Reichstein process, and is used in cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, surfactants and toothpaste, where it helps retain moisture and does not contribute to tooth decay.

Medical significance

Because sorbitol is poorly absorbed from the small intestine, large amounts draw water into the bowel and have a laxative effect; foods containing it may carry a warning that excess consumption may have a laxative effect. In people with diabetes, sorbitol produced inside cells (via the polyol pathway) has been implicated in complications such as retinopathy and neuropathy, since sorbitol cannot easily cross cell membranes and its accumulation creates osmotic stress.

Production

Commercial sorbitol is produced by the catalytic hydrogenation of glucose, converting the aldehyde group to a primary alcohol. It was first isolated from the berries of the rowan tree (Sorbus aucuparia) in 1872 by French chemist Joseph Boussingault, from which it takes its name.
