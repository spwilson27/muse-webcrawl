Ammonites are an extinct group of marine mollusc animals of the subclass Ammonoidea in the class Cephalopoda. They are best known for their planispiral, chambered shells, stiffened by internal walls (septa) whose junctions with the outer shell form intricate suture patterns used to classify species. Ammonites first appeared in the Devonian, about 409 million years ago, flourished in the oceans of the Paleozoic and Mesozoic, and were wiped out in the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event 66 million years ago, along with the non-avian dinosaurs.

Anatomy and life

The ammonite animal lived in the largest, outermost chamber (the body chamber) of its shell; the inner chambers were filled with gas and fluid and connected by a cord of tissue, the siphuncle, which regulated buoyancy much as in the living nautilus. Most ammonites were predators or scavengers with beak-like jaws (aptychi sometimes served as an operculum); their soft anatomy is inferred from rare fossil impressions and from their closer living relatives, the coleoids. Shells ranged from a few centimeters to giants such as Parapuzosia seppenradensis, over 1.7 meters across. Many show strong sexual dimorphism, with large females (macroconchs) and small males (microconchs).

Evolution and diversity

Ammonoids evolved from straight-shelled bactritids. Goniatites, with simple sutures, dominated the late Paleozoic; ceratites prevailed in the Triassic; and the true ammonites (order Ammonitida), with frilled, complex sutures, radiated explosively in the Jurassic and Cretaceous. The group survived several mass extinctions, re-diversifying each time, and experimented with uncoiled and partially uncoiled (heteromorph) shells, especially in the Cretaceous.

Importance to geology

Because ammonites evolved rapidly, were abundant, and floated or swam widely, their fossils are among the best index fossils: distinct species characterize narrow intervals of geological time, allowing strata across the world to be correlated and dated. This biostratigraphy was pioneered by nineteenth-century geologists such as William Smith and Alcide d'Orbigny. Their name derives from the Egyptian god Ammon, whose ram's horns the coiled shells were said to resemble.
