Hamilton–Jacobi equation

In physics, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, named after William Rowan Hamilton and Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi, is an alternative formulation of classical mechanics, equivalent to other formulations such as Newton's laws, Lagrangian mechanics and Hamiltonian mechanics. It expresses the dynamics of a system through a single first-order partial differential equation for Hamilton's principal function S: −∂S/∂t = H(q, ∂S/∂q, t), where H is the Hamiltonian.

Formulation

Solving the Hamilton–Jacobi equation for a complete integral S(q, α, t) with constants α yields the motion by differentiation: the momenta are p = ∂S/∂q, and the coordinates follow from ∂S/∂α = constant. The method is especially powerful for separable systems and central-force problems, since action-angle variables arise naturally.

Applications

The equation forms a bridge between classical mechanics, geometrical optics (through the eikonal approximation) and quantum mechanics: Hamilton's principal function relates to the phase of the wavefunction in the semiclassical (WKB) limit, and Schrödinger's development of wave mechanics was guided by the Hamilton–Jacobi formulation. It is also used in optimal control theory (the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation) and in the study of Hamilton–Jacobi equations for viscosity solutions.
