Alkalosis

Specialty: Internal medicine, nephrology
Key measure: Arterial blood pH > 7.45

Alkalosis is the result of a process reducing hydrogen ion concentration in the arterial blood plasma (alkalemia). In contrast to acidemia (serum pH 7.35 or lower), alkalemia occurs when the serum pH is higher than normal (7.45 or higher). Alkalosis is usually divided into the categories of respiratory alkalosis and metabolic alkalosis or a combined respiratory/metabolic alkalosis.

Types

Respiratory alkalosis

Respiratory alkalosis is caused by hyperventilation, which lowers the partial pressure of carbon dioxide in the blood and thereby raises pH. Causes include anxiety and panic attacks, fever, pain, altitude, and conditions such as pulmonary embolism or early sepsis. It is the most common acid–base disturbance in critically ill patients.

Metabolic alkalosis

Metabolic alkalosis arises from an increase in serum bicarbonate, commonly through loss of hydrogen ions from the gastrointestinal tract (for example, protracted vomiting or nasogastric suction), volume depletion with chloride loss, or diuretic use. It is often maintained by hypovolemia, hypokalemia and increased aldosterone activity, which prevent the kidneys from excreting the excess bicarbonate.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms of alkalosis include neuromuscular irritability, tetany, paresthesias, muscle cramps and, in severe cases, seizures and cardiac arrhythmias. Alkalosis lowers the concentration of ionised calcium by increasing calcium binding to plasma proteins, which accounts for many of the neuromuscular symptoms. The body compensates through hypoventilation in metabolic alkalosis and through renal bicarbonate excretion in chronic respiratory alkalosis.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause: rebreathing or anxiolysis for hyperventilation, and volume and chloride replacement (often with potassium repletion) for chloride-responsive metabolic alkalosis. Acetazolamide or, rarely, dilute hydrochloric acid may be used for severe or chloride-resistant cases.
