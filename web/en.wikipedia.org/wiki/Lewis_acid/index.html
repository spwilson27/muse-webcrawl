Lewis acid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Lewis acid (named after the American physical chemist Gilbert N. Lewis) is a chemical species that contains an empty orbital capable of accepting an electron pair from a Lewis base, thereby forming a Lewis adduct. Lewis proposed the definition in 1923, generalizing the concept of an acid beyond the proton-transfer (Brønsted–Lowry) model: a Lewis acid is an electron-pair acceptor, and a Lewis base is an electron-pair donor.

Examples

Classic Lewis acids include boron trifluoride (BF3) and boron trichloride, which accept a pair of electrons into boron's empty p orbital, and metal halides such as aluminium chloride (AlCl3), iron(III) chloride (FeCl3), and zinc chloride (ZnCl2), widely used as catalysts. Metal cations such as Mg2+, Cu2+, and Fe3+ act as Lewis acids in coordination chemistry, accepting lone pairs from ligands. The proton (H+) itself is a Lewis acid, which makes Brønsted acids a special case of the broader Lewis model.

Reactions and applications

Lewis acids are workhorse catalysts in organic chemistry. They activate carbonyl compounds toward nucleophilic attack, underpinning the Friedel–Crafts alkylation and acylation (using AlCl3), the Diels–Alder reaction (where coordination to the dienophile accelerates reaction and improves selectivity), aldol and Mukaiyama aldol reactions, and many cycloadditions and polymerizations. Lewis acidity can be measured by methods such as the Gutmann–Beckett method, and is rationalized qualitatively by hard–soft acid–base (HSAB) theory, which pairs hard acids preferentially with hard bases. Frustrated Lewis pairs — sterically hindered acid/base combinations that cannot form an adduct — have become an important class of metal-free catalysts, including for hydrogen activation.
