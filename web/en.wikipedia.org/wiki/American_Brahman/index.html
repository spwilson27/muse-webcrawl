The American Brahman is an American breed of zebuine beef cattle. It was bred in the United States from 1885 from cattle originating in India, principally the Gir, Guzerá, and Nelore breeds, imported mainly through Brazil and Mexico. It was the first beef breed developed in the United States and became the foundation for many other breeds adapted to hot climates.

History

Zebu cattle reached the Gulf Coast states in small numbers during the 19th century. Between 1905 and the 1940s, substantial imports of Indian cattle via Brazil allowed breeders in Texas and Louisiana to select systematically for beef production in hot, humid conditions. The American Brahman Breeders Association was founded in 1924, adopting the name "Brahman" — from the Sanskrit priestly caste — suggested by the association's secretary. Brahmans were later exported to Australia, Africa, and Latin America, where they transformed tropical beef industries.

Characteristics

Brahmans are distinguished by a prominent hump over the shoulders and neck, long drooping ears, abundant loose skin (dewlap) that increases surface area for cooling, and a short, glossy coat, usually grey or red. They tolerate heat, sunlight, and humidity well, resist many insects and parasites, and can thrive on poor forage. Cows are long-lived and noted for maternal ability and abundant milk for their calves in harsh environments.

Use and crosses

The breed is raised for beef production in tropical and subtropical regions. Brahman genetics underpin numerous recognised crossbreeds and composite breeds, including the Santa Gertrudis, Brangus, Beefmaster, Braford, and Charbray, which combine Brahman hardiness with the meat qualities of European breeds.
