Hypergolic propellant

A hypergolic propellant combination consists of a fuel and an oxidizer that ignite spontaneously on contact with each other, without any ignition source. The term hypergolic derives from the Greek hyper ("over"), ergon ("work"), and the Latin oleum ("oil"), and was coined in Germany during the Second World War. Because hypergolic engines need no igniter and can be restarted reliably many times, they have been widely used in spacecraft maneuvering systems, missile propulsion, and launch vehicle upper stages, despite the extreme toxicity and corrosiveness of the chemicals involved.

Common hypergolic combinations pair a hydrazine-family fuel — monomethylhydrazine (MMH), unsymmetrical dimethylhydrazine (UDMH), or hydrazine itself — with the oxidizer dinitrogen tetroxide (NTO). An older and widely used combination in Russian and Chinese rockets pairs UDMH with nitric acid. Aerozine 50, a mixture of hydrazine and UDMH, was used with NTO in the American Titan missiles and the Apollo Lunar Module.

Properties

The defining property of a hypergolic pair is that ignition occurs within milliseconds of the fuel and oxidizer meeting in the combustion chamber. This eliminates the need for spark plugs, glow plugs, or pyrotechnic igniters, removes a common failure mode, and allows an engine to be stopped and restarted an essentially unlimited number of times simply by opening and closing the propellant valves. Hypergolic propellants are also typically storable: they remain liquid at ordinary temperatures, unlike cryogenic propellants such as liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, which boil away and cannot be kept in a vehicle for long periods. These qualities make hypergolics the standard choice for spacecraft reaction control systems and orbital maneuvering engines, and for ballistic missiles that must be kept fuelled and ready to launch at short notice.

The disadvantages are severe. Hydrazine-family fuels are highly toxic and suspected carcinogens; dinitrogen tetroxide is corrosive and produces poisonous nitrogen dioxide gas (responsible for the orange-brown clouds seen in accidents). Handling requires technicians to wear full protective suits (SCAPE suits), and spills can cause serious environmental damage. Hypergolic propellants also deliver lower specific impulse than cryogenic combinations, so they are rarely used for large first-stage boosters.

History

Hypergolic ignition was discovered in Germany in the 1930s; the combination of hydrogen peroxide with a fuel was investigated by Hellmuth Walter's company for rocket-assisted takeoff and the Messerschmitt Me 163 Komet rocket fighter, which used the hypergolic pair C-Stoff (hydrazine hydrate in methanol) and T-Stoff (concentrated hydrogen peroxide). In the Soviet Union, Valentin Glushko's design bureau developed nitric acid–based hypergolic engines that became the mainstay of Soviet and Russian space launchers such as Proton, whose first stage uses UDMH and NTO.

In the United States, hypergolic propellants powered the Titan family of ICBMs and launch vehicles, the Apollo Lunar Module's ascent and descent engines — chosen because absolute reliability of ignition was essential — and the Space Shuttle's orbital maneuvering and reaction control systems. The Apollo–Soyuz and Shuttle-era spacecraft, the International Space Station's Russian segment service module, and the Dragon 2 spacecraft's SuperDraco abort engines all rely on hypergolic MMH/NTO or hydrazine/NTO combinations. China's Long March 2, 3, and 4 families use UDMH/NTO, as do India's PSLV second stage and GSLV.

The dangers of hypergolics were illustrated by the 1980 Damascus Titan missile explosion in Arkansas, and by the 2007 and 2013 Proton-M launch failures in Kazakhstan, which scattered toxic propellant over the steppe. Partly in response, newer designs such as SpaceX's Starship use methalox (methane/liquid oxygen) instead, and agencies have investigated "green" alternatives such as the hydroxylammonium nitrate–based AF-M315E (flown on NASA's Green Propellant Infusion Mission) and the Swedish-developed LMP-103S, which promise much of the storability of hypergolics with far lower toxicity.

Common combinations

MMH / NTO is the dominant combination in Western spacecraft. UDMH / NTO is used in Proton and many Chinese and Indian stages. Hydrazine / NTO appears in some spacecraft thrusters. UDMH / red fuming nitric acid was the classic early Soviet combination, used in the Scud missile. Monopropellant hydrazine, decomposed over a catalyst bed, is also hypergolic-like in its reliability and is ubiquitous in small satellite thrusters, though it is not a bipropellant hypergolic in the strict sense.
