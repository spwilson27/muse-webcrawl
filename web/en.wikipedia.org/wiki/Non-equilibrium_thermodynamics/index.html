Non-equilibrium thermodynamics

Non-equilibrium thermodynamics is a branch of thermodynamics that deals with physical systems that are not in thermodynamic equilibrium but can be described in terms of macroscopic quantities that represent an extrapolation of the variables used for equilibrium systems. It treats transport processes — heat conduction, diffusion, chemical reaction, electrical conduction — as fluxes driven by thermodynamic forces such as gradients of temperature, chemical potential and velocity.

Linear regime and Onsager relations

Near equilibrium, fluxes are assumed proportional to forces: heat flux to the temperature gradient, diffusion flux to the chemical-potential gradient, and so on. Lars Onsager showed in 1931 that the matrix of cross-coefficients is symmetric — the Onsager reciprocal relations — a result he called the fourth law of thermodynamics and for which he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1968. Cross-effects such as thermoelectricity (the Seebeck and Peltier effects) and thermal diffusion are unified in this framework. Entropy production, the product of fluxes and forces, is non-negative, expressing the second law.

Far from equilibrium

Far from equilibrium, linear laws fail and systems can spontaneously form organised patterns — dissipative structures — studied by Ilya Prigogine, who received the 1977 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work. Examples include Bénard convection cells and oscillating chemical reactions such as the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction. Prigogine's minimum entropy production theorem characterises steady states in the linear regime.

Approaches

Theories of non-equilibrium systems include classical irreversible thermodynamics, rational thermodynamics, extended irreversible thermodynamics, and statistical-mechanical approaches founded on fluctuation theorems, which quantify the probability of transient violations of the second law in small systems.
