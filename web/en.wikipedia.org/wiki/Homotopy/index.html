Homotopy

In topology, a continuous deformation between two continuous functions from one topological space to another.


A homotopy continuously deforms one path into another.

In topology, a homotopy is a continuous deformation of one continuous function into another, keeping the domain and codomain fixed. Two functions related by a homotopy are called homotopic; homotopy defines an equivalence relation fundamental to algebraic topology, where algebraic invariants (such as groups) are attached to spaces in ways that depend only on homotopy classes of maps.

Definition

Formally, given topological spaces X and Y and continuous functions f, g : X → Y, a homotopy from f to g is a continuous function H : X × [0, 1] → Y such that H(x, 0) = f(x) and H(x, 1) = g(x) for all x. Intuitively, the interval parameter acts as "time" during which f is continuously deformed into g.

Homotopy of paths and the fundamental group

Applied to loops based at a point, homotopy (with endpoints fixed) defines the equivalence classes that form the fundamental group π1(X), the first and most basic homotopy group. A loop that can be shrunk to a point (null-homotopic) represents the identity element. Spaces with trivial fundamental group are called simply connected. Higher homotopy groups πn generalise this idea to maps of spheres.

Homotopy equivalence

Two spaces X and Y are homotopy equivalent if there are maps f : X → Y and g : Y → X whose compositions are homotopic to the respective identity maps. Homotopy equivalent spaces share the same algebraic invariants — homology and cohomology groups, and homotopy groups (weakly). A space homotopy equivalent to a point is contractible. For example, a solid disk is contractible, and a circle is homotopy equivalent (but not homeomorphic) to a Möbius band's boundary-like core circle.

Homotopy theory

Homotopy theory studies spaces up to homotopy equivalence. Its modern development includes model categories (Quillen), spectra and stable homotopy theory, and ∞-categories. Homotopy type theory applies these ideas to foundations of mathematics and proof assistants.
