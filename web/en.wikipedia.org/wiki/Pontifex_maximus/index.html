Pontifex maximus

The pontifex maximus (Latin for "greatest priest", literally "greatest bridge-builder") was the chief high priest of the College of Pontiffs (Collegium Pontificum) in ancient Rome. It was the most important position in the ancient Roman state religion, open only to patricians until 254 BCE, when a plebeian, Tiberius Coruncanius, first held it.

Duties

The pontifex maximus supervised the entire religious apparatus of the state: the pontiffs, flamines, augurs, and Vestal Virgins. He regulated the calendar, including the insertion of intercalary months, oversaw adoptions and wills, kept the official annals (annales maximi), and resided at the Regia in the Roman Forum. The word "pontifex" is traditionally explained as "bridge-builder" (pons + facere), perhaps referring to the sacred Sublician Bridge over the Tiber.

Imperial period and later use

Julius Caesar was elected pontifex maximus in 63 BCE. From Augustus onward, every emperor held the office, which was listed among the imperial titles. The Christian emperor Gratian is said to have declined the title in the 380s CE. The style "Pontifex Maximus" was later adopted as one of the official titles of the Pope, head of the Roman Catholic Church, and appears on buildings and inscriptions in Rome to this day.
