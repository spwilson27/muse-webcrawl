Praxiteles (Greek: Πραξιτέλης, c. 400–330 BCE) of Athens was the most renowned of the Attic sculptors of the 4th century BCE. Working principally in marble rather than bronze, he introduced a new sensuousness and grace into Greek sculpture: soft modelling, the celebrated "Praxitelean curve" of the resting figure, and the first monumental female nude in Greek art.

Works

His most famous work, the Aphrodite of Knidos (c. 350 BCE), depicted the goddess preparing for her bath, nude; rejected by the people of Kos, who commissioned it, it was bought by Knidos, where it became a tourist attraction of the ancient world and the model for centuries of Venus statues (see Venus de Milo). Other works include the Apollo Sauroktonos (the Lizard-Slayer), the Satyr Pouring Wine, and the Hermes and the Infant Dionysus found at Olympia in 1877 — the most likely surviving original from his hand, though its attribution remains debated. His model and mistress was the courtesan Phryne, who also posed for Apelles' painting of Aphrodite Anadyomene.

Style and influence

Praxiteles favoured marble, whose surface he had finished by the painter Nicias, allowing subtle effects of light and flesh. His languid, dreamlike figures, supported on one leg with the hip thrown out, mark the transition from classical idealism to Hellenistic naturalism, and his Aphrodite set the canonical type of female beauty for the rest of antiquity.
