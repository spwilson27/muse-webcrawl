Wall Street Crash of 1929

The Wall Street Crash of 1929, also known as the Great Crash, was a major stock market crash in the United States in late October 1929. It began on Thursday 24 October ("Black Thursday") and reached its worst on Monday 28 and Tuesday 29 October ("Black Monday" and "Black Tuesday"), when the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell about 13% and 12% on consecutive days amid record trading volumes. The crash ended the speculative boom of the Roaring Twenties and is conventionally taken as the start of the Great Depression.

Background

Through the 1920s, US share prices climbed steeply on optimism, easy credit and margin buying — investors purchasing shares with as little as 10% down. The Dow rose from 63 in 1921 to a peak of 381 on 3 September 1929. Warning signs included faltering industrial output, the Federal Reserve's tightening against speculation, and controversial calls for a coming correction by figures such as Roger Babson. Margin loans and leveraged investment trusts magnified the fragility.

The crash

On Black Thursday, 24 October, nearly 12.9 million shares changed hands as prices plunged; a bankers' pool organised by J.P. Morgan & Co. briefly stabilised the market, and Richard Whitney's bid for U.S. Steel became famous. Over the weekend, however, margin calls spread panic. On Black Monday the Dow fell 12.8%, and on Black Tuesday another 11.7% on 16.4 million shares — a record that stood for decades. Leading bankers' reassurances failed, and although there were rallies, the market trended down until July 1932, by which time the Dow had lost nearly 89% of its peak value.

Consequences

The crash destroyed wealth and confidence, accelerated bank failures, and — interacting with the gold standard, debt deflation and policy errors — deepened into the worldwide Great Depression. It prompted sweeping reform: the Securities Act of 1933, the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 creating the SEC, and the Glass–Steagall separation of commercial and investment banking. Historians debate how far the crash itself, as opposed to monetary contraction, caused the Depression, but it remains the archetypal stock market collapse.
