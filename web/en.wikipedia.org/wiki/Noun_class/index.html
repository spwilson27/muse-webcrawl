Noun class

FieldLinguistics (morphology, typology)
DefinitionA category of nouns reflected in agreement on other words
Famous systemsBantu languages (10–20+ classes); Dyirbal (4 classes)
Related conceptGrammatical gender (small class systems)

A noun class is a particular category of nouns, distinguished not just by the noun's form but by the agreement patterns it triggers on verbs, adjectives, pronouns and other elements of the sentence. Every noun in a noun-class language belongs to one class, and classes are often partly organised by meaning — for example humans, animals, plants, long thin objects, liquids or abstracts — though assignment of many nouns is arbitrary or historically opaque. Noun class is closely related to grammatical gender; the terms are often used interchangeably, with "gender" usually reserved for smaller systems (two or three classes) with a sex-based core.

Bantu noun classes

The classic example is the Bantu languages of Africa. Proto-Bantu is reconstructed with around twenty classes, marked by prefixes on the noun and by concord prefixes throughout the sentence. In Swahili, for example, m-toto "child" (class 1) has plural wa-toto (class 2); ki-tabu "book" (class 7) has plural vi-tabu (class 8); and each class controls matching prefixes on verbs and adjectives. Classes pair into singular/plural "genders".

Other systems

The Australian language Dyirbal became famous (through R. M. W. Dixon's work and George Lakoff's Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things) for its four classes, one of which includes women, fire, water, fighting and some animals — grouping driven by mythology and association as much as by physical features. Niger-Congo languages beyond Bantu, some Indo-European languages (with gender systems), and Caucasian languages such as Chechen also have noun classes. Classifiers in languages like Chinese and Thai are a different but related device: words chosen by noun type, used mainly with numerals, without agreement.

Function

Noun classes help track referents in discourse, disambiguate meanings, and sometimes encode speakers' classification of the world. Class membership can shift a word's meaning — the same root may mean different things in different classes — and speakers exploit classes derivationally, for instance to form diminutives or augmentatives.
