Cultural change

Cultural change is the modification of a society through innovation, invention, discovery, or contact with other societies. Cultures are internally affected by both forces encouraging change and forces resisting it, and externally by interaction — whether trade, migration, colonization, or communication — between societies. The study of cultural change is a core concern of anthropology and sociology.

Mechanisms

Classical accounts identify several mechanisms. Invention creates new technology or practices; discovery brings recognition of existing facts or possibilities; and diffusion spreads traits between groups — selected, reinterpreted, and adapted in the process, as when crops, writing systems, or religions spread along trade routes. Acculturation describes change arising from sustained first-hand contact between cultures, while assimilation is its extreme form in which a group loses its distinct identity. Imposed change through conquest or colonialism, and internal change driven by demographic shifts, environmental pressures, or charismatic movements (revitalization movements, in Anthony Wallace's term), also reshape cultures. Modernization theory, world-systems analysis, and theories of globalization attempt large-scale explanations of change, while evolutionary approaches model variation, selection, and transmission of cultural traits.

Rates and resistance

Change proceeds unevenly: William Ogburn's concept of cultural lag holds that material culture (technology) changes faster than the non-material culture of norms and laws, producing periods of maladjustment. Ethnocentrism, vested interests, and the perceived threat of new practices generate resistance, from sumptuary laws and technology panics to organized traditionalism. Archaeology documents cultural change over millennia through shifts in material remains, while contemporary change is accelerated by mass media and the internet, which diffuse innovations almost instantaneously.
