A pilchard is a small, oily forage fish of the herring family Clupeidae. The term is most precisely used for the adult European pilchard (Sardina pilchardus), while younger fish of the same species are generally called sardines; in practice, "pilchard" and "sardine" are marketing and regional terms applied to several species of small clupeids, including the South African pilchard (Sardinops sagax) and related species in the Pacific and southern oceans.

Description and distribution

The European pilchard grows to about 25 cm (10 in) in length. Its body is silvery with a greenish-blue back and a series of dark spots along the flank. It inhabits the northeastern Atlantic Ocean, from Iceland and the North Sea to Senegal, as well as the Mediterranean and Black Seas. It forms large schools, typically at depths of 25 to 100 metres, feeding on planktonic crustaceans and fish larvae. Spawning occurs in open water, with females releasing tens of thousands of eggs.

Fisheries and use

Pilchards have supported important fisheries for centuries, historically off Cornwall (where the towns of the pilchard fishery watched for shoals from cliff-top lookouts called huers), Brittany, Portugal, and Spain, and off South Africa and Namibia in the Benguela Current system. The fish are sold fresh, frozen, smoked, or canned in oil or tomato sauce; sardines canned in olive oil are a classic product of Portugal and France. Pilchard fisheries have periodically collapsed — notably the South African fishery in the 1960s — leading to strict quota management.

Stargazy pie, a traditional Cornish dish of baked pilchards arranged with their heads poking through the pastry crust, commemorates the legendary catch of Tom Bawcock of Mousehole.
