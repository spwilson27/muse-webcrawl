The Lexington-class aircraft carriers were a pair of aircraft carriers built for the United States Navy during the 1920s: Lexington (CV-2) and Saratoga (CV-3). Begun as battlecruisers, they were converted to carriers while under construction to comply with the Washington Naval Treaty of 1922, which limited warship tonnage. For more than a decade they were the largest aircraft carriers in the world, and they pioneered American fast-carrier doctrine in the pre-war fleet problem exercises.

Design

The ships retained the powerful turbo-electric propulsion plant of the battlecruiser design, producing 180,000 shaft horsepower and a speed of over 33 knots—fast enough to operate with cruiser forces. Their flight decks measured 866 feet, and a large hangar deck below could accommodate around 78 aircraft. As built they carried a battery of eight 8-inch guns in twin turrets fore and aft of the island, intended to fight off enemy cruisers; these were removed or replaced during World War II as antiaircraft armament was strengthened.

Service history

Both ships participated in the annual Fleet Problem manoeuvres between the wars, demonstrating the potential of carrier air power for scouting and surprise attacks, including simulated raids on the Panama Canal and Pearl Harbor. Lexington famously supplied emergency electrical power to the city of Tacoma, Washington, during a drought in the winter of 1929–30.

In World War II, Lexington fought in early Pacific raids and was sunk by Japanese aircraft at the Battle of the Coral Sea in May 1942, the first American fleet carrier lost in the war. Saratoga survived torpedo damage twice, supported the Guadalcanal campaign and later operations, and ended the war as a training carrier. She was expended as a target ship in the Operation Crossroads atomic bomb tests at Bikini Atoll in July 1946.
