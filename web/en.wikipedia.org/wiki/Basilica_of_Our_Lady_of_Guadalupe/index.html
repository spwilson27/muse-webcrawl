The Basilica of Our Lady of Guadalupe (Spanish: Basílica de Nuestra Señora de Guadalupe) is a Roman Catholic church and National Shrine of Mexico located at the foot of Tepeyac Hill in northern Mexico City. It houses the cloak (tilma) of Saint Juan Diego bearing the image of Our Lady of Guadalupe, said to have appeared to him in December 1531. The shrine is among the most visited Catholic pilgrimage sites in the world, receiving millions of pilgrims each year — especially around the feast day of 12 December.

History

A chapel was built at the site soon after the reported apparitions to Juan Diego in 1531. Successive churches followed: the Old Basilica, completed in 1709 in Mexican Baroque style, served for nearly three centuries but suffered structural damage from the soft lakebed soils of Mexico City and was closed for safety. The current basilica, consecrated on 12 October 1976, was designed by Pedro Ramírez Vázquez and collaborators; its vast circular, tent-like roof shelters up to 10,000 people inside, and a moving walkway carries pilgrims beneath the displayed tilma image. The Old Basilica reopened after restoration and functions as a parish church.

The complex

The sanctuary grounds include the Chapel of the Little Well (Capilla del Pocito), the Chapel on the Hill where the apparitions are said to have concluded, the baptistery, a museum, and the great plaza (Atrio de las Américas) that accommodates tens of thousands of pilgrims. Guadalupe is a potent symbol of Mexican national and religious identity; the Virgin's image was carried as a banner by Miguel Hidalgo at the start of the War of Independence in 1810.

Pilgrimage

Estimates of annual visitors commonly range from several million to around 20 million, making the basilica one of the most-visited sacred sites in the world. Many pilgrims approach the shrine on foot from across Mexico, some completing the final stretch on their knees.
