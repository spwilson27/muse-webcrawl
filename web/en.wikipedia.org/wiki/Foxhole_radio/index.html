Foxhole radio

A foxhole radio is an improvised crystal radio receiver built by soldiers during World War II from scavenged parts. Unlike a standard crystal set, it replaced the galena crystal and cat's whisker detector with a blued razor blade and a pencil lead or safety pin as the contact, materials available at the front. The name refers to the foxholes in which soldiers sheltered.

Construction

A typical foxhole radio used a coil of wire (sometimes wound around a toilet-paper tube or other form), an antenna and ground connection, and earphones salvaged from other equipment. The slightly oxidised surface of a blued razor blade acts as a semiconductor, and touching it with a graphite pencil lead creates a crude point-contact diode that rectifies the radio signal. Moving the contact point across the blade's surface found sensitive spots, and moving a tap along the coil tuned the station. The receiver required no power source, drawing all its energy from the radio waves themselves—an advantage in the field, and a safety benefit too, since it generated no signals that could betray a position.

History

Foxhole radios were built by Allied troops in the European and Pacific theatres, and similar improvised receivers were famously constructed by prisoners of war, including in camps where radios were forbidden; the razor-blade design was publicised in American newspapers and magazines during the war and passed into hobbyist lore afterwards.
