Pudding

Pudding is a type of food whose meaning differs between Britain and North America. In the United Kingdom and Commonwealth countries, "pudding" is a general term for a dessert course and also names a family of specific dishes, both sweet and savoury, that are typically boiled, steamed or baked in a dish. In the United States, "pudding" usually denotes a soft, sweet, milk-based dessert similar to a custard or mousse, eaten with a spoon.

British puddings

The oldest British puddings were savoury: mixtures of meat, fat, grains and seasoning stuffed into animal stomachs or intestines and boiled — ancestors of haggis, black pudding (a blood sausage) and white pudding. With the development of pudding cloths and moulds, and later of suet pastry, cooks produced sweet steamed puddings such as Christmas pudding, jam roly-poly, spotted dick and treacle pudding, often served with custard. Baked batter puddings include Yorkshire pudding, served with roast beef despite the name. Rice pudding, bread-and-butter pudding and summer pudding are further classics, and the word survives in the generic British use of "pudding" (or "pud") for any dessert.

North American puddings

In the United States, pudding is a sweet milk-based dessert thickened with cornstarch, eggs or gelatin, flavoured with chocolate, vanilla, butterscotch or banana and sold in cups or made from instant mixes (Jell-O brand being the best known). It derives partly from British custards and partly from early American dishes such as Indian pudding, a baked cornmeal-and-molasses dessert of colonial New England.

Etymology

The word is thought to come through Old French boudin, originally meaning a sausage, from a root related to the Latin botellus — reflecting the dish's origins in encased, boiled mixtures.
