Michelson interferometer

Optical layout of the Michelson interferometer

The Michelson interferometer is a common configuration for optical interferometry and was invented by the American physicist Albert Abraham Michelson. Using a beam splitter, a light source is split into two arms. Each of those light beams is reflected back toward the beam splitter which then combines their amplitudes using the superposition principle. The resulting interference pattern that is not directed back toward the source is typically directed to some type of photoelectric detector or camera. For different applications of the interferometer, the two light paths can differ in length or incorporate optical elements or even materials placed in the path.

Principle of operation

Light from a source strikes a partially silvered mirror (the beam splitter) oriented at 45 degrees, which divides the beam into two equal parts. One part is transmitted to a fixed mirror, the other reflected to a movable mirror. Both beams reflect back and recombine at the beam splitter, producing alternating bright and dark interference fringes. When the path difference between the two arms changes by half a wavelength, the fringe pattern shifts by one fringe, which allows extremely precise measurements of distance and of the wavelength of light.

History

Michelson first described the instrument in 1881 while working in Berlin. Its most famous application was the Michelson–Morley experiment of 1887, performed with Edward Morley, which failed to detect the motion of the Earth through the supposed luminiferous aether. The null result became one of the key pieces of evidence leading to Einstein's theory of special relativity. Michelson also used the interferometer to measure the exact length of the standard metre in terms of the wavelength of cadmium light.

Applications

Michelson interferometers are used in Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), astronomical interferometry, optical coherence tomography, and gravitational-wave detectors. The LIGO observatories use large-scale Michelson interferometers with Fabry–Pérot arm cavities to detect ripples in spacetime; the first direct detection of gravitational waves was announced in 2016. Twyman–Green and Mach–Zehnder interferometers are related configurations derived from the same basic design.
