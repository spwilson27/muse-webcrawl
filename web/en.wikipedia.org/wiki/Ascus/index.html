Ascus

Asci of a morel, each containing eight ascospores

An ascus (plural asci; from Greek ἀσκός, "sac" or "wineskin") is the sexual spore-bearing cell produced in ascomycete fungi. Each ascus usually contains eight ascospores, produced by meiosis followed by, in most species, a mitotic division. In many species the ascus forcibly ejects its spores in a puff, sometimes visible as a fine mist above fruiting bodies such as morels and truffles.

Structure and classification

Asci are classified by the structure of their wall and the way they open (dehisce): unitunicate asci have a single wall layer, opening by a lid (operculum) or a pore; bitunicate asci have a double wall, the inner wall stretching out at maturity; prototunicate asci simply dissolve to release their spores. Asci typically develop within a fruiting body, the ascocarp, whose types — apothecium, perithecium, cleistothecium and pseudothecium — are used in ascomycete taxonomy.

Function

The ascus is the defining structure of the phylum Ascomycota, the largest phylum of fungi, which includes yeasts, morels, truffles and many moulds. Within the ascus, two haploid nuclei fuse (karyogamy) and immediately undergo meiosis, so the ascus is the only diploid cell in the life cycle of most ascomycetes.
