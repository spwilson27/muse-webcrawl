Pope Clement V

Born: Bertrand de Got, c. 1264, Gascony

Papacy: 1305 – 20 April 1314

Predecessor: Benedict XI

Successor: John XXII

Pope Clement V (Latin: Clemens V; born Bertrand de Got, c. 1264 – 20 April 1314) was head of the Catholic Church from 1305 until his death. He is remembered for moving the papal court to Avignon — beginning the period known as the Avignon Papacy — and for suppressing the Knights Templar under pressure from King Philip IV of France.

Election and move to Avignon

Bertrand de Got, Archbishop of Bordeaux, was elected pope in 1305 after an eleven-month deadlock in the conclave at Perugia; he was not a cardinal, and his election was widely seen as favourable to the French crown. Crowned at Lyon, he never went to Rome, travelling in southern France before settling in 1309 at Avignon, then a papal fief near the French border. The papacy would remain there until 1377.

Suppression of the Templars

Intending to recover the Templars' wealth and destroy a powerful order he envied, Philip IV had the Templars in France arrested on Friday 13 October 1307 on charges of heresy and blasphemy, extracting confessions by torture. After initial resistance, Clement acquiesced; in 1312 he issued the bull Vox in excelso, dissolving the order, and transferred most of its property to the Hospitallers. The Templar Grand Master, Jacques de Molay, was burned at the stake in Paris in 1314 — legend holds that he cursed both king and pope from the flames, and both men were dead within the year.

Other acts

Clement convened the Council of Vienne (1311–12), founded universities at Orléans and Perugia, and compiled the Clementine constitutions of canon law. His pontificate was marked by nepotism and fiscal expansion, and Dante, in the Inferno, condemned him as a simoniac shepherd.
