Gian-Carlo Rota

Born: April 27, 1932, Vigevano, Italy

Died: April 18, 1999 (aged 66), Cambridge, Massachusetts, US

Alma mater: Princeton University, Yale University

Known for: Combinatorial theory, umbral calculus

Institutions: Massachusetts Institute of Technology

Gian-Carlo Rota (April 27, 1932 – April 18, 1999) was an Italian-American mathematician and philosopher. He spent most of his career at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, where he was a professor of mathematics and, later, professor of applied mathematics and philosophy. Rota is widely credited with transforming combinatorics from a loose collection of techniques into a unified and rigorous discipline, earning him the description of the "father of modern combinatorics."

Early life and education

Rota was born in Vigevano, Italy, into an academic family. His father, Giovanni Rota, was an architect and anti-fascist, and his uncle was the physicist Gleb Wataghin. The family left Italy when Gian-Carlo was thirteen, moving first to Ecuador and eventually to the United States. He attended Princeton University, graduating in 1953, and received his Ph.D. from Yale University in 1956 under Jacob T. Schwartz, with a thesis in functional analysis.

Career

After a postdoctoral fellowship, Rota joined the MIT faculty in 1959. He moved briefly to Rockefeller University in 1965 before returning to MIT in 1967, where he remained for the rest of his life. From 1990 he held the chair of Professor of Applied Mathematics and Philosophy. Rota was also a consultant for Los Alamos National Laboratory for many years.

Work

Rota's early work was in functional analysis, but in the early 1960s he turned to combinatorics. His 1964 paper "On the Foundations of Combinatorial Theory I: Theory of Möbius Functions" applied the Möbius inversion formula of number theory to partially ordered sets, unifying a broad range of enumerative results. The paper effectively founded modern enumerative combinatorics and earned the Leroy P. Steele Prize in 1988. Rota also revived and rigorized the nineteenth-century umbral calculus, developed the theory of binomial type polynomial sequences, and worked on invariant theory and probability.

As a philosopher, Rota wrote extensively on phenomenology, particularly the work of Edmund Husserl. His essay collection Indiscrete Thoughts (1997) mixes mathematical essays with philosophy, memoir, and aphorisms, and remained widely read after his death.

Death and legacy

Rota died in his sleep in Cambridge, Massachusetts, on April 18, 1999, shortly before his 67th birthday. His lectures were famously polished performances; his course notes on probability were published posthumously. The Gian-Carlo Rota Prize in algebra, combinatorics, and applications is awarded in his memory.
