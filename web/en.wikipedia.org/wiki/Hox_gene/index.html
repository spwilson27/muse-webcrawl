Hox gene

Hox genes
Hox clusters of fly and mouse show conserved collinearity with the body axis
TypeHomeobox transcription factor genes
FunctionPattern the anterior–posterior body axis in animal embryos
Human clustersHOXA, HOXB, HOXC, HOXD — 39 genes on chromosomes 7, 17, 12, 2

Hox genes are a group of related genes that serve as master control genes that regulate the body plan of an embryo along the head-tail axis. After the embryonic segments have formed, the Hox proteins determine the type of segment structures (e.g. legs, antennae, and wings in fruit flies or the different types of vertebrae in humans) that will form on a given segment. Hox proteins thus confer segmental identity, but do not themselves form the specific structures of the segments.

Discovery

The genes were discovered through homeotic mutants of the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster — flies in which one body part is transformed into another, such as the Antennapedia mutant with legs growing where antennae should be and the bithorax mutant with a duplicated thorax and four wings. Edward B. Lewis's studies of the bithorax complex (1978, Nobel Prize 1995) showed the genes are arranged on the chromosome in the same order as the body segments they control — the principle of collinearity. In 1983–84 the shared 180-base-pair homeobox sequence was identified, revealing Hox genes to be a deeply conserved family across animals.

Organisation and function

In vertebrates, whole-cluster duplications produced four Hox clusters (in mammals 39 genes in 13 paralogy groups), expressed in nested domains along the embryo: genes at the 3′ end of a cluster act in the head and are turned on earliest, genes at the 5′ end in the tail and latest ("posterior prevalence"). Hox proteins are transcription factors whose DNA-binding homeodomain recognises target genes, with specificity sharpened by cofactors such as Pbx and Meis.

Evolution and medicine

The near-universal Hox system is a cornerstone of evolutionary developmental biology ("evo-devo"), showing that animal body plans are variations on an ancient genetic toolkit. In humans, mutations cause malformations such as synpolydactyly (HOXD13) and some forms of hand-foot-genital syndrome (HOXA13), and misexpressed Hox genes feature in several leukaemias.
