The Syriac language (Classical Syriac: ܠܫܢܐ ܣܘܪܝܝܐ) is a dialect of Aramaic that emerged in the first century AD from the local Aramaic spoken in Edessa (modern Şanlıurfa) in Upper Mesopotamia. It became the major literary language of the Middle East from the 4th to the 8th centuries, the classical language of Edessa, and the liturgical language of Syriac Christianity throughout the Fertile Crescent, the Arabian Peninsula, parts of India and even China.

History

Syriac literature began with the translation of the Bible — the Peshitta — and the works of writers such as Ephrem the Syrian (4th century). After the Christological controversies of the 5th century, the Syriac tradition split into western (Jacobite) and eastern (Nestorian) branches with distinct script styles (Serto and Madnhaya, alongside the older Estrangela). Syriac scholars preserved Greek philosophy and science, translating Aristotle, Galen and others, and later transmitted them into Arabic in Abbasid Baghdad, whence much reached medieval Europe. Use of Syriac as a spoken and written vernacular declined as Arabic spread after the 7th century.

Modern situation

Neo-Aramaic varieties descended from Syriac's dialect continuum — such as Suret (Assyrian Neo-Aramaic) and Turoyo — survive among Assyrian communities in northern Iraq, southeastern Turkey, Syria, and a worldwide diaspora, though many are endangered. Classical Syriac remains the liturgical language of the Syriac Orthodox Church, the Church of the East, the Maronite Church and others.
