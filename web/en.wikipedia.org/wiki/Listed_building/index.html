In the United Kingdom, a listed building is a structure of special architectural or historic interest that has been placed on one of the four statutory lists maintained by the heritage bodies of England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. Listing protects the building — inside and out — and alterations, extensions or demolition require listed building consent from the local planning authority.

Scotland and Northern Ireland

Scotland uses Categories A, B and C, administered by Historic Environment Scotland. Northern Ireland uses Grades A, B+ and B, administered by the Department for Communities.

History

Statutory listing began during the Second World War, when lists were drawn up partly to record buildings damaged by bombing. The Town and Country Planning Acts of 1944 and 1947 formalised the system; the rescue of the Euston Arch campaign (1962, unsuccessful) and increasing demolition losses led to stronger protections in 1968, when consent requirements were tightened.

Criteria

Buildings are selected for architectural interest, historic interest, close association with significant people or events, and group value. In general, all buildings built before 1700 that survive in anything like their original condition are listed, as are most built between 1700 and 1840; post-1945 buildings must be exceptionally important, and a building normally must be over 30 years old to be eligible.
