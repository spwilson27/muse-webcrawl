Character theory

In mathematics, more specifically in group theory, the character of a group representation is a function that associates to each group element the trace of the corresponding matrix. Character theory studies these functions, which capture essential information about a representation in a compact and computable form, and it is a central tool in the classification of finite groups.

The character table of a finite group summarises all its irreducible representations.

Definitions

If V is a finite-dimensional vector space over a field F and ρ : G → GL(V) is a representation of a group G, the character χρ is defined by χρ(g) = Tr(ρ(g)). Characters are class functions — constant on conjugacy classes — and they determine their representations up to isomorphism (over the complex numbers, for finite groups). A character of a one-dimensional representation is a group homomorphism into the multiplicative group of the field; the trivial character sends every element to 1.

Orthogonality and character tables

The irreducible characters of a finite group form an orthonormal set with respect to a natural inner product, and the number of irreducible representations equals the number of conjugacy classes. These facts are summarised in the group's character table, whose rows and columns satisfy row and column orthogonality relations. Character degrees divide the group order, a constraint used decisively in Burnside's paqb theorem and the Feit–Thompson theorem.

History

The theory was created by Ferdinand Georg Frobenius in 1896, initially for finite groups using determinants, and developed by Issai Schur and William Burnside. Generalisations include characters of Lie groups and the character theory underlying the Langlands program and monstrous moonshine.
