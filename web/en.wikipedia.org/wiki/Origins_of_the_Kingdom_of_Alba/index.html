Origins of the Kingdom of Alba

Pictland and Dál Riata, the two kingdoms from which Alba emerged.

The origins of the Kingdom of Alba concern the formation, in the ninth and tenth centuries, of the kingdom that would become Scotland. Alba arose from the union — by conquest, dynastic accident or gradual assimilation — of the Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata in the west and the Pictish kingdoms of the east and north.

Background

The Picts occupied most of what is now Scotland north of the Forth, while the Scots of Dál Riata ruled Argyll and the western isles, with close ties to Ireland. Viking raids from the late eighth century weakened both peoples, and the Pictish kingdom suffered a catastrophic defeat in 839 in which King Eóganan and much of his aristocracy were killed.

The MacAlpin tradition

Later tradition credited Kenneth MacAlpin (Cináed mac Ailpín), king of Dál Riata, with conquering the Picts around 843 and founding a united kingdom; medieval king-lists presented him as the first king of Scots. Modern historians debate how sudden and violent the union was: some see a Gaelic takeover, others a gradual Gaelicisation of the Pictish elite under dynasties of mixed descent. By the reign of Donald II (889–900), the kings of the territory were styled rí Alban, "king of Alba", and Pictish language and identity faded from the record over the following century.
