The first transcontinental railroad in North America (originally known as the "Pacific Railroad" and later as the "Overland Route") was a 1,912-mile (3,077 km) continuous railroad line built between 1863 and 1869 that connected the existing eastern U.S. rail network at Council Bluffs, Iowa, with the Pacific coast at Oakland, California. Authorised by the Pacific Railway Acts of 1862 and 1864 and supported by federal bonds and land grants, it was constructed by the Central Pacific Railroad building eastward from Sacramento and the Union Pacific Railroad building westward from Omaha.

Construction

The Central Pacific, backed by Sacramento's "Big Four" (Leland Stanford, Collis P. Huntington, Mark Hopkins, and Charles Crocker), employed more than 10,000 Chinese immigrant workers at its peak to blast tunnels through the granite of the Sierra Nevada, including the 1,659-foot Summit Tunnel at Donner Pass, in a famously brutal winter campaign. The Union Pacific laid track rapidly across the Great Plains using Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans, clashing with Native American nations — including the Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho — whose lands the line crossed and whose resistance, and the railroad's destruction of the bison herds, accelerated the conquest of the Plains. The two companies, paid by the mile, raced past each other in parallel grades before Congress fixed the meeting point in Utah.

Completion and impact

The lines met at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, on 10 May 1869, where a ceremonial golden spike (the "Last Spike") was driven by Leland Stanford. The completion cut the coast-to-coast journey from months by wagon or sea to about a week, unified the continental United States economically and politically, and triggered a boom of further transcontinental lines. It also brought financial scandal (the Crédit Mobilier affair), dispossession of Native peoples, and exploitation of immigrant labour. Much of the original line remains in service; the Promontory route was bypassed by the Lucin Cutoff in 1904, and the site is preserved as Golden Spike National Historical Park.
