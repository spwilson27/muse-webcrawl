A monarch is a head of state for life or until abdication, and therefore the head of state of a monarchy. A monarch may exercise the highest authority and power in the state, or others may wield that power on behalf of the monarch. Usually a monarch either personally inherits the lawful right to exercise the state's sovereign rights (often referred to as the throne or the crown) or is selected by an established process from a family or cohort eligible to provide the nation's monarch.

Characteristics

Monarchs, as such, bear a variety of titles — king or queen, prince or princess, emperor or empress, duke or duchess. In many monarchies, especially constitutional ones, the monarch is the guardian of the state but does not personally govern: executive power is exercised by a government accountable to a parliament, summed up in the phrase "the king reigns but does not rule." In absolute monarchies — a small minority today, such as Saudi Arabia and Eswatini — the monarch holds extensive governing power.

Succession

Hereditary succession is most common, governed in many European monarchies today by absolute primogeniture, in which the eldest child succeeds regardless of sex. Historically, male-preference primogeniture and Salic law (barring female succession entirely) were widespread. Elective monarchy, in which an assembly or college chooses the monarch, existed historically in the Holy Roman Empire and the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, and survives in Malaysia, Cambodia, and the papacy.

Roles

Depending on the constitutional order, a monarch may appoint governments, grant assent to legislation, command the armed forces in name, conduct diplomacy, and serve as a symbol of national continuity and unity. Monarchs are typically protected by some form of legal immunity and addressed with distinctive styles such as "Majesty."
