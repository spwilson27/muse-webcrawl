Dyirbal is an Australian Aboriginal language of the rainforest region of north-east Queensland, traditionally spoken around the upper Tully River and the Atherton Tableland. It became one of the most famous languages in linguistics through R. M. W. Dixon's 1972 grammar, which documented its unusual noun classification and its special avoidance register.

Noun classes

Dyirbal has four noun classes, marked by the words bayi, balan, balam and bala. Their contents are partly semantic—one class covers men and most animals, another women, fire and fighting, a third edible plants, and a fourth everything else—but many memberships are explained by mythology and association: birds, for example, belong to the "feminine" class because they are believed to be the spirits of dead women, while fishing equipment joins the class of the fish it catches. George Lakoff's book Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things (1987) took this system as its starting point.

Mother-in-law language

Alongside the everyday style (Guwal), Dyirbal had an avoidance style called Jalnguy, the "mother-in-law language", used in the presence of taboo relatives such as a mother-in-law. Jalnguy shared Guwal's grammar and phonology but used an entirely different vocabulary, in which one general word did the work of many everyday terms.

Status

Dyirbal is severely endangered: the last fully fluent speakers of the traditional language died in the late 20th and early 21st centuries, and younger generations largely speak a simplified "Young Dyirbal" or English. Revival efforts draw on Dixon's extensive recordings and texts.
