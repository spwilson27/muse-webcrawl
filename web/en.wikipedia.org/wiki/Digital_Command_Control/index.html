Digital Command Control

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Digital Command Control (DCC) is a standard for a system for the digital operation of model railways that permits locomotives on the same stretch of track to be independently controlled. Instead of varying the voltage on the rails — which moves every locomotive at once, as in traditional direct-current control — a DCC system sends a continuous stream of digital packets through the rails. A decoder installed in each locomotive listens for packets addressed to it and adjusts its motor, lights, and sound accordingly.

History

Digital train control systems appeared in the 1980s, including Märklin's digital system for its three-rail range and the technology developed by the German firm Lenz Elektronik. The American National Model Railroad Association (NMRA) adopted standards based on the Lenz system in the early 1990s, publishing the first DCC standards and recommended practices in 1994. Because the standard was open, many manufacturers — including Digitrax, NCE, Lenz, ESU, and SoundTraxx — produced compatible command stations, throttles, boosters, and decoders.

How it works

The command station encodes instructions as a series of binary pulses superimposed on the track power (about 12–16 V depending on scale). A "1" bit is a short pulse and a "0" bit a longer one. Decoders rectify the track signal for power and read the data stream, acting only on packets matching their programmed address. Each decoder's behaviour — acceleration, top speed, lighting effects, and other parameters — is set through numbered configuration variables (CVs), which can be programmed on a dedicated programming track or on the main line ("operations mode" programming).

Advantages and features

  • Independent control of speed and direction for many locomotives on one track, including standing locos with lights and sound on
  • Simplified layout wiring compared with block-control DC systems
  • Sound decoders that synchronise chuff, horn, and bell effects with motion
  • Consisting (multiple locomotives operating as one unit), accessory decoders for turnouts and signals, and computer control via interfaces such as JMRI
  • Two-way communication systems, such as RailCom, that let decoders report back to the command station
