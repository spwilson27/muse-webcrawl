The GNU General Public License (GNU GPL or simply GPL) is a series of widely used free software licenses, or copyleft licenses, that guarantee end users the freedoms to run, study, share, and modify the software. The license was the first copyleft license for general use; it was originally written by Richard Stallman, the founder of the Free Software Foundation (FSF), for the GNU Project.

History

The GPL was written in 1989 for use with programs released as part of the GNU project. It is based on a similar license used for early versions of GNU Emacs. The second version, GPLv2, was released in 1991 and became one of the most widely used free software licenses in history; the Linux kernel is licensed under GPLv2. GPLv3, released in 2007, was designed to address concerns including software patents, digital rights management ("tivoization"), and license compatibility.

Terms and conditions

The GPL grants recipients the rights of the free software definition: the freedom to use the work for any purpose, to study and change it, and to redistribute copies with or without changes. In return, the license imposes copyleft conditions: distributed copies and derivative works must be licensed under the same terms, and the source code must be made available. These conditions are intended to prevent the software from being incorporated into proprietary products.

Use and adoption

Software under the GPL includes the Linux kernel, much of the GNU toolset (including GCC and the GNU Core Utilities), and many other well-known projects. Related licenses include the GNU Lesser General Public License (LGPL), which permits linking with proprietary code under certain conditions, and the GNU Affero General Public License (AGPL), which closes the network-service loophole.

Criticism and reception

The GPL has been praised for preserving user freedom and creating a large commons of reusable code, and criticized by some for its "viral" nature and complexity. Its enforceability has been tested in courts in several countries, broadly upheld, and compliance is commonly negotiated through community enforcement organizations.
