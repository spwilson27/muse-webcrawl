Nobility

Nobility is a social class that traditionally possesses more acknowledged privilege or status than most other classes in a society and often has hereditary titles. Membership has usually been granted by a monarch or government, and nobility has typically been associated with landholding, political and military power, and exemptions from certain taxes and duties. Noble status survives today in many constitutional monarchies, though in most countries it now carries little or no legal privilege.

History

Noble classes arose independently in many civilisations, from the patricians of Rome and the kshatriya of India to the scholar-gentry of China and the samurai of Japan. In medieval Europe, nobility crystallised within feudalism as a warrior landholding class owing military service to a king or overlord in return for land and jurisdiction over peasants. Nobles were distinguished by coats of arms, titles, and legal privileges such as trial by peers and exemption from many taxes.

Ranks and orders

European nobility is generally graded into ranks: sovereigns and princes; dukes; marquesses (margraves); counts (earls); viscounts; and barons, with knights and untitled nobility below. Britain distinguishes the peerage (holders of the five top ranks, historically entitled to sit in the House of Lords) from the gentry. Elsewhere, systems include the Spanish grandees, the German Uradel and Briefadel, the Russian Table of Ranks, and the imperial Chinese orders of nobility.

Ennoblement and decline

New nobles could be created by letters patent, purchase of office, or distinguished service, allowing wealthy commoners to enter the class — a path of social mobility that also diluted the prestige of birth. From the 18th century, revolutions and democratisation stripped nobility of legal privilege: the French Revolution abolished noble privileges in 1789, and many 20th-century republics (Germany, Austria, Russia, China) abolished noble status outright. Where hereditary peerages survive, as in the United Kingdom, Spain and Japan (until 1947), their political role has largely ended.

Nobility today

Titles continue to be granted in several monarchies and are regulated by heraldic authorities such as the College of Arms in London. Noble families retain social prestige, and genealogical and heraldic societies maintain records of descent. Chivalric orders, once the preserve of the nobility, are now generally awarded for merit regardless of birth.
