Culmination


A celestial object at culmination as it crosses the observer's meridian

In observational astronomy, culmination is the passage of a celestial object (such as the Sun, the Moon, a planet, a star, a constellation, or a deep-sky object) across the observer's local meridian. These events are also known as meridian transits, used in timekeeping and navigation. During a day, a celestial object crosses the meridian twice, giving an upper culmination — the highest point reached in the sky — and a lower culmination — the lowest point, roughly 12 hours later.

Upper and lower culmination

For an object at declination δ observed from latitude φ, the altitude at upper culmination is 90° − φ + δ (when culminating south of the zenith in the northern hemisphere). Circumpolar objects never set and have both culminations above the horizon; objects that never rise have both below it. The Sun's upper culmination defines local apparent noon, the basis of the historical definition of time of day before standard time zones.

Observing significance

Astronomers generally prefer to observe objects near upper culmination, where the object is highest, its light passes through the least amount of atmosphere, and atmospheric extinction and refraction are minimised. The moment of culmination of a star also gives its right ascension relative to local sidereal time, a principle used by transit telescopes and meridian circles to compile star catalogues.

Everyday usage

Outside astronomy, "culmination" denotes the highest point or climax of something, attained after a long development — a usage derived from the Latin culmen, meaning summit or top.
