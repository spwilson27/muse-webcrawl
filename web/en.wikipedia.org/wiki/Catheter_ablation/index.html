Catheter ablation

Catheter ablation is a procedure that uses radio-frequency energy or other types of energy to treat some types of arrhythmia. It is a procedure used to remove a faulty electrical pathway from the hearts of those suffering from cardiac arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, supraventricular tachycardias (SVT) and Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome.

Procedure

Catheter ablation is usually performed by an electrophysiologist in a catheterisation laboratory. Thin, flexible catheters are advanced through blood vessels, usually from the groin, into the heart, where mapping electrodes identify the tissue responsible for the abnormal rhythm. Energy — most commonly radiofrequency energy, or cold in cryoablation — is then delivered through the catheter tip to destroy (ablate) a small area of tissue, blocking the faulty electrical pathway.

Uses

Ablation can potentially cure many types of tachycardia. For typical atrial flutter and many supraventricular tachycardias, success rates exceed 90 per cent. Pulmonary vein isolation by ablation is a common treatment for symptomatic atrial fibrillation when medication is ineffective or not tolerated.

Risks

Risks include bleeding or infection at the insertion site, damage to blood vessels or heart valves, blood clots, stroke, and, rarely, damage to the heart's electrical system requiring a pacemaker. Overall major complication rates are low, on the order of a few per cent.
