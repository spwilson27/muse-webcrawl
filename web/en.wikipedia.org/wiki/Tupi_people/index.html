The Tupi people were one of the main ethnic groups of Indigenous peoples of Brazil, inhabiting much of the Brazilian coast when the Portuguese first arrived in 1500. Speakers of Tupian languages, the Tupi were among the first Amerindians to come into contact with European settlers, and their languages and culture left a deep mark on Brazilian society.

History

The Tupi are believed to have originated in the Amazon basin and to have migrated southward and eastward over many centuries, reaching the Atlantic coast roughly a thousand years before European contact. Along the coast they formed numerous semi-sedentary villages, practising slash-and-burn agriculture centred on manioc, maize, peanuts, tobacco and cotton. Major Tupi-speaking groups included the Tupinambá, Tupiniquim, Potiguara, Temiminó, Caeté and Tabajara.

When Pedro Álvares Cabral landed in 1500, Tupi peoples occupied most of the coast. Relations with the Portuguese were initially based on trade in brazilwood, but warfare, enslavement and epidemics of European diseases devastated Tupi populations during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Jesuit missionaries such as José de Anchieta and Manuel da Nóbrega gathered many Tupi into mission villages (aldeias) and documented their language, producing the first grammar of Old Tupi in 1595.

Language

The Tupi language, sometimes called Old Tupi or Tupinambá, served as a lingua franca throughout colonial Brazil. A simplified form, Nheengatu (the "língua geral"), was spoken widely in the Amazon into the nineteenth century and remains an official language in the municipality of São Gabriel da Cachoeira. Hundreds of Brazilian Portuguese words, place names and fauna and flora terms derive from Tupi, including capoeira, jaguar, piranha and tapioca.

Culture

Tupi society was organised in villages of large communal longhouses (malocas) housing extended families. Warfare between villages was common, and captives could be ritually executed and eaten in ceremonies described by European chroniclers such as Hans Staden and Jean de Léry — accounts that strongly shaped European views of the New World. Tupi cosmology featured spirits of nature, shamans (pajés), and creator figures such as Tupã, later associated with the Christian God.

Legacy

Although the coastal Tupi societies were largely destroyed in the colonial era, their genetic, linguistic and cultural legacy persists throughout Brazil. Related peoples, notably the Guarani, survive as distinct Indigenous nations today, and the Tupi contribution to Brazilian identity is celebrated in literature such as José de Alencar's novel Iracema.
