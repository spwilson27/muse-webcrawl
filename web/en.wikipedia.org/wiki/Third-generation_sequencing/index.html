Third-generation sequencing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nanopore sequencer reads native DNA molecules directly

Third-generation sequencing (also known as long-read sequencing) refers to DNA sequencing technologies that read single molecules of DNA or RNA directly, without the amplification and short-fragment synthesis used by second-generation platforms. The principal commercial technologies are Pacific Biosciences' single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing and Oxford Nanopore Technologies' nanopore sequencing.

Advantages

Long reads — routinely tens of thousands of bases and, for nanopore reads, sometimes exceeding a million — can span repetitive regions that defeat short-read assembly, resolve structural variants, phase haplotypes, and close gaps in reference genomes. Because native molecules are read, base modifications such as methylation can be detected directly from the signal. Nanopore devices also sequence RNA directly and can stream data in real time, enabling field applications such as outbreak surveillance.

Limitations

Historically, single-molecule platforms had substantially higher raw error rates than short-read sequencing; circular consensus ("HiFi") reads on the PacBio platform and improved nanopore chemistries and base-calling have narrowed this gap considerably. Long-read methods also tend to require higher-quality, higher-molecular-weight input DNA and can cost more per base.
