Jupiter (Latin: Iūpiter or Jove) is the god of the sky and thunder, and king of the gods in ancient Roman religion and mythology. Jupiter was the chief deity of Roman state religion throughout the Republican and Imperial eras, until Christianity became the dominant religion of the Empire. In Roman mythology, he negotiates with Numa Pompilius, the second king of Rome, to establish principles of Roman religion such as offering, or sacrifice.

Jupiter is thought to have originated as a sky god. His identifying implements are the thunderbolt and his primary sacred animal is the eagle, which held precedence over other birds in the taking of auspices and became one of the most common symbols of the Roman army. The two were often presented together, as in the god holding the lightning bolt in one hand and an eagle-topped sceptre in the other. As the sky god, he was a divine witness to oaths, the sacred trust on which justice and good government depend.

Roles and epithets

Jupiter was worshipped under many epithets expressing his functions, including Jupiter Optimus Maximus ("the Best and Greatest"), Jupiter Tonans ("the Thunderer"), Jupiter Fulgur (lightning), and Jupiter Pluvius (sender of rain). As Jupiter Capitolinus he presided over the Capitoline Triad with Juno and Minerva, and his great temple on the Capitoline Hill stood at the centre of Roman religious life from the late Roman Kingdom onward.

Cult and festivals

Jupiter's most important festivals included the Ludi Romani (Roman Games) in September and the Feriae Latinae. The flamen Dialis, one of the major priests of Rome, was dedicated to his cult and was surrounded by distinctive taboos. Triumphant generals dressed as Jupiter and processed to his Capitoline temple, dedicating their spoils to the god.

Equivalents

The Romans identified Jupiter with the Greek Zeus, and much of Zeus's mythology was adapted to him, including his parentage as son of Saturn (Cronus) and his struggles against the Titans and giants. He also corresponds to the Germanic god Thor/Tyr in some interpretive traditions (whence Thursday, "Jove's day" associations varying by culture), and to the Indo-European sky father *Dyēus.
