The Osmanya alphabet (Somali: Farta Cismaaniya; Osmanya: 𐒍𐒖𐒇𐒂𐒖 𐒋𐒘𐒈𐒑𐒛𐒒𐒘𐒕𐒖), also known as Far Soomaali ("Somali writing"), is a writing script created to transcribe the Somali language. It was invented between 1920 and 1922 by Osman Yusuf Kenadid, the son of Sultan Yusuf Ali Kenadid of the Sultanate of Hobyo.

History

While Osmanya gained widespread acceptance in Somalia and was rapidly used to produce a large body of literature, it proved difficult to spread among the general population, due in large part to competition from the long-established Arabic script (including the adapted Wadaad writing) and the emerging Somali Latin alphabet developed by scholars such as Shire Jama Ahmed. Kenadid, a poet and member of the royal family of Hobyo, devised the script partly in response to nationalist debates over how Somali should be written, being inspired by reports of script reforms in other countries. The letters of Osmanya were largely newly invented, though some forms were influenced by Arabic and other scripts.

Use and decline

The script was used in correspondence, official documents in the sultanates of the northeast, publications and school materials during its inventor's lifetime. After the military government of Siad Barre officially adopted the Latin alphabet for Somali in 1972, Osmanya fell out of general use, though it retains cultural and historical significance, is still taught and used by some enthusiasts, and was encoded in Unicode in 2003. Another indigenous Somali script of the same period is the Borama (Gadabuursi) script.

Description

Osmanya is written left to right, has distinct letters for consonants and vowels, and includes numerals. It represents the phonemes of Somali, including the long vowels and the distinctive pharyngeal and glottal consonants, with separate letters for sounds such as /ħ/ and /ʕ/ transliterated x and c in the Latin orthography.
