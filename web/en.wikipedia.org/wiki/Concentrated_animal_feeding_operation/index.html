Concentrated animal feeding operation

A concentrated animal feeding operation (CAFO), as defined by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), is an intensive animal feeding operation (AFO) in which over 1,000 animal units (or a smaller number if the facility discharges pollutants into waters of the United States) are confined for over 45 days a year. CAFOs are the largest category of industrial livestock production facilities and are associated with the system often called factory farming.

Definition

Under the Clean Water Act, an animal feeding operation becomes a CAFO based on the number of animals confined, the duration of confinement, and whether pollutants are discharged into surface waters. Large CAFOs include operations with 700 or more mature dairy cattle, 1,000 or more beef cattle, 2,500 or more swine above 55 pounds, 10,000 or more swine under 55 pounds, or 125,000 or more chickens (other than laying hens) using non-liquid manure handling systems.

Environmental concerns

CAFOs generate enormous quantities of animal manure, often stored in open lagoons and spread on nearby fields. Critics cite water pollution from nutrient runoff, ammonia and hydrogen sulfide air emissions, greenhouse gas emissions, and the spread of pathogens and antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Defenders argue that CAFOs produce meat, milk and eggs more efficiently and cheaply than traditional farming, and that modern manure management mitigates many impacts.

Regulation

In the United States, CAFOs that discharge or propose to discharge pollutants must obtain permits under the National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES). Regulation varies considerably by state, and the scope of federal oversight has been the subject of prolonged litigation.
