The French and Indian War (1754–1763) was the North American theatre of the worldwide Seven Years' War. It pitted the colonies of British America against those of New France, each side supported by military units from the parent country and by Native American allies. The war ended with France's expulsion from Canada and the mainland east of the Mississippi.


French and British claims in North America

Origins

Conflict grew out of competition for the Ohio Country and the fur trade. In 1754 a young Virginia officer, George Washington, ambushed a French detachment at Jumonville Glen and then surrendered at Fort Necessity — skirmishes that ignited open war before either empire formally declared it (formal declarations came in 1756).

Course of the war

Early French successes included the destruction of General Braddock's expedition near Fort Duquesne (1755) and Montcalm's captures of Oswego and Fort William Henry (1756–57), the latter followed by a notorious attack on the departing garrison by France's Native allies. The tide turned after 1758, when Britain, under William Pitt's direction, poured in resources: Louisbourg fell, Jeffrey Amherst and James Wolfe took Quebec after the Battle of the Plains of Abraham (13 September 1759) — in which both Wolfe and Montcalm were killed — and Montreal surrendered in 1760.

Consequences

By the Treaty of Paris (1763) France ceded Canada and all mainland territory east of the Mississippi (except New Orleans) to Britain, and Louisiana west of the river to Spain. Britain's attempt to pay its war debts by taxing the American colonies, together with disputes over westward settlement addressed in the Royal Proclamation of 1763, set the stage for the American Revolution. Many colonists, Washington among them, had gained their military experience in this war.
