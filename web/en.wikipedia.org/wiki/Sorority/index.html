Sorority

A sorority is an organization for women, most commonly a social society at a college or university in North America. Sororities are the counterpart of fraternities for men; together they form the "Greek life" system, as most organizations are identified by Greek letters. The word derives from the Latin soror, meaning "sister".

History

The first women's Greek-letter society, Kappa Alpha Theta, was founded in 1870 at DePauw University. The term "sorority" itself was coined by Syracuse University professor Frank Smalley for Gamma Phi Beta, founded in 1874. Early organizations were often called "women's fraternities" because no suitable word existed. The National Panhellenic Conference (NPC), founded in 1902, became the umbrella organization for women's fraternities and sororities in the United States and Canada.

Historically Black sororities, including Alpha Kappa Alpha (1908), Delta Sigma Theta (1913), Zeta Phi Beta (1920) and Sigma Gamma Rho (1922), are governed by the National Pan-Hellenic Council (the "Divine Nine") and place particular emphasis on community service and graduate chapters.

Membership and activities

Students typically join through a recruitment process known as "rush", which may end with a bid and a period of new-member education before initiation. Many chapters maintain a sorority house where members live. Sororities organize social events, philanthropy and community service, intramural sports and academic support, and emphasize leadership development and lifelong alumni networks.

Criticism

Sororities and fraternities have been criticized for exclusivity, cost, hazing incidents, and fostering risky drinking cultures. Universities and national organizations have responded with anti-hazing policies, risk-management rules and reforms to recruitment practices.
