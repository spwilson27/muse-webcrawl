Trehalose is a disaccharide sugar formed by two glucose units joined by an α,α-1,1-glycosidic bond. It is found widely in nature—in fungi, plants, insects, and other invertebrates—where it serves as an energy source and, notably, as a protectant against desiccation, freezing, and heat. It is sometimes called "resurrection sugar" for its role in organisms such as tardigrades and resurrection plants that survive near-total drying.

Properties

Trehalose is a non-reducing sugar, which makes it unusually stable: it resists hydrolysis by acid and does not readily participate in Maillard reactions with proteins. It has about 45% of the sweetness of sucrose. On drying, trehalose forms a glassy matrix that immobilises and protects proteins and membranes, a property known as anhydrobiosis support or vitrification.

Biological role

In insects, trehalose is the principal blood sugar, circulating in the haemolymph and fuelling flight. In bacteria, yeasts, and plants it accumulates under stress. The enzyme trehalase breaks it down to glucose. In humans, dietary trehalose is hydrolysed by intestinal trehalase; deficiency of this enzyme can cause digestive intolerance, similar in effect to lactose intolerance.

Production and uses

Trehalose was once expensive to extract from yeast, but an enzymatic process using bacterial enzymes (developed by the Japanese company Hayashibara in the 1990s) made it cheap to produce from starch. It is now used as a food ingredient (E473-related applications and as a sweetener/humectant, especially in Japan), as a stabiliser for pharmaceuticals, vaccines, and enzymes, and in cosmetics. Research has also explored links between dietary trehalose and the virulence of certain Clostridioides difficile strains.
