Abbe number

An Abbe diagram of optical glasses

The Abbe number, Vd, of a transparent material is a measure of its dispersion — the variation of refractive index with wavelength — in the visible spectrum. It is named after the German physicist Ernst Abbe (1840–1905), who developed it while working with Carl Zeiss and Otto Schott on optical glass. A high Abbe number indicates low dispersion (a "crown" glass), while a low Abbe number indicates high dispersion (a "flint" glass). The number is used to classify glasses and polymers for lenses, and to predict the chromatic aberration of a finished optical system.

Definition

The Abbe number is defined from the refractive indices of the material at three Fraunhofer spectral lines: the yellow sodium D line (589.3 nm), the blue hydrogen F line (486.1 nm), and the red hydrogen C line (656.3 nm):

Vd = (nd − 1) / (nF − nC)

The denominator (nF − nC) is the principal dispersion. An alternative definition, Ve, uses the mercury e line (546.1 nm) with the F′ and C′ cadmium lines and is standard for ophthalmic optics in some countries.

Typical values

Fluorite / fused silica~95 / ~68

Dense flint glasses25–40

Applications

Glass manufacturers plot Abbe number against refractive index on an "Abbe diagram" to map their catalogues; pairing a high-V crown element with a low-V flint element of opposite power is the standard way to build an achromatic doublet, cancelling chromatic aberration at two wavelengths. In ophthalmics, the Abbe number of a lens material governs how much colour fringing a wearer perceives off-axis: high-index materials focus sharply but have low Abbe numbers, so spectacle design trades thinness against chromatic aberration.
