Augustus (title)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Roman coin naming the emperor Augustus

Augustus (plural Augusti; Latin for "majestic", "great" or "venerable") was an ancient Roman title given as both name and title to Gaius Julius Caesar Octavianus, the first emperor of Rome, in 27 BC. It remained in use, in both the Latin-speaking and Greek-speaking East (rendered Σεβαστός, Sebastos), as the principal title of the senior Roman and Byzantine emperors for over a thousand years.

Origin

On 16 January 27 BC, the Roman Senate conferred on Octavian the honorific Augustus, a word with religious connotations of dignity and authority, previously used of sacred places and objects. The historian Cassius Dio notes that the title placed its holder somewhere between human and divine status while avoiding the politically toxic name of king (rex). Augustus also took the name Imperator Caesar, and "Augustus" became hereditary in practice as each successor adopted it.

Use in the Roman Empire

Under the Principate, Augustus was the distinctive title of the reigning emperor, while Caesar came to designate the designated heir or junior co-ruler. When Diocletian established the Tetrarchy in AD 293, the empire was ruled by two senior emperors, each styled Augustus, assisted by two junior Caesars. The feminine form, Augusta, was granted to empresses and other women of the imperial family, beginning with Livia Drusilla, who received it in Augustus's will.

Byzantine and later use

In the Byzantine Empire the Greek equivalent Sebastos remained an imperial title until it was demoted by Alexios I Komnenos in the 11th century, when new higher dignities such as sebastokrator were created above it. In the medieval West, "Augustus" survived in the titulature of the Holy Roman Emperors (as semper Augustus, "ever Augustus/exalted"), and echoes of it persisted in later European monarchic styles.

The month of August

The Roman month Sextilis was renamed August in 8 BC in honour of the title's first holder, matching July, renamed for Julius Caesar; tradition holds that August was allotted 31 days to equal July.
