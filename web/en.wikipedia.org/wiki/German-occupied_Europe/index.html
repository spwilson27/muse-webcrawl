German-occupied Europe

German-occupied Europe refers to the sovereign countries of Europe which were wholly or partly occupied and civil-occupied (including puppet governments) by the Axis powers, principally Nazi Germany, at various times between 1939 and 1945, during World War II. At its greatest extent in 1942, the German military and its occupation administrations controlled territory from the Atlantic coast of France to the approaches to Moscow and the Caucasus, and from Norway to Greece and North Africa's European shore.

Occupied countries and territories

Countries and territories under German occupation included Poland (from 1939), Denmark and Norway (from 1940), Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Belgium, and France (from 1940), Yugoslavia and Greece (from 1941), and large parts of the western Soviet Union (from 1941). Regions were governed in various forms: some were annexed outright to the Reich (e.g., western Poland, Alsace-Lorraine, Luxembourg, Austria earlier in 1938); others were administered by civilian Reichskommissariats (Norway, the Netherlands, Ostland, Ukraine), by military administrations (Belgium and northern France, Serbia), or as "General Government" in central Poland. Italy, Germany's former ally, was partly occupied after September 1943, and Hungary was occupied in March 1944 to prevent its defection.

Nature of the occupation

The occupation was characterized by racial ideology and exploitation: the systematic murder of Europe's Jews in the Holocaust (about six million victims), the deaths of millions of Soviet prisoners of war and civilians, forced labour programs that deported millions to work in the Reich, plunder of food, industry, and art, and brutal reprisals against resistance movements. Nazi plans under Generalplan Ost envisaged the decimation, expulsion, and Germanization of Slavic populations in the East. At the same time, occupation regimes varied: Denmark retained its king and government until 1943, while Poland's and the Soviet territories' administrations were exceptionally violent.

Resistance and liberation

Across occupied Europe, resistance movements — partisans in Yugoslavia, Greece, and the Soviet territories; the French Resistance; underground armies in Poland (the Home Army, which launched the Warsaw Uprising of 1944) and elsewhere — fought the occupiers, aided by the Western Allies and the USSR. Occupation ended piecemeal from 1943 (with the Red Army's advance and the Allied landings in Italy and Normandy) and was complete by Germany's surrender in May 1945. The occupations left tens of millions dead and remain central to European memory and politics.
