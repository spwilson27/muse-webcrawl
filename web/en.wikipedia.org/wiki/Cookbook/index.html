Cookbook

[Image: an open cookbook on a kitchen counter beside ingredients and a wooden spoon, a finished dish in soft focus behind]

A cookbook or cookery book is a kitchen reference containing a collection of recipes, usually organised by type of dish, ingredient, technique or cuisine. Cookbooks may be general (covering a whole cuisine), or specialised — devoted to a single ingredient, method, diet, chef or restaurant — and increasingly, cookbook content also appears in digital and video forms.

Beyond recipes, good cookbooks convey techniques, equipment guidance, ingredient knowledge and the food culture of their authors and communities.

History

The earliest surviving recipe collections include the Roman Apicius (compiled in late antiquity) and medieval works such as the English Forme of Cury (c. 1390). Printed cookbooks spread from the sixteenth century; in the nineteenth century, books such as Mrs Beeton's Book of Household Management (1861) in Britain and, following it, standardised-measures works like Fannie Farmer's Boston Cooking-School Cook Book (1896) professionalised the home recipe.

Twentieth-century landmarks include Julia Child's Mastering the Art of French Cooking (1961), which reshaped American home cooking, and national-cuisine classics and chef-driven books that turned cookbooks into a major publishing category.

Modern cookbooks

Contemporary cookbooks emphasize photography, narrative and authorial voice, and range from rigorously tested reference works (such as those of America's Test Kitchen) to single-subject deep dives and preservation of regional and diaspora cuisines. Online recipes and video have displaced some uses, but printed cookbook sales remain strong.

Community and charity cookbooks — compiled by churches, schools and clubs — form a distinct tradition, documenting everyday foodways that commercial publishing overlooked.
