Ngô Quyền (12 March 897 – 944) was a Vietnamese warlord and monarch who defeated the Chinese Southern Han fleet at the Battle of Bạch Đằng River in 938, ending roughly a thousand years of Chinese domination of Vietnam. In 939 he proclaimed himself king, moved the capital to Cổ Loa, and founded the short-lived Ngô dynasty — generally regarded as the beginning of Vietnam's enduring independence.

Battle of Bạch Đằng

Ngô Quyền was the son-in-law and general of Dương Đình Nghệ, who had seized power in Annam (Tĩnh Hải quân) from the crumbling Tang sphere. After Dương was assassinated in 937 by the usurper Kiều Công Tiễn, Ngô Quyền marched north to avenge him. Kiều appealed to the Southern Han, which sent a fleet under Liu Hongcao up the Bạch Đằng River. Ngô Quyền had iron-tipped wooden stakes planted in the riverbed, hidden at high tide; his smaller boats lured the Han fleet in on the flood tide and, as the tide ebbed, the Chinese warships were impaled on the stakes and destroyed. Liu Hongcao was killed and the invasion collapsed.

Reign and legacy

As king, Ngô Quyền organized a court and administration modelled in part on Chinese practice but independent of it. On his death in 944, his brother-in-law Dương Tam Kha usurped the throne from Ngô's sons, plunging the country into the instability that culminated in the Anarchy of the 12 Warlords, ended only by Đinh Bộ Lĩnh in 968. Ngô Quyền is nevertheless venerated in Vietnam as a national founding hero: streets, schools, and a district of Hanoi bear his name, and his Bạch Đằng stratagem was famously repeated by Trần Hưng Đạo against the Mongols in 1288.
