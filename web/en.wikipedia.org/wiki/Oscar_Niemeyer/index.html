Oscar Niemeyer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Niterói Contemporary Art Museum: a red flying saucer on a mirrored pond, the curve of the Guanabara Bay behind it]

Oscar Ribeiro de Almeida Niemeyer Soares Filho (15 December 1907 – 5 December 2012) was a Brazilian architect, considered one of the key figures in the development of modern architecture. Famous for the sensual curve — 'not the straight line, hard and inflexible, but the free and sensual curve' (in his much-quoted formulation, paraphrased here) — he designed the civic buildings of Brasília, the planned capital inaugurated in 1960, which UNESCO lists as a World Heritage Site.

Born in Rio de Janeiro, Niemeyer worked with Le Corbusier and Lúcio Costa on Rio's Ministry of Education (1936–43) and the Brazilian Pavilion in New York (1939); his Pampulha complex in Belo Horizonte (1940–43) announced the free-form concrete that he and engineer Joaquim Cardozo made sing. Exiled by Brazil's military dictatorship (he was a lifelong communist, a member of the party from 1945), he worked from Paris — the French Communist Party headquarters among his works — and returned in the 1980s.

Brasília

President Kubitschek's 'fifty years in five' built a capital in the empty cerrado to Costa's pilot plan and Niemeyer's palaces: the Cathedral's crown of concrete ribs, the National Congress's twin towers flanked by dome and bowl, the Itamaraty Palace's floating arches. Brasília is modernism's boldest total artwork and its most debated — a city designed from the air whose superquadra housing and monumental axis generations have alternately celebrated and humanised against the plan.

Longevity

Niemeyer worked past 100 (the Niterói museum, his late signature, opened 1996; the Oscar Niemeyer Museum in Curitiba, with its eye tower, 2002), married at 98, and died days before his 105th birthday. His curve — drawn from Brazil's mountains and, as he put it, the curves of its women — made concrete lyrical and gave the tropics a modernism of their own.
