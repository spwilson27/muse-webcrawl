Mircea Eliade (13 March 1907 – 22 April 1986) was a Romanian historian of religion, fiction writer, philosopher and professor at the University of Chicago. He was one of the most influential scholars of religion of the twentieth century, known for interpreting religious experience through concepts such as the sacred and the profane, hierophany (the manifestation of the sacred), and the myth of the eternal return.

Life

Eliade was born in Bucharest and studied at the University of Bucharest, where he became a leading figure of Romania's interwar intellectual generation. A fellowship took him to India (1928–1931), where he studied Sanskrit and yoga in Calcutta and lived for a time in a Himalayan ashram; his doctoral thesis became the book Yoga: Immortality and Freedom. Back in Bucharest he taught at the university, wrote bestselling novels such as Maitreyi (Bengal Nights), and became associated — controversially — with the era's nationalist politics, including sympathy for the far-right Iron Guard, an episode that has shadowed his reputation.

After the Second World War he settled in Paris, then moved in 1956 to the University of Chicago, where he spent the rest of his career, editing the Encyclopedia of Religion and training generations of scholars.

Ideas

In works such as The Myth of the Eternal Return (1949), Patterns in Comparative Religion, The Sacred and the Profane (1957) and Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy, Eliade argued that religious humanity (homo religiosus) experiences the world through a division between sacred and profane space and time, and periodically regenerates time by re-enacting archetypal myths. His phenomenology of religion was hugely influential, though later scholars criticised its universalism, its treatment of historical context, and Eliade's politics.
