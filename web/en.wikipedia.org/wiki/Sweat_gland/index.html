Sweat gland

A sweat gland, also known as a sudoriferous gland, is a small tubular structure of the skin that produces sweat. Sweat glands are a type of exocrine gland, which produce and secrete substances onto an epithelial surface by way of a duct. There are two main types of sweat glands that differ in their structure, function, secretory product, mechanism of excretion, anatomic distribution, and distribution across species: eccrine sweat glands and apocrine sweat glands.

Eccrine glands

Eccrine glands are distributed over much of the human body, in varying densities, reaching a density of up to 200–400/cm² of skin surface. In humans, eccrine glands are the major sweat glands and are found in virtually all skin, with the highest density in the palm and soles (300–400/cm²), then on the head, but less on the torso and extremities. They produce a clear, odorless substance consisting primarily of water and sodium chloride. The activity of the eccrine glands is controlled by the sympathetic nervous system.

Apocrine glands

Apocrine glands develop during puberty and are found mainly in the axillae (armpits), perianal region, and areolae of the breasts. They secrete a more viscous, potentially odorous substance. Body odor results largely from bacterial decomposition of these secretions. Apocrine glands are innervated by adrenergic fibers.

Function

The primary function of sweating is thermoregulation: evaporation of sweat from the skin surface cools the body. A healthy person can lose several litres of sweat per day during heavy exercise in the heat. Sweat glands also respond to emotional stimuli such as stress and anxiety, particularly on the palms and soles.
