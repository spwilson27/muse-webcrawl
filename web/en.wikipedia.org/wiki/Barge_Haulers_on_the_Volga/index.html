Barge Haulers on the Volga (Russian: Burlaki na Volge) is an 1870–1873 oil painting by the Russian artist Ilya Repin. It depicts eleven burlaki — men employed to haul barges upstream by hand — dragging a vessel along the bank of the Volga River under a harsh summer sun. The work is considered a landmark of Russian critical realism and a sympathetic yet unsentimental portrait of human endurance.

Background

Repin first saw barge haulers on the Neva River in 1870 and was struck by the contrast between their ragged labour and the well-dressed holidaymakers nearby. That summer he travelled to the Volga, living near Stavropol-on-Volga and making studies of actual haulers, several of whom can be identified in the finished canvas. He completed the painting in Saint Petersburg in 1873.

Description

The haulers lean into leather breast-straps in a diagonal procession across the canvas. At the front walks Kanin, a defrocked priest whom Repin regarded with particular admiration, shown as strong and thoughtful; at the centre a young hauler, Larka, struggles against his strap, the one figure who visibly resists the group's resigned rhythm. In the distance a steam-powered tug smokes on the river — a pointed contrast, since steam towing was already rendering the burlaki obsolete elsewhere.

Reception

Exhibited in 1873, the painting made Repin's reputation. Fyodor Dostoevsky praised it in print, and the critic Vladimir Stasov championed it as a defining work of the Peredvizhniki (Wanderers) movement. It was purchased by Grand Duke Vladimir Alexandrovich and is now held by the State Russian Museum.
