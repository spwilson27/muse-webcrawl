Steve Paxton

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Steve Paxton (January 21, 1939 – February 20, 2024) was an American experimental dancer and choreographer best known as the originator of contact improvisation, a dance form based on the physics of two or more bodies in contact — sharing weight, rolling, falling, and following points of physical contact. His work with the Judson Dance Theater and the improvisational collective Grand Union helped redefine postmodern dance.

Early career

Paxton was born in Phoenix, Arizona, and trained in gymnastics before studying dance, including with Merce Cunningham and José Limón. In New York in the early 1960s he performed with the Cunningham company and became a founding member of the Judson Dance Theater, where his pedestrian-movement works, such as Proxy (1961) and Satisfyin Lover (1967), challenged conventions of virtuosity and spectacle. In 1970 he co-founded Grand Union, an improvisational performance group whose members included Yvonne Rainer, Trisha Brown, and David Gordon.

Contact improvisation

In 1972, while teaching at Oberlin College, Paxton developed the performance Magnesium, in which dancers threw themselves at a mat and at each other; from these experiments, and studies of aikido and gymnastics, he distilled contact improvisation. First performed as a series of duets in New York in 1972, the form spread worldwide as a social and artistic practice, taught in open "jams" rather than through a fixed technique. Paxton continued to develop related practices, including "Material for the Spine," a study of spinal movement and the senses, released as a DVD in 2008.

Later life

Paxton settled in Vermont in the 1970s, where he farmed and taught internationally for five decades. He received a Guggenheim Fellowship and a Bessie Award for lifetime achievement, among other honours. He died on his farm in 2024, aged 85.
