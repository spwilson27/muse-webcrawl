Allied-occupied Germany refers to the period from the defeat of Nazi Germany in 1945 to the founding of the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) and the German Democratic Republic (East Germany) in 1949, during which the country was divided into four occupation zones administered by the United States, the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom, and France.

Occupation zones

At the Yalta and Potsdam conferences, the Allies agreed to divide Germany — and its capital Berlin, although deep inside the Soviet zone — into four zones of occupation. Supreme authority rested with the four commanders-in-chief acting jointly through the Allied Control Council. The territories east of the Oder–Neisse line were placed under Polish and Soviet administration, and millions of ethnic Germans were expelled westward.

Policies

Initial occupation policy, summarised as the "four Ds" — demilitarisation, denazification, decentralisation, and democratisation — included the Nuremberg trials of major war criminals, removal of Nazis from public life, dismantling of war industry, and reparations, especially to the USSR. Living conditions were harsh amid destroyed cities, housing shortages, and food scarcity. From 1947 the Western powers pivoted toward economic reconstruction: the American and British zones merged into Bizonia, the Marshall Plan extended aid, and the 1948 currency reform in the western zones (the Trizone including France) stabilised the economy. The Soviet response to the currency reform, the Berlin Blockade of 1948–49, was defeated by the Berlin Airlift.

Division

As cooperation between the wartime allies collapsed into the Cold War, the zones hardened into two states: the Federal Republic of Germany in May 1949 and the German Democratic Republic in October 1949. Germany remained divided until reunification in 1990.
