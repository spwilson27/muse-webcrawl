Replicon (genetics)

A replicon is a DNA molecule, or a region of DNA, that replicates as a single unit from a single origin of replication. The concept was proposed by François Jacob, Sydney Brenner and Jacques Cuzin in 1963 to explain how replication of bacterial chromosomes and plasmids is controlled.

The replicon model

Jacob, Brenner and Cuzin postulated that each replicon carries two elements: a replicator, a cis-acting DNA site (now identified with the origin of replication), and a gene encoding a trans-acting initiator protein that binds the replicator to start a round of replication. The model correctly predicted positive control of initiation and has framed the study of replication control ever since.

Replicons in different organisms

Most bacterial chromosomes form a single replicon with one origin (oriC in Escherichia coli), from which two replication forks proceed bidirectionally. Plasmids and bacteriophage genomes are independent replicons, each with its own origin and copy-number control. Eukaryotic chromosomes, by contrast, contain many replicons — tens of thousands in a human cell — each with its own origin, firing at characteristic times during S phase. Mitochondrial and chloroplast DNAs are also separate replicons.
