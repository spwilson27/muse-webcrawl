The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA), Pub. L. 104–191, is a United States federal statute enacted by the 104th United States Congress and signed into law by President Bill Clinton on August 21, 1996. Its original purpose was to improve the portability and continuity of health insurance coverage and to combat waste, fraud and abuse in health insurance and health care delivery; it has become best known for its privacy and security rules governing health information.

Titles

  • Title I — Health Care Access, Portability, and Renewability: limits restrictions on coverage for pre-existing conditions and allows workers to keep coverage when changing jobs.
  • Title II — Preventing Health Care Fraud and Abuse; Administrative Simplification; Medical Liability Reform: requires national standards for electronic health care transactions and national identifiers, and led to the Privacy Rule and Security Rule.
  • Titles III–V — tax-related provisions, group health plan requirements, and revenue offsets.

Privacy and Security Rules

The Privacy Rule (standards effective 2003) protects individually identifiable protected health information (PHI) held by covered entities — health plans, clearinghouses and most health care providers — and their business associates, setting standards for its use and disclosure and giving patients rights to access and amend their records. The Security Rule specifies administrative, physical and technical safeguards for electronic PHI. The Breach Notification Rule (2009) requires notification of affected individuals after a breach. Enforcement is by the Department of Health and Human Services' Office for Civil Rights; penalties were strengthened by the HITECH Act of 2009.

Criticism and impact

HIPAA is often misunderstood as barring almost any sharing of health information; providers sometimes cite it to withhold information the rule actually permits sharing. Critics have also pointed to compliance costs and to the persistence of large health-data breaches despite the Security Rule.
