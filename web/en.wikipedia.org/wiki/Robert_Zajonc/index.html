Robert Bolesław Zajonc (November 23, 1923 – December 3, 2008) was a Polish-American social psychologist known for his research on social and cognitive processes, including the mere-exposure effect, social facilitation, and the role of affect in judgment. His surname was anglicised from Zając.

Life

Born in Łódź, Zajonc fled the German invasion in 1939, losing both parents in the bombing of Warsaw; he reached England after time in a labour camp and studied with the Polish forces before emigrating to the United States. He took his PhD at the University of Michigan in 1955 and spent most of his career there, founding and directing its Institute for Social Research groups, before moving to Stanford University in 1994.

Research

In 1968 Zajonc proposed the mere-exposure effect: people tend to prefer things merely because they are familiar with them, an effect demonstrated with nonsense words, faces and ideographs across hundreds of studies. His 1965 drive theory account of social facilitation explained why the presence of others improves performance on simple tasks and impairs it on complex ones. Later, Zajonc famously argued that preferences need no inferences — that affect can precede and operate independently of cognition — and, with Hazel Markus, developed the confluence model of birth order, family size and intellectual environment.
