Acid rock is a loosely defined type of rock music that evolved out of the mid-1960s garage punk movement and helped launch the psychedelic subculture. Named after lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD, commonly known as "acid"), the style is generally defined by long instrumental solos, few (if any) lyrics, and musical improvisation. Many acid rock groups centred on the San Francisco music scene, performing at venues and dance halls such as the Fillmore and the Avalon Ballroom.

Psychedelic poster art became a hallmark of the acid rock scene.

Characteristics

Acid rock emphasised extended jam-based structures, distorted electric guitar, swirling organ and keyboard textures, and lyrics influenced by psychedelic experience, Eastern spirituality and the counterculture. Songs frequently stretched well beyond radio-friendly lengths on albums and in live performance.

History

The term appeared in the mid-1960s as bands such as the 13th Floor Elevators — among the first to describe their own music as "psychedelic rock" — and the Charlatans played music explicitly connected with LSD culture; author Ken Kesey's Acid Tests in the San Francisco Bay Area fused live music with the drug experience. Jefferson Airplane, the Grateful Dead, Big Brother and the Holding Company (with Janis Joplin) and the Doors became the style's best-known exponents. By the early 1970s the sound had fragmented into progressive rock, hard rock and heavy metal.

Legacy

Acid rock's improvisational ethos carried into jam bands, stoner rock and later psychedelic revivals, and its poster and light-show aesthetics remain closely associated with the 1960s counterculture.
