Uncodified constitution

An uncodified constitution is a type of constitution where fundamental rules of government are not gathered in a single written document but derive from multiple sources: statutes, case law, conventions, and authoritative works. The United Kingdom is the classic example; New Zealand and, in part, Israel (through its Basic Laws) also have uncodified constitutions.

ExamplesUnited Kingdom, New Zealand, Israel
OppositeCodified constitution

Sources in the UK

The constitution of the United Kingdom draws on landmark statutes (Magna Carta 1215, Bill of Rights 1689, Acts of Union, Parliament Acts, Human Rights Act 1998), common law judgments, parliamentary conventions such as ministerial responsibility, and academic authorities like Dicey and Bagehot. Because Parliament is sovereign, no statute is formally entrenched against repeal.

Arguments

Supporters cite flexibility and evolutionary stability; critics argue uncodified rules are unclear, easily changed by simple majorities, and weak in protecting rights — debates renewed after Brexit and proposals for a written UK constitution.
