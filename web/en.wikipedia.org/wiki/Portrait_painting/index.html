Portrait painting

[Image: A Renaissance portrait in three-quarter view — a sitter in dark dress against a distant landscape, hands resting on a parapet.]

Portrait painting is a genre in painting in which the intent is to depict a specific human subject. Well-executed portraits show not only the sitter's appearance but something of their character, status and inner life. Terms such as portraiture and likeness distinguish the individualised portrait from generic figure painting.

History

Portraiture is ancient: Egyptian funerary portraits and Roman busts recorded real individuals. After a medieval period in which donors appeared small within religious scenes, the independent portrait revived in the fifteenth century in the work of Jan van Eyck, Piero della Francesca and Antonello da Messina, evolving from strict profile to three-quarter and full face. High Renaissance masters — Leonardo da Vinci, Raphael and Titian — set the enduring models, and Titian's state portraits shaped the image of power for courts across Europe.

In the seventeenth century Rembrandt's penetrating self-portraits and group portraits, Velázquez's court images and Van Dyck's elegant aristocratic style defined the Baroque portrait. The eighteenth century brought the conversational intimacy of Gainsborough, the pastel portraits of Rosalba Carriera and grand-manner Reynolds. In the modern era, portraiture absorbed photography's challenge: Sargent, Cézanne, Picasso and Lucian Freud each reinvented the genre, and it remains a central form in contemporary art.

Formats and conventions

Portraits are described by how much of the body is shown — full-length, three-quarter-length, half-length, bust, or head — and by pose (profile, three-quarter view, full face). Attributes such as books, instruments, uniforms or settings signal the sitter's identity and claims to status, while the self-portrait, from Dürer onwards, became a vehicle for artists' self-examination.
