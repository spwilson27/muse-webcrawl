Abel–Ruffini theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Abel–Ruffini theorem (also known as Abel's impossibility theorem) states that there is no solution in radicals to general polynomial equations of degree five or higher with arbitrary coefficients. In other words, while quadratic, cubic and quartic equations can all be solved by formulas involving only arithmetic operations and root extractions, no such universal formula exists for the quintic.

History

Paolo Ruffini published the first, nearly complete proof of impossibility in 1799, though it contained a gap. In 1824 the Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel supplied the first fully accepted proof. The theorem does not say that individual quintic equations cannot be solved — many have simple radical solutions — but that no general formula in radicals can cover all of them. It also does not preclude solving quintics using special functions such as elliptic or hypergeometric functions.

Galois theory

Galois theory later explained the result: a polynomial equation is solvable in radicals if and only if its Galois group is a solvable group. The general quintic has Galois group S5, which is not solvable, providing the deeper reason behind Abel and Ruffini's theorem and launching modern abstract algebra.
