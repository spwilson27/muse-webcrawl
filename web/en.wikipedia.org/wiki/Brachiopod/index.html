Brachiopods, phylum Brachiopoda, are a phylum of marine animals that have hard "valves" (shells) on the upper and lower surfaces, unlike the left and right arrangement in bivalve molluscs. Brachiopod valves are hinged at the rear end, while the front can be opened for feeding or closed for protection. They are commonly known as "lamp shells" because the curved shells of some articulate forms resemble ancient Roman oil lamps.

Biology

Inside the shell, brachiopods possess a distinctive feeding organ, the lophophore — a coiled or folded structure bearing ciliated tentacles that generate currents and capture food particles. Most brachiopods attach to the seafloor by a muscular stalk, the pedicle, emerging from the ventral (pedicle) valve; lingulids instead burrow in sediment using a long pedicle. Brachiopods release gametes into the water, and their larvae are planktonic.

Evolutionary history

Brachiopods appeared in the early Cambrian, over 520 million years ago, and dominated Palaeozoic marine ecosystems, evolving into thousands of genera. They suffered catastrophic losses in the end-Permian mass extinction, the largest extinction event in Earth's history, after which bivalve molluscs largely took over their ecological niches. Fewer than 400 species survive today, mostly in cold, deep, or cryptic habitats, though a few — such as Lingula, a famous "living fossil" — inhabit intertidal burrows.

Classification

Modern classifications divide living brachiopods into three classes: Lingulata (inarticulate, with phosphatic shells), Craniata (inarticulate, cemented directly to the substrate), and Rhynchonellata (articulate, with calcitic shells joined by teeth and sockets).
