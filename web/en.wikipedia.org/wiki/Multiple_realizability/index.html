Multiple realizability is a thesis in the philosophy of mind according to which a single mental state or property can be realized (implemented) by many different physical states or structures. Introduced by Hilary Putnam in the 1960s, it became the central argument against the type identity theory, which held that each type of mental state is identical to one type of brain state.

The argument

Putnam observed that pain, for example, is felt by humans, other mammals, birds, reptiles and possibly molluscs such as octopuses, whose nervous systems differ radically. It seemed implausible that one and the same neural state type is present in all these creatures; what they share is better characterized functionally — by the state's causal role (caused by tissue damage, causing avoidance behaviour, and so on). The same point was extended to computers: a single program can run on many hardware configurations.

Consequences

Multiple realizability motivated functionalism, the view that mental states are defined by their causal roles rather than their material composition. Jerry Fodor generalized the argument to defend the autonomy of the "special sciences": psychological, economic and other higher-level kinds cross-classify physical kinds, so their generalizations cannot be smoothly reduced to physics.

Criticism

Critics such as Jaegwon Kim have argued that multiple realizability does not block local, species-specific reductions, and that taking the thesis seriously threatens the causal efficacy of mental properties. Others note that the claimed realizers may differ so much that the "single" mental kind fragments into distinct kinds. The debate remains central to discussions of reductionism and consciousness.
