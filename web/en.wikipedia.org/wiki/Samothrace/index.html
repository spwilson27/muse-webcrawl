Samothrace

Samothrace (Greek: Σαμοθράκη, Samothráki) is a Greek island in the northern Aegean Sea, part of the Evros regional unit of Thrace. It is a rugged, mountainous island of about 178 square kilometres, dominated by Mount Saos (1,611 metres), one of the highest peaks in the Aegean. The island is best known in the ancient world for the Sanctuary of the Great Gods, a famous mystery-cult centre, and as the findspot of the celebrated statue known as the Winged Victory of Samothrace.

History

Samothrace was inhabited from prehistoric times and colonised by Greeks from Samos (from which its name derives, "Thracian Samos") in the sixth century BC. Its sanctuary of the Great Gods (the Cabeiri and associated deities) attracted initiates from across the Greek and Roman world; legend held that Philip II of Macedon met his wife Olympias there, and their son Alexander the Great was conceived in connection with the rites. The island remained prosperous in the Hellenistic and Roman periods and later passed through Byzantine, Genoese, and Ottoman hands before becoming part of Greece in the twentieth century.

Winged Victory

The Winged Victory (Nike) of Samothrace, a marble sculpture dating to about 200–190 BC, was discovered on the island in 1863 by the French consul Charles Champoiseau. Representing the goddess Nike alighting on the prow of a ship, it is considered one of the masterpieces of Hellenistic sculpture and is displayed at the Louvre in Paris at the head of the Daru staircase.

Geography and modern life

The island's main town is Chora, with the port at Kamariotissa. The economy rests on fishing, agriculture, and tourism; visitors are drawn by the Sanctuary of the Great Gods, the medieval Genoese towers, and the natural pools and waterfalls of mountain streams such as the Fonias. Samothrace has a ferry connection to Alexandroupoli on the mainland.
