Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille (15 May 1713 – 21 March 1762) was a French astronomer and geodesist who named 14 of the 88 constellations recognised today. From his observatory at the Cape of Good Hope in South Africa, he conducted the first comprehensive survey of the southern sky.

Early career

Originally destined for the church, Lacaille turned to astronomy and worked on the verification of the French meridian arc, helping to establish the shape of the Earth. He became a professor of mathematics at the Collège Mazarin in Paris and a member of the French Academy of Sciences.

Expedition to the Cape

From 1750 to 1754 Lacaille led an expedition to the Cape of Good Hope. In just two years of observing he catalogued nearly 10,000 southern stars — more than all previous astronomers combined for that hemisphere — and observed over 40 nebulous objects, including globular clusters and galaxies later incorporated into the Messier catalogue. To fill gaps in the southern sky he created fourteen new constellations, many named for instruments of science and art, including Telescopium, Horologium, Norma, Circinus and Mons Mensae (the only constellation named after a geographical feature, Table Mountain).

He also measured a meridian arc in South Africa and determined the positions of the Moon, Venus and Mars in cooperation with observers in Europe, contributing to measurements of stellar parallax and the solar system scale. His posthumous star catalogue Coelum Australe Stelliferum appeared in 1763. The lunar crater La Caille and asteroid 9135 Lacaille are named in his honour.
