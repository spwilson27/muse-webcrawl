Profit sharing

Profit sharing is a system in which employees receive, in addition to their regular wages or salaries, a share of the profits of the enterprise that employs them. Payments may be made in cash, in company shares, or as contributions to a retirement plan. Profit sharing is intended to align employees' interests with those of the business, improve motivation and retention, and give compensation some flexibility across the business cycle.

History

Early schemes appeared in the 19th century: the Parisian house-painting firm of Edme Jean Leclaire introduced a celebrated plan in 1842, and Procter & Gamble adopted profit sharing in 1887, making it one of the oldest such programmes in the United States. The idea attracted support from economists and reformers who hoped it would reduce industrial conflict. In France, employee profit sharing (participation) became legally mandatory for larger firms in 1967. In the United States, profit-sharing retirement plans grew rapidly after the Second World War, encouraged by favourable tax treatment.

Types of plans

  • Cash plans pay employees a percentage of profits directly, usually annually.
  • Deferred plans place contributions in a trust for retirement, as in US profit-sharing 401(k)-style plans, where the employer's contribution is discretionary.
  • Combination plans split the benefit between immediate cash and deferred savings.
  • Gainsharing, a related system, distributes rewards based on measured improvements in productivity or cost savings rather than company-wide profit.

Allocation formulas

Employers commonly allocate the shared pool in proportion to each employee's salary, sometimes weighted by years of service, or occasionally in equal shares. The percentage of profit distributed may be fixed by formula or set at the discretion of management or the board. Eligibility rules, vesting schedules and caps vary widely by plan and jurisdiction.

Effects and criticism

Research generally finds modest positive associations between profit sharing and productivity, job satisfaction and firm survival, particularly when combined with employee participation in decision-making. Critics note that in large firms an individual worker's effort has little visible effect on total profit (the "1/N problem"), that profit-based pay exposes employees to income risk beyond their control, and that profits can be manipulated through accounting choices. Profit sharing is nonetheless often contrasted favourably with purely individual incentives because it encourages cooperation. See also employee stock ownership and bonus.
