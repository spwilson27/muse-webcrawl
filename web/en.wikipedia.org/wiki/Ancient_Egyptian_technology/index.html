Ancient Egyptian technology describes the devices, methods, and infrastructure developed in Egypt from the unification of the country around 3100 BC through the Persian and Macedonian conquests. Without iron tools, wheeled heavy transport, or pulleys for most of their history, the Egyptians nonetheless raised the largest stone structures of the ancient world and developed sophisticated crafts in metallurgy, glass, medicine, and hydraulics.

Construction and engineering

The pyramids of Giza (c. 2560 BC) required quarrying, transporting, and dressing millions of limestone blocks, with granite brought by barge from Aswan some 800 km away. Blocks were moved on wooden sledges, with water poured on the sand to reduce friction — a technique confirmed both by tomb paintings and modern experiments. Ramps of various designs are thought to have raised stones to the upper courses. Egyptian surveyors aligned the Great Pyramid to true north within a fraction of a degree, using stellar observations and simple tools such as the plumb line, the square level, and the merkhet.

Agriculture and hydraulics

Farming depended on the annual Nile flood. Basin irrigation trapped floodwater in diked fields; later the shaduf (a counterweighted sweep) and, in the Ptolemaic period, the Archimedes screw and saqia waterwheel lifted water to higher ground. The nilometer measured flood levels to set taxes and predict harvests. The Egyptians operated a 365-day civil calendar of twelve 30-day months plus five extra days, used for administration for thousands of years.

Materials and crafts

Egyptians smelted copper and later bronze, worked gold with extraordinary skill, and produced faience — a glazed ceramic — from the Predynastic period. Glassmaking flourished in the New Kingdom, with core-formed vessels and coloured glass traded across the eastern Mediterranean. Papyrus, made from the pith of the papyrus sedge, was writing material exported throughout the ancient world. Linen weaving, shipbuilding with sewn and mortise-and-tenon planks (as in the Khufu ship), pottery, and carpentry were all highly developed.

Medicine

Egyptian medicine combined empirical treatment with ritual. The Edwin Smith Papyrus (c. 1600 BC, copying older material) describes 48 surgical cases in a strikingly systematic, observational manner, while the Ebers Papyrus collects hundreds of remedies. Physicians set fractures, used splints and sutures, and prescribed honey and mouldy bread for wounds — applications with genuine antiseptic value.
