Kitasato Shibasaburō

Born29 January 1853, Kumamoto, Japan
Died13 June 1931, Tokyo, Japan
Known forCo-discovery of the plague bacillus; tetanus antitoxin

Kitasato Shibasaburō (1853–1931) was a Japanese physician and bacteriologist, regarded as one of the founders of modern bacteriology in Japan. He studied in Berlin under Robert Koch and made fundamental contributions to immunology and the study of infectious disease.

Career

Working with Emil von Behring in Koch's institute, Kitasato achieved the first pure culture of the tetanus bacillus (1889) and helped demonstrate that serum from immunised animals could neutralise diphtheria and tetanus toxins — the basis of serum therapy. In 1894, during an outbreak in Hong Kong, he co-discovered (independently of Alexandre Yersin) the bacterium responsible for bubonic plague, Yersinia pestis.

Institution building

Returning to Japan, Kitasato founded the Institute for Infectious Diseases in Tokyo and trained a generation of Japanese researchers. After a dispute with the government over control of the institute, he founded the Kitasato Institute in 1914. He was nominated for the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work on antitoxins, though the prize went to von Behring alone — an omission still discussed by historians of science.
