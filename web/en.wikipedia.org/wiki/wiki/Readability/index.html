Readability

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Readability is the ease with which a reader can understand written text. It depends on the content of the text (its vocabulary, syntax, and cohesion) and on its presentation (typeface, line length, spacing, and layout). Researchers have used readability measures to define the suitability of books for school curricula and, more recently, to assess legal, medical, and technical documents intended for the general public.

Readability formulas

From the 1920s onward, researchers developed formulas that estimate reading difficulty from surface features of a text, typically average sentence length and word length or word frequency. Well-known examples include the Flesch Reading Ease test and the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level (developed with the United States Navy in the 1970s), the Gunning fog index, the SMOG index, the Coleman–Liau index, and the Automated Readability Index. Most output a score corresponding to a United States school grade level needed to comprehend the text on first reading.

Research and criticism

Early studies found that formulas were useful predictors of comprehension-test difficulty, and they were widely adopted by educators, publishers, and government agencies; several US states require insurance or consumer documents to meet minimum readability scores. Critics note that the formulas ignore background knowledge, motivation, text structure, and meaning, and that writing deliberately to a formula can produce choppy or unnatural prose. Readability is also distinguished from legibility, which concerns the visual ease of distinguishing letters and words on the page.

Applications

Plain-language movements in law, healthcare, and public administration use readability assessment as one tool among several, alongside reader testing and usability methods, to make essential documents understandable to their intended audiences.
