Legibility

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Legibility is the ease with which a reader can recognize individual characters in a piece of text. It is a property of the typeface and its rendering — how readily one letterform can be distinguished from another — and is distinct from readability, which concerns the ease of reading whole words, sentences, and blocks of text and depends on layout, spacing, and language.

Factors

Legibility is influenced by x-height, stroke contrast, the size of counters and apertures, letter spacing, and the distinctness of easily confused glyphs (such as I, l, and 1, or O and 0). Rendering conditions matter as well: size, resolution, contrast, viewing distance, and lighting all affect how legible text is in practice. Typefaces designed for signage and screens — for example Frutiger, Clearview, and Verdana — are often cited as highly legible.

Measurement

Researchers have measured legibility through reading-speed tests, recognition thresholds at small sizes or short exposures, and error rates. Since the early 20th century, typographers and psychologists have debated the relative merits of serif and sans-serif faces; the consensus is that familiar letterforms and generous proportions matter more than the presence of serifs alone.

Applications

Legibility research informs the design of road signs, aviation and medical displays, ballots, pharmaceutical labels, and user interfaces, where misreading a single character can have serious consequences.
