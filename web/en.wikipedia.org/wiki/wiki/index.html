Wiki

A wiki (pronounced "wicky") is a form of website and knowledge-management system that allows users to create and collaboratively edit content directly in a web browser, using a simplified markup language or a rich-text editor. A key characteristic of wiki technology is the ease with which pages can be created and updated, generally without the review required by traditional publishing: most wikis are open to alteration by any visitor, or at least by registered users. Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia, is the best-known example.

History

The first wiki, WikiWikiWeb, was created by American programmer Ward Cunningham in 1994–95 as a supplement to the Portland Pattern Repository, a site for discussing software design patterns. Cunningham chose the name from the Hawaiian word wiki, meaning "quick", which he had seen on the "Wiki Wiki Shuttle" buses at Honolulu International Airport. Wiki software was originally called "WikiWikiWeb"; later implementations include UseModWiki, TWiki, and MediaWiki (developed for Wikipedia from 2002). Cunningham described the wiki's principles as: open, incremental, organic, mundane, universal, overt, unified, precise, tolerant, observable, and convergent.

Characteristics

Wiki pages are hyperlinked into a web-like structure; new pages are typically created simply by linking to them. Most wikis keep a complete revision history, allowing any earlier version to be restored and changes to be compared (diffs) — a key safeguard that makes openness practical. Many wikis support "watchlists", recent-changes feeds, and separate discussion or "talk" pages. Access rules vary: from fully public encyclopedias to corporate intranet wikis used for documentation and project management, to personal "notebook" wikis.

Uses and notable wikis

Beyond Wikipedia and its sister projects (Wiktionary, Wikivoyage, etc.), wikis host fan encyclopedias of fictional universes (often on the Fandom platform), software documentation, community knowledge bases, and internal company handbooks. The technology influenced later collaboration tools, though modern services have partly displaced standalone wiki software.
