Typography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Typography is the art and technique of arranging type to make written language legible, readable, and appealing when displayed. It involves selecting typefaces, point sizes, line lengths, line spacing (leading), letter spacing (tracking), and kerning, and arranging type on a page or screen. Typography is performed by typesetters, compositors, typographers, graphic designers, and increasingly by software.

History

Typography with movable type began with ceramic and wooden type in China and metal type in Korea, and was industrialized in Europe by Johannes Gutenberg's printing press around 1440. Punchcutting and metal type dominated for centuries; the 19th century added mechanized composition (Linotype and Monotype), the 20th phototypesetting, and from the 1980s desktop publishing and digital fonts (PostScript, TrueType, OpenType), which put type design and layout in the hands of individual users.

Scope

  • Typefaces and fonts — serif, sans-serif, script, display, and monospace families, each with characteristic voices and uses.
  • Page layout — grids, margins, columns, hierarchy, and white space.
  • Microtypography — hyphenation, justification, spacing, and punctuation details such as the Oxford comma and hanging punctuation.
  • Web typography — responsive sizing, web fonts, and variable fonts for screens.

Principles

Good typography is often described as invisible: it serves the text rather than calling attention to itself. Legibility and readability research, historical conventions, and the material constraints of each medium guide practice. Influential formulations include Jan Tschichold's The New Typography, Josef Müller-Brockmann's grid systems, and Robert Bringhurst's The Elements of Typographic Style.
