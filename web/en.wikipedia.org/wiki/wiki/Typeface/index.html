Typeface

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A typeface (or font family) is a design of letters, numbers, and other symbols unified by consistent visual properties. Each typeface consists of glyphs, usually including a full alphabet, numerals, and punctuation, and may comprise many fonts of different weights, widths, and styles (such as roman, italic, and bold). Examples of well-known typefaces include Times New Roman, Helvetica, Garamond, and Georgia. The term derives from the "face", or printing surface, of a piece of metal type in letterpress printing.

Terminology

In traditional metal type, a font was a complete set of sorts of one size and style of a typeface. In digital typography the words "font" and "typeface" are often used interchangeably, although strictly a typeface is the design itself while a font is a file or set of files implementing that design in a particular style and size. Type designers create typefaces; the discipline and art of arranging type is typography.

Classification

Typefaces are commonly grouped by their appearance and historical development. Major classes include serif typefaces (such as old-style, transitional, Didone, and slab serif designs), sans-serif typefaces (grotesque, neo-grotesque, humanist, and geometric), script typefaces imitating handwriting, and display or decorative faces intended for headlines. Systems such as the Vox-ATypI classification organise these groups more formally. Anatomical terms describe the parts of letters: the x-height, ascenders, descenders, serifs, bowls, counters, and terminals.

History

Typeface design began with the punchcutters of the fifteenth century, whose roman types were modelled on humanist handwriting; figures such as Claude Garamond, John Baskerville, and Giambattista Bodoni gave their names to enduring designs. Hot metal typesetting in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries industrialised type founding, and phototypesetting and then digital type in the 1980s made font creation and distribution widely accessible, producing an enormous expansion in the number of available typefaces.
