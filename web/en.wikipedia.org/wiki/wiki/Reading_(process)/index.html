Reading (process)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Reading is the process of taking in the sense or meaning of symbols, often specifically of written language, by sight or touch. A complex cognitive process, reading involves decoding symbols to derive meaning, drawing on word recognition, vocabulary, background knowledge, and reasoning. For visually impaired readers, braille provides a tactile form of reading.

Components

A widely used model distinguishes two broad components: decoding — translating print into language sounds and words, supported by phonological awareness, phonics knowledge, and fluency — and language comprehension — understanding the words and sentences once decoded. Skilled reading requires both; weakness in either produces distinct patterns of difficulty, as in dyslexia (a decoding difficulty) or hyperlexia.

Eye movements

Reading does not involve a smooth sweep across the page. The eyes make short rapid movements (saccades) separated by brief fixations of about 200–250 milliseconds during which information is taken in. Skilled readers fixate most content words, skip many short function words, and make regressions to earlier text about 10–15% of the time. Typical adult reading speeds for English prose are around 200–300 words per minute for comprehension.

Learning to read

Reading instruction has been the subject of long-running debates ("reading wars") between phonics-based and whole-language approaches. Research consensus — reflected in reports such as the US National Reading Panel (2000) — supports systematic phonics instruction as one component alongside vocabulary, fluency practice, and comprehension strategy teaching.
