Writing style

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In literature, a writing style is the manner in which an author chooses to write to their audience. It is the author's distinctive way of using language — diction, syntax, tone, rhythm, structure, and figures of speech — that distinguishes one writer's work from another's and shapes how meaning and effect are produced.

Style is what differentiates a legal brief from a poem, or Hemingway's spare, paratactic prose from Henry James's elaborate periodic sentences, even where the underlying subject matter is similar. Instructors commonly distinguish style from grammar and usage: style concerns the choices available among correct forms, while usage concerns correctness itself.

Elements

  • Diction — word choice, including formality, concreteness, and connotation.
  • Syntax — sentence length, complexity, and patterning.
  • Tone and voice — the author's attitude toward subject and reader.
  • Structure — organization of ideas at sentence, paragraph, and whole-text levels.
  • Figurative language — metaphor, simile, imagery, and sound devices.

Style guides

For nonfiction and institutional writing, style guides — such as The Chicago Manual of Style, APA style, and the AP Stylebook — prescribe conventions for consistency. In rhetoric, the classical levels of style (plain, middle, grand) have been discussed since antiquity, and Strunk and White's The Elements of Style popularized injunctions such as "omit needless words".
