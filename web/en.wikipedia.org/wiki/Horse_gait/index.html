Horse gait

Horse gaits

Ambling gaitsRunning walk, rack, pace, tölt, fox trot, and others

Horses can use various gaits (patterns of leg movement) during locomotion across solid ground, either naturally or as a result of specialised training by humans.

The natural gaits

  • Walk — a four-beat gait averaging about 6.4 km/h, in which each hoof strikes the ground separately; always at least two hooves on the ground. Footfall sequence: left hind, left fore, right hind, right fore.
  • Trot — a two-beat gait in which diagonal pairs of legs move together (left fore + right hind, then right fore + left hind), with a moment of suspension between beats; roughly 13 km/h. The slower, collected version ridden in Western riding is the jog.
  • Canter (and the Western lope) — a three-beat gait, 16–27 km/h, with one diagonal pair striking together and a suspension phase; it is asymmetric, having a right lead and a left lead.
  • Gallop — the fastest gait, a four-beat gait averaging 40–48 km/h in the wild, with a suspension phase in which all four feet leave the ground.

Ambling gaits

"Gaited" breeds — such as the Tennessee Walking Horse, Icelandic horse, Paso Fino, and Standardbred pacers — perform intermediate-speed four-beat ambling gaits in which the feet move in lateral or diagonal pairs without a suspension phase, making them notably smooth to ride. These include the pace (a two-beat lateral gait), the running walk, the rack, the tölt, and the fox trot. The ability to amble is heritable and is linked to a mutation in the gene DMRT3, identified in 2012, which affects spinal coordination circuits.

History of study

The question of whether all four hooves leave the ground at the gallop was settled by Eadweard Muybridge's sequential photographs of 1878, commissioned by Leland Stanford — work that became a landmark in the prehistory of cinema and in the scientific study of animal locomotion.
