Cabbage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A green cabbage cut in half, showing its tightly layered pale leaves]

Cabbage is a leafy cruciferous vegetable, Brassica oleracea var. capitata — one of several radically different crops (alongside kale, broccoli, cauliflower and Brussels sprouts) bred from a single wild coastal plant by selecting for different parts. Layers of leaves wrap into a dense round or pointed head that stores for months, making cabbage a pillar of northern and eastern European and East Asian winter cooking: sauerkraut in Germany, kimchi (with napa cabbage) in Korea, stuffed rolls from Poland to the Levant, and coleslaw everywhere. China is the largest producer.

History

Wild cabbage grows on the limestone coasts of Britain and the Mediterranean, and headed cabbages were developed in Europe by the Middle Ages; the Romans had known leafy forms, and Cato prescribed cabbage for nearly everything. Fermentation made it strategic: sauerkraut's vitamin C protected sailors, and Captain Cook enforced it on his crews against scurvy. In Korea, whole napa cabbages salted and packed with chilli, garlic and fish sauce became kimchi, fermented in earthenware jars buried against the winter. The same species, selected for buds, stems or curds, produced the entire brassica clan — a standing lesson in what selection can do.

Use and character

Cabbage is cheap, filling and nutritionally serious — high in vitamin C and K and in the glucosinolates studied across the brassica family. It is also famously flatulent and sulfurous when overcooked, which colours its reputation ("cabbage" has served as slang for money, a term of endearment in French — mon petit chou — and an insult in English playgrounds). Long slow cooking sweetens it; quick pickling keeps it bright. Stuffed cabbage, borscht with cabbage, stir-fried cabbage with chilli: few vegetables work so hard for so little money.
