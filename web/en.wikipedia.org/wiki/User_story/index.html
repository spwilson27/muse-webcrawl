User story

In software development and product management, a user story is an informal, natural language description of a feature of a software system, written from the perspective of an end user. User stories are a central practice of agile software development, originating in extreme programming (XP) in the late 1990s, and are used to focus discussion on the value a feature delivers rather than on technical specifications.

Format

User stories are often written on index cards or sticky notes, or stored in project management software. A widely used template, popularised by Mike Cohn, is:

As a <type of user>, I want <some goal> so that <some reason>.

For example: "As a customer, I want to save my shopping cart so that I can continue shopping later." The three parts identify who the feature is for, what they want, and why it matters.

INVEST criteria

Bill Wake proposed the INVEST mnemonic for evaluating user stories: they should be Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, and Testable. A story is accompanied by acceptance criteria that define when it is complete, and teams estimate its size using story points or similar relative measures. Large stories, sometimes called epics, are split into smaller ones before being scheduled.

Use in agile development

User stories form the items of a product backlog. During sprint or iteration planning, the team discusses stories with the product owner — the "conversation" in Ron Jeffries' "Card, Conversation, Confirmation" model of the practice — and commits to delivering the highest-value stories first. The emphasis on collaboration distinguishes user stories from traditional requirements documents: the written card is a reminder to have a conversation, not a complete specification.
