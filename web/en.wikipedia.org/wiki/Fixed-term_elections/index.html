Fixed-term elections

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fixed-term elections are elections whose date is set in advance by law, removing or limiting the executive's power to call a snap election at a chosen moment. Presidential systems are built on fixed terms; among parliamentary systems, Norway's Storting cannot be dissolved at all, Germany's Basic Law permits dissolution only after a failed confidence process, and several countries (Sweden, Canada in form) have fixed election dates subject to early dissolution in defined circumstances.

The United Kingdom adopted the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011, under which early elections required a two-thirds Commons vote or a no-confidence motion not reversed within 14 days; the Act structured the elections of 2017 (called by the two-thirds route) and 2019 (required a separate short Act), and was repealed in 2022, restoring the prerogative of dissolution exercised by the monarch on the prime minister's advice. The debate is balanced: fixed terms promise fairness and planning, while flexibility offers an escape from deadlock and dead governments.
