Clerical celibacy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Clerical celibacy is the discipline by which, in certain churches, clergy are required to remain unmarried and sexually abstinent. In the Latin Church of the Catholic Church, priestly celibacy is a discipline (not a dogma) reaffirmed by the Second Vatican Council. Eastern Catholic and Orthodox churches ordain married men as priests but require bishops to be celibate.

History

Early councils such as Elvira (c. 306) imposed continence in parts of the West, but married clergy remained common. The Gregorian Reform of the 11th century, followed by the First (1123) and Second (1139) Lateran Councils, made clerical marriage invalid in the Latin Church — motivated by theology, ritual purity, and the desire to keep church property from becoming hereditary.

Theological rationale

Supporters cite Christ's counsel of the eunuch "for the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 19:12), Paul's preference for the unmarried state, total availability for ministry, and the priest acting in persona Christi. Critics argue it lacks scriptural warrant as a requirement and contributes to clergy shortages.

Other traditions

Most Protestant churches dropped mandatory celibacy at the Reformation. Buddhist monasticism mandates celibacy for monks and nuns under the vinaya. Hindu sannyasins and Jain monastics likewise take celibacy vows, though not under the term "clerical celibacy".
