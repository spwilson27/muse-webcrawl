Commodity fetishism

Goods on display, their social origins obscured.

In Marxist theory, commodity fetishism is the perception of the social relationships involved in production—among people—as economic relationships among things, especially the commodities exchanged in markets. Karl Marx introduced the concept in the first chapter of Capital: Critique of Political Economy (1867), where he argues that in a society of generalized commodity production, the social character of private labor appears as an objective character of the products themselves, above all as their value.

Marx's argument

Because producers work independently and meet only through exchange, the total labor of society is coordinated by the market rather than consciously. Value then seems to be a natural property of things, "as if" inherent in the commodity, rather than the expression of relations between people. Marx borrowed the term "fetishism" from the anthropology of religion, comparing the commodity to a fetish: a human product that appears to have a life and power of its own and to stand over against its makers.

Related concepts

Marx presents money as the developed form of the fetish, and the analysis extends to interest-bearing capital. Later Marxists, notably Georg Lukács under the term reification (Verdinglichung), generalized the idea into a critique of consciousness under capitalism. The concept also influenced theories of consumer culture and spectacle, and contrasts in anthropology with gift economies, where exchanges visibly encode personal relationships.
