Electrodynamic tether

Electrodynamic tether
TypeLong conductive wire deployed in space
UsesPropellantless propulsion; electrical power generation; deorbiting
PrincipleLorentz force on current in a planetary magnetic field
Notable testsTSS-1/TSS-1R (1992, 1996); TiPS; YES2

Electrodynamic tethers (EDTs) are long conducting wires, such as those deployed from a satellite or other spacecraft, which can operate on electromagnetic principles as generators, converting their kinetic energy to electrical energy, or as motors, converting electrical energy to kinetic energy. When an electric current is passed through a tether in the presence of a planet's magnetic field, the Lorentz force acts on the tether, either boosting or lowering the spacecraft's orbit without expending propellant.

Principle of operation

As the tether moves through the ionosphere and the geomagnetic field, a motional electric field induces a voltage along its length — tens to hundreds of volts per kilometre. If the circuit is closed through the surrounding plasma (using plasma contactors or electron emitters at the ends), current flows. In generator mode the induced current produces a drag force that lowers the orbit while delivering usable power. In motor mode, a power supply drives current against the induced electromotive force, producing thrust that raises the orbit.

Applications

Proposed uses include reboosting space stations, generating auxiliary power, and — most prominently in recent years — deorbiting defunct satellites to reduce space debris. A passive electrodynamic tether can drag a spent satellite down to burn up in the atmosphere within months rather than the decades that natural decay would take from high low Earth orbits.

Flight experiments

The best-known demonstrations were the Tethered Satellite System missions flown on the Space Shuttle: TSS-1 in 1992 and TSS-1R in 1996, the latter of which deployed nearly 20 km of tether, generated several thousand volts, and then broke due to an electrical arc. Smaller experiments such as the Naval Research Laboratory's TiPS tether and ESA's YES2 have flown on other spacecraft, and several commercial deorbit devices are under development.

Challenges

Tethers face risks from micrometeoroid and debris impacts that can sever the wire, from dynamic instabilities, and from the difficulty of maintaining reliable electrical contact with the ionospheric plasma. Bare-tape tether designs, which collect electrons along their length, improve current collection and survivability.
