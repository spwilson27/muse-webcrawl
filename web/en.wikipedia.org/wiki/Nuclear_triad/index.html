Nuclear triad

A nuclear triad is a three-pronged military force structure of land-based intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs), submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs) and strategic bombers carrying nuclear gravity bombs and missiles. Having a three-branched nuclear capability gives a nation a greater ability to strike an enemy with a second strike and therefore increases a state's nuclear deterrence.

The three legs

  • Land-based ICBMs — silo-based and mobile missiles (e.g. the American Minuteman III) offer rapid launch and high accuracy but have known locations.
  • Submarine-launched ballistic missiles — carried by ballistic missile submarines on deterrent patrol, these are considered the most survivable leg because the submarines are extremely difficult to detect and destroy.
  • Strategic bombers — aircraft such as the B-52 and B-2 can be recalled after launch, carry stand-off cruise missiles, and visibly signal resolve in a crisis.

Rationale

Each leg has different strengths and vulnerabilities, so an adversary cannot eliminate a nation's ability to retaliate with a single first strike against one component. Diversification also complicates an opponent's defensive problem, since each leg requires different defences.

Operators

The United States and Russia maintain full triads. China has developed a triad with its Jin-class submarines and H-6 bombers alongside its large missile force. India describes its deterrent in triad terms, with land and air legs and the Arihant-class sea-based leg. Other nuclear states rely on dyads or monads, such as the United Kingdom's submarine-only deterrent.
