Shūsaku Endō

Shūsaku Endō (遠藤 周作, Endō Shūsaku; 27 March 1923 – 29 September 1996) was a Japanese author who wrote from the perspective of a Japanese Roman Catholic, a religious minority in Japan. He is best known outside Japan for his 1966 novel Silence (Chinmoku), about the persecution of Japanese Christians in the 17th century, which has been called one of the finest novels of the 20th century and was adapted into a 2016 film by Martin Scorsese. Together with Junnosuke Yoshiyuki, Shōtarō Yasuoka, Junzo Shono, Hiroyuki Agawa, Ayako Sono, and Shumon Miura, Endō is categorised as a member of the "Third Generation" of postwar Japanese writers.

Life

Endō was born in Tokyo but spent his early childhood in Dalian, Manchuria, where his father worked. After his parents divorced in 1933, he returned to Japan with his mother and was baptised a Catholic at the age of eleven or twelve. He studied French literature at Keio University and, from 1950 to 1953, in Lyon, France, an experience that sharpened his sense of the gulf between Japanese and Western Christian culture. Lifelong ill health, including tuberculosis and later kidney disease, repeatedly interrupted his work and informed his writing about suffering and weakness.

Work and themes

Endō's fiction explores the tension between his Catholic faith and Japanese culture, the silence of God in the face of suffering, and sympathy for the weak and apostate rather than the heroic strong. His early prize-winning novellas White Men (1955) and Yellow Men examined these cultural divides. The Sea and Poison (1958) treated the vivisection of American prisoners by Japanese doctors during the Second World War. Silence (1966), his masterpiece, follows the Portuguese Jesuit Sebastião Rodrigues in persecuted 17th-century Japan. Later novels include The Samurai (1980), about Hasekura Tsunenaga's embassy to Mexico and Rome, and his final novel Deep River (1993), a multi-faith journey to the Ganges. Endō also wrote widely read humorous essays, plays, and a life of Christ, A Life of Jesus (1973), aimed at Japanese readers.

Legacy

Endō received nearly every major Japanese literary prize and was awarded the Order of Culture in 1995. He was repeatedly rumoured to be a candidate for the Nobel Prize in Literature; Graham Greene, an admirer, called him one of the finest novelists of his generation. The Endō Shūsaku Literary Museum in Sotome, Nagasaki (now part of Nagasaki City), overlooking the area where Silence is set, commemorates his life and work.
