Shōsō-in (正倉院)
LocationTōdai-ji, Nara, Japan
Built8th century (Nara period)
Holdings~9,000 treasures of the imperial household
StatusNational Treasure; UNESCO World Heritage (Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara)

The Shōsō-in (Japanese: 正倉院) is the treasure house of the temple of Tōdai-ji in Nara, Japan. Built in the azekura (log-cabin) style, elevated above the ground on stilts, it preserves some 9,000 objects — the personal effects of Emperor Shōmu (701–756), ritual implements, musical instruments, weapons, textiles, documents, and imported luxury goods — making it one of the most important surviving collections of 8th-century material culture anywhere in the world.

History

The collection began in 756, when Empress Kōmyō, widow of Emperor Shōmu, donated more than 600 of the late emperor's belongings to the Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji as a memorial offering; further donations followed. The storehouse's triangular-log construction regulates humidity naturally — the logs swell in damp weather to seal the interior and shrink in dry weather to ventilate it — which helped preserve delicate objects for over 1,200 years. Access was strictly controlled by imperial seal, a system formalised under the Imperial Household Agency in the Meiji era.

The treasures

Because Nara was the eastern terminus of the Silk Road, the Shōsō-in preserves items from Persia, Central Asia, China, and beyond that have vanished in their countries of origin: a five-stringed lute (biwa) inlaid with mother-of-pearl, Persian-style glassware, brocades, board games, and the only surviving Tang-dynasty examples of many object types. The archive also holds thousands of administrative documents of the Nara state.

Exhibition

Once a year, in autumn, a rotating selection of about 60–70 treasures is exhibited at the Nara National Museum in the annual Shōsō-in Exhibition, a tradition dating to 1946 that draws large crowds. The building itself is normally closed to the public.
