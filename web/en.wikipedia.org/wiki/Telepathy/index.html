Telepathy

Telepathy (from the Greek τῆλε, tele, meaning "distant", and πάθος/-πάθεια, pathos or -patheia, meaning "feeling, perception, passion, affliction, experience") is the purported vicarious transmission of information from one person's mind to another's without using any known human sensory channels or physical interaction. The term was first coined in 1882 by the classical scholar Frederic W. H. Myers, a founder of the Society for Psychical Research (SPR), as a replacement for the earlier expression "thought-transference".

Telepathy experiments have historically been criticized for lack of proper controls and repeatability, and there is no convincing evidence that telepathy exists. The topic remains controversial in parapsychology, and telepathy is considered a pseudoscientific concept by the scientific consensus.

Research history

Early SPR investigations examined spontaneous cases and card-guessing experiments. In the 1930s, J. B. Rhine at Duke University popularized quantitative tests using Zener cards, and reported results he interpreted as evidence of extrasensory perception, though critics cited methodological flaws and failures to replicate. Later approaches included the Ganzfeld experiments, in which a "receiver" in a state of mild sensory deprivation attempted to identify images sent by a "sender"; meta-analyses of these studies have been debated, with proponents claiming a small significant effect and skeptics attributing results to publication bias and study quality.

In popular culture

Telepathy is a widespread device in science fiction, fantasy, and superhero fiction, where it ranges from communication between minds to full mind reading and control. The concept also features in folklore and religious traditions worldwide as a form of revelation or thought transference.
