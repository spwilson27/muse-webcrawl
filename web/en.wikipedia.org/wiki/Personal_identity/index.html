Personal identity

Personal identity is the philosophical problem of what makes a person at one time the same person as a person at another time, despite physical and psychological change. It asks what persistence conditions persons have: what, if anything, necessarily and sufficiently accounts for the fact that a person continues to exist through time.

Main theories

Psychological continuity views, descending from John Locke, ground identity in overlapping chains of memory, character and intention; Locke defined a person as a thinking being able to consider itself as itself in different times and places. Bodily or animalist views hold that we are numerically identical with a living human organism, so identity follows the body and brain. Soul theories, historically important but less defended today, posit an immaterial substance. Four-dimensionalists instead treat persons as space-time "worms" with temporal parts.

Problems and thought experiments

Objections accumulated quickly. Thomas Reid and Joseph Butler argued Lockean memory presupposes identity rather than constituting it. Derek Parfit's fission cases — one person's psychology branching into two successors — suggest identity cannot capture everything that matters in survival; Parfit concluded that psychological continuity ("Relation R") is what matters, not identity itself. Other staples include teletransportation, brain transplants, the Ship of Theseus, Bernard Williams's body-transfer cases, and real-world puzzles raised by dementia and conjoined twins. Marya Schechtman's "narrative self-constitution" view instead ties identity to a coherent self-told life story.

Significance

The debate matters beyond metaphysics: theories of personal identity bear on moral responsibility, compensation, abortion and euthanasia debates, survival of death, and legal identity. It also connects with empirical questions in psychology and neuroscience about memory and self-recognition.
