Predictive coding

Predictive coding is a theory of brain function in which the brain is conceived as a prediction machine that constantly generates and updates a mental model of its environment. According to the theory, the brain uses this model to predict incoming sensory input, and only the unpredicted portion of the signal — the prediction error — is passed up the cortical hierarchy for further processing.

Origins

Ideas resembling predictive coding appear in the work of Hermann von Helmholtz on unconscious inference in perception. In modern computational neuroscience, Rao and Ballard's 1999 model of visual cortex proposed that feedback connections from higher to lower visual areas carry predictions, while feedforward connections carry the residual errors between predictions and actual input. The framework is closely related to the free energy principle developed by Karl Friston.

Mechanism

In hierarchical predictive coding, each level of processing maintains a generative model that predicts the activity of the level below. Prediction errors are computed by comparing the prediction with actual activity; these errors are weighted by their estimated precision (reliability) and used to update beliefs at higher levels. Attention is often interpreted within the framework as the optimization of precision weighting.

Applications and influence

Predictive coding has been applied to perception, motor control (where predictions of the sensory consequences of movement guide action), and psychiatry, where disorders such as schizophrenia and autism have been hypothesized to involve aberrant precision weighting of prediction errors. The framework has also influenced machine learning and theories of consciousness.
