Tennessee whiskey

Tennessee whiskey is a type of straight whiskey produced in the U.S. state of Tennessee. It is legally defined under the North American Free Trade Agreement (and its successor agreements) and the law of several other countries as a distinct product of the United States, and since 2013 Tennessee state law has specified detailed requirements for the category. Although Tennessee whiskey is technically a bourbon-style whiskey, its producers have historically preferred to market it under its own name.

Production and the Lincoln County Process

The defining step in Tennessee whiskey production is the Lincoln County Process: before ageing, the new-make spirit is filtered slowly through a thick bed — typically about ten feet — of charcoal made from sugar maple, a step known as "charcoal mellowing". The process takes several days, strips harsh congeners from the spirit, and gives Tennessee whiskey its characteristic smoothness. It is named for Lincoln County, the original home of the Jack Daniel's distillery.

Under the 2013 Tennessee law (with an exception grandfathering one producer), Tennessee whiskey must be made from at least 51 percent corn, filtered through maple charcoal, distilled, aged in new charred oak barrels, and produced and aged entirely within Tennessee — requirements closely mirroring those for bourbon, plus the charcoal filtration.

Major producers

The category is dominated by two brands: Jack Daniel's, made in Lynchburg, Moore County — the best-selling American whiskey brand in the world, famously produced in a dry county where its sale was long prohibited — and George Dickel, made at the Cascade Hollow distillery near Tullahoma, which spells its product "whisky" in the Scottish manner and chills its spirit before charcoal filtration. A growing number of craft distillers, such as Nelson's Green Brier and Chattanooga Whiskey, have joined the category since the repeal of restrictive distilling laws in 2009.
