Sandstorm

A sandstorm (or dust storm) is a meteorological phenomenon common in arid and semi-arid regions in which strong winds lift large quantities of loose sand and dust from bare, dry soil into the atmosphere. A sandstorm transports coarse sand grains mainly by saltation close to the ground, while finer dust can be carried thousands of kilometres. The two terms are often used interchangeably, though technically "dust storm" refers to finer particles that reduce visibility over a wider area.

Formation

Sandstorms occur when strong pressure gradients or thunderstorm outflows sweep across dry, unvegetated terrain — deserts, dry lake beds, and drought-stricken farmland. As wind speed increases past a threshold, sand grains begin to bounce along the surface (saltation), dislodging finer dust into suspension. Thunderstorm-driven dust walls are called haboobs. Famous regional storms include the khamsin of Egypt, the simoom of Arabia, the shamal of Iraq, and the great dust storms of China's Gobi Desert that affect Beijing and can cross the Pacific.

Effects

Sand and dust storms reduce visibility to near zero, disrupt transport and aviation, damage machinery and crops, and aggravate respiratory disease. Wind-blown dust also fertilizes oceans and soils far away — Saharan dust nourishes the Amazon rainforest with phosphorus — but can carry pathogens and pollutants. The American Dust Bowl of the 1930s, worsened by unsound farming practices, remains the archetypal human-caused dust catastrophe. Climate change and desertification are expected to increase storm frequency in some regions, and similar storms occur on Mars, where global dust storms can encircle the planet.
