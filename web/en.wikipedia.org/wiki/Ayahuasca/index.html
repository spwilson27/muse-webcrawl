Ayahuasca is a South American psychoactive brew traditionally made by prolonged decoction of the Banisteriopsis caapi vine together with leaves of the chacruna plant (Psychotria viridis), which contain the psychedelic compound N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT). The vine contributes harmala alkaloids that inhibit monoamine oxidase, rendering the orally inactive DMT active. The name comes from Quechua aya ("spirit, soul, corpse") and waska ("rope, vine") — "vine of the soul".

Traditional use

Indigenous peoples of the western Amazon — including the Shipibo-Conibo, Asháninka, and many others — have used ayahuasca (known by names such as natem, yage, caapi and daime) in healing ceremonies led by a curandero or shaman. Ceremonies typically involve purging (vomiting, la purga), visionary experiences, and icaros (sacred songs). Use may extend back centuries or more; a 1,000-year-old ritual bundle found in Bolivia contained traces of ayahuasca-related compounds.

Religious movements

In the 20th century, syncretic churches formed around sacramental ayahuasca use in Brazil, notably Santo Daime (founded in the 1930s by Raimundo Irineu Serra) and the União do Vegetal (founded 1961). Brazil legalised religious use in the 1980s. In 2006 the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in Gonzales v. O Centro Espírita Beneficente União do Vegetal that the U.S. branch of the UDV could use the tea under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act.

Pharmacology and research

The brew combines DMT, a serotonin 5-HT2A receptor agonist, with β-carbolines that act as reversible MAO-A inhibitors. Effects last roughly 4–6 hours and include vivid imagery, emotional introspection, nausea and vomiting. Modern clinical research has investigated ayahuasca for depression and PTSD with promising early results, though evidence remains preliminary.
