Horse-flies and deer flies are true flies in the family Tabanidae in the insect order Diptera. They are stout-bodied, often large flies, ranging from about 6 to 25 mm in length, with prominent compound eyes that in living males and females of some species show striking colour patterns. The family contains roughly 4,500 described species worldwide.

Feeding

Adult females of most species feed on the blood of mammals — including horses, cattle, deer, and humans — using blade-like mouthparts to slash the skin and lap up the pooling blood. Their bites are painful, and tabanids can mechanically transmit diseases and parasites such as anthrax, tularemia, and equine infectious anemia. Males feed only on nectar and pollen and are harmless.

Life cycle

Females lay egg masses on vegetation overhanging water or damp ground. The larvae live in wet soil, mud, or shallow water, where many are predators of other invertebrates. After a larval period that may last one to three years, they pupate in drier soil and emerge as adults.

Ecology

Horse-flies are strong, fast fliers and are among the fastest flying insects; they are attracted to movement, dark colours, warmth, and carbon dioxide. Despite their pest status, adults also serve as pollinators of some plants, and the flies are prey for birds and other animals.
