Moral fictionalism

Moral fictionalism is a position in meta-ethics holding that moral statements, taken literally, are false or not literally true, but that it is nevertheless useful to continue making them "within the fiction" of morality, much as one talks about fictional characters. It combines an error theory about the semantics of moral discourse with a recommendation to preserve moral practice.

Variants

Revolutionary fictionalism proposes reforming practice: people should cease believing moral claims while continuing to use them as a useful fiction. Hermeneutic fictionalism instead claims that ordinary moral speakers are already fictionalists — that they engage with morality in a spirit of make-believe rather than full belief. A further distinction contrasts prefix fictionalism, where the fiction operator is scoped over whole utterances ("according to morality, stealing is wrong"), with figuralism based on metaphor.

Objections

Critics question whether moral engagement can survive the loss of belief, whether deciding moral questions by whim undermines the point of the fiction, and whether fictionalism collapses into moral subjectivism or nihilism in practice.
