Linothorax

The linothorax (from Ancient Greek λινοθώραξ, "linen breastplate") is a type of upper-body armour used by the ancient Greeks, as well as other Mediterranean peoples, from the Mycenaean period through the Hellenistic era. It was made of linen—layers of fabric stiffened and bonded together, possibly with animal glue—rather than bronze or leather.

A modern reconstruction of a linothorax based on vase paintings.

History and use

The linothorax appears frequently in Greek art from the late Archaic period onward and is mentioned by authors such as Homer, Herodotus, Xenophon and Livy. It is often associated with hoplites and with the soldiers of Alexander the Great, whose linen breastplate is described by Plutarch. Compared with a bronze cuirass, linen armour was cheaper, lighter and cooler to wear in Mediterranean heat, while experiments by modern researchers—notably the Linothorax Project at the University of Wisconsin–Green Bay—have shown that laminated linen panels offer surprisingly good resistance to arrows and cutting weapons.

Construction

Reconstructions picture the garment as a tube of layered linen wrapped around the torso, fastened on the left side, with double shoulder flaps (epomides) tied down at the chest and a skirt of hanging leather or linen strips (pteruges) protecting the hips and groin. Because linen decays, no complete ancient example survives, and details of its manufacture—including how many layers were used and whether the layers were glued, quilted or both—remain debated.
