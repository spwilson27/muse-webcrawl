Toy camera

The Diana, a classic plastic toy camera of the 1960s, now prized for its dreamlike, imperfect images.

The term toy camera refers to a simple, inexpensive film camera, usually made largely of plastic and fitted with a simple plastic meniscus lens. Although originally marketed as novelties or children's cameras, toy cameras have been embraced by photographers for their distinctive aesthetic defects: soft focus, vignetting, light leaks, colour shifts and unpredictable exposures. Their use is closely associated with the lomography movement.

Notable cameras

  • Diana — a 1960s Hong Kong-made medium format camera, sold cheaply and often given away as a promotional item; revived as the Diana+ by Lomography.
  • Holga — a medium format camera introduced in China in 1981, famous for its vignetting and light leaks; widely used in art photography.
  • Lomo LC-A — a Soviet compact camera whose saturated colours inspired the Lomography company and movement from the 1990s.
  • ActionSampler, SuperSampler and similar multi-lens novelty cameras.

Aesthetic and use

Toy camera photography values spontaneity and imperfection over technical precision. Lo-fi images produced by these cameras have been used in fine art, advertising and editorial photography, and exhibitions have been devoted to Diana and Holga work. The aesthetic later influenced digital photography, where filters and apps — most famously Instagram's early filters — imitate the vignettes, faded colours and light leaks of toy cameras.

Digital toy cameras

Very cheap digital cameras and deliberately low-resolution devices have been marketed in the same spirit, and smartphone apps reproduce the analogue toy camera look.
