Freyja

In Norse mythology, Freyja (Old Norse: "the Lady") is a goddess associated with love, beauty, fertility, sex, war, gold, and seiðr (a form of magic). She is the daughter of the god Njord and twin sister of the god Freyr. Like her father and brother, she belongs to the Vanir tribe of deities and came to live among the Æsir after the Æsir–Vanir War.

Mythology

Freyja rides a chariot pulled by two cats, is accompanied by the boar Hildisvíni, and possesses a cloak of falcon feathers and the precious necklace Brísingamen. She rules over the heavenly field Fólkvangr, where she receives half of those who die in battle — the other half going to Odin's hall, Valhalla. By her husband Óðr, who is frequently absent, she is the mother of the daughters Hnoss and Gersemi. Freyja weeps tears of red gold for her missing husband, and in his absence she travels under several names in search of him.

Freyja is a frequent object of desire in the myths: giants attempt to obtain her in marriage, and in the poem Þrymskviða, the giant Thrym steals Thor's hammer and demands Freyja as his bride; Thor instead disguises himself as Freyja to recover it. In Lokasenna, the god Loki taunts her before the assembled gods. Freyja also assists other deities, lending her feather cloak to Loki on his missions.

Attestations and worship

Freyja is attested in the Poetic Edda and the Prose Edda, compiled in 13th-century Iceland from earlier traditional material, in Heimskringla, and in various sagas. Numerous place names in Scandinavia are compounded with her name, particularly in Sweden and southern Norway. The Old Norse name for the planet Venus was associated with her, and the weekday Friday in several Germanic languages is often interpreted as "Freyja's day" (or Frigg's day — the two goddesses are sometimes conflated, and their possible relationship remains debated among scholars).

Modern influence

Freyja has appeared in numerous works of art, literature, opera and popular culture, and her name has been given to ships, places and products. She remains one of the most prominent figures of Germanic pagan revival movements.
