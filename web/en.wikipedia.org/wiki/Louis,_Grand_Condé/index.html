Louis II de Bourbon, Prince of Condé (8 September 1621 – 11 December 1686), known as the Grand Condé (le Grand Condé), was a French general and the most famous representative of the Condé branch of the House of Bourbon. Celebrated for his brilliant victory at Rocroi at the age of 21, he later led the princely rebellion of the Fronde against Cardinal Mazarin and the young Louis XIV, served for a time as a general of France's enemy Spain, and was finally pardoned and restored.

Early victories

Born in Paris, Louis was styled Duke of Enghien until he inherited the Condé title in 1646. Given command of the army in the Spanish Netherlands during the Thirty Years' War, he destroyed the Spanish army at Rocroi on 19 May 1643 — the first decisive defeat of the famed Spanish tercios — and won further hard-fought victories at Freiburg (1644), Allerheim/Nördlingen (1645), and Lens (1648), which helped bring about the Peace of Westphalia.

The Fronde

During the civil wars of the Fronde, Condé at first supported the regent Anne of Austria and Mazarin, defeating the Parisian rebels, but his arrogance and demands led to his arrest in 1650. Released, he turned against the court in the "Fronde of the Princes", was defeated by Turenne, and in 1652 fled to the Spanish Netherlands, entering Spanish service against France. At the Battle of the Dunes (1658) he fought on the losing Spanish side against his great rival Turenne.

Pardon and last campaigns

The Treaty of the Pyrenees (1659) pardoned Condé and restored his estates. Louis XIV employed him again in the War of Devolution (conquest of Franche-Comté, 1668) and the Franco-Dutch War, where his bloody, indecisive battle at Seneffe (1674) was his last major command. He spent his final years at Chantilly, patronising writers such as Molière, Racine, and Boileau, and died at Fontainebleau in 1686. Bossuet's funeral oration made him a byword for the heroic, turbulent grandee of the old France.
