Industrial unionism

Industrial unionism is a trade union organising method through which all workers in the same industry are organised into the same union, regardless of skill or trade, giving workers in that industry greater bargaining power. It contrasts with craft unionism, under which workers are organised by their specific craft or occupation.

Origins

Industrial unionism emerged in response to mechanised mass production in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, which created large workforces of semi-skilled workers whom craft unions often excluded. The Knights of Labor and later the American Railway Union organised across trades. The Industrial Workers of the World (IWW), founded in Chicago in 1905, championed "One Big Union" of all workers and rejected racial and skill divisions.

CIO era

In the United States, industrial unionism triumphed in the 1930s with the Committee (later Congress) of Industrial Organizations, which organised mass-production industries — steel, automobiles, rubber, meatpacking — under leaders such as John L. Lewis, using tactics including the sit-down strike. Legislative support from the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 (Wagner Act) enabled the CIO's growth before its 1955 merger with the craft-based American Federation of Labor to form the AFL–CIO.

Elsewhere

Industrial unions are also characteristic of labour movements in countries such as Germany (e.g. IG Metall, IG Bergbau, Chemie, Energie) and South Africa, where industrial union federations played major political roles.
