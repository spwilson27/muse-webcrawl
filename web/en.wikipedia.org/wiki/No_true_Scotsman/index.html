No true Scotsman

No true Scotsman, or the appeal to purity, is an informal fallacy in which one attempts to protect a universal generalization from a falsifying counterexample by excluding the counterexample on the grounds that it is not a "true" or "pure" member of the group. Rather than denying the counterexample or rejecting the original claim, the speaker modifies the claim so that the counterexample falls outside it.

Origin

The term was coined by the philosopher Antony Flew in his 1971 paper "The Presumption of Atheism" and developed in his 1975 book Thinking About Thinking. Flew's example imagines a Scotsman reading a newspaper report of a crime committed by an Englishman and declaring "No Scotsman would do such a thing." Confronted with a similar crime by a Scotsman, he retreats to "No true Scotsman would do such a thing."

Analysis

The fallacy works by redefining the terms of a claim in an ad hoc way so that it becomes unfalsifiable. Philosophers distinguish such illegitimate redefinition from legitimate refinements of a definition based on independent criteria. The fallacy is frequently identified in arguments about religious, political, and ideological identities, where unwelcome members of a group are declared not to be genuine members.
