Demand response

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Demand response (DR) is a set of strategies in electric power systems aimed at changing the electric usage patterns of end-use customers in response to changes in the price of electricity over time, or to incentive payments designed to induce lower electricity use at times of high wholesale market prices or when system reliability is jeopardised. Instead of building additional generation to meet peak demand, grid operators can pay consumers to reduce or shift their consumption.

Background

Electricity demand varies over the day and across seasons, and electricity has historically been difficult to store in large quantities. Utilities therefore built peaking power plants that run only a few hours a year to cover maximum demand — an expensive and carbon-intensive solution. Demand response treats flexible demand as a resource comparable to generation: reducing load during a peak is functionally equivalent to generating more power.

Types of programs

Demand response programs are commonly divided into two broad categories:

  • Price-based programs, in which customers face time-varying prices — such as time-of-use rates, critical peak pricing, or real-time pricing — and voluntarily adjust their consumption.
  • Incentive-based programs, in which customers agree in advance to curtail load when called upon, in exchange for payments or rate discounts. Examples include direct load control of air conditioners and water heaters, interruptible tariffs for industrial users, and capacity market programs.

Applications

Industrial and commercial customers have traditionally provided most demand response capacity, because they can reschedule energy-intensive processes. Residential participation has grown with smart meters, smart thermostats, and automated control of appliances, pool pumps, and electric vehicle charging. Aggregators combine the flexible load of many small customers into blocks large enough to bid into wholesale markets.

Demand response also supports grid reliability during emergencies, provides ancillary services such as frequency regulation, and helps integrate variable renewable energy by shifting consumption toward periods of abundant solar or wind generation. In the United States, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission's Order 745 (2011) established compensation for demand response in wholesale energy markets, and FERC Order 2222 (2020) opened wholesale markets to aggregations of distributed energy resources.
