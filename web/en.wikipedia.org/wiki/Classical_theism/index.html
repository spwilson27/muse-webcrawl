Classical theism

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), whose synthesis of Aristotelian metaphysics and Christian doctrine shaped classical theism.

Classical theism is the form of theism in which God is characterised as the singular, absolute, ultimate, omnipotent, omniscient, eternal and personal being, creator and sustainer of all that exists. It is the traditional doctrine of God developed in the patristic and medieval periods and held, with variations, by mainstream Christianity, Judaism and Islam, as well as by Neoplatonism and other philosophical traditions.

Classical theism holds that God possesses a cluster of distinctive metaphysical attributes:

  • Divine simplicity — God has no parts or composition; God's essence and existence are identical, and each divine attribute is identical with God.
  • Immutability — God does not and cannot change.
  • Impassibility — God cannot suffer or be involuntarily affected by creation.
  • Eternity and timelessness — God exists outside time, possessing the whole of life at once (tota simul).
  • Aseity — God exists wholly from and through himself, depending on nothing else.
  • Pure actuality — God is actus purus, with no unrealised potential.
  • Omnipresence, omniscience and omnipotence — God is present everywhere, knows all truths, and can do all that is logically possible.

History

The doctrine was formed by the Church Fathers, notably Augustine of Hippo, drawing on Platonic and Aristotelian philosophy, and received its most systematic statement in the work of Thomas Aquinas, whose Five Ways argue for a first mover and first cause identifiable with God. Parallel formulations appear in Jewish philosophy (Maimonides) and in Islamic kalām and falsafa (Avicenna, Averroes, al-Ghazālī).

Modern discussion

In contemporary philosophy of religion, classical theism has been both defended — by Thomists and others — and criticised. Open theism and process theology reject immutability and timelessness in favour of a God who changes in relation to the world; some analytic philosophers have questioned the coherence of divine simplicity; and "theistic personalists" argue that God is better conceived as a person among persons, albeit the greatest. Classical theists respond that their doctrine alone secures God's ultimacy and distinction from creation.
