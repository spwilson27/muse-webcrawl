Dowry death

A dowry death is the murder or suicide of a married woman caused by a dispute over her dowry — the money, goods or estate that a bride's family gives to the groom's family at marriage. Such deaths occur predominantly in South Asia, particularly India, and often take the form of "bride burning", in which a woman is set alight, typically in a staged kitchen accident.

Prevalence

India's National Crime Records Bureau has recorded roughly 6,000–7,000 dowry deaths per year in recent decades — around 18–20 per day — and the true number is believed to be higher due to underreporting and misclassification as accidents or suicides. Dowry harassment typically escalates when the groom's family demands further payments after the wedding.

Law

Dowry has been illegal in India since the Dowry Prohibition Act of 1961. The Indian Penal Code added section 304B (dowry death) and section 498A (cruelty by husband or relatives) in the 1980s, with special evidentiary presumptions where a woman dies unnaturally within seven years of marriage after dowry harassment; the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act 2005 added civil remedies. Similar laws exist in Pakistan and Bangladesh, though enforcement remains a major problem.
