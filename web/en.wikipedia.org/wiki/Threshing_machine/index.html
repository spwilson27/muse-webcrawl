A threshing machine or thresher is a piece of farm equipment that threshes grain — that is, it removes the seeds from the stalks and husks. It does so by beating the plant to make the seeds fall out. Before such machines were developed, threshing was done by hand with a flail: such hand threshing was very laborious and time-consuming, taking about one-quarter of agricultural labour by the 18th century.

Mechanization of this process removed a substantial amount of drudgery from farm labour. The first threshing machine was invented circa 1786 by the Scottish engineer Andrew Meikle. It was subsequently adopted across the world, transforming agriculture and contributing significantly to the Industrial Revolution's impact on farming.

History

For thousands of years, grain was separated by hand using flails, then later by the treading of animals or by dragging weighted sledges over the harvest. Early attempts at mechanization included pliny the Elder's descriptions of reaping machines in Gaul. Andrew Meikle's design of 1786 used a rotating drum with beaters that rubbed the grain against a concave grate, a principle that became standard.

Through the 19th century, threshing machines grew larger and more powerful, driven first by horse treadmills and portable steam engines, then by steam traction engines and eventually internal combustion tractors. Threshing became a social as well as an agricultural event in many farming communities: large crews would move from farm to farm during the harvest season, and threshing dinners were a notable tradition.

Operation

In a typical thresher, sheaves of cut grain are fed into the machine, where a rapidly rotating beater drum strikes them against a concave surface. The loosened grain falls through grates to be cleaned by a fan and sieves (the winnowing process), while the straw passes out the rear of the machine, often onto an elevator that stacks it. Chaff is blown away by the fan. Later machines incorporated straw balers or blowers to deposit the straw in piles.

Decline

The separate threshing machine was largely displaced in the mid-20th century by the combine harvester, which integrates reaping, threshing and winnowing in a single self-propelled machine. Threshers survive at agricultural shows and in some regions where small-scale farming persists.
