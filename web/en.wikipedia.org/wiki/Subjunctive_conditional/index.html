Subjunctive conditional

Subjunctive conditionals are conditional statements typically expressed with the subjunctive mood, often used to reason about hypothetical or counterfactual situations: for example, "If Oswald had not shot Kennedy, someone else would have." They are contrasted with indicative conditionals such as "If Oswald did not shoot Kennedy, someone else did," which concern the actual world.

Philosophical analysis

Philosophers of language and logicians analyse subjunctive (or counterfactual) conditionals using possible-worlds semantics, most famously in the systems developed by Robert Stalnaker and David Lewis. On Lewis's account, a counterfactual "If A had been, then B would have been" is true when, in the possible worlds most similar to actuality in which A holds, B also holds. Such conditionals are notoriously context-dependent and non-monotonic: strengthening the antecedent can turn a true subjunctive conditional into a false one.

Grammar

English marks subjunctive conditionals with "fake past" tense morphology — past-tense forms used with present or future meaning ("If I were rich...") — a pattern found in many languages, where irrealis or past marking signals distance from actuality.
