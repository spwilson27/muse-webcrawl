Everett massacre

The Everett massacre, also known as Bloody Sunday, was an armed confrontation between local authorities and members of the Industrial Workers of the World (IWW, or "Wobblies") that took place in Everett, Washington, on Sunday, November 5, 1916. It was the deadliest episode in a long-running labour conflict in the Pacific Northwest timber industry and remains one of the most notorious events in American labour history.

Background

Everett's shingle weavers had been on strike in 1916 for the restoration of a wage cut, and the IWW supported the strikers with free-speech fights — public speaking campaigns intended to fill jails and assert the right to organise. Everett's mill owners and the local Commercial Club backed Sheriff Donald McRae, who organised vigilante "citizen deputies" to suppress union activity. On October 30, 1916, deputies met a boatload of Wobblies arriving from Seattle, beat them severely at Beverly Park, and forced them to run a gauntlet — an outrage widely reported in the labour press.

The shooting

In response, the IWW announced a public meeting in Everett on November 5 and chartered the steamers Verona and Calista to bring supporters from Seattle. About 300 Wobblies boarded the Verona, singing labour songs as they approached the Everett dock. Sheriff McRae and some 200 armed citizen deputies waited ashore. When the Verona attempted to dock, McRae demanded to know who was in charge; the reply — "We all are!" — was followed by gunfire. Who fired first has never been settled: each side blamed the other.

The shooting lasted several minutes. At least five Wobblies were killed outright — Felix Baran, Hugo Gerlot, Gustav Johnson, John Looney and Abraham Rabinowitz — and about 27 wounded; more may have drowned or died unrecorded. On the shore, two deputies, Charles O. Curtis and Jefferson Beard, were killed, most likely by fire from their own side, and around 20 deputies and citizens were wounded.

Aftermath

When the Verona returned to Seattle, 74 Wobblies were arrested and charged with the murder of Deputy Beard. The first defendant tried, Thomas Tracy, was acquitted in May 1917 after a defence led by attorney George Vanderveer established that the deputies' deaths were consistent with crossfire; charges against the remaining defendants were then dropped. The massacre galvanised the IWW, but federal repression during the First World War soon crippled the union nationally. The five dead Wobblies are buried at Mount Pleasant Cemetery in Seattle, and the event is commemorated as a landmark in the history of free speech and labour rights.
