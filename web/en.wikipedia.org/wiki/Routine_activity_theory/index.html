Routine activity theory

Routine activity theory is a theory in criminology, proposed by Lawrence E. Cohen and Marcus Felson in 1979, which explains crime as a product of everyday patterns of life rather than of offender motivation alone. It holds that a crime occurs when three elements converge in time and space: a motivated offender, a suitable target, and the absence of a capable guardian.

Development

Cohen and Felson used the theory to explain why crime rates in the United States rose during the post-war economic boom: changes in routine activities — more women working outside the home, more households empty during the day, more lightweight portable goods — dispersed activities away from households and increased criminal opportunity. Marcus Felson later elaborated the framework into a broader "crime and everyday life" approach, and the theory underlies situational crime prevention and the VIVA criteria for target suitability (value, inertia, visibility, access).

Influence

Routine activity theory is one of the foundations of environmental criminology, alongside rational choice theory and crime pattern theory. Critics note that it takes offender motivation as given and can understate structural causes of crime.
