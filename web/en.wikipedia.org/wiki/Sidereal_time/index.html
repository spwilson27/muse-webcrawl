Sidereal time is a system of timekeeping used by astronomers to locate celestial objects. It measures time by the rotation of the Earth with respect to the distant stars rather than the Sun. A sidereal day — the time between two successive transits of the vernal equinox across the local meridian — is about 23 hours, 56 minutes and 4.09 seconds of ordinary (solar) time, roughly four minutes shorter than a solar day.

Because Earth orbits the Sun while rotating, it must turn about one extra degree each day for the Sun to return to the same meridian.

Why sidereal and solar days differ

Over a year, the Earth completes one more rotation relative to the stars than relative to the Sun: there are about 366.24 sidereal days in a year but only 365.24 solar days. The difference arises because Earth's orbital motion shifts the Sun's apparent position eastward against the stars by about one degree per day, so after one full rotation relative to the stars, Earth must rotate a little further before the Sun is again on the meridian.

Uses

Local sidereal time equals the right ascension of objects currently crossing the meridian, which makes it essential for aiming telescopes: an astronomer who knows the sidereal time and a star's coordinates can compute its hour angle directly. Sidereal time is defined at the prime meridian as Greenwich sidereal time (GMST, and GAST when corrected for nutation) and adjusted by longitude for local sidereal time. Historically, observatories kept sidereal clocks regulated by transit observations of stars.
