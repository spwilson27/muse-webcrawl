The Atlantic Wall (German: Atlantikwall) was an extensive system of coastal fortifications built by Nazi Germany between 1942 and 1944 along the western coast of occupied Europe and Scandinavia, from Norway to the border with Spain. Ordered by Führer Directive No. 40 in March 1942, it was intended to deter or repel an anticipated Allied amphibious invasion of Europe.

Construction

The Organisation Todt mobilised hundreds of thousands of workers — including conscripted and forced labour — to pour millions of cubic metres of concrete into bunkers, artillery casemates, radar stations, beach obstacles and anti-tank walls. The strongest defences were concentrated around the Pas-de-Calais, the shortest crossing from Britain, and around major ports designated as "fortresses."

D-Day and after

In 1943–44 Field Marshal Erwin Rommel, appointed to inspect and improve the defences, ordered extensive minefields and beach obstacles. On 6 June 1944 the Allies landed in Normandy, a comparatively weaker sector, and breached the Wall within hours at most beaches — at heavy cost on Omaha Beach. Bypassed fortress ports such as Lorient and Saint-Nazaire held out until the end of the war.

Legacy

Thousands of bunkers and gun batteries survive along the French, Belgian, Dutch, Danish and Norwegian coasts, many preserved as museums and memorials of the Second World War.
