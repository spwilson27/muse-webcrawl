Conversion (word formation)

In linguistics, conversion, also called zero derivation or null derivation, is a kind of word formation involving the creation of a word (of a new part of speech) from an existing word (of a different part of speech) without any change in form, which is to say, derivation using only zero. For example, the noun green in golf (referring to a putting-green) is derived ultimately from the adjective green.

Conversions from adjectives to nouns and vice versa are both very common and unnotable in English; much more remarked upon is the creation of a verb by converting a noun or other word (for example, the noun e-mail giving the verb to e-mail, or the noun Google in the sentence "let me google that").

Verbification

Verbification, or verbing, is the creation of a verb from a noun, adjective, or other word. In English, verbification typically involves simple conversion; examples include the use of access, impact, and host as verbs. Verbification may have a bad reputation with some English users because examples such as prioritize can be regarded as jargon; other coinages, however, became well established.

Other directions

Conversion also produces nouns from verbs, as in a run, a call, a guess, and adjectives from nouns, as when a noun is used attributively. The process is especially productive in English because the language has relatively little inflectional morphology, so changes of category need not be marked overtly.

Debates

Linguists debate whether conversion is a distinct derivational process involving a zero morpheme, or whether words simply belong to multiple categories without derivation. The zero-derivation analysis treats the derived word as having an invisible affix; the alternative treats conversion as a case of flexible lexical categorization.
