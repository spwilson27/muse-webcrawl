The TIM barrel, also known as the alpha/beta barrel, is a conserved protein fold consisting of eight alpha helices and eight parallel beta strands that alternate along the peptide backbone. The beta strands are arranged in a closed cylindrical sheet — the "barrel" — surrounded by the helices, which lie on the outside. The fold is named after triose phosphate isomerase (TIM), the enzyme in which it was first described, in 1975.

Structure

The sequence of secondary structures along the chain is a repeating β–α unit, repeated eight times. The eight β-strands hydrogen-bond to one another to form a closed barrel with a shear that staggers the strands relative to the barrel axis. The α-helices pack against the outer surface of the barrel, and loops connect each strand to the following helix. Loops at the C-terminal ends of the β-strands are typically longer and more variable than those at the N-terminal face; they form the binding pocket and active site in nearly all TIM barrel enzymes, while the N-terminal face tends to be involved in stability.

Function and diversity

The TIM barrel is one of the most common enzyme folds: roughly ten per cent of all enzymes of known structure adopt it, and TIM barrel enzymes catalyse an unusually wide range of reactions — isomerisations, aldol reactions, glycoside hydrolysis, and many others — grouped into numerous superfamilies in classification schemes such as SCOP and CATH. Because the scaffold holds catalytic residues in a similar geometric arrangement at the barrel mouth while the surrounding loops vary freely, the fold is an attractive chassis for protein engineering and de novo enzyme design. Classical examples include triose phosphate isomerase, the alpha-amylases, numerous glycosidases, and the HisA/HisF enzymes of histidine biosynthesis.

Evolution

Many TIM barrels show evidence of an internal two-fold symmetry, suggesting that the modern eight-fold barrel arose by duplication and fusion of a half-barrel ancestor made of four β–α units; some natural proteins still exist as stable half-barrels, and engineered half-barrels can dimerise to form functional barrels. Whether all TIM barrels descend from a single common ancestor or arose convergently remains debated, though the shared active-site location and several conserved superfamilies argue for deep homology among many of them.
