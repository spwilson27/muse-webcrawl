Learned optimism

Learned optimism is the idea, developed within positive psychology, that a talent for joy and a hopeful outlook can be cultivated. The concept was popularised by the American psychologist Martin Seligman, notably in his 1990 book Learned Optimism. It arose as the counterpart to Seligman's earlier work on learned helplessness, in which organisms exposed to uncontrollable aversive events learn to give up even when escape becomes possible.

Explanatory style

The core of the theory is explanatory style — how a person habitually explains the causes of good and bad events along three dimensions:

  • Permanence — optimists treat bad events as temporary and good events as lasting; pessimists do the reverse.
  • Pervasiveness — optimists give specific explanations for setbacks ("this exam went badly") and universal ones for successes; pessimists globalise failure ("I fail at everything").
  • Personalisation — optimists externalise blame for bad events where appropriate, while pessimists internalise it.

Learning optimism

Seligman proposed that pessimistic explanatory styles can be changed using techniques adapted from cognitive therapy, summarised in the "ABCDE" model: recognise the Adversity, the Belief it triggers, and its emotional Consequence; then Dispute the negative belief with evidence and alternatives, and notice the resulting Energisation. School-based programmes derived from this work, such as the Penn Resiliency Program, aim to reduce symptoms of depression in adolescents.

Criticism

Critics caution that optimism is not uniformly beneficial: unrealistic optimism can lead people to underestimate risks, and some degree of "defensive pessimism" can be adaptive. The benefits reported for optimism training are generally modest, and the theory's claims about achievement and health have been debated.
