Tourette syndrome (abbreviated as TS or Tourette's) is a common neurodevelopmental disorder that begins in childhood or adolescence. It is characterised by multiple movement (motor) tics and at least one vocal (phonic) tic. Common tics are blinking, coughing, throat clearing, sniffing, and facial movements. These are typically preceded by an unwanted urge or sensation in the affected muscles, can sometimes be suppressed temporarily, and characteristically change in location, strength, and frequency.

Tourette's is at one end of the spectrum of tic disorders. The tics are often unnoticed by casual observers. Once a rarely recognised condition, Tourette's is no longer considered rare: about 1% of school-age children and adolescents have Tourette's, and coprolalia (the involuntary utterance of obscenities) occurs in only a minority of cases despite its prominent portrayal in popular culture.

Signs and symptoms

Tics are sudden, repetitive, nonrhythmic movements or vocalisations involving discrete muscle groups. Motor tics range from simple movements such as eye blinking, nose wrinkling and shoulder shrugging to complex sequences including touching objects or echopraxia (imitating others' movements). Vocal tics include sniffing, throat clearing, grunting, and, less commonly, words or phrases. Coprolalia, the involuntary swearing often associated with the condition in the media, is present in only about 10% of people with Tourette's.

Most people with Tourette's also experience premonitory urges — uncomfortable sensations that build until the tic is performed, after which there is a feeling of relief. Tics wax and wane in severity and typically reach their worst in the early teenage years, improving for most people by early adulthood.

Causes

The exact cause of Tourette's is unknown, but it is established that genetic and environmental factors are involved. Genetic studies have shown that the large majority of cases are inherited, although the precise mode of inheritance has not been determined and no single gene has been identified. Twin and family studies suggest a substantial heritability. Abnormalities in the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuits of the brain and in neurotransmitters such as dopamine are thought to play a role.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on clinical observation and history. Under the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), Tourette's is diagnosed when a person has had multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic over a period of at least one year, with onset before the age of 18, and the symptoms are not attributable to another medical condition or substance. No blood test, brain scan or other laboratory test can diagnose the disorder. Conditions frequently co-occurring with Tourette's include attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD).

Management

There is no cure for Tourette's and no medication that reliably eliminates all symptoms. Education and reassurance are the foundation of management for mild cases. Behavioural therapy, in particular Comprehensive Behavioral Intervention for Tics (CBIT), which includes habit reversal training, is recommended as a first-line treatment. Medications — including alpha-2 adrenergic agonists such as guanfacine and clonidine, and antipsychotics such as risperidone, aripiprazole and haloperidol — may be used when tics cause substantial impairment. Treatment of co-occurring ADHD and OCD often improves overall functioning.

History

The condition is named after the French neurologist Georges Gilles de la Tourette, who in 1885 published an account of nine patients with the syndrome at the behest of his teacher Jean-Martin Charcot of the Salpêtrière Hospital in Paris. Earlier descriptions include the case of the Marquise de Dampierre, a noblewoman whose vocal tics, including coprolalia, were documented in the nineteenth century. Jean Marc Gaspard Itard had described a similar case in 1825.

Society and culture

People with Tourette's are found at all levels of intellectual ability, and many are high achievers. The condition has been portrayed — often inaccurately, by exaggerating coprolalia — in films and television. Advocacy organisations such as the Tourette Association of America work to raise awareness and reduce stigma.
