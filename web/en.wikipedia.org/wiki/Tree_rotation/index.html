Tree rotation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In discrete mathematics, tree rotation is an operation on a binary tree that changes the structure without interfering with the order of the elements. A tree rotation moves one node up in the tree and one node down. It is used to change the shape of the tree, and in particular to decrease its height by moving smaller subtrees down and larger subtrees up, resulting in improved performance of many tree operations.

Direction and pivots

A rotation may be in one of two directions: left or right. In a right rotation on a node, the node's left child becomes the new root of the subtree, the original node becomes the right child of the new root, and the left child's former right subtree becomes the original node's left subtree. A left rotation is the mirror image of this operation. The node around which the rotation is performed is sometimes called the pivot.

Properties

Rotations preserve the inorder traversal of the tree, which is why they can be used to restructure a binary search tree without violating the binary search property. A single rotation runs in constant time, O(1), since it requires changing only a fixed number of pointers. Pairs of adjacent rotations are related by the associativity-like geometry studied in combinatorics: the set of all binary trees with a given number of nodes, connected by rotations, forms the rotation graph, which is the skeleton of the associahedron.

Use in self-balancing trees

Rotations are the fundamental rebalancing operation in self-balancing binary search trees, including AVL trees, red–black trees, treaps and splay trees. In a splay tree, sequences of rotations (zig, zig-zig and zig-zag steps) bring a node to the root; in AVL and red–black trees, single and double rotations restore balance invariants after insertion and deletion.
