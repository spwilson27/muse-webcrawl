Learning-by-doing (economics)

Learning-by-doing is a concept in economic theory by which productivity is increased through practice, self-perfection, and minor innovations. The idea dates to John R. Hicks and was formalised by Kenneth Arrow in his influential 1962 paper "The Economic Implications of Learning by Doing", which treated experience — indexed by cumulative investment or output — as a factor that raises the productivity of new capital.

Learning curves

The empirical counterpart is the learning curve (or experience curve): unit costs fall by a roughly constant percentage each time cumulative production doubles. The phenomenon was first quantified in airframe manufacture in the 1930s, where direct labour hours per aircraft declined steadily with cumulative output, and was later applied to semiconductors, shipbuilding, solar modules, and many other industries. A common rule of thumb is an 80% learning curve — costs falling to 80% of their previous level per doubling.

Role in growth theory

Learning-by-doing became a central mechanism in endogenous growth theory. Because knowledge gained by one firm may spill over to others, learning can generate economy-wide increasing returns and sustain long-run growth without exogenous technical change. Paul Romer's 1986 model built directly on Arrow's framework, treating knowledge as a partially excludable input produced as a by-product of investment.

Policy implications

If early production generates learning spillovers that firms cannot capture, there may be a case for protecting or subsidising infant industries and for policies supporting technologies — such as renewable energy — whose costs are expected to fall along a learning curve with cumulative deployment. Critics note that learning can plateau, be forgotten, or fail to transfer between organisations and countries.
