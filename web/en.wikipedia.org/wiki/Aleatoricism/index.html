Aleatoricism

Aleatoricism
Chance — symbolised by dice (Latin: alea) — as a compositional tool
EtymologyFrom Latin alea, "die"
Key figuresJohn Cage, Pierre Boulez, Karlheinz Stockhausen, Iannis Xenakis, Witold Lutosławski
Related termsChance music, indeterminacy, stochastic music

Aleatoricism (or aleatorism) is the incorporation of chance into the creation of a work of art, most prominently in music. In aleatoric or chance music, some element of the composition is left to chance, or some primary element of a composed work's realisation is left to the determination of its performers. The term derives from the Latin word alea, meaning "die".

History

Early precedents include the Musikalisches Würfelspiel ("musical dice game") of the 18th century, in which melodies were assembled by rolling dice, and Charles Ives's passages left to performers' discretion. The modern movement began in the early 1950s with John Cage, whose Music of Changes (1951) was composed using the I Ching, and whose 4′33″ (1952) made ambient sound itself the content of the work. In Europe, Pierre Boulez coined "aleatory" in his 1957 essay Aléa, while Karlheinz Stockhausen and others developed controlled forms of performer choice, as in Stockhausen's Klavierstück XI, whose fragments may be played in any order.

Types

Scholars distinguish chance in composition (the composer uses random procedures but fixes the result in the score), indeterminacy in performance (the score specifies structures but leaves details or ordering to the performer), and fully chance-generated performance. Witold Lutosławski developed "limited aleatorism," in which players repeat short passages unsynchronised within conductor-controlled sections. Iannis Xenakis's probabilistic "stochastic music" is a related, though mathematically distinct, approach.

Criticism and influence

Aleatoricism provoked debate about authorship and the role of the composer; Boulez criticised Cage's surrender of control even while adopting mobile forms himself. Chance procedures influenced later experimental music, improvisation practices, and generative art in other media.
