Capital gains tax

TypeDirect tax
Tax baseProfit on disposal of a capital asset
ExamplesStocks, bonds, real estate, businesses

A capital gains tax (CGT) is the tax on profits realized on the sale of a non-inventory asset. The most common capital gains are realized from the sale of stocks, bonds, precious metals, real estate, and property. Not all countries impose a capital gains tax, and most have different rates of taxation for individuals compared to corporations.

Overview

A capital gain arises when an asset is sold for more than its purchase price (its "basis"). Most systems tax gains only when they are realised by a disposal, rather than as assets appreciate on paper. Many jurisdictions distinguish short-term from long-term gains: in the United States, assets held for more than one year qualify for preferential long-term rates, while short-term gains are taxed as ordinary income. Losses on disposals can usually be offset against gains.

By country

United States. Federal long-term capital gains are taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on taxable income, with an additional 3.8% net investment income tax for higher earners. United Kingdom. CGT applies above an annual exempt amount, with rates differing for residential property and other assets, and reliefs such as Private Residence Relief for a main home and Business Asset Disposal Relief. Canada taxes a portion (the inclusion rate) of capital gains as income. Some countries, including Switzerland (for private investors), Singapore, and New Zealand, levy no general capital gains tax.

Economic debate

Supporters argue that taxing gains is necessary for fairness, since gains are economic income and preferential rates mainly benefit the wealthy. Critics contend that capital gains taxes discourage saving and investment, penalise risk-taking, and tax inflationary gains that do not represent real increases in wealth. The "lock-in effect" — investors holding appreciated assets to defer tax — is widely discussed in the policy literature.
