The Iberian Pyrite Belt is a vast geological and mining region in the southwest of the Iberian Peninsula, stretching roughly 250 km from near Seville in Spain westward through Huelva province and into southern Portugal. It is one of the world's largest concentrations of massive sulfide deposits, formed by hydrothermal activity on the sea floor during the Devonian and Carboniferous periods.

Geology

The belt's orebodies are volcanogenic massive sulfide (VMS) deposits hosted in a volcano-sedimentary complex. They consist mainly of pyrite with chalcopyrite, sphalerite, and galena, carrying economically important quantities of copper, zinc, lead, silver, and gold. More than 80 deposits have been documented, with total original reserves estimated in the billions of tonnes of sulfide ore.

Mining history

Mining in the belt dates back some 5,000 years, to the Chalcolithic and Tartessian periods; Phoenicians and Romans extracted silver, copper, and gold. In the nineteenth century, British companies industrialised the district — most famously the Rio Tinto Company at Minas de Riotinto — and the region became a centre of the Spanish labour movement. Major modern mines have included Riotinto, Tharsis, Aznalcóllar, Neves-Corvo in Portugal, and Las Cruces.

Environmental issues

Centuries of mining have left severe acid mine drainage; the Río Tinto ("red river") runs blood-red and highly acidic, an environment studied by astrobiologists as an analogue for Mars. The 1998 Aznalcóllar tailings dam failure released millions of cubic metres of toxic sludge toward Doñana National Park, one of Spain's worst environmental disasters.
