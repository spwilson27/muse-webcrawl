Empire style

Empire style

Period: c. 1800 – 1815 (First French Empire)

Origin: France

Influences: Neoclassicism, ancient Rome, Egypt, Greece

Key designers: Charles Percier, Pierre-François-Léonard Fontaine

Related: Directoire style, Biedermeier, American Empire, Regency

The Empire style (French: style Empire) is an early-19th-century design movement in architecture, furniture, other decorative arts, and fashion, representing the second phase of Neoclassicism. It flourished during the period of Napoleon's First French Empire, from which it takes its name, and spread from France throughout Europe and to the United States.

Characteristics

The Empire style deliberately evoked the grandeur of imperial Rome — and, after Napoleon's Egyptian campaign, of ancient Egypt as well. It favoured massive, severe forms; rich, dark mahogany veneers; lavish gilt-bronze (ormolu) mounts; and a repertory of imperial and military motifs: eagles, laurel wreaths, the Napoleonic bee and the letter "N", stars, sphinxes, swans (associated with Empress Joséphine), torches, and classical figures such as winged victories. Colours were strong and saturated — deep greens, crimson, gold, and royal blue.

Origins and designers

The style developed from the lighter Directoire style of the 1790s and was codified by Napoleon's court architects Charles Percier and Pierre-François-Léonard Fontaine, whose Recueil de décorations intérieures (1801–1812) became its pattern book. They remodelled interiors at Malmaison, Saint-Cloud, and the Tuileries, while firms such as the Jacob brothers produced the style's monumental furniture and Sèvres and Paris porcelain works supplied its ceramics.

Spread and variants

Empire taste was exported wherever French influence reached: it became the court style of Napoleon's satellite kingdoms and allies, strongly influenced Russian architecture (the "Russian Empire style" of St Petersburg), German and Austrian Biedermeier, British Regency, and the American Empire style of furniture. In women's fashion, the same name denotes the high-waisted "Empire silhouette" dress of roughly 1795–1820.

Decline

After Napoleon's fall in 1815 the style softened into the Restoration and Biedermeier manners, but its bold neoclassical vocabulary has been repeatedly revived and remains a byword for imperial grandeur.
