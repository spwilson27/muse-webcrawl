Exploration geophysics

Exploration geophysics is the practical application of physical methods — measuring gravity, magnetism, electrical and electromagnetic properties, and seismic wave propagation — to study the subsurface of the Earth for economic purposes, principally the exploration for petroleum, minerals, and groundwater, and for engineering and environmental investigations. It is a branch of applied geophysics, distinct from the purely scientific study of the Earth's interior.

Methods

The dominant method is reflection seismology, adapted from earthquake seismology and developed commercially from the 1920s. Controlled sources (vibroseis trucks, air guns at sea, or explosives) generate waves that reflect from boundaries between rock layers; arrays of geophones or hydrophones record the echoes, and processing reconstructs images of geological structures that may trap oil and gas. Modern three-dimensional (3D) and time-lapse (4D) seismic surveys are standard in the petroleum industry.

Other methods include gravity surveys (detecting density contrasts, e.g. salt domes), magnetic surveys (mapping magnetic basement and mineral deposits, often flown from aircraft), electrical resistivity and induced polarisation, electromagnetics, magnetotellurics, and ground-penetrating radar for shallow targets. Well logging measures physical properties in boreholes. Interpretation integrates geophysical data with geological knowledge; because inverse problems are inherently non-unique, geophysicists use multiple complementary methods and calibrate against drilling results.

Applications

Exploration geophysics is used by the oil and gas and mining industries, in groundwater and geothermal exploration, in civil engineering site investigation, in environmental remediation, and in archaeology. Professional bodies include the Society of Exploration Geophysicists (SEG), founded in 1930.
