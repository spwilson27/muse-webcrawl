Shaping (psychology)

Shaping
FieldBehaviorism, operant conditioning
Key figureB. F. Skinner

In psychology, shaping is the process of training a learned behaviour by reinforcing successive approximations toward a target behaviour. It is used when the desired behaviour does not occur spontaneously, so the trainer initially reinforces any response that resembles it, then gradually raises the criterion until only the target behaviour earns reinforcement.

Method

Shaping begins by identifying a terminal behaviour and a starting response already in the learner's repertoire. The trainer reinforces closer and closer approximations — for example, in teaching a rat to press a lever, first reinforcing movement toward the lever, then touching it, then pressing. As each step is mastered, reinforcement for earlier approximations is withdrawn (extinction), which produces variability from which the next approximation is selected.

History

The technique was systematised within B. F. Skinner's operant conditioning framework and described in works such as The Behavior of Organisms (1938) and Science and Human Behavior (1953). Skinner demonstrated shaping by hand-training pigeons to perform complex sequences, showing that elaborate behaviour could be built from simple reinforcement contingencies.

Applications

Shaping is fundamental to animal training (including marine mammal and service-dog work), applied behavior analysis for children with developmental disabilities, speech and rehabilitation therapy, and programmed instruction. Clicker training is a popular form of shaping in which a marker signal bridges the delay between behaviour and reward.
