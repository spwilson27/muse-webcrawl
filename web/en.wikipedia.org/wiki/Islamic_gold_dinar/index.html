Islamic gold dinar

The Islamic gold dinar refers to the historic gold coin of the Islamic world and to modern projects to revive a gold-backed currency for use among Muslims. The classical dinar was introduced by the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan in 696–697 CE (77 AH), weighing one mithqal, conventionally 4.25 grams of gold, and bearing Islamic inscriptions instead of images.

Modern revival

In the late 20th century, figures such as the Spanish economist Umar Ibrahim Vadillo and movements like the Murabitun promoted the gold dinar (and silver dirham) as an alternative to fiat currency, arguing that Islamic law prohibits riba (usury) and requires money with intrinsic value. In 2001 Malaysia's prime minister Mahathir Mohamad proposed using a gold dinar for trade settlement between Muslim countries, though the plan was not adopted multilaterally. The Malaysian state of Kelantan issued dinar and dirham coins from 2006, and similar coins have been minted in Indonesia and elsewhere, generally circulating as collectibles or local exchange media rather than legal tender.

In 2015 the militant group Islamic State announced its own gold dinar currency in territory it controlled, a move widely reported but of limited practical circulation.
