Zirconium dioxide (ZrO2), sometimes known as zirconia (not to be confused with zircon, ZrSiO4), is a white crystalline oxide of zirconium. Its most naturally occurring form, with a monoclinic crystalline structure, is the mineral baddeleyite. A cubic zirconia (stabilized) form is used as a diamond simulant and for dental crowns.

Structure

Three crystalline phases are known: monoclinic below about 1,170 °C, tetragonal between 1,170 °C and 2,370 °C, and cubic above 2,370 °C. The transformation between the tetragonal and monoclinic phases on cooling involves a volume expansion of roughly 3–5%, which can crack pure zirconia ceramics. Adding stabilising oxides such as yttria (Y2O3), magnesia (MgO), calcia (CaO) or ceria (Ce2O3) produces stabilized zirconia, which retains the high-temperature phases at room temperature and exhibits exceptional fracture toughness through transformation toughening.

Production

Zirconia is produced commercially by calcining zirconium compounds, exploiting the element's high thermal stability. It is the principal oxide obtained from the mineral zircon, one of the main ores of zirconium.

Uses

  • Ceramics and refractories: its very high melting point, low thermal conductivity and chemical inertness make it valuable for furnace linings, crucibles and thermal barrier coatings on turbine blades.
  • Dentistry and medicine: yttria-stabilized zirconia is used for dental crowns, bridges and implants, and for femoral heads in hip replacements.
  • Jewellery: cubic zirconia is a widely used, inexpensive diamond simulant.
  • Electronics: zirconia is an oxygen-ion conductor at high temperature and is used in lambda (oxygen) sensors and in solid oxide fuel cells, as well as a high-κ dielectric.
  • Knives and tools: "ceramic" knife blades and grinding media are made from partially stabilized zirconia.
