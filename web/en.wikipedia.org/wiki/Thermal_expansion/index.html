Thermal expansion

Thermal expansion is the tendency of matter to change its shape, area, volume and density in response to a change in temperature. Heating increases the kinetic energy and average separation of particles, so most materials expand when heated and contract when cooled; a notable exception is water between 0 °C and 4 °C, which contracts on warming.

Linear coefficientα, in K⁻¹
Linear changeΔL = α·L·ΔT
Volumetric coefficient≈ 3α for isotropic solids

Coefficients

The coefficient of linear thermal expansion α ranges from about 1×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ for Invar and fused silica, through ~12×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ for steel and ~23×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ for aluminium, to much larger values for polymers. For isotropic materials the volumetric coefficient is approximately three times the linear coefficient, and the area coefficient twice it.

Effects and applications

Engineers allow for expansion with gaps in bridges and railway rails, loops in pipelines, and bimetallic strips that bend with temperature and are used in thermostats and thermometers. Unconstrained expansion is harmless, but constrained expansion generates large thermal stresses that can buckle rails or crack structures.

Negative expansion

A few materials, including zirconium tungstate and water in its anomalous range, contract on heating over certain intervals, a property exploited in zero-expansion composites for precision instruments.
