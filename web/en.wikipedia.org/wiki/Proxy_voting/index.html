Proxy voting

Proxy voting is a form of voting whereby a member of a decision-making body may delegate their voting power to a representative, to enable a vote in their absence. The representative is called a proxy, and the person delegating is the principal. Proxy appointments can be used to form a voting bloc that can exercise greater influence, or to ensure quorum is reached.

Corporate governance

Proxy voting is most prominent in corporate governance: shareholders of a company who cannot attend the annual general meeting appoint a proxy — often the board of directors — to vote their shares. In the United States, the process is regulated by the Securities and Exchange Commission; public companies must issue proxy statements and proxy cards soliciting votes on directors, executive pay, auditors and shareholder proposals. Because most shares are held through brokers and institutions, nearly all shareholder voting occurs by proxy. Proxy advisory firms such as ISS and Glass Lewis recommend votes to institutional investors, and contested "proxy fights" occur when dissident shareholders solicit proxies to replace directors.

Political and other uses

Some legislatures allow proxy voting: members of the British House of Commons have been permitted proxy votes for parental absence since 2019, and the U.S. House of Representatives controversially permitted proxy voting during the COVID-19 pandemic from 2020 to 2023. Political parties, unions, and condominium and cooperative associations also use proxies. Proxy voting in general elections exists in some countries (for example, in the United Kingdom voters may appoint a proxy to vote in person on their behalf) but is generally restricted to prevent fraud and coercion.

Criticism

Critics argue that proxies can concentrate power, enable uninstructed ("blanket") delegation, and weaken deliberation, since the proxy holder may not reflect the principal's view on unanticipated questions.
