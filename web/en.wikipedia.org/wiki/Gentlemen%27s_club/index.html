Gentlemen's club

A gentlemen's club is a private social club originally established by men of the upper classes in Britain in the 18th century, centred on London's St James's district, often called "clubland". Such clubs provided members with dining, drinking, lodging, libraries, and a place to conduct business and social life away from home.

History

The clubs evolved from London coffeehouses. Early examples include White's (founded 1693), Brooks's (1764), and Boodle's (1762), which grew out of political and gambling circles around figures such as Charles James Fox. The 19th century saw a proliferation of clubs aligned by profession, university, military service, or politics, including the Reform Club, the Athenaeum, and the Carlton Club.

Characteristics

Membership traditionally required nomination by existing members and election by committee, often by secret ballot ("blackballing"). Clubhouses typically feature a dining room, bar, library, billiards room, and bedrooms, staffed to hotel standards, with strict dress codes and rules of conduct.

Modern era

Many historic clubs have merged, moved, or opened membership to women since the late 20th century. The term "gentlemen's club" is also used, chiefly in North America, as a euphemism for a strip club, a usage distinct from the traditional social club.
