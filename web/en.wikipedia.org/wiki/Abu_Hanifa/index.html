Abu Hanifa al-Nu'man ibn Thabit (c. 699–767 CE) was a Muslim scholar, jurist, and theologian who founded the Hanafi madhhab, the oldest and most widely followed of the four major schools of Sunni Islamic jurisprudence. He is often referred to by the honorific al-Imam al-A'zam ("the Greatest Imam").

Life

Born in Kufa to a family of Persian origin, Abu Hanifa prospered as a silk merchant while pursuing religious studies, chiefly under Hammad ibn Abi Sulayman. Living through the transition from the Umayyad to the Abbasid caliphate, he declined judicial office under both dynasties and was imprisoned by the caliph al-Mansur; he died in Baghdad in 767 while in custody, according to many accounts.

Jurisprudence

Abu Hanifa's method emphasized systematic reasoning: alongside the Quran and Sunnah, he made extensive use of qiyas (analogical reasoning) and istihsan (juristic preference). His students Abu Yusuf and Muhammad al-Shaybani codified his doctrine, and the Hanafi school later became dominant under the Abbasid, Mughal, and Ottoman empires and remains prevalent across Central and South Asia, Turkey, and the Balkans.
