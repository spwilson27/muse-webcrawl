Urban renewal

Post-war renewal districts replaced older neighborhoods wholesale.

Urban renewal (also called urban regeneration or urban redevelopment) is a program of land redevelopment in cities, involving the demolition and rebuilding of districts considered blighted, slum clearance, relocation of residents and businesses, and the construction of new housing, infrastructure, and commercial space. It has been pursued by governments worldwide since the 19th century, from Haussmann's renovation of Paris to post-war programs in the United States, Britain, and beyond.

United States

The Housing Act of 1949 funded large-scale clearance administered by local authorities using eminent domain. Under officials such as Robert Moses in New York, hundreds of thousands of residents — disproportionately Black, leading James Baldwin to call urban renewal "Negro removal" — were displaced, and cohesive neighborhoods were replaced by expressways, civic centers, and housing projects such as Pruitt–Igoe in St. Louis, whose failure and 1972 demolition became a symbol of modernist planning's collapse. Federal urban renewal funding ended in the 1970s.

Criticism and later approaches

Jane Jacobs's The Death and Life of Great American Cities (1961) mounted the classic critique: renewal destroyed the fine-grained social and economic fabric that makes districts safe and prosperous. Later practice shifted toward rehabilitation rather than clearance, historic preservation, community participation, and mixed-income redevelopment (such as the HOPE VI program), though debates continue over gentrification and displacement caused by market-led regeneration.

International

British comprehensive redevelopment replaced Victorian terraces with tower blocks; China has conducted renewal at vast scale, relocating millions; and European programs have emphasized renovation of historic fabric and social housing estates.
