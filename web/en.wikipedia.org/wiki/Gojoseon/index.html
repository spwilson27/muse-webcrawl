Gojoseon

Gojoseon (Korean: 고조선; Hanja: 古朝鮮, "Ancient Joseon") was the first Korean kingdom, located in the north of the Korean Peninsula and southern Manchuria. According to Korean tradition, it was founded by the legendary figure Dangun in 2333 BCE. Modern scholarship generally places its emergence in the first millennium BCE, during the Bronze Age cultures of the Liaoning and northwestern Korean regions. The kingdom fell to the Han dynasty of China in 108 BCE.

Founding legend

The founding myth of Gojoseon is recorded in the Samguk Yusa (13th century) and other Korean texts. Dangun Wanggeom, son of the heavenly prince Hwanung and a bear transformed into a woman, is said to have founded the kingdom with its capital at Asadal. The date 2333 BCE remains symbolically important in Korea, where the founding is commemorated on Gaecheonjeol (National Foundation Day).

Historical development

Chinese sources such as the Guanzi and the Records of the Grand Historian mention Joseon as a polity trading with the Yan state. In the late 3rd century BCE, Wiman (Wei Man), a refugee from Yan, seized the throne and established what historians call Wiman Joseon. Under Wiman's grandson, King Ugeo, Gojoseon obstructed trade between Han China and the peoples of the southern peninsula, provoking an invasion by Emperor Wu of Han in 109 BCE. After a year of fighting, the capital Wanggeom-seong fell in 108 BCE, and the Han established the Four Commanderies, including Lelang, on its territory.

Society and law

Chinese histories record that Gojoseon society was governed in part by the so-called Eight Prohibitions (범금팔조), of which three survive in summary: murder was punished by death; injury required compensation in grain; and theft was punished by enslavement to the victim's household, redeemable by payment. These laws suggest a stratified society with private property and slavery.

Legacy

Gojoseon occupies a foundational place in Korean national historiography as the first Korean state. Its bronze culture — including Liaoning-type daggers, dolmens, and Mumun pottery — is a central subject of Korean archaeology.
