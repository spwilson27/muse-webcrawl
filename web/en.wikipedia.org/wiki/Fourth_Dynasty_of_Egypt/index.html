The Fourth Dynasty of ancient Egypt (notated Dynasty IV) is characterised as a "golden age" of the Old Kingdom. The dynasty lasted roughly from 2613 to 2494 BC and its kings built the most famous monuments of Egyptian civilisation: the pyramids of Giza and the Great Sphinx.

Kings

Sneferu, the dynasty's founder, built the Meidum pyramid, the Bent Pyramid and the Red Pyramid at Dahshur — in total moving more stone than any other pharaoh — and experimented toward the true smooth-sided pyramid. His son Khufu (Cheops) built the Great Pyramid of Giza, long the tallest human-made structure. After the brief reign of Djedefre, Khufu's son Khafre built the second Giza pyramid and is credited with the Great Sphinx; Menkaure built the third and smallest. The dynasty closed with Shepseskaf and possibly short-reigned successors, before the Fifth Dynasty shifted royal burial to Abusir and added sun temples.

Art and administration

The period produced masterworks of sculpture, including the reserved statues of Rahotep and Nofret and the diorite statue of Khafre. A centralised administration, staffed largely by royal relatives as viziers, organised the labour and logistics for pyramid construction, supported by expeditions for turquoise and copper in Sinai and trade with the Levant and Nubia.
