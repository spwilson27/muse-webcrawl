In geometry, inversive geometry is the study of inversion, a transformation of the Euclidean plane that maps circles or lines to other circles or lines and preserves the angles between crossing curves. Intuitively, inversion "swaps" the interior and exterior of a reference circle: a point at distance r from the centre of a circle of radius R is sent to a point on the same ray at distance R2/r.

P ↦ P′,   where   OP · OP′ = R²

Properties

Inversion is its own inverse: applying it twice returns every point to its original position. The centre of the circle of inversion has no image within the plane, and is conventionally treated as mapping to a point at infinity; adjoining that point turns the plane into the one-point compactification, topologically a sphere. Under inversion, a circle not passing through the centre maps to another such circle; a circle passing through the centre maps to a line not passing through the centre, and vice versa. Angles between curves are preserved in magnitude but reversed in orientation, so inversion is an anti-conformal map.

Applications

Inversion is a powerful tool for proving theorems about coaxal circles and tangent configurations, and underlies the Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage (1864), the first exact mechanism for converting rotary into straight-line motion. In three dimensions, inversion in a sphere generalises the construction and connects with Möbius transformations of the Riemann sphere, which in complex analysis take the form of fractional linear transformations. Inversive geometry also models the geometry of the Poincaré disk model of hyperbolic geometry, whose "lines" are circular arcs orthogonal to the boundary circle.

History

Although circle inversion appeared implicitly in earlier work, it was systematically developed in the 19th century by Jakob Steiner, Ludwig Immanuel Magnus and others, and was popularised in complex-variable theory through the work of August Ferdinand Möbius.
