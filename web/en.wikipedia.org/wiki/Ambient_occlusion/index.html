Ambient occlusion

In 3D computer graphics, modelling, and animation, ambient occlusion is a shading and rendering technique used to calculate how exposed each point in a scene is to ambient lighting. The result is a diffuse, non-directional shading effect that casts soft, realistic shadows in crevices, holes, and surfaces that are close to each other.

Principle

Ambient occlusion attempts to approximate the way light radiates in real life, especially off what are normally considered non-reflective surfaces. At each surface point, the technique estimates the fraction of the hemisphere above the point that is occluded by nearby geometry; points in corners and creases receive less ambient light and are rendered darker, while open points remain brighter. Unlike local methods such as Phong shading, ambient occlusion takes surrounding geometry into account and produces a global effect similar to the appearance of an overcast day.

Implementation

Ambient occlusion can be baked into texture maps (often called AO maps) for static scenes, or computed in real time in video games. Real-time methods include screen-space ambient occlusion (SSAO), developed in 2007 and popularised by the game Crysis, its improved variant HBAO, and ray-traced ambient occlusion on modern hardware. In film production, ambient occlusion passes are commonly rendered as separate layers and composited with other lighting passes.

Usage

The technique adds perceived depth and realism, particularly in architectural visualisation, product renders, and games, by grounding objects in their environment. Because it is an approximation rather than a physically accurate simulation of global illumination, it is normally used in combination with other lighting models.
