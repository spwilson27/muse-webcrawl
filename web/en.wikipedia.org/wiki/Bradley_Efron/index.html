Bradley Efron, Max H. Stein Professor of Humanities and Sciences at Stanford.

Bradley Efron (born 24 May 1938) is an American statistician best known for introducing the bootstrap method of resampling, one of the most influential ideas in modern statistics. He has been a professor at Stanford University for most of his career and has held the Max H. Stein endowed chair there.

Biography

Efron was born in St. Paul, Minnesota, the son of Russian Jewish immigrants. He won a National Merit Scholarship to the California Institute of Technology, graduating in mathematics in 1960, and completed his PhD in statistics at Stanford in 1964 under Rupert Miller and Herbert Solomon. He joined the Stanford faculty that year, working in the departments of statistics and biostatistics, and served as dean of the School of Humanities and Sciences.

Work

The bootstrap, proposed in a 1979 paper, estimates the sampling distribution of a statistic by repeatedly resampling from the observed data, replacing error-prone analytic approximations with computer computation. It exemplified a broader shift Efron championed: computer-intensive statistical inference. With Robert Tibshirani he wrote the widely read monograph An Introduction to the Bootstrap (1993). His other contributions include Stein's paradox and the geometry of exponential families, the jackknife-after-bootstrap, Bayesian and frequentist comparisons, empirical Bayes methods, and the local false discovery rate used in large-scale testing such as microarray analysis. He also invented a set of nontransitive dice ("Efron's dice"). Later work, summarised in Computer Age Statistical Inference (2016, with Trevor Hastie), surveys the algorithms of 21st-century statistics.

Honours

Efron has served as president of the American Statistical Association and of the Institute of Mathematical Statistics, and as editor of the Journal of the American Statistical Association. He received the MacArthur Prize Fellowship in 1983, the National Medal of Science in 2005, the Guy Medal in Gold of the Royal Statistical Society in 2014, and the International Prize in Statistics in 2019.
