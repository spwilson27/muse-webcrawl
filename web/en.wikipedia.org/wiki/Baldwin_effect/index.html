Baldwin effect


Plasticity first, genes later: the Baldwin effect in outline.

The Baldwin effect, also known as Baldwinian evolution or ontogenic evolution, is a theory of phenotypic evolution proposed in 1896 by the American psychologist James Mark Baldwin, who proposed a mechanism whereby learned or acquired characteristics could become, over generations, instinctive and eventually innate. The idea was set out in Baldwin's paper "A New Factor in Evolution", published in The American Naturalist.

According to the theory, organisms that can adjust their behaviour or development in response to environmental challenges — through learning, or more generally through phenotypic plasticity — gain a selective advantage. Because they can survive in a new environment long enough for natural selection to act, genetic variants that make the adaptive trait cheaper, faster, or more reliable are favoured. Over time, the trait that was originally learned may come to be produced without learning at all, a process sometimes called genetic assimilation.

History

Baldwin proposed the effect in the same year that similar ideas were put forward independently by Conwy Lloyd Morgan and Henry Fairfield Osborn; the phenomenon is sometimes called the "Baldwin–Lloyd Morgan–Osborn effect". The proposal was intended to reconcile Darwinian natural selection with the apparent inheritance of acquired traits, without invoking Lamarckian inheritance directly: learning does not write information into the genes, but it changes the selective environment so that selection itself can favour genetic variants aligned with the learned trait.

The concept was largely neglected during the early twentieth century but was revived in 1942 by Julian Huxley, and later discussed by Ernst Mayr and George Gaylord Simpson, who coined the term "Baldwin effect" in 1953. Simpson remained sceptical about how often the process occurs in nature.

Relationship to Waddington's work

The Baldwin effect is closely related to Conrad Hal Waddington's experiments on genetic assimilation in the fruit fly Drosophila in the 1950s. Waddington exposed fly pupae to heat shock, producing adults lacking a crossvein in the wing; after generations of selection, the trait appeared even without heat shock. Although often cited together, Waddington's assimilation and Baldwin's effect differ in emphasis: Baldwin stressed learning and behaviour, while Waddington's experiments concerned developmental plasticity generally.

Examples and evidence

  • The evolution of lactose tolerance in human populations with a long history of dairying is sometimes discussed as a Baldwin-like process: a cultural practice (drinking milk into adulthood) altered selection on genes affecting lactase persistence.
  • Computer simulations, such as those of Geoffrey Hinton and Steven Nowlan (1987), demonstrated that learning can smooth a fitness landscape and accelerate genetic evolution toward configurations that would otherwise be hard to find.
  • Studies of spadefoot toad tadpoles and other plastic organisms are used to test "plasticity-first" evolution in the field.

Criticism

Critics note that the Baldwin effect is difficult to distinguish from ordinary selection on standing genetic variation, and that learning itself carries costs. Some biologists, including Simpson, regarded the effect as real but rare. Others argue that plasticity-led evolution is an important and underappreciated route by which novel traits originate.
