Maria Callas

Callas, "La Divina" (1923–1977)

Maria Callas (born Maria Anna Cecilia Sofia Kalogeropoulos; December 2, 1923 – September 16, 1977) was an American-born Greek soprano and one of the most renowned and influential opera singers of the 20th century. Critics praised her bel canto technique, wide-ranging voice and dramatic interpretations, and her musical and dramatic talents led to her being hailed as La Divina ("the Divine one").

Early life and career

Born in Manhattan to Greek immigrant parents, Callas was raised in Greece after her parents separated, and studied at the Athens Conservatoire with Elvira de Hidalgo. She made her professional debut in Athens in 1941. Her breakthrough came in Italy in 1947–1949, when, after debuting at the Verona Arena in La Gioconda, she famously replaced an indisposed colleague in Bellini's I puritani while still singing Wagner's Brünnhilde, demonstrating a range from dramatic Wagnerian roles to coloratura bel canto.

Stardom

In the 1950s Callas revived neglected bel canto operas by Bellini, Donizetti and Rossini, transforming them with unprecedented dramatic intensity, and became the defining Norma, Lucia di Lammermoor, Violetta and Tosca of her generation at La Scala, Covent Garden, the Paris Opéra and the Metropolitan Opera. Her dramatic weight loss in 1954 and increasingly publicised private life made her a celebrity beyond the opera house; a famous feud with Met director Rudolf Bing and a cancelled 1958 Rome Norma damaged her standing.

Later years and legacy

Her voice declined in the early 1960s; her last stage performance was as Tosca in London in 1965. Her relationship with shipping magnate Aristotle Onassis, who left her to marry Jacqueline Kennedy, drew worldwide coverage. She gave master classes at Juilliard and a final concert tour in 1973–74 before dying in Paris in 1977. Her recordings remain best-sellers, and many critics regard her as the greatest singing actress in operatic history.
