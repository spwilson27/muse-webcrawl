Descriptive set theory

In mathematical logic, descriptive set theory (DST) is the study of certain classes of "well-behaved" subsets of the real line and other Polish spaces (complete separable metric spaces). It developed out of the work of the French school (Baire, Borel, Lebesgue) and the Polish school (Sierpiński, Lusin, Suslin) in the early twentieth century. Along with areas such as computability theory and model theory, it forms one of the main branches of modern set theory's interaction with analysis.

The Borel and projective hierarchies

The central objects of the classical theory are the hierarchies of pointclasses:

  • The Borel hierarchy starts with the open sets and closes under complementation and countable unions and intersections, generating sets of increasing complexity indexed by countable ordinals.
  • The projective hierarchy begins with the analytic sets — continuous images of Borel sets, equivalently projections of Borel sets in a product space — and iterates projection and complementation.

Regularity properties

Much of the theory asks which sets of reals are "regular": having the perfect set property (containing a homeomorphic copy of the Cantor set or being countable), being Lebesgue measurable, or having the Baire property. Analytic sets enjoy all three properties, but for higher projective sets the answers can be independent of ZFC and sensitive to large cardinal axioms. Under projective determinacy — a consequence of large cardinals — all projective sets are regular.

Modern developments

Contemporary descriptive set theory includes effective (lightface) theory connecting pointclasses to computability; the study of Borel equivalence relations and their classification complexity; and applications to ergodic theory, operator algebras, and dynamical systems through invariant descriptive set theory.
