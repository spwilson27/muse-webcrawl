Characterization

Characterization is the representation of characters in narrative and dramatic works of art. Through characterization an author conveys a character's personality, motivations, appearance, and history to the audience, either by stating those qualities directly or by showing them through the character's actions, speech, and interactions with others. Together with plot, setting, and theme, characterization is one of the central elements of fiction writing.

Characterization is a core craft of narrative fiction

Direct and indirect characterization

Direct (explicit) characterization occurs when the narrator or another character simply tells the reader what a character is like. Indirect (implicit) characterization requires the reader to infer qualities from what a character does, says, thinks, and looks like, and from how others react to them — often summarized with the acronym STEAL (Speech, Thoughts, Effect on others, Actions, Looks). Indirect characterization is generally considered the more engaging technique because it lets the audience judge the character for itself.

Character types

Critics describe characters as round (complex, many-sided, capable of change) or flat (built around a single quality), following E. M. Forster's distinction in Aspects of the Novel (1927), and as dynamic (developing through the story) or static (remaining essentially unchanged). Stock characters, foils, protagonists, and antagonists are all products of deliberate characterization choices.

In drama and film

In theatre and cinema, characterization is shared between the writer and the performer: casting, costume, gesture, and delivery add information beyond the script. Actors' methods, such as Stanislavski's system, are systematic approaches to building a consistent characterization from the text.
