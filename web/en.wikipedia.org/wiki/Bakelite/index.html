Bakelite (officially polyoxybenzylmethylenglycolanhydride) is a thermosetting phenol-formaldehyde resin formed by the condensation of phenol with formaldehyde. Invented by Belgian-born chemist Leo Baekeland in Yonkers, New York, in 1907 and patented in 1909, it was the first plastic made entirely from synthetic components and launched the modern plastics industry. Baekeland founded the General Bakelite Company in 1910 to commercialize the material, billed as "the material of a thousand uses".

Properties and manufacture

Bakelite is produced by reacting phenol and formaldehyde under heat and pressure, usually with a filler such as wood flour. Once cured, the cross-linked polymer cannot be remelted or reshaped, which distinguishes thermosets from thermoplastics. It is hard, resistant to heat, water, solvents, and electricity, and can be machined and moulded into precise shapes.

Applications

Bakelite's insulating and heat-resistant qualities made it ideal for the burgeoning electrical and automotive industries: distributor caps, switches, sockets, and radio cabinets. It became ubiquitous in consumer goods of the 1920s–1940s, including telephones, radios, cameras, billiard balls, jewellery, and kitchenware. Its dark brown or black marbled appearance is emblematic of Art Deco industrial design.

Legacy

From the 1940s onward, cheaper and more colourful thermoplastics displaced Bakelite in many consumer products, but phenolic resins remain in industrial use for brake pads, adhesives, laminates, and electrical components. Vintage Bakelite objects, especially jewellery, are collectible. In 1993 the American Chemical Society designated Bakelite a National Historic Chemical Landmark.
