Patriotism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Patriotism is the feeling of love for, and devotion and sense of attachment to, one's country. It is commonly distinguished from nationalism: patriotism is pride in and loyalty to one's own country, while nationalism adds the belief that one's country is superior to others or should be politically independent and assertive — though the two terms overlap in ordinary usage, and scholars dispute how clean the distinction is.

The word derives from the Greek patriōtēs (fellow countryman) and Latin patria (fatherland). In the eighteenth century, “patriot” was an opposition term for defenders of liberty against corrupt government — the Patriot Whigs, and the American “Patriots” of the Revolution — before acquiring its modern sense of general national loyalty. Samuel Johnson's oft-quoted 1775 remark that patriotism is “the last refuge of the scoundrel” was aimed, his biographer insisted, at false patriotism used as a cloak for self-interest.

Forms and debate

Political theorists distinguish blind from constructive patriotism (loyalty compatible with criticism), and constitutional patriotism — attachment to democratic principles rather than ethnic identity — proposed by Jürgen Habermas for post-war Germany and the European Union. Patriotism is expressed in symbols and rituals: flags, anthems, national holidays and military service. Philosophers debate whether special loyalty to compatriots can be justified impartially, cosmopolitans arguing that moral concern should be universal.
