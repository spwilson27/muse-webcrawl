Electrorefining

Electrolytic refining cells at a copper refinery

In metallurgy, electrorefining, also called electrolytic refining, is the purification of a metal by means of an electrolytic cell. It is a form of electrowinning used to produce metals of very high purity, such as the copper used for electrical wiring.

Process

In electrorefining, a block of the impure metal is made the anode of the cell and a thin sheet of the pure metal (or a suitable substrate such as stainless steel or titanium) is made the cathode. The electrolyte is a solution of a salt of the metal, for example copper(II) sulfate acidified with sulfuric acid in copper refining. When current passes, metal dissolves from the anode and deposits in pure form on the cathode.

Impurities behave in two ways. Metals less reactive than the refined metal, such as gold, silver and platinum, do not dissolve; they fall to the bottom of the cell as a valuable residue called anode slime or anode mud. More reactive impurities dissolve but are not deposited under the cell conditions, and accumulate in the electrolyte, which must be periodically purified.

Applications

Electrorefining is the standard final step in the production of copper, and is also used for nickel, lead, tin, silver, gold and several other metals. Electrolytic copper is typically refined to 99.99% purity or better. The anode slimes from copper refining are an important source of silver, gold and selenium.

History

The electrolytic refining of copper was patented by James Elkington in 1865 and first operated industrially in 1869 at Pembrey in Wales. The process made possible the large-scale production of the high-conductivity copper demanded by the growing electrical industry.
