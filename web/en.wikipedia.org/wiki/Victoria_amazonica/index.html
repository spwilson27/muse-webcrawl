Victoria amazonica is a species of flowering plant in the water lily family Nymphaeaceae, native to the shallow waters of the Amazon River basin. It is famous for its enormous floating leaves, among the largest of any plant, which can reach up to about 3 metres (10 feet) in diameter.

Description

The leaves float on the water surface, supported by a network of air-filled ribs on the underside that gives them remarkable buoyancy; a healthy leaf can support a considerable evenly distributed weight. The leaf margins are upturned, forming a rim several centimetres high, and the undersides are armed with sharp spines that deter herbivorous fish and manatees.

The flowers are large, fragrant and short-lived. Each bloom opens white on the first night, attracting scarab beetles that become trapped inside as the flower closes; on the second night the flower reopens, having turned pink, and releases the pollen-covered beetles to fertilise other plants.

Taxonomy and history

The species was named in honour of Queen Victoria. It was described scientifically in the 1830s–1840s from specimens collected in British Guiana and the Amazon. Its first successful flowering in cultivation, at Chatsworth House in England in 1849 under gardener Joseph Paxton, was a celebrated horticultural event; the leaf's ribbed structure is said to have inspired Paxton's design for the Crystal Palace.

Cultivation

Victoria amazonica is grown as an ornamental in heated pools at botanical gardens worldwide. It is the national flower of Guyana and appears on that country's coat of arms.
