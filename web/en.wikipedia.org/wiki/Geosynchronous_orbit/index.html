Geosynchronous orbit

A geosynchronous orbit (GSO) is an Earth-centred orbit with an orbital period equal to Earth's rotation period — one sidereal day, about 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds. A satellite in such an orbit returns to the same position in the sky after each sidereal day. By Kepler's third law, this requires a semi-major axis of 42,164 km from Earth's centre, corresponding to an altitude of approximately 35,786 km above mean sea level.

Geostationary orbit

A circular geosynchronous orbit lying in the plane of Earth's equator is a geostationary orbit (GEO): the satellite appears motionless in the sky, making it ideal for communications and weather satellites, whose ground antennas can remain fixed. Science-fiction writer Arthur C. Clarke popularised the concept in 1945, and the geostationary belt is sometimes called the Clarke Belt. The first geosynchronous satellite was Syncom 2 (1963); Syncom 3 (1964) was the first in geostationary orbit.

Other geosynchronous orbits

Inclined geosynchronous orbits trace a figure-eight (analemma) ground pattern over the day. Eccentric variants include the highly elliptical Tundra orbit, used by Sirius satellite radio, which dwells over high northern latitudes. At end of life, GEO satellites are typically boosted into a higher "graveyard orbit" to clear the operational belt. Geosynchronous slots are regulated by the International Telecommunication Union because the equatorial belt is a limited natural resource.
