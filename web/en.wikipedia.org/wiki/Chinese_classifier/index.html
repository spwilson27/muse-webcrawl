Chinese classifier

Common Chinese measure words

In Chinese grammar, a classifier (量词, liàngcí), also called a measure word or counter, is a word or morpheme that must accompany a numeral (and often a demonstrative) when quantifying a noun. Whereas English says "three books," Mandarin requires the pattern numeral + classifier + noun: 三本书 (sān běn shū, "three [bound-volume] books").

Types

Classifiers are divided into nominal classifiers, which categorize the noun itself, and verbal classifiers, which count actions. Nominal classifiers often reflect properties of the referent such as shape, size, animacy, or function: 本 běn for bound volumes, 张 zhāng for flat objects like paper and tables, 条 tiáo for long thin objects, 只 zhī for animals and one of a pair, and 位 wèi as a polite classifier for people. The most general classifier in modern Mandarin is 个 gè, which can substitute for many specific classifiers in colloquial speech.

History and variation

Classifiers developed gradually in Chinese, becoming pervasive by the medieval period. Different Sinitic languages, and different registers of Mandarin, use somewhat different classifier inventories; Cantonese, for example, uses classifiers more extensively and permits "bare classifier" constructions in which the classifier alone with a noun has a definite meaning. Related systems of counters exist throughout East and Southeast Asia, including in Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, and Thai.
