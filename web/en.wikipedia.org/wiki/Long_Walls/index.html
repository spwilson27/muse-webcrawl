Long Walls

Long Walls
LocationAthens to Piraeus and Phalerum, Attica, Greece
Builtc. 461–456 BC
LengthApproximately 6 km (to Piraeus)

The Long Walls (Ancient Greek: τὰ μακρὰ τείχη) were defensive walls built in ancient Athens connecting the city to its ports at Piraeus and Phalerum. They ensured that Athens could be supplied by sea even when besieged by land, and were a cornerstone of the city's strategy during the Peloponnesian War.

Construction

The first phase of construction began around 461 BC, in the aftermath of the Greco-Persian Wars and during Athens' growing rivalry with Sparta. Two walls were built: one running to Piraeus, the principal naval harbour, and one to the older port of Phalerum. A third, middle wall, parallel to the Piraeus wall, was added around 445 BC, creating a protected corridor between the two.

Together with the circuit walls of Athens and Piraeus, the Long Walls enclosed a defensible system within which the entire population of Attica could shelter. Pericles' strategy in the Peloponnesian War depended on this: the Athenians abandoned the countryside to Spartan invasion, withdrew behind the walls, and relied on their navy and imported supplies.

Destruction and rebuilding

Following Athens' defeat in 404 BC, the peace terms imposed by Sparta required the demolition of the Long Walls and the walls of Piraeus. According to Xenophon, they were torn down to the music of flute-girls amid great celebration by Athens' enemies. The walls were rebuilt a decade later, around 393 BC, with Persian money and under the admiral Conon, after his victory at the Battle of Cnidus.

The walls were again demolished after the Battle of Chaeronea (338 BC) era arrangements lapsed, and Sulla destroyed them in 86 BC during his sack of Athens. By the Roman period the fortifications had fallen into ruin, though traces of their foundations remain visible today.

Legacy

The Long Walls symbolised Athenian dependence on sea power and empire. Similar defensive corridors were built by other Greek cities, including Megara (whose long walls to Nisaea were built with Athenian help) and Argos.
