The Banu Nadir (Arabic: بنو النضير) were one of the three major Jewish tribes that lived in the oasis of Yathrib, later known as Medina, in the Hejaz region of Arabia in the 7th century CE. Wealthy from date cultivation and trade, they occupied fortified settlements south of the town and were, for a time, parties to the agreements collectively known as the Constitution of Medina.

Conflict and expulsion

Relations between the Banu Nadir and the early Muslim community under Muhammad deteriorated after the Battle of Badr and the killing of the poet Ka'b ibn al-Ashraf. In 625 CE, following an alleged plot against Muhammad's life, Muslim forces besieged the Banu Nadir's strongholds. After a siege of about two weeks, during which their date palms were cut down — an episode referred to in the Qur'an's Sura al-Hashr — the tribe surrendered and was expelled from Medina.

Aftermath

Most of the Banu Nadir resettled at the oasis of Khaybar, and some continued to Syria. From Khaybar, leaders such as Huyayy ibn Akhtab supported the Meccan coalition at the Battle of the Trench (627). Khaybar itself was conquered by Muslim forces in 628.
