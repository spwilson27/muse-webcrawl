Elaiosome

An elaiosome (Greek élaion "oil" + sóma "body") is a fleshy structure rich in lipids and proteins that is attached to the seeds of many plant species. The elaiosome attracts ants, which take the seed to their nest, feed the elaiosome to their larvae, and then dispose of the seed in an underground waste chamber or outside the nest. This seed dispersal strategy, in which ants act as dispersers, is called myrmecochory.

Function

Elaiosomes develop from various parts of the seed or fruit, including the seed coat, the funiculus, or the fruit wall, depending on the species. They are typically rich in fatty acids, amino acids and sugars, making them a nutritious food reward for ants comparable in attractiveness to insect prey. The seeds themselves usually remain unharmed: after the elaiosome is eaten, the intact seed is discarded in the ant nest's refuse area, an environment rich in nutrients and protected from predators and fire, where it may germinate.

Distribution

Elaiosomes occur in more than 11,000 plant species worldwide, in families such as the Euphorbiaceae, Violaceae, Boraginaceae and Ranunculaceae. Well-known examples include violets (Viola), bloodroot (Sanguinaria canadensis), trilliums and many spring ephemerals of temperate forests, as well as numerous Australian shrubs adapted to nutrient-poor soils.
