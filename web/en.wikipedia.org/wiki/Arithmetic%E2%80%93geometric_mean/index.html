Arithmetic–geometric mean

In mathematics, the arithmetic–geometric mean of two positive real numbers x and y is defined as follows: first compute the arithmetic mean of x and y, then compute the geometric mean of x and y; these become the new pair. Repeating this process produces two sequences that converge rapidly to a common limit, which is the arithmetic–geometric mean of x and y. It is denoted by agm(x, y), or sometimes M(x, y).

Example

To find the arithmetic–geometric mean of a0 = 24 and g0 = 6, iterate: a1 = 15, g1 = 12; then a2 = 13.5, g2 ≈ 13.4164; the sequences quickly converge to about 13.458.

History

The arithmetic–geometric mean was studied independently by Adrien-Marie Legendre and Carl Friedrich Gauss in the late 18th and early 19th centuries. Gauss used it in connection with elliptic integrals and the lemniscate; the first complete elliptic integral can be expressed in terms of the agm.

Properties and applications

The arithmetic–geometric mean satisfies min(x,y) ≤ ... ≤ agm(x,y) ≤ (x+y)/2, and is homogeneous: agm(tx, ty) = t·agm(x, y) for positive t. It underlies fast algorithms for computing π, such as the Brent–Salamin algorithm, and provides efficient evaluations of elliptic integrals and the arithmetic–geometric mean appears in the theory of modular equations.

Related concepts

The geometric–harmonic mean and the arithmetic–harmonic mean are defined by analogous iterations. The reciprocal of the arithmetic–geometric mean of 1 and √2 is Gauss's constant, approximately 0.8346268.
