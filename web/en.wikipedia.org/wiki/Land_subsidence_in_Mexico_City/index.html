Land subsidence in Mexico City

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Overview
Location:Mexico City, Mexico
Cause:Groundwater extraction from beneath former lakebed clays
Total subsidence:up to about 9–10 m in the historic centre since the early 1900s
Peak rates:up to ~50 cm/year (mid-20th century); still tens of cm/year in some eastern districts

Mexico City has sunk dramatically over the past century as a result of land subsidence caused by the extraction of groundwater from the aquifer beneath the city. The city is built on the soft, water-saturated clays of the former Lake Texcoco basin in the Valley of Mexico. As water is pumped out to supply the metropolitan area's more than 20 million inhabitants, the clay layer compacts irreversibly, and the ground surface descends — unevenly, because the thickness of the clay varies across the ancient lakebed.

History and effects

Subsidence was documented from the late 19th century and reached extreme rates of up to about 40–50 cm per year in the 1940s and 1950s. The historic centre has subsided by roughly 9 to 10 metres in total. Famous structures such as the Metropolitan Cathedral and the Palacio de Bellas Artes have sunk and tilted, and the Angel of Independence monument has periodically required additional steps at its base as the street around it descends. Differential subsidence cracks buildings, breaks water and drainage pipes — worsening water losses in a city already short of water — and has left much of the city below the level of its drainage outlets, increasing flood risk and forcing reliance on pumping.

Response

Pumping restrictions within the city proper from the 1950s slowed subsidence in the centre, but extraction shifted to surrounding areas of the basin, and fast subsidence continues in outer districts such as Chalco and Iztapalapa. Mexico City increasingly imports water from the Cutzamala and Lerma systems, itself contributing to subsidence and environmental strain in those source regions. Satellite radar (InSAR) monitoring now maps the deformation in detail; studies continue to warn that the compaction of the clay is essentially irreversible.
