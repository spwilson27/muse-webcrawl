Ferret

Ferret
Scientific name: Mustela furo
Family: Mustelidae
Domesticated from: European polecat
Lifespan: 7–10 years
Diet: Obligate carnivore

The ferret (Mustela furo) is a small domesticated species of mustelid, descended from the European polecat (Mustela putorius). Ferrets have a typically long and slender body, averaging about 50 cm in length including the tail, and weighing between 0.7 and 2.0 kg. Their fur comes in several colourations, including albino, sable, silver, and cinnamon.

History

Ferrets were domesticated at least 2,000 years ago, probably in Europe, most likely for hunting rabbits and rodents — a practice known as ferreting. The Roman writers Pliny and Strabo mentioned rabbit-hunting animals consistent with ferrets. Albino ferrets were favoured historically because they were easier to see in burrows. Ferrets reached North America in the 17th century and were for a time used to protect grain stores from rodents.

Biology

Ferrets are crepuscular, spending much of the day asleep — often in deep sleep phases of up to 18 hours — and are active mainly at dawn and dusk. They are obligate carnivores: their short digestive tract lacks a caecum, so they require a diet high in animal protein and fat and cannot thrive on plant matter. Intact females are induced ovulators and can develop fatal oestrogen toxicity (aplastic anaemia) if they remain in heat without mating. When excited, ferrets perform a hopping sideways dance known as the "weasel war dance".

As pets

Ferrets are popular companion animals, valued for their playful and curious behaviour, but they require supervision, a ferret-proofed environment, and regular veterinary care. They are illegal to keep in some jurisdictions, including California and Hawaii in the United States and Queensland in Australia, largely over concerns about escaped animals establishing feral populations. In New Zealand, hybrid ferret–polecats released in the 19th century became a serious threat to native birds.

Uses in research

Ferrets are important model organisms in biomedical research, especially for influenza and other respiratory viruses, because they are susceptible to human influenza and show similar symptoms, including sneezing. They have also been used in studies of cystic fibrosis and, more recently, in vaccine and virology research.
