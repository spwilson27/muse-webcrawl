Climate change in the United States

Climate change in the United States has led to the country warming by 2.6 °F (1.4 °C) since 1970. The climate of the United States is shifting in ways that are widespread and varied between regions. From 2010 to 2019, the United States experienced its hottest decade on record. Extreme weather events, invasive species, and other changes are already being seen and recorded.

The United States has emitted more greenhouse gases cumulatively than any other country, although China is currently the largest annual emitter. Global warming has led to more frequent and intense storms, droughts, floods, and heat waves across the country. These effects have implications for agriculture, water supplies, human health, and ecosystems.

Greenhouse gas emissions

According to the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), total U.S. greenhouse gas emissions were about 6,340 million metric tons of CO2 equivalent in 2022. The largest sources are fossil fuel combustion for transportation and electricity generation, followed by industry, agriculture, and commercial and residential buildings. Carbon dioxide accounts for roughly 79 percent of total emissions, with methane making up most of the remainder.

Impacts

Temperature and precipitation

Average temperatures have risen across nearly all regions, with Alaska warming roughly twice as fast as the global average. Heavy precipitation events have increased in intensity and frequency, particularly in the Northeast and Midwest, while the Southwest has experienced prolonged drought and declining snowpack.

Sea level rise

Sea levels along U.S. coastlines have risen by roughly 10–12 inches since 1920 in many locations, contributing to increased tidal flooding in cities such as Miami, Norfolk, and Charleston. Projections suggest a further rise of 10–12 inches by 2050 relative to 2020 in many areas.

Extreme weather

Heat waves have become more frequent and intense. The country has also seen an increase in billion-dollar weather and climate disasters; the average number of such events per year has risen markedly since the 1980s.

Mitigation and policy

U.S. climate policy has historically been divided. The country signed but never ratified the Kyoto Protocol, joined the Paris Agreement in 2016, withdrew in 2020, and rejoined in 2021. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 committed roughly $369 billion to climate and clean energy measures, the largest such investment in U.S. history. Many states, including California and those in the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative, operate their own emissions targets and cap-and-trade programs.

Public opinion

Surveys show a majority of Americans accept that global warming is occurring, though views on its causes and the urgency of action remain divided along partisan lines.
