Electrical resistivity and conductivity

Electrical resistivity (also called volume resistivity or specific electrical resistance) is a fundamental property of a material that measures how strongly it resists electric current. A low resistivity indicates a material that readily allows current flow. Resistivity is commonly represented by the Greek letter ρ (rho); its SI unit is the ohm-metre (Ω·m). Electrical conductivity, symbol σ, is the reciprocal of resistivity and is measured in siemens per metre (S/m).

Definition

For a uniform specimen, resistance R, resistivity ρ, length l and cross-sectional area A are related by R = ρ l / A. More generally, resistivity links the electric field E and current density J by E = ρ J, the local form of Ohm's law.

Values and temperature dependence

Silver has the lowest resistivity of any metal at room temperature (about 1.59 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m), followed closely by copper (≈1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m), the standard for wiring. Insulators such as glass and rubber have resistivities many orders of magnitude higher. In metals resistivity generally rises with temperature as lattice vibrations scatter electrons; in semiconductors it falls as temperature frees more charge carriers. Below a critical temperature, superconductors exhibit exactly zero resistivity — a phenomenon explained by the quantum theory summarised at quantum mechanics. The classic law governing the electrostatic force between charges is Coulomb's law.
