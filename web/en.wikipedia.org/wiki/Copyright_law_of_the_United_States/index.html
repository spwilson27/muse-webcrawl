Copyright law of the United States grants authors of original works a set of exclusive rights for a limited time, as authorised by the Copyright Clause of the Constitution (Article I, Section 8, Clause 8) "to promote the Progress of Science and useful Arts". The current federal statute is the Copyright Act of 1976, codified primarily in Title 17 of the United States Code, administered by the US Copyright Office within the Library of Congress.

The US Copyright Office, part of the Library of Congress, administers registration.

Subject matter and rights

Protection subsists in original works of authorship fixed in a tangible medium — literary, musical, dramatic, pictorial, graphic, sculptural, audiovisual, sound recordings, and architectural works — but not in ideas, facts, procedures, or short phrases. Section 106 grants exclusive rights to reproduce, prepare derivative works, distribute, publicly perform, publicly display, and (for sound recordings) perform by digital audio transmission. Copyright arises automatically on fixation; registration is optional but is generally required before suit for US works and affects available remedies.

Duration

Following the Sonny Bono Copyright Term Extension Act of 1998, works created on or after 1 January 1978 are protected for the author's life plus 70 years; anonymous, pseudonymous, and works made for hire endure 95 years from publication or 120 years from creation, whichever is shorter. Works published before 1923 are in the public domain, and pre-1978 works follow the 1909 Act's system of initial and renewal terms, up to 95 years in total.

Limitations

The fair use doctrine (17 USC §107) permits uses based on purpose and character, nature of the work, amount used, and market effect — developed in cases such as Campbell v. Acuff-Rose (1994) and Google LLC v. Oracle America (2021). Other limitations include the first-sale doctrine, library and educational exemptions, compulsory licences, and the DMCA's safe harbours and anti-circumvention rules (1998).

History

The first federal Copyright Act of 1790 covered books, maps, and charts for 14 years plus a 14-year renewal. Successive Acts (1831, 1870, 1909, 1976) expanded subject matter and duration; the US joined the Berne Convention in 1989, dropping formalities requirements.
