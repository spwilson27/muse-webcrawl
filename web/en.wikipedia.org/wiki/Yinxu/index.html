Yinxu

Location: Near Anyang, Henan, China
Period: Late Shang dynasty, c. 1250–1046 BC
Discovered: 1899 (oracle bones); excavations from 1928
Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site (2006)

Yinxu (Chinese: 殷墟; "Ruins of Yin") is the site of Yin, the last capital of the Shang dynasty of ancient China, located near modern Anyang in Henan province. Occupied for roughly 255 years by twelve Shang kings beginning with Pan Geng, it is one of the oldest and largest archaeological sites in China and the source of the earliest known body of Chinese writing.

Discovery

The site came to scholarly attention in 1899 when the scholar Wang Yirong recognised ancient script on inscribed "dragon bones" sold as medicine. The inscriptions were traced to Xiaotun village near Anyang, and the Academia Sinica began systematic excavations in 1928 under Li Ji, often called the father of modern Chinese archaeology. The discovery of the oracle bone script dramatically confirmed the historicity of the Shang dynasty, previously known only from much later texts.

Oracle bones

More than 150,000 inscribed fragments have been recovered, principally turtle plastrons and ox scapulae used in royal divination. The diviners applied heat to produce cracks, which the king interpreted, and the question, forecast, and outcome were carved into the bone. The inscriptions record royal genealogy matching the king lists in Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, and they document warfare, agriculture, weather, illness, and ritual.

The royal tombs and Fu Hao

The royal cemetery at Xibeigang contains huge cruciform shaft tombs, most looted in antiquity. In 1976 excavators found the intact tomb of Fu Hao, a consort and military commander of King Wu Ding, containing more than 1,900 artefacts, including 468 bronzes and 755 jades — among them the famous Houmuwu ding stands at the capital complex, the largest bronze vessel of the ancient world. The site was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2006.
