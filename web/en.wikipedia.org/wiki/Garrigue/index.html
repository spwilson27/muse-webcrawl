Garrigue

Garrigue is a type of low scrubland vegetation found on limestone soils in southern France and other areas around the Mediterranean Basin. It is a secondary vegetation type, generally the result of the degradation of the original oak forest (maquis) through grazing and fire. The term comes from the Provençal word garriga, derived from a root meaning "rock" or stony ground.

Characteristics

Garrigue consists of scattered, drought-resistant shrubs typically less than one metre tall, interspersed with bare stony ground. Characteristic plants include kermes oak, rosemary, wild thyme, lavender, rock roses (Cistus), juniper, and various aromatic herbs of the mint family. Many of the plants are strongly scented, and the landscape is celebrated for the fragrance of its herbs, especially in spring.

Distribution and ecology

Garrigue is typical of the French regions of Languedoc and Provence, and analogous formations occur elsewhere in the Mediterranean: the phrygana of Greece, the tomillares of Spain, and the batha of the Levant. The habitat supports a distinctive fauna, including many insects, reptiles, and birds, and is rich in orchids and other flowering plants in spring.

Human use

Traditionally, garrigue was used for rough grazing of sheep and goats, charcoal production, and the gathering of wild herbs. In wine terminology, "garrigue" is also used as a tasting note describing the herbal, resinous aromas found in wines from southern France.
