Garment industry in Bangladesh

The garment industry in Bangladesh — the ready-made garment (RMG) sector — is the backbone of the country's economy and the world's second-largest apparel exporter after China. Clothing and textiles account for roughly 80 per cent or more of Bangladesh's export earnings, and the sector employs around four million workers, a majority of them women.

History

The modern industry began in the late 1970s, when entrepreneurs such as Noorul Quader founded early export factories and Korean firm Daewoo trained Bangladeshi workers in garment production. Growth accelerated under the Multi-Fibre Arrangement quota system, which steered orders to low-cost producers. After quotas ended in 2005, Bangladesh's low wages and large workforce allowed it to expand its share of the world market, supplying major Western brands and retailers.

Working conditions and safety

The industry has long been criticised for low wages, long hours and unsafe buildings. The 2012 Tazreen Fashions fire and, above all, the collapse of the Rana Plaza factory building in Savar on 24 April 2013 — which killed more than 1,100 workers and injured over 2,500 — became the deadliest disaster in garment-industry history. The catastrophe prompted the creation of the Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh and the Alliance for Bangladesh Worker Safety, programmes of factory inspection and remediation backed by international brands.

Economy

Export earnings from garments have exceeded US$40 billion in peak years. The sector is represented by the Bangladesh Garment Manufacturers and Exporters Association (BGMEA) and its knitwear counterpart BKMEA. Wages are set by a government minimum wage board; periodic reviews have repeatedly sparked protests by workers demanding higher pay. The industry faces competition from Vietnam, Ethiopia and other producers, and pressure to move into higher-value products, man-made fibres and greener production; Bangladesh hosts many of the world's top-rated LEED-certified green garment factories.
