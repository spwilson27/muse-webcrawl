Praying town

Praying towns were settlements established during the 17th century in New England by English colonists for Native Americans who had converted to Christianity, known as "Praying Indians". The towns were organised under the direction of the Puritan missionary John Eliot, who began preaching to the Massachusett people in their own language in the 1640s and translated the Bible into Massachusett (published 1663).

History

The first praying town, Natick, was founded in 1651 on the Charles River, followed by others including Punkapog, Hassanamesit, Okommakamesit, Wamesit, Nashobah and Magunkaquog. By 1675 there were fourteen praying towns in the Massachusetts Bay Colony. Residents were expected to adopt English dress, agriculture, governance and religious observance, living under codes that combined biblical law with local self-government by Native officials. The towns were intended both as instruments of conversion and as a buffer between English settlements and unconverted Native peoples.

King Philip's War and aftermath

During King Philip's War (1675–1676), the Praying Indians found themselves distrusted by both sides. The colonial government interned hundreds of them on Deer Island in Boston Harbor, where many died of exposure and disease, despite the loyalty of many as scouts for the English. After the war the praying town system collapsed; surviving communities retained small landholdings at places such as Natick, Punkapog (Canton), and Hassanamesit (Grafton), whose descendants include the present-day Nipmuc Nation.
