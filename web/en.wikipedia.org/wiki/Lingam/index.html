Lingam

A lingam (Sanskrit: लिङ्गम्, IAST: liṅga, lit. "sign, symbol or mark"), sometimes referred to as linga or Shiva linga, is an abstract or aniconic representation of the Hindu god Shiva in Shaivism. The lingam is the primary object of worship in Shaiva temples and shrines, typically a smooth cylindrical or rounded stone set in a circular base, the yoni, which represents the goddess Shakti. Together, the lingam and yoni symbolise the union of the masculine and feminine principles, creation, and the totality of existence. The word liṅga in Sanskrit texts has many meanings — sign, mark, characteristic, gender, and the subtle body (liṅga śarīra) — and interpretations of the symbol range from the purely aniconic "pillar of cosmic fire" to phallic readings debated since the colonial era.

Forms and worship

Temple lingams are generally carved in three parts: the square Brahmā portion buried in the pedestal, the octagonal Viṣṇu portion within the yoni, and the visible rounded Rudra portion that receives offerings. Worship (puja) involves abhisheka — ritual bathing of the lingam with water, milk, honey and other substances that drain through the yoni's spout (pranala) — and offerings of bilva leaves, flowers and sacred ash (vibhuti) with the three white lines (tripundra) associated with Shiva. Twelve self-manifested (svayambhu) Jyotirlingas, from Somnath in Gujarat to Kedarnath in the Himalayas, are the holiest seats of Shiva worship. Natural ice lingams, such as the one in the Amarnath cave in Kashmir, attract large pilgrimages.

History and interpretation

Objects resembling lingams have been reported from Indus Valley sites, though their interpretation is disputed. The earliest securely identifiable lingam is the Gudimallam lingam (Andhra Pradesh), dated variously between the 3rd century BC and 2nd century AD, which bears a standing figure of Shiva carved on its shaft. Puranic myth connects the lingam with the story of the infinite pillar of fire (the Lingodbhava), in which Brahma and Vishnu seek the ends of Shiva's blazing column and fail, acknowledging his supremacy; another cycle of myths links the lingam's origin to the sage Bhrigu's curse. Shaiva theology and the Shaiva Agamas primarily treat the lingam as an aniconic mark of the formless absolute, while Tantric traditions emphasise creative union with the yoni. Nineteenth-century European writers commonly gave the symbol an exclusively phallic reading, which many later scholars and practitioners consider reductive, though fertility associations are also present in parts of the tradition.

Variants

  • Mukhalinga — a lingam carved with one or more faces of Shiva (ekamukha, panchamukha).
  • Sahasralinga — a lingam carved with a thousand small lingams.
  • Banalinga — naturally water-worn stones from the Narmada river, worshipped in homes.
  • Dhatu and sphatika lingams — made of metal or crystal; temporary lingams of clay, sand, butter or flowers are also made for particular rites.
