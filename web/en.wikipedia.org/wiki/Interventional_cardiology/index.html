Interventional cardiology

Interventional cardiology
Specialty: Cardiology
Focus: Catheter-based treatment of structural heart diseases
Key procedures: Angioplasty, stenting, valvuloplasty, TAVI

A cardiac catheterization laboratory, where interventional procedures are performed under X-ray guidance.

Interventional cardiology is a branch of cardiology that deals specifically with the catheter-based treatment of structural heart diseases. Andreas Gruentzig is considered the father of interventional cardiology after the development of angioplasty by interventional radiologist Charles Dotter.

Procedures are performed by inserting a thin, flexible tube called a catheter into a blood vessel — most commonly through the femoral artery in the groin or the radial artery in the wrist — and guiding it to the heart under fluoroscopic (X-ray) imaging. A large number of procedures can be performed on the heart by catheterization, avoiding the large scars and long recovery of open-heart surgery.

Procedures

  • Angioplasty: a small balloon is inflated within a narrowed coronary artery to open it and restore blood flow.
  • Stenting: a small wire-mesh tube is placed in the artery to keep it open; modern stents are often drug-eluting to reduce restenosis.
  • Valvuloplasty: balloon dilation of a stenotic heart valve.
  • Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI/TAVR): replacement of a diseased aortic valve via catheter.
  • Congenital heart defect correction: closure of defects such as patent foramen ovale and atrial septal defects with occluder devices.
  • Atherectomy and thrombectomy: removal of plaque or clot material from vessels.

History

The field traces its origins to diagnostic cardiac catheterization pioneered by Werner Forssmann in 1929 and developed by André Cournand and Dickinson Richards, who shared the Nobel Prize in 1956. Charles Dotter performed the first percutaneous transluminal angioplasty in 1964, and Andreas Gruentzig performed the first successful percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty (PTCA) on a conscious patient in 1977. Coronary stents followed in the 1980s, drug-eluting stents in the early 2000s, and transcatheter valve therapies from 2002 onward.

Training

Interventional cardiologists complete internal medicine residency, a cardiology fellowship, and an additional dedicated fellowship in interventional cardiology, with board certification available in many countries. Training emphasizes radiation safety, vascular access, and management of complications such as coronary dissection and stent thrombosis.
