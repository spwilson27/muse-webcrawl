Prometheism

Prometheism (Polish: Prometeizm) was a political strategy and movement of interwar Poland, conceived chiefly by Józef Piłsudski, which aimed to weaken the Russian Empire and later the Soviet Union by supporting national independence movements among the non-Russian peoples living within their borders — particularly Ukrainians, Georgians, Azerbaijanis, Crimean Tatars, Cossacks and the peoples of the Caucasus and Turkestan. The name derives from the Greek myth of Prometheus, who defied a tyrant god to bring fire to mankind.

Origins

Piłsudski developed the idea from his experience in the Polish Socialist Party and his 1904 mission to Japan, where he sought support against Russia during the Russo-Japanese War. His reasoning was that Poland's independence could only be secured if Russia were dismantled into its constituent nations. After Polish independence in 1918, Prometheism became an unofficial but important strand of Polish eastern policy, closely connected with Piłsudski's Intermarium (Międzymorze) federation concept.

Activities

Promethean work was coordinated by Polish military intelligence (Section II of the General Staff) in cooperation with the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. Poland supported émigré governments and organisations in exile — including the Ukrainian People's Republic's government-in-exile of Symon Petliura (Piłsudski's ally in the 1920 Kyiv offensive), Georgian Mensheviks, and delegations of Azerbaijani, Crimean Tatar, Kuban Cossack and North Caucasian nationalists. Warsaw funded journals (such as Prométhée, published in Paris from 1926, and later Prometeusz), conferences, and clandestine liaison with activists inside the USSR. France, and to a lesser extent Britain and Turkey, cooperated at times with the Promethean network.

Decline

The movement declined after Piłsudski's death in 1935 and Poland's turn toward "normalisation" with its neighbours (including the 1932 non-aggression pact with the USSR). It was extinguished by the Second World War and Poland's incorporation into the Soviet bloc, although its ideas resurfaced after 1989 in Polish eastern policy and are sometimes cited as a forerunner of modern support for the sovereignty of Ukraine, Georgia and other post-Soviet states.
