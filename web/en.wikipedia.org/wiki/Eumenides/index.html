Eumenides (Ancient Greek: Εὐμενίδες, Eumenídes) is the third and final play of the Oresteia trilogy by the ancient Greek playwright Aeschylus, first performed in 458 BC, when it won first prize at the City Dionysia festival in Athens. The title refers to the Eumenides ("the Kindly Ones"), a euphemistic name given to the Erinyes, or Furies, after their transformation at the end of the play.

Plot

The play opens at the temple of Apollo at Delphi, where Orestes, who has murdered his mother Clytemnestra to avenge his father Agamemnon, seeks purification. Apollo sends him to Athens, pursued by the Furies, who demand vengeance for matricide. In Athens, the goddess Athena establishes a court — the Areopagus — to try the case. Apollo defends Orestes, arguing that the mother is not a true parent; the Furies prosecute. When the jury's votes are tied, Athena casts the deciding vote for acquittal. The enraged Furies are persuaded by Athena to accept a new role as benevolent guardians of Athens, and they are renamed the Eumenides.

Themes

The play dramatizes the transition from blood vengeance and the lex talionis to civic justice administered by a court of law. It is often read as a mythical charter for the Athenian homicide court of the Areopagus and as an aetiology for the cult of the Semnai Theai ("August Goddesses") worshipped beneath the Areopagus hill. The trilogy as a whole traces the curse on the House of Atreus from its origins through to its resolution in law and reconciliation.

Reception

Together with Agamemnon and The Libation Bearers, the Oresteia is the only surviving trilogy of Greek tragedy and is regarded as one of the supreme achievements of the genre. The play's treatment of justice, gender, and the founding of legal institutions has been discussed by classicists, philosophers, and legal theorists ever since antiquity.
