Ecumenopolis

An ecumenopolis (from Greek: oikoumene, "the inhabited world", and polis, "city") is the theoretical future state of a planet in which all of its inhabitable land has merged into a single continuous city. The concept was coined by the Greek city planner Constantinos Apostolou Doxiadis in 1967 as the final stage in his projected hierarchy of human settlements, from the individual dwelling through villages, towns, cities, metropolises and conurbations, and megalopolises to a single worldwide city.

Doxiadis's concept

Doxiadis, founder of the science of ekistics, projected that population growth and economic development would eventually fuse the great urban clusters of the world into one planetary settlement containing, in his estimate, some tens of billions of people. He regarded the ecumenopolis as both inevitable and, if properly planned, workable: it would require a strict hierarchy of communities, planned transportation networks, and zoning that preserved areas for agriculture and nature. Critics argued the vision underestimated problems of governance, resources, waste heat and quality of life.

In fiction

Planet-wide cities are a recurring setting in science fiction:

  • Trantor, the capital of the Galactic Empire in Isaac Asimov's Foundation series, is covered by a single city housing over 40 billion people and dependent on food imports from agricultural worlds.
  • Coruscant, in the Star Wars universe, is a planet entirely covered by one vast city, seat of the Galactic Republic and later the Empire.
  • The planet Helior in Stanisław Lem's works, and various city-planets in anime and comics, develop similar themes, often dystopian ones about crowding, pollution and the loss of nature.

Fictional ecumenopoleis often raise practical issues Doxiadis also acknowledged: importing food, disposing of waste heat (a fully urbanised planet must radiate heat generated by its population), and governing a single settlement of planetary scale.
