Television in South Korea

Television in South Korea began in 1956 with the launch of the commercial station HLKZ-TV in Seoul, one of the earliest television services in Asia. The industry is dominated by the major terrestrial broadcasters — the public broadcasters KBS (Korean Broadcasting System) and MBC (Munhwa Broadcasting Corporation), the educational broadcaster EBS, and the commercial network SBS (Seoul Broadcasting System) — alongside a large cable and satellite sector.

History

Television broadcasting in the Republic of Korea started on 12 May 1956 with HLKZ-TV, a commercial station affiliated with the Korean Broadcasting Corporation's predecessor arrangements; that station was taken over by the state in 1961 and became KBS TV. MBC launched television in 1969, and TBC, a rival commercial network, was absorbed into KBS in 1980 under the media consolidation measures of the Chun Doo-hwan government. SBS was founded in 1990 and began broadcasting in 1991, restoring a commercial competitor after deregulation.

Colour television broadcasting began in 1980. Cable television was introduced legally in 1995, ending the terrestrial broadcasters' monopoly of programme supply, and satellite broadcasting followed. South Korea completed its switchover from analogue to digital terrestrial television at the end of 2012.

Broadcasters and channels

The principal free-to-air channels are KBS1, KBS2, MBC TV, SBS TV, and EBS1/EBS2. Major cable networks include tvN and Mnet (operated by CJ ENM), JTBC (launched 2011 by the JoongAng media group), TV Chosun, Channel A, and MBN; news is also served by the dedicated channels YTN and Yonhap News TV. IPTV services from the major telecom operators and streaming platforms compete with conventional channels.

Programming

South Korean television is internationally known for its Korean dramas (K-dramas), whose export across Asia from the late 1990s was a central phenomenon of the Korean Wave (Hallyu). Variety shows, reality formats, news, and music programmes featuring K-pop acts are also prominent. South Korean formats such as King of Mask Singer have been adapted in many countries. Programming is regulated by the Korea Communications Commission and the Korea Communications Standards Commission, which enforce standards on content and advertising.
