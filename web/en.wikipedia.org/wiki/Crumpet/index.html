A crumpet is a small griddle bread made from an unsweetened batter of water or milk, flour and yeast, eaten in the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa. Crumpets are distinguished by their spongy texture and the characteristic holes on top, formed by bubbles rising through the batter as it cooks in a ring on a griddle.

Preparation and serving

The batter is poured into greased crumpet rings on a hot griddle and cooked on one side only, leaving a smooth toasted base and a pitted top. Crumpets are then toasted before serving so butter melts down into the holes. They are commonly served at breakfast or tea with butter, jam, honey, Marmite or cheese, and accompany — though are distinct from — the scone-based cream tea of clotted cream fame.

History and variants

Crumpets date at least to the Anglo-Saxon period in some form; the modern yeasted version developed in the Victorian era, when baking powder was sometimes added. Regional variants include the Staffordshire pikelet — thinner and without a ring — and related griddle cakes such as the Welsh pice ar y maen. The term "crumpet" in British slang has other colloquial uses. Compare the English muffin, which is cooked on both sides.
