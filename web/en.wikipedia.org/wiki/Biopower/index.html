Biopower

Biopower (from the French bio-pouvoir) is a term coined by the philosopher Michel Foucault for the technologies of power through which modern states govern human beings as biological beings — regulating populations, health, reproduction, and life itself. Foucault introduced the concept in the first volume of The History of Sexuality (1976) and in his 1975–76 Collège de France lectures, Society Must Be Defended.

Foucault's account

Foucault contrasted biopower with the older sovereign power "to take life or let live": biopower instead works "to make live and let die". It operates at two poles. The first, anatomo-politics, disciplines individual bodies — in schools, barracks, hospitals, and factories — optimising their capabilities. The second, biopolitics, targets the population as a whole through statistics and regulation of birth rates, mortality, public health, housing, and longevity. Rather than repressing through prohibition, biopower is productive: it administers, optimises, and multiplies life, while also underpinning modern forms of state racism, which Foucault argued justifies killing in the name of purifying and protecting the population.

Reception and development

The concept has been enormously influential in political theory, sociology, public health, and science studies. Giorgio Agamben extended it in Homo Sacer (1995), linking biopower to the state of exception and the figure of "bare life"; Antonio Negri and Michael Hardt used it to analyse contemporary capitalism and immaterial labour; Roberto Esposito developed the related theme of immunity. The term is routinely invoked in analyses of pandemic governance, reproductive politics, genetic screening, and surveillance.
