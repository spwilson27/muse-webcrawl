Vulgar Latin

Vulgar Latin, also known as Popular or Colloquial Latin, is the range of non-formal registers of Latin spoken from the Late Roman Republic onward. Vulgar Latin as a term is sometimes used to refer to the everyday speech of the Roman world, in contrast to the standardised Classical Latin of literature. It is from Vulgar Latin that the Romance languages developed.

Everyday graffiti, such as those preserved at Pompeii, are a principal source for Vulgar Latin.

Characteristics

Vulgar Latin simplified the classical case system, replaced synthetic verb forms with analytic constructions (e.g. infinitive plus habeo giving the Romance future), and replaced many classical words: caballus "nag" displaced equus "horse", yielding French cheval, Spanish caballo and Italian cavallo. Sources include the Appendix Probi, a late-antique list of common "errors", letters of soldiers, and Christian texts such as the Vulgate.

Development into Romance

Regional varieties of Vulgar Latin diverged after the fall of the Western Roman Empire into Proto-Romance and eventually into Italian, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Romanian and the other Romance languages. The Strasbourg Oaths of 842 are among the earliest texts recognisably distinct from Latin.
