PatronHenry Carey, 1st Baron Hunsdon (Lord Chamberlain); later George Carey

The Lord Chamberlain's Men was a company of actors, or a "playing company", for which William Shakespeare wrote during most of his career. Formed in 1594 under the patronage of Henry Carey, 1st Baron Hunsdon, then Lord Chamberlain, it became the leading theatrical company in London. In 1603 it was taken into royal service by James I and renamed the King's Men.

History

The company was unusual in being organised as a joint-stock enterprise: a core of "sharers", including the tragedian Richard Burbage, the clown Will Kempe (until 1599) and Shakespeare himself, owned shares in the company and its theatres, while hired men and boy apprentices were paid wages. After a dispute over the lease of The Theatre in Shoreditch, the company dismantled it in December 1598 and used its timbers to build the Globe Theatre on Bankside, which opened in 1599. From 1608 they also played indoors at the Blackfriars Theatre.

Repertoire

The company staged the first productions of most of Shakespeare's plays from Richard III and A Midsummer Night's Dream to Hamlet, Othello and (as the King's Men) The Tempest, alongside works by Ben Jonson, John Fletcher and other contemporaries. After the theatres were closed in 1642 the company dissolved; its sharers Heminges and Condell had preserved its legacy by publishing the First Folio of Shakespeare's plays in 1623.
