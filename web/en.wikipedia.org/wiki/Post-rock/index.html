Post-rock

Post-rock is a form of experimental rock characterized by a focus on exploring textures and timbres over traditional rock song structures, chords, or riffs. Post-rock artists are often instrumental, typically using rock instrumentation — guitars, bass, drums — for non-rock purposes, building compositions through repetition, layering, and long dynamic arcs from quiet passages to climactic crescendos. The term was popularized by critic Simon Reynolds in 1994 writing about Bark Psychosis and the band Talk Talk's late work.

History

Precursors include the Velvet Underground, krautrock bands like Can, and ambient pioneers. Talk Talk's Spirit of Eden (1988) and Laughing Stock (1991), and Slint's Spiderland (1991), are foundational texts. In the mid-1990s, Chicago's Tortoise and the Montreal scene around Constellation Records, led by Godspeed You! Black Emperor, defined the genre's epic, orchestral wing, while Scotland's Mogwai became known for extreme quiet–loud dynamics.

2000s and beyond

Iceland's Sigur Rós brought ethereal vocals and bowed guitar to international success, and American bands Explosions in the Sky and Mono popularized a cinematic, crescendo-driven style sometimes dubbed "crescendo-core." Post-rock's influence spread widely into film scores and advertising, even as many of its leading artists rejected the label as reductive.
