Honeypot (computing)

Honeypot
A honeypot lures attackers away from production systems
TypeComputer security mechanism
PurposeDetect, deflect, and study unauthorised use of information systems

In computer security, a honeypot is a mechanism set to detect, deflect, or, in some manner, counteract attempts at unauthorised use of information systems. Generally, a honeypot consists of data (for example, in a network site) that appears to be a legitimate part of the site and seems to contain information or a resource of value to attackers. It is actually isolated, monitored, and capable of blocking or analysing the attackers. This is similar to police sting operations, colloquially known as "baiting" a suspect.

Types

Honeypots are classified by their level of interaction. Low-interaction honeypots emulate only a few services and are cheap and safe to run, but capture limited information. High-interaction honeypots run real operating systems and services, so an intruder can be observed in depth — at correspondingly higher risk and maintenance cost. A honeynet is a whole network of such decoys. Specialised variants include malware honeypots, spam traps (honey addresses), and database honeypots.

Uses

  • Research — observing attacker tools, techniques, and zero-day exploits in the wild, as pioneered by the Honeynet Project.
  • Detection — because a honeypot has no legitimate users, any traffic to it is suspicious by definition, giving low-noise intrusion alerts.
  • Deception and delay — wasting an intruder's time and resources while real systems are hardened.
  • Spam and botnet study — collecting samples and mapping command-and-control behaviour.

Risks and limitations

A poorly contained high-interaction honeypot can be hijacked and used to attack other networks, creating legal liability for its operator. Honeypots also see only attacks directed at them, so they complement rather than replace firewalls and intrusion-detection systems. Sophisticated attackers may fingerprint and avoid known decoys.

History

The concept grew out of Clifford Stoll's 1980s hunt for a hacker at Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory, recounted in The Cuckoo's Egg, and Bill Cheswick's 1990 "Evening with Berferd" paper describing an early decoy. Lance Spitzner and the Honeynet Project popularised the practice in the early 2000s.
