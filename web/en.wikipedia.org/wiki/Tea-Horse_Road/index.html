Tea-Horse Road


A mule caravan on the old Tea-Horse Road

The Tea-Horse Road (Chinese: 茶馬古道) was a network of caravan paths winding through the mountains of Sichuan, Yunnan and Tibet in Southwest China, linking the tea-producing regions of China with the Tibetan Plateau. It is also sometimes called the Southern Silk Road, and for more than a thousand years it carried Chinese tea westward in exchange for Tibetan horses, along with salt, sugar, and other goods.

History

The trade began in the Tang dynasty, when Tibetan demand for tea met the Chinese military's need for hardy plateau horses, and was formalised under the Song dynasty through official tea-horse agencies and barter markets at border towns such as Ya'an in Sichuan and Lijiang in Yunnan. Under the Ming and Qing dynasties the trade was a state monopoly and an instrument of frontier policy, with tea compressed into bricks that served as currency in Tibet.

Routes

Two main corridors existed: the Sichuan route from Ya'an via Kangding to Lhasa, and the Yunnan route from the tea mountains of Xishuangbanna and Pu'er via Dali and Lijiang to Lhasa, with branches extending into Nepal, Bhutan and northern India. The full journey could take many months over passes exceeding 4,000 metres; porters famously carried loads of tea bricks weighing more than their own bodies.

Decline and legacy

The opening of motor roads in the 1950s, including the Sichuan–Tibet Highway, ended the caravan era within a few years. Surviving sections of cobbled path, caravan towns such as Shaxi, and the culture of pu'er tea preserve the memory of the road, which is now promoted in China as a heritage and tourism corridor.
