The Solway Firth is a firth that forms part of the border between England and Scotland, between Cumbria (including the Solway Plain) and Dumfries and Galloway. It stretches from St Bees Head, just south of Whitehaven in Cumbria, to the Mull of Galloway on the western end of Dumfries and Galloway, and forms part of the Irish Sea.

Geography

The coastline is characterised by lowland hills and small coastal towns. The firth receives the rivers Eden, Esk, Nith, Urr, Dee and Annan, and is known for its extensive mudflats, salt marshes and shifting sandbanks, as well as a large tidal range. Its treacherous quicksands have been fished for centuries using the traditional haaf net method.

History

The western end of Hadrian's Wall terminated at Bowness-on-Solway. The frontier zone was long disputed between England and Scotland, and the drowning of Alexander III's forces and later the Battle of Solway Moss (1542) are among the episodes tied to the area. Mary, Queen of Scots crossed the firth to England after her defeat at Langside in 1568.

Wildlife

The firth is internationally important for wintering wildfowl and waders, including barnacle geese from Svalbard, and is protected under several national and international designations. It supports commercial fisheries, notably for cockles, mussels and shrimp.
