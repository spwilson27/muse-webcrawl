Puʻu ʻŌʻō (often written Puu Oo) is a volcanic cone in the eastern rift zone of the Kīlauea volcano on the island of Hawaiʻi. The cone was formed by a protracted eruption that began on 3 January 1983 and continued, with few pauses, until 2018 — one of the longest-lived eruptions of the historical era. The name is Hawaiian; puʻu means "hill" or "cone", and ʻōʻō refers both to a digging stick and to the extinct Hawaiian ʻōʻō bird.

Eruption

The eruption began with fissures opening in the remote rainforest of what is now Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park. Activity soon localised at a single vent, building the Puʻu ʻŌʻō cone through high lava fountains in discrete episodes. After 1986 the main vent shifted to Kūpaianahā, and later returned to the flank of Puʻu ʻŌʻō, which remained the centre of activity for most of the eruption's 35 years.

Lava flows from the eruption repeatedly inundated the coastal communities of Kalapana, destroying hundreds of homes, and in 1990 buried the town and the famous black-sand beach at Kaimū. Flows reached the sea for long periods, building new land as lava entered the ocean. In April–May 2018 the vent drained as magma migrated downrift to feed the lower Puna eruption, the cone floor collapsed, and the Puʻu ʻŌʻō episode came to an end.
