En-bloc clip

An en-bloc clip is a type of clip used to load and hold multiple cartridges together as a unit for insertion into the internal magazine of a repeating firearm. Unlike a stripper clip, which is removed after the cartridges are loaded, an en-bloc clip is inserted into the weapon along with the cartridges and typically remains inside until the last round has been fired, at which point it is ejected or falls free.

Design and operation

In an en-bloc system, the clip itself forms part of the feeding mechanism: the bolt strips cartridges from the clip as it cycles, and the follower pushes the remaining rounds upward. Because the clip functions as an integral part of the magazine, most en-bloc designs cannot easily be "topped up" with individual loose rounds while partially full, and the weapon may not function reliably — or at all — without the clip in place.

Historical use

The en-bloc clip was pioneered in the 1880s by Ferdinand Mannlicher, whose straight-pull bolt-action rifles for the Austro-Hungarian army used five-round en-bloc clips that dropped out of an opening in the bottom of the magazine when empty. The system was widely copied: French Berthier rifles, Italian Carcano rifles (which used six-round clips), and various Steyr and Mauser export designs all employed versions of the concept.

The most famous en-bloc rifle is the American M1 Garand, which used an eight-round en-bloc clip in .30-06 Springfield. When the last cartridge was fired, the Garand ejected the empty clip automatically with a distinctive metallic "ping" sound. Earlier American use included the Pedersen device era experiments and the M1917 Enfield was stripper-clip fed by contrast. Other notable examples include the French MAS-36 (stripper-fed) versus the earlier Berthier, and the Austrian Steyr-Mannlicher M1895, which served through both world wars.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages of en-bloc loading include very fast loading of a full complement of cartridges and positive feeding. Disadvantages include the inability to top off a partial magazine easily, dependence on having clips available, and in some designs the opening in the receiver that admits dirt. Modern firearms have almost universally adopted detachable box magazines or stripper clips instead.
