The papillary muscles are muscles located in the ventricles of the heart. They attach to the cusps of the atrioventricular valves (the mitral and tricuspid valves) via the chordae tendineae and contract to prevent inversion or prolapse of these valves into the atria during systole (ventricular contraction).

Structure

There are five papillary muscles in total. The left ventricle has two — the anterolateral and posteromedial papillary muscles — which anchor the two leaflets of the mitral valve. The right ventricle has three: anterior (the largest), posterior and septal, serving the tricuspid valve. Despite common misconception, the papillary muscles do not open or close the valves; valve closure is driven by pressure gradients, while the muscles tense the chordae to stop the leaflets everting under ventricular pressure.

Blood supply and clinical significance

The posteromedial papillary muscle of the left ventricle typically receives a single blood supply (usually from the right coronary artery), whereas the anterolateral muscle has a dual supply; the posteromedial muscle is therefore more vulnerable to ischaemia. Rupture of a papillary muscle after myocardial infarction is a catastrophic complication causing acute, severe mitral regurgitation and pulmonary oedema, requiring emergency valve surgery. Papillary muscle dysfunction without rupture can also cause mitral regurgitation, and displacement of the muscles in a dilated ventricle contributes to functional mitral regurgitation.
