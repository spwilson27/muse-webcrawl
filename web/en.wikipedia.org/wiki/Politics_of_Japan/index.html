Politics of Japan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The politics of Japan are those of a constitutional monarchy and parliamentary democracy under the Constitution of 1947. The Emperor is “the symbol of the State and of the unity of the People” with a purely ceremonial role; legislative power sits with the bicameral National Diet (the stronger House of Representatives and the House of Councillors), the executive with a cabinet headed by a prime minister chosen by the Diet, and the judiciary under a Supreme Court that has been notably restrained in striking down legislation.

The defining fact of post-war Japanese politics is the dominance of the Liberal Democratic Party (LDP), in power almost continuously since its founding in 1955 (exceptions: 1993–94 and 2009–12) — the “1955 system” of one-and-a-half-party rule, sustained by factional competition within the LDP, close ties among politicians, bureaucrats and business (the “iron triangle”; amakudari retirement of officials into industry), and electoral rules changed in 1994 to a mixed parallel system of single-member districts and proportional blocs. Opposition parties (currently led by the Constitutional Democratic Party) have struggled to coordinate. Prime-ministerial turnover was rapid in the 2000s–2010s, punctuated by the long Abe administration (2012–20) and its “Abenomics” programme.
