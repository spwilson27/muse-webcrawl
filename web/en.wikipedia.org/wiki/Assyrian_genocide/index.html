The Assyrian genocide (also known as the Sayfo, Syriac for "sword") was the mass murder and deportation of Assyrian Christians in the Ottoman Empire and neighbouring Persia during and after the First World War, carried out by Ottoman forces and Kurdish irregulars. Alongside the Armenian genocide and the Greek genocide, it formed part of the late Ottoman destruction of the empire's Christian minorities. Estimates of the death toll range from about 150,000 to 300,000 people — a substantial fraction of the Assyrian population.

Background

The Assyrians are an indigenous Christian ethnic group of northern Mesopotamia, divided among the Church of the East, the Chaldean Catholic Church and the Syriac Orthodox Church. Under the Ottoman millet system they lived as a protected but subordinate community. During the nineteenth century, growing nationalism, missionary activity, and Ottoman defeats intensified suspicion of Christian minorities; Assyrians had already suffered massacres under the Kurdish emir Bedir Khan in the 1840s and during the Hamidian massacres of 1894–1896.

Massacres, 1914–1918

After the Ottoman entry into the First World War in late 1914, Ottoman forces invaded north-western Persia, where Assyrian villages in the Urmia region were attacked; many fled to Russian lines. In the spring and summer of 1915, massacres swept the Assyrian districts of Hakkari, Tur Abdin, Diyarbakır and Van provinces: men were killed, women and children were deported, enslaved or died on forced marches. In Hakkari, the Assyrian tribes resisted under their patriarch, Mar Shimun XIX Benyamin, before retreating to Persia, where the patriarch was assassinated in 1918. Survivors eventually reached refugee camps in Iraq, notably at Baqubah.

Aftermath and recognition

The genocide shattered the Assyrian presence in its Hakkari homeland; survivors scattered to Iraq, Syria and a worldwide diaspora. In 1933 many Iraqi Assyrians were killed in the Simele massacre. The events remained relatively little studied until the late twentieth century. Assyrian organisations observe 7 August as a day of commemoration. Several parliaments and states — including Sweden (2010), Armenia (2015), and a number of US states — have formally recognised the Assyrian genocide, and the International Association of Genocide Scholars recognised it (together with the Greek genocide) in 2007. The Turkish government does not accept the term genocide, attributing the deaths to wartime conditions and intercommunal conflict.
