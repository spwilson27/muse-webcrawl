Exegesis (from the Greek ἐξήγησις, from ἐξηγεῖσθαι, "to lead out") is a critical explanation or interpretation of a text, usually a religious or scriptural text. Traditionally the term was used primarily for work with the Bible, and a person who practises it is called an exegete. Exegesis involves historical and literary analysis, attention to the original languages, genre, context, and textual transmission, in order to draw the author's intended meaning out of the text — in contrast to eisegesis, reading one's own ideas into it.

Biblical exegesis

Jewish exegesis includes the interpretive traditions of midrash and the medieval commentators such as Rashi, operating at levels known by the acronym PaRDeS (literal, allegorical, comparative, and mystical senses). Christian exegesis developed patristic schools at Alexandria (allegorical, associated with Origen) and Antioch (more literal and historical); the medieval church recognised a fourfold sense of scripture (literal, allegorical, moral, anagogical). The Reformation emphasised the literal sense and sola scriptura, while modern historical-critical exegesis, from the 18th century onward, applies source, form, and redaction criticism. Islamic exegesis of the Qur'an is called tafsir, and Hindu traditions maintain elaborate commentarial exegesis of the Vedas and other texts.

Methods

Modern exegetical method typically proceeds through textual criticism (establishing the best text), translation, historical and cultural background, literary structure and genre analysis, and synthesis. It is closely related to hermeneutics, the theory of interpretation, though exegesis usually denotes the practical application of hermeneutical principles to a specific text.
