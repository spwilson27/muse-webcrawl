An electronic health record (EHR) is the systematized collection of patient and population electronically stored health information in a digital format. These records can be shared across different health care settings through network-connected, enterprise-wide information systems. Records may include a range of data: demographics, medical history, medication and allergies, immunization status, laboratory test results, radiology images, vital signs and billing information.

EHR versus EMR

The term electronic medical record (EMR) is often used for the digital chart maintained within a single practice or hospital, while EHR denotes the broader, shareable record that follows the patient across organisations and conforms to nationally recognised interoperability standards. In everyday usage the two terms are frequently interchanged.

Benefits

Advocates point to improved legibility and access, clinical decision support (such as drug-interaction alerts), electronic prescribing, better coordination of care, and the ability to use aggregated data for quality measurement and research. Computerised provider order entry and barcode medication administration have been shown to reduce certain prescribing and administration errors.

Challenges

Adoption of EHRs has been criticised for increasing documentation burden and clinician burnout, for poor usability and interoperability between vendors, and for creating new error types ("e-iatrogenesis"). Privacy and security are major concerns: health data breaches can expose sensitive information, and systems must comply with regimes such as HIPAA in the United States and the GDPR in Europe. In the US, adoption was driven by the HITECH Act of 2009 and the "meaningful use" incentive programme; most hospitals and physician offices now use certified EHR systems.

Exchange standards

Data exchange relies on standards including HL7 version 2 messaging, the newer FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) API standard, DICOM for imaging, and terminologies such as SNOMED CT, LOINC and ICD for coding diagnoses and laboratory tests.
