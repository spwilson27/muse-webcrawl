Papaver somniferum, commonly known as the opium poppy or breadseed poppy, is a species of flowering plant in the family Papaveraceae. It is the species of plant from which both opium and poppy seeds are derived, and it is a valuable ornamental plant grown in gardens. Its native range is probably the eastern Mediterranean, but it has been cultivated since prehistoric times and is now widely naturalised.

Description

The plant is a robust annual, growing to about 100 cm (39 in) tall, with glaucous, lobed leaves that clasp the stem. The flowers are large — up to 10 cm across — with four white, mauve or red petals and a dark basal blotch; numerous cultivated varieties exist. The fruit is a hairless, rounded capsule topped by a disc of stigmatic rays; when the unripe capsule is incised, it exudes a milky latex that dries to form raw opium.

Alkaloids and uses

Opium latex contains more than 40 alkaloids, of which the most important are morphine (typically about 10–15%), codeine, thebaine, papaverine and noscapine. Morphine and codeine are essential analgesic medicines; thebaine is the industrial precursor for oxycodone and naloxone. Most licit production today uses the "poppy straw" process — harvesting and extracting the whole dried plant — in countries including Turkey, India, Australia (Tasmania), France and Spain, under the supervision of the International Narcotics Control Board. Illicit opium production has been concentrated historically in the Golden Triangle of Southeast Asia and the Golden Crescent, especially Afghanistan.

Seeds and cultivation

The ripe seeds contain negligible alkaloid and are used as a spice and for poppyseed oil in baking across Europe and Asia. Cultivation of the plant, even as an ornamental, is regulated or prohibited in many countries because of its association with heroin production, although enforcement for garden plants varies widely.

History

Archaeological evidence suggests the opium poppy was domesticated in the western Mediterranean by Neolithic farmers and spread across Europe and Asia in antiquity; Sumerian, Egyptian, Greek and Roman sources all knew opium's medicinal and narcotic properties. In the 19th century the British East India Company's opium trade with China led to the Opium Wars, a defining episode in modern Chinese history.
