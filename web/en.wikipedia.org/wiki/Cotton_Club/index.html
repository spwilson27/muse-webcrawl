The Cotton Club was a New York City nightclub from 1923 to 1940, first operating on 142nd Street and Lenox Avenue in Harlem and later downtown on Broadway. During Prohibition it became the most famous showcase of African-American entertainment in the United States — while enforcing a whites-only audience policy that epitomised the contradictions of the Jazz Age.

History

The venue began as the Club Deluxe, owned by the heavyweight boxing champion Jack Johnson. In 1923 the gangster and bootlegger Owney Madden took it over and relaunched it as the Cotton Club, staging lavish revues with a "jungle" décor theme. Duke Ellington's orchestra, installed from 1927, became nationally famous through radio broadcasts from the club, and Ellington composed pieces such as "Mood Indigo" during the engagement. Cab Calloway's band succeeded Ellington in 1931, popularising "Minnie the Moocher". Other performers included Louis Armstrong, Ethel Waters, Bill "Bojangles" Robinson, the Nicholas Brothers and a teenage Lena Horne in the chorus.

Segregation and image

Black performers played to exclusively white audiences, and even employees faced colour rules; the club's chorus dancers were famously required to be light-skinned and under a certain age. Harlem residents largely could not attend. After the Harlem riot of 1935 and the repeal of Prohibition, the club moved to Broadway and 48th Street in 1936, closing for good in 1940 amid rising rents and investigations.

Legacy

The Cotton Club symbolised both the flowering of jazz in the Harlem Renaissance and the exploitation of Black artistry by white organised crime. It inspired Francis Ford Coppola's 1984 film The Cotton Club, and a later Harlem club revived the name.
