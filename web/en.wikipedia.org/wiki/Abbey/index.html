Abbey

An abbey is a type of monastery used by members of a religious order under the governance of an abbot or abbess. The term derives from the Aramaic abba ("father") via the Latin abbatia. Abbeys provide a complex of buildings and structures serving a community of monks or nuns who live under religious vows and devote their lives to prayer, study and work.

A typical medieval abbey: church, cloister and chapter house arranged around a courtyard.

Origins and development

Christian monasticism began in the deserts of Egypt and Syria in the third and fourth centuries. In the West, the Rule of Saint Benedict (c. 530) established the pattern of the self-governing, self-sufficient abbey, with a daily rhythm (the horarium) balancing liturgical prayer, sacred reading (lectio divina) and manual labour. Benedictine abbeys such as Monte Cassino, founded by Benedict of Nursia himself, became centres of learning, agriculture and manuscript preservation in early medieval Europe.

Reform movements repeatedly renewed abbey life: the Cluniac reforms of the tenth and eleventh centuries created a vast network of priories subject to the great abbey of Cluny, while the Cistercians, founded at Cîteaux in 1098 under figures such as Bernard of Clairvaux, sought a stricter observance in remote valleys and developed a distinctive austere architecture. Other orders with abbeys include the Carthusians, Premonstratensians, Augustinian canons and Trappists.

Layout

The standard medieval abbey was arranged around a cloister — a covered walkway around an open court (the garth) — adjoining the south side of the abbey church. Around the cloister stood the chapter house, where the community met daily; the refectory (dining hall); the calefactory or warming room; and the dormitory, usually on an upper floor, connected to the church by a night stair for the night offices. Beyond lay the infirmary, guest house, kitchens, barns, workshops, fishponds and gardens. The ninth-century Plan of Saint Gall is the most famous surviving ideal layout of such a complex.

Decline and survival

Many abbeys were suppressed at the Reformation: in England, Henry VIII's Dissolution of the Monasteries (1536–1541) closed some 800 religious houses, leaving ruins such as Fountains Abbey and Rievaulx that are now celebrated landmarks. Others were dissolved during the French Revolution and under Napoleon and Joseph II. Some abbeys survived in continuous use — Westminster Abbey in London (a former Benedictine abbey, now a royal peculiar), Saint Gall in Switzerland, and Melk and Göttweig in Austria — while the nineteenth and twentieth centuries saw notable monastic revivals and new foundations worldwide.
