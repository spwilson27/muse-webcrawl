Alaungsithu (Burmese: အလောင်းစည်သူ, pronounced [ʔəláʊɰ̃ sʰìθù]; 13 December 1089 – 1167) was king of the Pagan dynasty of Burma (Myanmar) from 1112/13 to 1167. Sithu, as he is also called, is considered a great king who brought unity to the country's regions, revived the economy, and standardized weights, measures and coinage.

Early life

Alaungsithu was a grandson of two earlier Pagan kings: his grandfather was Kyanzittha, and he claimed descent from the empire's founder, Anawrahta. Chronicles describe his youth at the court in Pagan (present-day Bagan), where he was raised as heir presumptive.

Reign

On the death of Kyanzittha in 1112 or 1113, Alaungsithu ascended the throne. His long reign of more than five decades was marked by extensive royal tours throughout the empire — a practice that strengthened central authority over distant provinces. He visited regions as far as the Malay coast and Bengal, and Burmese chronicles credit him with sea voyages to foreign lands, from which the epithet "Alaungsithu" (the future Buddha, or embryo Buddha, Sithu) is said to derive from his Buddhist devotion.

At home, he undertook public works including canals and irrigation, encouraged trade, and promulgated standard weights and measures across the kingdom. He also reformed the calendar. Alaungsithu was a devout Theravada Buddhist and a prolific builder of temples; his most famous construction is the Thatbyinnyu Temple in Bagan, completed around 1144, the tallest temple in the city, as well as the Shwegugyi Temple.

Death and succession

In old age, the king fell ill and was moved from the palace. In 1167 he was murdered by his son Narathu, who smothered him while he lay sick and then seized the throne. Narathu's reign proved violent and brief.

Legacy

Alaungsithu is remembered in Burmese tradition as one of the great kings of Pagan, and the Thatbyinnyu Temple remains one of the most prominent monuments of the Bagan Archaeological Zone. His standardizations are considered an important step in the economic integration of medieval Burma.
