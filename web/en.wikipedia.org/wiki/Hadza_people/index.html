Hadza people

The Hadza, or Hadzabe, are a Tanzanian indigenous ethnic group native to the area around Lake Eyasi in the central Rift Valley and the neighbouring Serengeti Plateau. Numbering roughly 1,200–1,500 people, they are among the last hunter-gatherer groups in Africa, and their language, Hadzane, is a language isolate notable for its click consonants.

Subsistence

Hadza men hunt game — from birds and baboons to large antelope — with bows and poison-tipped arrows, and collect honey, while women gather tubers, berries, and baobab fruit. Meat and honey are prestigious foods shared widely within camps. The Hadza do not keep livestock or cultivate crops traditionally, live in temporary camps of grass huts, and have an egalitarian social organization without formal chiefs or inherited status.

Language and genetics

Hadzane was once grouped with the Khoisan languages because of its clicks, but is now generally treated as an isolate. Genetic studies indicate the Hadza carry some of the most divergent lineages in modern humans, suggesting an ancient population history in East Africa.

Threats

Hadza land and subsistence have been pressured by encroaching pastoralism and agriculture, tourism hunting concessions, and government settlement schemes. Much of their traditional territory has no formal legal title, though some areas have been secured through certificates of customary right of occupancy.
