Anamnesis (philosophy)

Anamnesis
[Image: Socrates questioning the slave boy in the Meno, eliciting a geometrical proof]
EtymologyGreek ἀνάμνησις, "recollection"
Key textsPlato, Meno and Phaedo
DoctrineLearning is recollection of knowledge possessed by the soul before birth

Anamnesis (Ancient Greek: ἀνάμνησις) is a concept in Plato's epistemology according to which learning is a process of recollecting knowledge that the immortal soul possessed before birth but forgot upon embodiment. The doctrine is introduced in the Meno and developed in the Phaedo and Phaedrus.

The Meno demonstration

In the Meno, Socrates responds to Meno's paradox — that inquiry is impossible because one cannot search for what one does not already know — by questioning an uneducated slave boy about a geometrical problem (doubling a square). With questions alone, and no instruction, the boy progresses from wrong answers to the correct construction. Socrates presents this as evidence that the boy recovered knowledge from within himself: what we call learning is in fact recollection.

Related uses

In Aristotle anamnesis denotes deliberate recollection as part of memory. The term was later taken up in Christian liturgy (the memorial of Christ's sacrifice in the Eucharist) and in medicine, where "anamnesis" means a patient's medical history — a different sense from the Platonic doctrine.
