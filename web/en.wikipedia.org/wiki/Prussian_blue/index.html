Prussian blue

Prussian blue (also known as Berlin blue, brandenburg blue or, in German, Preußischblau) is a dark blue pigment produced by oxidation of ferrous ferrocyanide salts. Its chemical formula is Fe7(CN)18 — ferric ferrocyanide. Discovered in Berlin around 1706, it was the first modern synthetic pigment, and it remains important in painting, blueprinting and medicine.

Discovery

The pigment was made accidentally by the Berlin colour-maker Johann Jacob Diesbach around 1704–1706, when potash contaminated with animal oil produced a striking blue precipitate instead of the expected red. The recipe was kept secret until published in 1724. Cheap and intensely coloured, Prussian blue transformed European painting — it appears, for example, in works by Watteau and later defined the blue of Japanese woodblock prints such as Hokusai's Great Wave off Kanagawa.

Uses

In engineering, Prussian blue is used as a layout dye and in the cyanotype process, which gives blueprints their colour. In medicine, orally administered insoluble Prussian blue (Radiogardase) is an approved antidote that traps radioactive caesium and thallium in the gut. Its crystal structure, a cubic framework with vacancies, makes it a prototype of the "Prussian blue analogues" studied for batteries and molecular sieves.
