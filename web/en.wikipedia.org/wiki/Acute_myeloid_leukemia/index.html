Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a cancer of the myeloid line of blood cells, characterized by the rapid growth of abnormal cells that build up in the bone marrow and blood and interfere with normal blood cell production. It is the most common acute leukemia affecting adults, and its incidence increases with age.

Because the malignant blasts crowd out normal hematopoiesis, people with AML typically present with symptoms of bone marrow failure: fatigue and pallor from anemia, recurrent infections from a lack of functional white blood cells, and easy bruising or bleeding from thrombocytopenia. Without treatment the disease is fatal within weeks to months, so it is regarded as a medical emergency.

Signs and symptoms

Common findings include tiredness, shortness of breath on exertion, fever, weight loss, and loss of appetite. Physical signs may include petechiae and ecchymoses, gum hypertrophy (especially in monocytic subtypes), skin infiltration (leukemia cutis), and occasionally enlargement of the liver, spleen, or lymph nodes. A rare presentation is a myeloid sarcoma (chloroma), a solid tumor of myeloblasts outside the marrow.

Causes and risk factors

Most cases arise without a clear cause. Established risk factors include exposure to ionizing radiation and benzene, previous treatment with alkylating chemotherapy or topoisomerase II inhibitors, smoking, and preceding blood disorders such as myelodysplastic syndrome and myeloproliferative neoplasms. Genetic predisposition plays a role in a minority of cases, including in people with Down syndrome, Fanconi anemia, and germline mutations in genes such as CEBPA, DDX41, and RUNX1.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on examination of the blood and bone marrow. A marrow blast percentage of at least 20 percent (or the presence of certain defining cytogenetic abnormalities, such as t(8;21), inv(16), or the PML-RARA fusion of acute promyelocytic leukemia) establishes the diagnosis. Flow cytometry confirms myeloid lineage, and cytogenetic and molecular testing (for mutations in FLT3, NPM1, CEBPA, TP53, IDH1, IDH2, and others) is essential for classification and prognosis. The older French-American-British (FAB) system classified AML by morphology (M0–M7); modern practice uses the World Health Organization and International Consensus Classification systems, which emphasize genetics.

Treatment

Treatment is usually divided into remission induction and post-remission (consolidation) therapy. For fit patients the standard induction regimen for decades has been continuous-infusion cytarabine combined with an anthracycline such as daunorubicin — the so-called "7+3" regimen. Consolidation may include high-dose cytarabine or allogeneic stem cell transplantation, depending on risk.

Targeted agents have transformed therapy for molecular subsets: midostaurin and quizartinib for FLT3-mutated disease, the IDH inhibitors ivosidenib and enasidenib, the antibody-drug conjugate gemtuzumab ozogamicin for CD33-positive disease, and, for older or unfit patients, the BCL-2 inhibitor venetoclax combined with a hypomethylating agent such as azacitidine. Acute promyelocytic leukemia is treated distinctly, with all-trans retinoic acid and arsenic trioxide, and has an excellent prognosis. CPX-351, a liposomal formulation of cytarabine and daunorubicin, is used in therapy-related AML and AML with myelodysplasia-related changes.

Prognosis

Outcomes depend strongly on age and on cytogenetic and molecular risk. Younger patients with favorable-risk genetics may be cured in a majority of cases, whereas adverse features — such as a complex karyotype or TP53 mutation — carry a poor prognosis. Risk stratification systems such as the European LeukemiaNet classification guide decisions about transplantation in first remission.
