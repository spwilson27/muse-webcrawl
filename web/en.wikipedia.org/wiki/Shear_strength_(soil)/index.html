Shear strength (soil)

The shear strength of soil is the maximum shear stress soil can resist before failure, arising from friction and interlocking between particles and, in fine-grained soils, from cohesion. It governs the stability of slopes, the bearing capacity of foundations, and lateral earth pressure on retaining structures, making it a central quantity in geotechnical engineering.

Mohr–Coulomb criterion

The standard model expresses peak shear strength τ on a failure plane as τ = c′ + σ′ tan φ′, where c′ is the effective cohesion intercept, φ′ is the angle of shearing resistance (friction angle), and σ′ is the effective normal stress — total stress minus pore-water pressure, per Terzaghi's effective stress principle. Because water pressure can reduce σ′ drastically, drainage conditions matter: saturated clay loaded quickly behaves "undrained" with strength governed by its undrained shear strength, while slow loading allows consolidation and higher drained strength.

Behaviour of sands and clays

Dense sands dilate during shear, showing a peak strength above the ultimate (critical-state) value; loose sands contract. Overconsolidated clays similarly soften from a peak to a lower residual strength along polished slip surfaces — a key mechanism in slope failures. Critical state soil mechanics provides a unifying framework linking strength, density and stress history.

Measurement

Laboratory tests include the direct shear box, triaxial compression tests (unconsolidated-undrained, consolidated-undrained and consolidated-drained), and the ring shear test for residual strength. In the field, the standard penetration test, cone penetration test and field vane are widely used to estimate strength in place.
