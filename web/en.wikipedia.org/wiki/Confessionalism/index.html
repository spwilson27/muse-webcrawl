Confessionalism

Confessionalism is a term used in several distinct senses. In political science it denotes a system of government that distributes power and representation among religious communities; in religious studies it refers to adherence to a church's formal confession of faith; and in literary criticism it describes a style of poetry centred on frank autobiographical self-disclosure.

Political confessionalism

The best-known example is Lebanon, whose National Pact of 1943 and later Taif Agreement allocate offices by sect: the president is a Maronite Christian, the prime minister a Sunni Muslim, and the speaker of parliament a Shia Muslim, with parliamentary seats divided between Christians and Muslims. Similar quota arrangements have been used in Bosnia and Herzegovina and, historically, in Cyprus and the Netherlands' pillarised society (verzuiling), in which public life was organised in separate Protestant, Catholic, socialist, and liberal "pillars".

Religious confessionalism

Within Christianity, confessionalism emphasises subscription to historic creeds and confessions—such as the Lutheran Book of Concord or the Reformed Westminster Confession—as the standard of doctrine, in contrast to more latitudinarian or purely biblical approaches. "Confessional Lutheranism" and "confessional Reformed" churches describe themselves this way.

Confessional poetry

In literature, the confessional mode is associated with mid-twentieth-century American poets such as Robert Lowell, Sylvia Plath, Anne Sexton, and John Berryman, whose work treated mental illness, family life, and personal trauma in direct, autobiographical terms.
