Stereoscope

A stereoscope is a device for viewing a stereoscopic pair of separate images, depicting left-eye and right-eye views of the same scene, as a single three-dimensional image. A typical stereoscope provides each eye with a lens that makes the image seen by that eye appear larger and more distant, and usually also shifts its apparent horizontal position, so that for a person with normal binocular depth perception the edges of the two images fuse into a single "stereo window".

History

The first stereoscope was described by the British scientist Charles Wheatstone in 1838, using mirrors to combine drawn images. Sir David Brewster improved the design with lenses, and the lenticular stereoscope became a sensation after being exhibited at the Great Exhibition of 1851, where Queen Victoria admired it. In the United States, Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. designed an inexpensive handheld viewer in 1861 that became the standard form.

Use

During the second half of the 19th century, stereoscopic photographs (stereograms) on cards were a hugely popular form of home entertainment and education, produced in the millions by companies such as the London Stereoscopic Company and, later, Underwood & Underwood and the Keystone View Company. The principle survives in the View-Master (introduced 1939), in aerial-photography interpretation stereoscopes, and conceptually in modern virtual-reality headsets.

Principle

Because the two eyes view a scene from slightly different positions, the brain combines the two retinal images to perceive depth. A stereoscope presents two photographs taken from points roughly 65 mm apart—the average interocular distance—reproducing this effect artificially.
