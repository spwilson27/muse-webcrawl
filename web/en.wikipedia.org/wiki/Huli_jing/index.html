Huli jing (Chinese: 狐狸精; pinyin: húli jīng) are Chinese mythological creatures usually capable of shapeshifting, who may be benevolent or malevolent spirits. In Chinese mythology and folklore, foxes are depicted as spirits possessed of magical powers; an aged fox that has cultivated itself for centuries—fifty to a thousand years—gains supernatural abilities, additional tails, and the power to take human form, most often that of a beautiful woman.

Traditions

The nine-tailed fox (九尾狐, jiǔwěihú) first appears in the Classic of Mountains and Seas (Shanhaijing). Early portrayals could be auspicious—associated with the legendary Yu the Great—but by the Tang and Song dynasties the fox spirit was increasingly cast as a seductress who bewitches and ruins men, a theme elaborated in zhiguai tales, Pu Songling's Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio (Liaozhai Zhiyi), which often treats fox spirits sympathetically, and the Ming novel Investiture of the Gods (Fengshen Yanyi), whose villainess Daji is possessed by a nine-tailed fox sent to corrupt King Zhou of Shang.

In folk religion, fox spirits were also worshipped as deities (notably the "Great Immortal Hu" of northeastern China), and fox possession was a traditional explanation for certain illnesses. The huli jing is the Chinese counterpart of the Japanese kitsune and the Korean kumiho, though the Korean figure is almost invariably depicted as malevolent and liver-eating.
