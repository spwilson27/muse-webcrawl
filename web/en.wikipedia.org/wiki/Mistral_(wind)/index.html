Mistral (wind)

The mistral (French: mistral; Occitan: mistrau) is a strong, cold, northwesterly wind that blows from southern France into the Lion Gulf of the northern Mediterranean. It produces sustained winds often exceeding 66 km/h (41 mph), sometimes more than 100 km/h, and is especially frequent in winter and spring. It is one of the most famous of the Mediterranean's regional winds.

The mistral is channelled down the Rhône valley between the Alps and the Massif Central. (Labelled placeholder image.)

Formation

The mistral arises when an anticyclone over the Atlantic or northern France coincides with low pressure over the Gulf of Genoa. Cold polar air is funnelled southward between the Alps and the Pyrenees/Cévennes, accelerating through the Rhône valley (a Venturi-like effect) and spreading out over Provence, the Languedoc coast and the Gulf of Lion. The wind typically blows for three, six or nine days in local tradition, brings exceptionally clear, dry air and brilliant visibility, and can chill the sea surface enough to drive the deep-water formation important to Mediterranean circulation.

Effects

The mistral shapes Provençal life and landscape: cypress hedges and shelter belts protect fields; traditional farmhouses (mas) face south with small north windows; vines and fruit trees are trained low. It disperses pollution and dries the soil, but it also exacerbates forest fires in summer and is reputedly maddening — old Provençal law and folklore blamed the mistral for irritability and worse. The name derives from the Latin magistralis, "masterly".
