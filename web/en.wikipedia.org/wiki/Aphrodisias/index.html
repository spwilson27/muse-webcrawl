Aphrodisias (Ancient Greek: Ἀφροδισιάς) was a small ancient Greek Hellenistic city in the historic Caria cultural region of western Asia Minor, now in Aydın Province, Turkey. It was named after Aphrodite, the Greek goddess of love, who had a unique cult image, the Aphrodite of Aphrodisias, worshipped in the city's central temple.

The site is remarkable for the exceptional preservation of its marble monuments, made possible by nearby marble quarries, and for its celebrated school of sculpture, whose works were exported across the Roman world.

History

The sanctuary of Aphrodite has prehistoric roots, but the city was founded as a planned Hellenistic foundation, probably in the 2nd century BC. Aphrodisias formed a close alliance with Rome: in 39 BC the Roman Senate confirmed its privileges, and under Augustus it received special favour. The city prospered through the imperial period, building a theatre, an odeon, a stadium — one of the best preserved in the ancient world, seating some 30,000 — baths, and the monumental Tetrapylon gateway.

Aphrodisias became the capital of the Roman province of Caria. In late antiquity the temple of Aphrodite was converted into a Christian church and the city was renamed Stauropolis ("City of the Cross"). It declined after Persian and Arab raids in the 7th century and was eventually abandoned in the Middle Ages.

Archaeology

Systematic excavations began under Kenan Erim in 1961 and continue under New York University and Oxford. Finds include the Sebasteion, a temple complex honouring the emperors, decorated with vivid relief panels, and a rich corpus of inscriptions, including the famous "Archive Wall" in the theatre. Aphrodisias was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2017.
