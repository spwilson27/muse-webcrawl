Scottish Agricultural Revolution

The Scottish Agricultural Revolution was the transformation of agriculture in Scotland during the 18th and early 19th centuries, in which traditional runrig farming, infield–outfield systems, and communal townships (fermtouns and clachans) gave way to enclosed fields, long leases, crop rotation, drainage, and selective livestock breeding.

Changes

Improving landlords and tenant farmers introduced turnips and sown grasses, lime and marling, the Norfolk-style rotation, and new implements; in the Lowlands this produced large gains in output and underpinned population growth and industrialization. In the Highlands, the same drive for commercial rents produced the Highland Clearances, as sheep farming and later deer forests displaced crofting communities, driving emigration.

Historiography

Historians debate the pace and social costs of the change: long viewed as a swift "revolution" led by improvers' societies such as the Society of Improvers (1723), it is now often seen as a century-long regional transition, gradual in the northeast but abrupt and traumatic in parts of the Highlands.
