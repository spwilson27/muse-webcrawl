Philip Marlowe

Philip Marlowe is a fictional character created by Raymond Chandler who was characteristic of the hardboiled crime fiction genre. Marlowe first appeared under that name in The Big Sleep (1939). Chandler's early short stories, published in pulp magazines such as Black Mask, featured similar characters with names like "Carmady" and "John Dalmas," later retrofitted by Chandler for the novels.

Marlowe is a wisecracking, hard-drinking private eye in Los Angeles—observant, morally upright beneath a cynical surface, and prone to first-person narration laced with vivid similes. He is the archetype of the hardboiled detective and a central figure of film noir.

Character

In The Big Sleep Marlowe introduces himself: "I'm thirty-three years old, went to college once and can still speak English when my business demands it. I was in the D.A.'s office as an investigator... I charge twenty-five a day and expenses." He is single, drinks whisky, plays chess against himself, and likes classical music and poetry. In Chandler's essay "The Simple Art of Murder" (1944), the detective is described as a romantic knight: "down these mean streets a man must go who is not himself mean, who is neither tarnished nor afraid."

Novels

Marlowe appears in seven completed Chandler novels: The Big Sleep (1939), Farewell, My Lovely (1940), The High Window (1942), The Lady in the Lake (1943), The Little Sister (1949), The Long Goodbye (1953), and Playback (1958), plus the unfinished Poodle Springs. Later authors, including Robert B. Parker and Benjamin Black, wrote authorised Marlowe sequels.

Portrayals

Humphrey Bogart's portrayal in Howard Hawks's The Big Sleep (1946), opposite Lauren Bacall, is the most famous. Others who have played Marlowe include Dick Powell (Murder, My Sweet, 1944), Robert Montgomery (Lady in the Lake, 1947), James Garner (Marlowe, 1969), Elliott Gould (Robert Altman's The Long Goodbye, 1973), Robert Mitchum (Farewell, My Lovely, 1975; The Big Sleep, 1978), and Liam Neeson (Marlowe, 2022).

Influence

Marlowe defined the modern private-eye archetype, influencing generations of crime writers and characters from Ross Macdonald's Lew Archer to the detectives of neo-noir film and television.
