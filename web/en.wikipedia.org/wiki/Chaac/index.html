Chaac

Chaac (also spelled Chac or, in Classic Maya, Chaahk) is the name of the Maya god of rain, thunder and lightning. Chaac was one of the oldest and most important deities in the Maya pantheon, venerated across the Maya world from the Preclassic period to the Spanish conquest and beyond. He corresponds broadly to the Aztec god Tlaloc and to other Mesoamerican rain gods. Chaac's Aztec counterpart and his Zapotec analogue Cocijo share the distinctive goggled eyes and fanged mouth of the rain-god complex.

Attributes

Chaac is generally depicted with a reptilian or amphibian body covered in scales, with a long, pendulous nose like an elephant's trunk and curling fangs at the mouth. He often carries a lightning axe and a serpent, and water pours from his body or from vessels he holds. In the Maya codices Chaac appears in numerous aspects, sometimes as four Chaacs associated with the cardinal directions and their colours: the red Chaac of the east, the white Chaac of the north, the black Chaac of the west and the yellow Chaac of the south.

Worship

Rain was essential to Maya agriculture in the Yucatán, where surface water is scarce, so Chaac was invoked constantly. Caves and cenotes — natural wells in the limestone — were regarded as entrances to the watery underworld and as particular abodes of Chaac; offerings, and in some cases human sacrifices, were cast into cenotes such as the Sacred Cenote at Chichen Itza to petition the god for rain. A ceremony called cha-chaac is still practised in Yucatecan villages, in which young boys tied to the corners of an altar-table imitate frogs to call the rain.

Architectural sculpture of Chaac is especially prominent in the Puuc region, where palace façades at sites like Uxmal, Kabah and Sayil are covered with stacked mosaic masks of the god, their long noses projecting from the building corners.

In the codices

Chaac is designated "God B" in the Schellhas classification of deities in the Maya codices. He appears frequently in the Dresden Codex and Madrid Codex in almanacs concerning weather, agriculture, hunting and beekeeping, often shown pouring water from a jar, wielding his axe, or walking through rain.
