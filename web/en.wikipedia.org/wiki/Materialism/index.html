Materialism

Democritus (c. 460–370 BCE), with Leucippus the founder of atomism, an early materialist philosophy.

Materialism is a form of philosophical monism which holds that matter is the fundamental substance in nature, and that all things, including mental states and consciousness, are results of material interactions. According to the philosophical materialist theory, mind and consciousness are by-products or epiphenomena of material processes (such as the biochemistry of the human brain and nervous system), without which they cannot exist. This theory contrasts with idealism, in which mind and consciousness are first-order realities to which matter is subject.

History

Materialist ideas appear independently in several ancient traditions. In India, the Cārvāka school held that only the four elements perceived directly are real. In Greece, Leucippus and Democritus developed atomism — the doctrine that reality consists of indivisible atoms moving in the void — which Epicurus and later Lucretius elaborated into a complete philosophy free of divine intervention.

In the early modern period, Thomas Hobbes argued that everything, including thought, is matter in motion. French Enlightenment thinkers such as La Mettrie (L'Homme Machine, 1747), Diderot, and Baron d'Holbach (System of Nature, 1770) advanced systematic materialisms. In the 19th century Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels developed historical materialism and dialectical materialism, arguing that material conditions of production shape society, politics, and ideas; versions of this became the official philosophy of the Soviet Union and other communist states.

Contemporary forms

In contemporary philosophy of mind, materialism is usually framed as physicalism: the thesis that everything is physical, or that mental properties supervene on physical properties. Variants include reductive physicalism (type identity theory), functionalism, and eliminative materialism, which denies that folk-psychological categories such as "beliefs" will survive in a mature neuroscience. Critics object that materialism cannot account for subjective experience (qualia) — the "hard problem of consciousness" — or for intentionality and mathematical truth.

The term materialism is also used in a non-philosophical sense for a preoccupation with material possessions and consumption.
