A tablet computer, commonly shortened to tablet, is a mobile device, typically with a mobile operating system and touchscreen display circuitry, a rechargeable battery, and other components in a single, thin, flat package. Tablets, being computers, do what other personal computers do, but lack some input/output abilities that others have; they are usually operated by touching the display rather than with a physical keyboard. Most tablets use capacitive touchscreens and support multi-touch gestures, and many also support a stylus and detachable keyboard accessories.

History

The concept of a tablet long predates practical devices: Alan Kay described the Dynabook in 1968, and fictional tablets appeared in 2001: A Space Odyssey and Star Trek. Commercial pen-based tablets appeared in the late 1980s and 1990s, including the GRiDPad (1989) and devices based on Microsoft's Pen Computing software, while Apple's Newton MessagePad (1993) pioneered the personal digital assistant category. Microsoft promoted the "Tablet PC" platform from 2002, but these heavy, stylus-driven, desktop-OS devices remained niche products.

The modern tablet era began with the Apple iPad in April 2010. Running a phone-derived operating system optimized for finger touch, with a long battery life and an app marketplace, the iPad sold millions of units in its first year and defined the category. Competitors rapidly followed with Android tablets such as the Samsung Galaxy Tab, and Microsoft reoriented its Surface line (2012 onward) toward "2-in-1" detachable and convertible designs running full Windows. Amazon's Kindle Fire line popularized low-cost media tablets. Tablet sales peaked in the mid-2010s at well over 200 million units a year and have since stabilized, with tablets used heavily for media consumption, education, retail point-of-sale, field work, and as laptop complements.

Types

Slate tablets are single flat units without an integrated keyboard. Convertible and detachable (2-in-1) tablets combine a tablet with a keyboard that rotates, folds, or detaches. Mini tablets have displays around 7–8 inches, between large phones ("phablets") and full-size tablets of 10 inches or more. Specialized rugged tablets serve industrial, medical, and military users, while e-readers with E Ink displays are sometimes classed separately because of their limited general computing capability.

Software

Consumer tablets principally run Apple's iPadOS and Google's Android; Microsoft's Windows dominates detachable tablets intended to replace laptops. App distribution is dominated by platform app stores. Tablets support tasks including web browsing, email, media playback, games, drawing and note-taking with styluses, video conferencing, and increasingly full productivity software with external keyboards and pointing devices.
