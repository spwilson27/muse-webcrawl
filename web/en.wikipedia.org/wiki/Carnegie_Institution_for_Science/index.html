The Carnegie Institution for Science, also known as Carnegie Science, is an American non-profit organization dedicated to scientific discovery, established in Washington, D.C. in 1902 with an endowment of $10 million from industrialist and philanthropist Andrew Carnegie. Its charter directs it to encourage investigation, research and discovery, and to support exceptional individuals to pursue fundamental science.

History and research

From the outset the institution funded work across astronomy, geophysics, biology and other fields, often at long time-scales that universities or industry would not support. Its historical departments included the Mount Wilson Observatory (where Edwin Hubble established the expansion of the universe), the Department of Terrestrial Magnetism, the Geophysical Laboratory, and a genetics station at Cold Spring Harbor. Barbara McClintock conducted her Nobel Prize-winning work on genetic transposition at Carnegie. The institution was also involved historically in the controversial eugenics movement, from which it later distanced itself.

Current structure

Carnegie Science today organizes its research principally through departments devoted to astronomy (Carnegie Observatories in Pasadena, California, and Las Campanas Observatory in Chile), Earth and planetary science, and plant and global ecology (including the Department of Plant Biology at Stanford). It is a partner in the Giant Magellan Telescope project.
