Carbon offsets and credits

A carbon offset and a carbon credit are both instruments representing the reduction, avoidance or removal of greenhouse gas emissions, measured in tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂e). Credits are tradable certificates: a company or individual that emits greenhouse gases can purchase credits generated by a project elsewhere — such as reforestation, renewable energy, methane capture or energy efficiency — to compensate for its own emissions and claim progress toward targets such as net zero.

How offsets work

Offset projects are assessed against methodologies set by standards bodies and registries, which issue credits after third-party verification. Credits are then sold, traded, and ultimately "retired" by the buyer so they cannot be reused. One credit generally represents one metric tonne of CO₂e reduced or removed.

Compliance and voluntary markets

In compliance markets, credits are created under regulatory regimes such as the Kyoto Protocol's Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) and Joint Implementation, and are used by firms or countries to meet binding targets; Article 6 of the Paris Agreement establishes frameworks for internationally transferred mitigation outcomes. Voluntary markets, overseen by standards such as Verra's Verified Carbon Standard and the Gold Standard, serve companies and individuals offsetting voluntarily.

Quality criteria and criticism

An offset is considered sound only if the reduction is additional (would not have happened anyway), permanent, accurately quantified, independently verified and not double counted. Journalistic and academic investigations — notably into forest-protection (REDD+ style) credits — have alleged widespread over-crediting, leading to market reforms, new integrity initiatives such as the Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market, and sharp debate over whether offsets delay genuine emissions cuts. Supporters argue offsets channel finance to mitigation that would otherwise go unfunded.
