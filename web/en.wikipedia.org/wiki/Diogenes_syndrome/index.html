Diogenes syndrome

Diogenes syndrome, also known as senile squalor syndrome, is a condition characterised by extreme self-neglect, domestic squalor, social withdrawal, apathy, compulsive hoarding of garbage or animals, and a lack of shame about one's living conditions. It occurs most often in elderly people and is associated with dementia, frontal lobe dysfunction, and certain psychiatric disorders, although it can also be found in people with no clear psychiatric diagnosis. The condition is named after the ancient Greek philosopher Diogenes of Sinope, who lived in voluntary poverty — an analogy some commentators consider misleading, since Diogenes chose his way of life while patients with the syndrome do not.

History

Cases of gross self-neglect in the elderly were described in the medical literature in the 1960s, notably in a 1966 study by Macmillan and Shaw of patients admitted in a state of severe neglect. The term "Diogenes syndrome" was coined in 1975 by Allison Clark and colleagues in a paper in The Lancet studying elderly patients who lived in squalor despite often being of above-average intelligence and not impoverished.

Presentation

Affected individuals typically show profound neglect of personal hygiene and nutrition, live amid accumulated rubbish, refuse help from family, neighbours and services, and display indifference to their condition. Hoarding may involve refuse, newspapers or large numbers of animals (sometimes termed "animal hoarding" or Noah syndrome). Many patients are discovered only when illness, a fall or complaints from neighbours bring them to official attention, and mortality is high following hospital admission.

Causes and management

The syndrome is not a standalone diagnosis in the DSM-5; it is regarded as a behavioural manifestation of underlying conditions such as dementia — particularly frontotemporal dementia — obsessive-compulsive disorder, schizophrenia, or personality change from stroke or head injury. Management focuses on treating the underlying condition, careful capacity assessment, community nursing and social support, and gradual, consent-based cleaning, since forced clear-outs frequently cause distress and relapse.
