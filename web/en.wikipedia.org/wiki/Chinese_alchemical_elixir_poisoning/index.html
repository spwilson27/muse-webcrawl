Chinese alchemical elixir poisoning

Chinese alchemical elixir poisoning refers to the deaths and illnesses caused by the immortality elixirs prepared in Chinese alchemy (waidan, "external alchemy"). Seeking eternal life, emperors, nobles, and scholars consumed elixirs whose principal ingredients often included mercury, lead, arsenic, and other highly toxic substances — most notably cinnabar (mercuric sulfide).

Background

External alchemy flourished from the Han dynasty onward. Because gold and cinnabar did not corrode or decay, alchemists reasoned that consuming compounds made from them would confer incorruptibility on the body. The first emperor, Qin Shi Huang, is said to have died in 210 BCE after taking mercury pills prescribed by his court alchemists in pursuit of immortality.

Victims

A number of Tang dynasty emperors — among them Xianzong, Muzong, Jingzong, Wuzong, and Xuanzong — are recorded as having died from elixir poisoning, often after exhibiting erratic behaviour consistent with heavy-metal poisoning. The Jiajing Emperor of the Ming dynasty was also a devoted consumer of elixirs. Rationalist scholars such as the Tang writer Han Yu criticised the practice, and over time Chinese alchemy shifted toward "internal alchemy" (neidan), meditation-based practices that reinterpreted elixir ingredients symbolically.
