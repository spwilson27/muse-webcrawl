Housing cooperative

A housing cooperative, or housing co-op, is a legal entity, usually a cooperative or corporation, that owns real estate consisting of residential buildings. Each resident is a shareholder in the cooperative, and ownership of a share confers the right to occupy a specific housing unit under a proprietary lease or occupancy agreement. Housing cooperatives are a form of home ownership distinct from condominiums, in which residents own their units directly.

Structure and governance

Residents buy shares corresponding to their unit and pay monthly maintenance charges covering the building's mortgage, taxes, insurance and upkeep. An elected board of directors, drawn from the shareholders, manages the property and typically must approve prospective buyers, giving co-ops greater control over membership than condominiums. Cooperatives operate on cooperative principles of democratic member control and are run on a non-profit or limited-equity basis; limited-equity co-ops restrict resale prices to preserve affordability.

Distribution

The model is widespread in several countries. In Sweden, the bostadsrätt tenant-ownership form houses about a fifth of the population; Finland and Norway have large cooperative sectors. In the United States, cooperatives are concentrated in New York City, where co-op apartments long outnumbered condominiums, and also appear in Chicago, Washington, D.C. and other cities, including student housing cooperatives. Related forms include mutual housing associations and community land trusts.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include affordability (especially in limited-equity co-ops), resident control and community stability. Drawbacks include board approval hurdles, restrictions on subletting and financing, and the risk to individual shareholders of the cooperative's collective obligations.
