Gear

A gear is a rotating circular machine part having cut teeth or, in the case of a cogwheel or gearwheel, inserted teeth (called cogs), which mesh with another (compatible) toothed part to transmit rotational power. While doing so, they can change the torque and rotational speed of the driven part and the direction or axis of rotation. Gears are among the oldest and most fundamental components of machinery.

Meshing spur gears: the gear ratio equals the ratio of their tooth counts.

Principles

For two meshing gears, the gear ratio is the ratio of the numbers of teeth: the driven gear rotates at the input speed multiplied by the ratio of the driving gear's teeth to its own teeth, while torque changes in inverse proportion. Gears reverse the direction of rotation between parallel shafts unless an idler gear is interposed. Backlash — a small clearance between meshing teeth — prevents binding but introduces lost motion.

Types

  • Spur gears: straight teeth on parallel shafts; simple and common, but noisy at high speed.
  • Helical gears: angled teeth engaging gradually, giving smoother, quieter operation.
  • Bevel gears: conical gears connecting intersecting shafts, as in a differential.
  • Worm gears: a screw-like worm driving a wheel; high reduction ratios, often self-locking.
  • Rack and pinion: a circular pinion driving a straight rack, converting rotation to linear motion (used in steering).
  • Planetary (epicyclic) gears: sun, planet and ring gears, used in automatic transmissions and hub gears.

History

Toothed wheels appear in China by the 4th century BC and in the Hellenistic world; the Antikythera mechanism (c. 100 BC) used intricate bronze gearing to model astronomical cycles. Gears drove watermills, clocks, and the machinery of the Industrial Revolution, and remain central to vehicles, turbines, robotics and precision instruments. Most modern gear teeth use an involute profile, which keeps the velocity ratio constant even with slight centre-distance errors.
