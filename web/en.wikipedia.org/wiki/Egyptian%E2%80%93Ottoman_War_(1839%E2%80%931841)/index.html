Egyptian–Ottoman War (1839–1841)

Date1839 – 1841
LocationSyria, Anatolia, Lebanon, the Levant
BelligerentsEgypt under Muhammad Ali vs the Ottoman Empire, later backed by Britain, Austria, Prussia, and Russia
ResultConvention of London (1840); Egypt evacuated Syria; Muhammad Ali recognised as hereditary ruler of Egypt

The Egyptian–Ottoman War of 1839–1841, also called the Second Egyptian–Ottoman War or Second Turko-Egyptian War, was a conflict between the Ottoman Empire and the forces of Muhammad Ali of Egypt, the empire's rebellious vassal whose son Ibrahim Pasha had occupied Syria after the first war of 1831–1833.

Course of the war

In April 1839 the Ottoman army invaded Syria hoping to recover the province, but on 24 June 1839 Ibrahim Pasha decisively defeated it at the Battle of Nezib. Days later the Ottoman Sultan Mahmud II died, and the Ottoman fleet defected to Alexandria, leaving the empire apparently helpless. However, the European great powers feared the collapse of the Ottoman Empire and the expansion of Russian influence.

Intervention and settlement

By the Convention of London (15 July 1840), Britain, Austria, Prussia, and Russia offered Muhammad Ali hereditary rule of Egypt if he withdrew from Syria; when he delayed, an Anglo-Austrian-Ottoman force bombarded Beirut and captured Acre in November 1840. Muhammad Ali accepted the terms. Under the settlement of 1841 he retained hereditary governorship of Egypt and Sudan but evacuated Syria, Arabia, and Crete, and reduced his army.

Significance

The crisis, part of the wider "Eastern Question", confirmed Britain's commitment to preserving the Ottoman Empire and marked the effective end of Muhammad Ali's bid for an independent Arab empire, while securing his dynasty in Egypt until 1952.
