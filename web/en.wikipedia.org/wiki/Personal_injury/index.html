Personal injury

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Personal injury is a legal term for an injury to the body, mind or emotions, as opposed to injury to property. In common-law jurisdictions, personal injury claims are usually brought in tort — most often negligence (road accidents, workplace accidents, medical accidents), but also assault, product liability and occupiers' liability — seeking damages for pain and suffering, loss of earnings, care costs and other losses. The phrase also covers the legal practice area and, in some places, no-fault compensation schemes for motor accidents.

The burden of litigation, delay and uncompensated victims led many jurisdictions to supplement or replace tort: New Zealand's Accident Compensation Corporation (from 1974) provides no-fault cover for personal injury and bars most lawsuits; workers' compensation schemes do the same for employment injuries in many countries. In systems retaining tort, damages for non-pecuniary loss are assessed by guidelines and precedent tariffs, and insurance — compulsory for drivers and employers — funds most awards. Limitation periods typically run from the injury or its discoverability, with extensions for children and latent disease.
