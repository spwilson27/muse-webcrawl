Reservation (law)

[Image: A delegate signing a multilateral treaty at a signing ceremony — states frequently attach reservations when signing or ratifying]

In international law, a reservation is a unilateral statement by which a state, when signing, ratifying or acceding to a treaty, purports to exclude or modify the legal effect of certain provisions of the treaty in their application to that state.

Vienna Convention rules

The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969) permits reservations unless the treaty prohibits them, allows only specified reservations, or the reservation is incompatible with the object and purpose of the treaty. Other parties may accept a reservation or object to it; an objection does not normally prevent the treaty entering into force between the two states unless the objecting state definitely expresses that intention.

Effect

A reservation modifies the treaty obligations reciprocally: the reserving state is not bound by the reserved provision, and other parties are not bound by it in their relations with the reserving state. Human rights treaties have generated particular debate, since their monitoring bodies have claimed a power to assess the validity of reservations and to sever invalid ones, keeping the state bound by the treaty as a whole.

Related terms

Reservations are distinguished from declarations (statements of interpretation that do not purport to modify obligations) and from derogations (temporary suspensions of obligations permitted, for example, in public emergencies).
