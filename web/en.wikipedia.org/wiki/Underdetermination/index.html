Underdetermination

In the philosophy of science, underdetermination is the thesis that the available evidence does not uniquely determine which scientific theory to accept: for any body of evidence there may exist rival theories that fit it equally well yet are incompatible with one another. The idea is associated with Pierre Duhem and, in a stronger form, with W. V. O. Quine.

Forms of the thesis

Contrastive underdetermination holds that evidence cannot by itself decide between a given theory and a specific rival. Holistic underdetermination, in the Duhem–Quine form, holds that hypotheses are tested only in bundles with auxiliary assumptions, so when a prediction fails, logic alone does not say which element of the bundle to blame. Quine further suggested that any statement can be retained in the face of recalcitrant experience if suitable adjustments are made elsewhere in one's web of belief.

Arguments and responses

Supporters point to episodes such as the historical rivalry between Ptolemaic and Copernican astronomy in their early, equally predictive forms, and to the ease of constructing empirically equivalent rivals by adding compensating hypotheses. Critics reply that genuinely equivalent rivals over all possible evidence are rare or contrived; that scientists legitimately use virtues such as simplicity, scope and explanatory unity to choose; and that underdetermination is usually transient, disappearing as new kinds of evidence accumulate. Larry Laudan and others distinguish the logical possibility of underdetermination from its practical force in actual science.

Relation to other problems

Underdetermination underlies many arguments for scientific anti-realism, some forms of the pessimistic induction, and discussions of theory choice in the work of Thomas Kuhn. It is also related to the problem of induction, though distinct: induction concerns inference beyond observed cases, underdetermination concerns the multiplicity of adequate total theories.
