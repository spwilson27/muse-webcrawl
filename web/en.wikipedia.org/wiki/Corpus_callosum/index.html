Corpus callosum

The corpus callosum (Latin for "tough body"), also called the callosal commissure, is a wide, thick nerve tract — a flat bundle of commissural fibers — beneath the cerebral cortex in the brain. It spans part of the longitudinal fissure, connecting the left and right cerebral hemispheres and enabling communication between them. It is the largest white matter structure in the human brain, about 10 cm in length and consisting of roughly 200–300 million axonal projections.

Structure

From front to back the corpus callosum is divided into the rostrum, genu, trunk (body), and splenium. Axons pass between corresponding regions of the two hemispheres: fibers through the genu and rostrum connect prefrontal areas, while the splenium carries visual and parietal fibers. It develops from the commissural plate in the first trimester of fetal life.

Function and clinical significance

The corpus callosum integrates motor, sensory, and cognitive information between the hemispheres. Surgical sectioning (corpus callosotomy), sometimes used to treat severe epilepsy, produces the split-brain phenomenon studied by Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga, revealing functional specialisation of the hemispheres. Congenital absence, called agenesis of the corpus callosum, is among the more common brain malformations and has variable cognitive effects. The structure is also affected in multiple sclerosis and Marchiafava–Bignami disease.
