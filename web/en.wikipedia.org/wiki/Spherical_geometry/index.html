Spherical geometry

Spherical geometry or spherics is the geometry of the two-dimensional surface of a sphere. It is an example of a geometry that is not Euclidean: the role of straight lines is played by great circles, and two great circles always intersect, so there are no parallel lines and Euclid's parallel postulate fails.

Properties

In spherical geometry, the angles of a triangle sum to more than 180°, with the excess proportional to the triangle's area. The shortest path between two points on a sphere is an arc of a great circle, which is why long-distance air routes appear curved on flat maps. Antipodal points — opposite ends of a diameter — function like single points in the related elliptic geometry.

Applications

Spherical geometry underlies navigation, astronomy and geodesy: positions on Earth are given by latitude and longitude, and computations of distance and bearing between them use spherical trigonometry. The subject was developed in antiquity by Greek mathematicians such as Theodosius of Bithynia and Menelaus of Alexandria, long before it was recognised as a non-Euclidean geometry.
