Pentelic marble
SourceMount Pentelicus, Attica, Greece
ColourUniform white, acquiring a golden patina
Key usesParthenon, Propylaea, Erechtheion, Theseion

Pentelic marble is a fine-grained, pure-white marble quarried from Mount Pentelicus (Pentelikon), northeast of Athens in Attica, Greece. It was the principal building stone of classical Athens: the Parthenon, the Propylaea, the Erechtheion, and much of the Hephaisteion (Theseion) were built entirely of it. Fresh Pentelic marble is uniformly milky white; exposed to the weather, trace iron in the stone oxidises and gives the surface a soft golden hue, famously visible on the Parthenon.

Quarries

The ancient quarries lie on the southwest flank of Mount Pentelicus at an altitude of roughly 400–700 m, in the area now called Spilia ("cave"), after a great vaulted cavern near the main workings. A paved quarry road, along which blocks were slid and hauled by mule teams some 16 km to Athens, can still be traced in part. More than 25 ancient quarry pits have been identified. Quarrying began in the early 5th century BC; the stone was exported across the Mediterranean in Roman times and used for statuary as well as architecture.

Modern use

The quarries are still worked today — uniquely, one designated area supplies marble for the ongoing restoration of the Acropolis monuments, so that repairs match the original fabric in composition and appearance. Pentelic marble has also been used in modern Athens landmarks such as the Academy of Athens and the Panathenaic Stadium renovation.

Characteristics

The stone is a calcitic marble of very fine, uniform grain with slight translucency, taking a crisp carved detail and a smooth polish. Its faint greenish-grey banding (from chlorite and epidote veins) is minimal in the best beds. It is distinguished from the coarser island marble of Paros, which was preferred for freestanding sculpture.
