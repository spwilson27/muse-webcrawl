Hydropneumatic suspension

Hydropneumatic suspension is a type of motor vehicle suspension system, designed by Paul Magès and invented by Citroën, and fitted to Citroën cars, as well as being used under licence by other car manufacturers. Similar systems are also widely used on modern tanks and other large military vehicles. The suspension was also used on the Rolls-Royce Silver Shadow and the Bentley T-series.

Principle

The purpose of this system is to provide a sensitive, dynamic and high-capacity suspension that offers superior ride quality on a variety of surfaces. A hydropneumatic system combines the advantages of two technological principles: hydraulic systems use torque multiplication in an easy way, independent of the distance between the input and output, and pneumatic systems are based on the fact that gas is compressible, so the equipment is less subjected to shock and vibration damage.

Instead of a conventional steel spring, each wheel is connected to a sphere containing a pressurised gas, usually nitrogen, separated from the hydraulic fluid by a flexible membrane. As the wheel rises over a bump, hydraulic fluid is forced into the sphere, compressing the gas and providing the springing action. A pump driven by the engine maintains pressure in the system, and a height corrector keeps the vehicle at a constant ride height regardless of load.

History

The system was first presented on the rear suspension of the Citroën Traction Avant in 1954, and reached its full form in the revolutionary Citroën DS of 1955, where it also powered the brakes, steering and clutch. Later developments included the computer-controlled Hydractive system, introduced on the XM in 1989, which used electronic sensors and additional spheres to switch between soft and firm modes. Citroën phased out hydropneumatic suspension in the 2010s, ending production with the C5 in 2017.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include self-levelling, adjustable ride height, an exceptionally smooth ride, and reduced body roll. Disadvantages are complexity, the need for specialised maintenance, and the cost of components compared with conventional coil springs and dampers.
