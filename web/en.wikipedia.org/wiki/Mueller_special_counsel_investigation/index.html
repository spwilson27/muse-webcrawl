The Mueller special counsel investigation was a United States Department of Justice investigation into Russian interference in the 2016 presidential election, links between associates of Donald Trump and Russian officials, and possible obstruction of justice. It was led by former FBI director Robert Mueller as special counsel from May 2017 until March 2019.

[Image: Robert Mueller testifying before Congress in 2019 about the investigation]

Origins

The investigation began as an FBI counterintelligence inquiry, code-named Crossfire Hurricane, opened in July 2016. After President Trump dismissed FBI Director James Comey in May 2017, Deputy Attorney General Rod Rosenstein — acting because Attorney General Jeff Sessions had recused himself — appointed Mueller to take over.

Scope and indictments

The probe charged 34 individuals and three companies. Indictments of Russian nationals and organizations, including the Internet Research Agency, described a social-media influence campaign and the hacking and leaking of Democratic Party emails. Several Trump associates pleaded guilty or were convicted, including Paul Manafort, Rick Gates, Michael Flynn, George Papadopoulos, and Roger Stone, as well as Michael Cohen on related charges referred to prosecutors in New York.

The Mueller report

The final report, released in redacted form in April 2019, had two volumes. Volume I concluded that Russia interfered in a sweeping and systematic fashion, but did not establish that members of the Trump campaign conspired or coordinated with the Russian government. Volume II examined possible obstruction of justice and, citing policy against indicting a sitting president, declined to reach a traditional prosecutorial judgment, stating that the report did not exonerate him. Attorney General William Barr concluded the evidence was insufficient to establish obstruction.

Reactions and aftermath

The investigation dominated American politics for two years. Supporters saw it as a necessary check on foreign interference; the President repeatedly denounced it as a partisan witch hunt. Mueller testified before two House committees in July 2019, and many of those convicted later received pardons or commutations.
