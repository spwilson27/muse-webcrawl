Jeu de paume

Origin: France, medieval period
Equipment: Ball; hand, glove, or racket
Descendants: Real tennis, lawn tennis, basque pelota variants
Olympic: 1908 (as jeu de paume, real tennis)

Jeu de paume (French for "palm game"), also called jeu de paulme in older spelling, is a French ball-and-court game that originated in medieval France and is the ancestor of real tennis and, through it, of modern lawn tennis. Players struck a ball over a net or cord with the palm of the hand, later with gloves, bats, and finally rackets.

History

The game emerged in monastic cloisters and town squares in France by the 12th–13th centuries and became immensely popular among royalty and townspeople alike; by the 16th century Paris alone had hundreds of courts. Two forms developed: the outdoor longue paume, played on open ground, and the indoor courte paume, played in enclosed courts with galleries and sloping roofs — the direct ancestor of real tennis. Rackets replaced the hand during the 16th century, and professional players, organized instruction, and wagering flourished. The game declined in France after the 18th century but survives as real tennis in Britain, the United States, Australia, and France.

Cultural significance

The Salle du Jeu de Paume at Versailles was the venue of the Tennis Court Oath of June 20, 1789, a pivotal event of the French Revolution, in which deputies of the Third Estate vowed not to disperse until France had a constitution. The phrase tennis itself is thought to derive from the French tenez ("take [this]"), called by the server.
