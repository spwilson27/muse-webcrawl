Severino Di Giovanni

Born: 17 March 1901, Chieti, Italy
Died: 1 February 1931 (aged 29), Buenos Aires, Argentina
Cause of death: Execution by firing squad
Known for: Anarchist activism and expropriations in Argentina

Severino Di Giovanni (17 March 1901 – 1 February 1931) was an Italian anarchist who immigrated to Argentina and became the most notorious figure of the "expropriator" anarchist current there, carrying out a campaign of bombings and robberies in the 1920s before his capture and execution under the military regime of José Félix Uriburu.

Early life and emigration

Born in Chieti, Abruzzo, Di Giovanni grew up amid the poverty of post-war Italy, was orphaned young, and became a typographer and self-taught anarchist. With the rise of Fascism, he emigrated in 1923 to Argentina, settling in Buenos Aires, where he worked as a printer, joined the Italian anarchist milieu, and founded the newspaper Culmine (1925), which championed violent direct action and polemicised sharply with the more moderate anarchist press.

Campaign of action

Di Giovanni's group financed itself and its propaganda through armed robberies ("expropriations") and carried out bomb attacks against symbols of United States and Italian Fascist power, especially after the execution of Sacco and Vanzetti in 1927. Targets included the United States embassy and consulates, Ford Motor Company premises, and the Italian consulate in Buenos Aires, where a 1928 bombing killed several bystanders — an atrocity that divided the anarchist movement, many of whose papers condemned him. His companion was América Scarfò, sister of the anarchists Paulino and Alejandro Scarfò.

Capture and execution

After the September 1930 coup, the Uriburu dictatorship intensified repression of anarchists. Di Giovanni was captured in January 1931 after a shootout, tried by a military tribunal, and executed by firing squad on 1 February 1931, reportedly crying "Evviva l'Anarchia!" His accomplice Paulino Scarfò was executed the following day. The executions were widely reported and protested, and Di Giovanni became a lasting, controversial figure in Argentine and anarchist memory — the subject of Osvaldo Bayer's classic study Severino Di Giovanni, el idealista de la violencia (1970).
