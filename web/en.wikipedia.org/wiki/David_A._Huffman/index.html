David A. Huffman

David Albert Huffman (August 9, 1925 – October 7, 1999) was an American pioneer of computer science, known for his invention of Huffman coding, the optimal lossless data compression algorithm still used in ZIP files, JPEG images, and countless other formats. He also made foundational contributions to the mathematical theory of paper folding (computational origami).

Huffman coding

In 1951, as a graduate student at MIT in a course taught by Robert Fano, Huffman chose to solve a term-paper problem on finding the most efficient binary code rather than take the final exam. Working for months before a breakthrough, he developed the bottom-up method of building an optimal prefix code tree, publishing it in 1952 as "A Method for the Construction of Minimum-Redundancy Codes." The algorithm outperformed the Shannon–Fano method and became a cornerstone of information theory. Huffman never patented his work.

Career and origami

Huffman joined the MIT faculty in 1953, moved to the University of California, Santa Cruz in 1967, and helped found its computer science department, serving as chair from 1970 to 1973. From the 1970s he produced influential work on the geometry of curved-crease paper folding, creating intricate folded-paper sculptures. He received the IEEE Computer Society's Computer Pioneer Award and the IEEE Hamming Medal (1999).
