Presumption of innocence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The presumption of innocence is a legal principle that every person accused of any crime is considered innocent until proven guilty. The prosecution must therefore prove a defendant's guilt; the defendant has no burden to prove innocence. It is a fundamental right in modern democracies and many legal systems, enshrined in Article 11 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (Art. 14(2)), the European Convention on Human Rights (Art. 6(2)) and numerous constitutions.

Content

Connected doctrines include proof beyond reasonable doubt, the right to silence and against self-incrimination, disclosure of the prosecution's evidence, and restrictions on prejudicial publicity. In Woolmington v DPP (1935), the House of Lords called the prosecution's duty to prove guilt the "golden thread" running through English criminal law. Exceptions and tensions arise with regulatory offences, reverse burdens on specific defences, pre-trial detention, asset-freezing regimes and media reporting — each balancing public protection against the principle's protective purpose.
