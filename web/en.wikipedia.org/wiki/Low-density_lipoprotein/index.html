Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) is one of the five major groups of lipoproteins that transport all fat molecules around the body in extracellular water. LDL delivers cholesterol to peripheral tissues and is the particle most closely associated with the development of atherosclerosis, hence its popular description as carrying "bad cholesterol".

Structure and metabolism

Each LDL particle consists of a core of cholesteryl esters and triglycerides surrounded by a shell of phospholipids, free cholesterol and a single copy of apolipoprotein B-100, which is recognised by LDL receptors on cell surfaces. LDL arises in the circulation from the progressive lipolysis of very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) secreted by the liver. Cells take up LDL by receptor-mediated endocytosis; the receptor (whose discovery earned Michael Brown and Joseph Goldstein the 1985 Nobel Prize) is recycled to the surface, while the particle is degraded in lysosomes. Genetic defects in the LDL receptor cause familial hypercholesterolaemia.

Clinical significance

Elevated LDL cholesterol is a major causal risk factor for coronary heart disease and stroke. Guidelines generally consider LDL-C below 100 mg/dL desirable, with lower targets for high-risk patients. LDL levels are lowered by diet, exercise, statins, ezetimibe, bile-acid sequestrants and PCSK9 inhibitors. Oxidation and retention of LDL particles within the arterial wall are central events in plaque formation.
