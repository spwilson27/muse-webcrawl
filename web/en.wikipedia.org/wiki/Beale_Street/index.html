Beale Street is a street in Downtown Memphis, Tennessee, running from the Mississippi River eastwards for about 1.8 miles. From the 1860s it was the commercial and cultural heart of Memphis's African American community, and it occupies a central place in the history of the blues: musicians including W. C. Handy, B. B. King, Muddy Waters and Howlin' Wolf performed in its clubs, and Handy published "Memphis Blues" (1912) and "Beale Street Blues" (1916) from here. In 1966 Beale Street was declared a National Historic Landmark, and in 1977 the United States Congress declared it the "Home of the Blues."

History

The street was laid out in the 1840s and named for Edward H. Beale, a Mexican–American War hero. After the Civil War it became a thriving district of Black-owned businesses, theatres and churches; Robert Church, America's first Black millionaire, built Church Park and an auditorium on the street, and Ida B. Wells published her anti-lynching journalism from offices there. In the early twentieth century its clubs, pawnshops and gambling rooms made it the capital of the Delta blues scene. The district declined from the 1940s — urban renewal demolitions in the 1960s and 1970s closed or razed much of it — before a redevelopment from the 1980s restored Beale as a music and entertainment district anchored by venues such as B. B. King's Blues Club.

Today

The historic entertainment district, roughly three blocks long, is closed to traffic at night and hosts live blues, soul and rock music, restaurants, and events including the Beale Street Music Festival, part of the Memphis in May celebrations. Statues and markers commemorate Handy, B. B. King and Elvis Presley, who absorbed the street's music as a teenager.
