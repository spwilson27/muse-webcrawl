A company voluntary arrangement (CVA) is a formal insolvency procedure under Part I of the Insolvency Act 1986 in the United Kingdom that allows a financially troubled company to reach a legally binding compromise with its unsecured creditors — for example, paying a proportion of its debts over time, or restructuring obligations — while continuing to trade, usually under its existing directors.

Procedure

Directors (or an administrator or liquidator, if one is in office) prepare a proposal with the help of a licensed insolvency practitioner, who acts first as nominee and reports to the court. Creditors vote on the proposal: it passes if approved by at least 75% in value of the creditors voting, provided not more than half by value of unconnected creditors vote against. Once approved, the arrangement binds all unsecured creditors who were entitled to vote, including those who dissented or did not receive notice, and the nominee becomes the supervisor overseeing implementation.

Use and controversy

CVAs became a prominent restructuring tool for British retailers and restaurant chains in the late 2010s, frequently used to close stores and reduce rents. Landlords have criticised "landlord-only" CVAs that compromise lease liabilities while other creditors are paid in full, and several high-profile arrangements were challenged in court. Compared with administration, a CVA leaves management in control and is cheaper, but it cannot bind secured or preferential creditors without their consent. The Corporate Insolvency and Governance Act 2020 added a separate restructuring plan and a freestanding moratorium, reshaping the toolkit around CVAs.
