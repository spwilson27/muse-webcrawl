Mixtec codices

Mixtec codices are pre-Columbian and early colonial pictorial manuscripts produced by the Mixtec people of the La Mixteca region of Oaxaca, Mexico. Painted on deerskin screenfolds coated with a polished white gesso, they record dynastic histories — the genealogies, marriages, births, wars, and deaths of the ruling lines of Mixtec kingdoms — alongside calendrical and ritual information.

Major surviving codices

Only a handful of pre-conquest Mixtec codices survive, most now held in European museums and libraries. They include the Codex Zouche-Nuttall, Codex Bodley, Codex Selden, Codex Vindobonensis Mexicanus I, and the Codex Colombino-Becker. Their modern names usually reflect former owners or the libraries where they are kept.

Content and reading

The codices are read along red guide lines in a boustrophedon path. Figures are drawn in profile with name glyphs attached; dates are given in the 52-year Mesoamerican calendar using year signs and day signs of the 260-day ritual calendar. The biography of Lord 8 Deer "Jaguar Claw" of Tilantongo dominates several of the manuscripts. Scholarship by Alfonso Caso in the mid-20th century first deciphered the historical content by matching the genealogies against colonial-era documents and maps, and later researchers such as Mary Elizabeth Smith refined the geographical interpretation of the place signs.

Colonial continuations

After the Spanish conquest, Mixtec scribes continued painting in the traditional style, sometimes adding alphabetic glosses in Mixtec, Nahuatl, or Spanish; such documents were used as evidence in colonial land litigation. Most original codices, however, were destroyed during the evangelisation campaigns.
