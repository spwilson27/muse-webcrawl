Album-equivalent unit

Music industry metric
MeasuresAlbum consumption
ComponentsAlbum sales; track-equivalent albums (TEA); streaming-equivalent albums (SEA)
Used byBillboard, RIAA, BPI and other chart/certification bodies

The album-equivalent unit, or album consumption unit, is a measurement unit in the music industry that defines the consumption of music that equals the purchase of one album copy. This consumption includes streaming and song downloads in addition to traditional album sales.

Background

The metric emerged in the 2010s as streaming displaced the album as the dominant form of music consumption, leaving charts and certification programs that counted only physical and digital album sales increasingly unrepresentative. By combining several consumption formats into a single figure, chart compilers sought a measure of an album's overall popularity.

Methodology

An album-equivalent unit is generally calculated from three components:

  • Album sales — one album sold (physical or digital) counts as one unit.
  • Track-equivalent albums (TEA) — typically, ten permanent downloads of individual songs from an album count as one unit.
  • Streaming-equivalent albums (SEA) — a fixed number of on-demand streams of an album's songs counts as one unit. Under rules used for the Billboard 200 in the late 2010s, 1,250 premium (paid) streams or 3,750 ad-supported streams equaled one album unit.

Exact ratios vary by country and chart, and are periodically revised. In the United States, the Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA) incorporated streaming and track sales into its Gold and Platinum album certifications beginning in 2013 for singles and 2016 for albums.

Usage

Album-equivalent units underpin the modern Billboard 200 chart in the United States, the albums charts of the United Kingdom's Official Charts Company, and similar rankings worldwide. The metric has been debated within the industry: critics argue the conversion ratios are arbitrary and can distort the chart in favor of albums with one or two hit singles, while supporters contend it best reflects how audiences actually consume music.
