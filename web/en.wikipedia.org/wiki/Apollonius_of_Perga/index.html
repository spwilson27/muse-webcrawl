Apollonius of Perga (Greek: Ἀπολλώνιος ὁ Περγαῖος; c. 240 – c. 190 BC) was an ancient Greek geometer and astronomer known for his work on conic sections. His treatise Conics is one of the greatest works of ancient mathematics, and his terminology — ellipse, parabola and hyperbola — remains in use today. He has been called "the Great Geometer."

Life

Little is known of Apollonius's life. Born at Perga in Pamphylia, he studied at Alexandria under the successors of Euclid and spent time at Pergamum, where he corresponded with King Attalus I and the geometer Eudemus. His surviving prefaces to the books of the Conics are the main sources for his biography.

Conics

The Conics comprised eight books, of which the first four survive in Greek and the next three in Arabic translation; the eighth is lost. Earlier writers had generated the conic curves from three different right cones; Apollonius showed that all of them arise by slicing a single (possibly oblique) cone with planes at different angles, and he systematically derived the properties of the curves from their "symptoms" — relations equivalent to their modern equations. Book V treats normals and maxima and minima; Book VII deals with conjugate diameters. The work so thoroughly exhausted its subject that it was little advanced upon until the analytic geometry of Descartes and Fermat in the 17th century, and Kepler's discovery that planets move in ellipses gave the curves physical significance.

Other work

Apollonius is credited with the problem of constructing a circle tangent to three given circles (the "problem of Apollonius"), reconstructed by later commentators and solved by Viète. In astronomy he developed the theory of epicycles and eccentric motion to account for planetary movement, work later used by Ptolemy. He also improved notation for large numbers and studied irrational lines.
