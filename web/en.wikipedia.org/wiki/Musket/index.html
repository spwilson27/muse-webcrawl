Musket

A musket is a muzzle-loaded long gun that appeared as a smoothbore weapon in the early 16th century, at first as a heavier variant of the arquebus, capable of penetrating heavy armour. By the mid-18th century most infantry soldiers were musketeers. The musket was superseded in the mid-19th century by the breech-loading rifle, whose rifled barrel and Minié ball gave far greater range and accuracy.

History

The earliest muskets were heavy matchlock weapons fired from a forked rest, used by Spanish troops in the Italian Wars of the early 1500s. As locks improved — wheellock, snaphance, and finally the true flintlock around 1610 — the musket became lighter and more reliable. The invention of the socket bayonet in the late 17th century let musketeers defend themselves against cavalry, eliminating the pikeman and making the musket-armed infantryman the universal soldier of the 18th century. Standardised patterns such as the British "Brown Bess" (Land Pattern Musket) and the French Charleville remained in service for many decades.

Characteristics

Most military muskets were smoothbore and of large calibre, typically .69 to .80 inch (17.5–20 mm), firing a round lead ball. Lacking rear sights and firing an undersized ball that bounced down the barrel, they were inaccurate beyond about 100 metres; tactics therefore emphasised massed volley fire by lines or columns, with trained soldiers managing roughly three to four shots per minute using paper cartridges. The percussion cap, adopted by major armies in the 1830s–1840s, was the last major improvement before rifled muskets and breech-loaders displaced the type.

Use today

Muskets survive in ceremonial drill, historical reenactment, and muzzleloading sport shooting and hunting, and original examples are widely held in museums.
