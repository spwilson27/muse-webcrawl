Closed shop

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A closed shop is a form of union security agreement under which an employer agrees to hire only members of a particular trade union and to require continued membership as a condition of employment. The pre-entry closed shop (only existing union members may be hired) was once common in British printing, dock work and construction and in American craft unions; it was outlawed in the United States by the Taft–Hartley Act of 1947, and in the United Kingdom was progressively restricted (1980s legislation requiring ballots and compensation) and then banned outright by the Employment Act 1990.

The milder post-entry closed shop (join after hiring) and the agency shop (pay fees without joining) survive in some systems, while “right-to-work” laws in a majority of US states ban even agency fees in the private sector, and Janus v. AFSCME (2018) extended the no-fair-share-fee rule to all US public employment on First Amendment grounds. Defenders of union security invoke the free-rider problem (unions must represent non-members by law in the US system); opponents invoke freedom of association's negative side — the right not to associate — which the European Court of Human Rights has protected against closed shops (Young, James and Webster, 1981; Sørensen and Rasmussen, 2006).
