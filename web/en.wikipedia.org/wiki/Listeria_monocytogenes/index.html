Listeria monocytogenes is a species of pathogenic Gram-positive bacterium that causes the infection listeriosis. It is a facultative anaerobic, rod-shaped bacterium capable of surviving in the presence or absence of oxygen, and unusually among foodborne pathogens, it can grow at refrigeration temperatures (4 °C). It is named after Joseph Lister, and for the monocytosis it provokes in some animal hosts.

Biology

The bacterium is motile at room temperature by means of peritrichous flagella, producing a characteristic end-over-end "tumbling" motility, but does not express flagella at body temperature. Within host cells it polymerises actin filaments using the surface protein ActA, propelling itself through the cytoplasm and spreading directly from cell to cell without entering the extracellular environment — a strategy that shelters it from antibodies and complement.

Pathogenesis

L. monocytogenes invades intestinal epithelial cells using surface proteins known as internalins (InlA and InlB) that bind host receptors. It escapes the phagosome with the pore-forming toxin listeriolysin O. In healthy adults, infection usually causes a self-limiting febrile gastroenteritis, but in pregnant women, neonates, the elderly and immunocompromised people it can cause invasive disease: septicaemia, meningitis and encephalitis, with a case-fatality rate of roughly 20–30 per cent.

Foodborne transmission

The organism is widespread in soil, water, decaying vegetation and the gut of many animals. Human infection is overwhelmingly foodborne, associated with unpasteurised milk and soft cheeses, ready-to-eat deli meats, hot dogs, smoked seafood and, in a notable 2011 United States outbreak, cantaloupe melons. Because it grows at refrigerator temperatures and tolerates high salt concentrations, it is a persistent problem in food-processing environments, where it forms biofilms on surfaces.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is confirmed by culture of blood or cerebrospinal fluid. The bacterium is intrinsically resistant to cephalosporins; the treatment of choice for invasive listeriosis is high-dose ampicillin or amoxicillin, often combined with gentamicin, with trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole as an alternative for penicillin-allergic patients.
