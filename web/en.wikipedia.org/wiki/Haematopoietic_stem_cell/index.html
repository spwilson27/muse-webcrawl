Haematopoietic stem cell

Haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) are the stem cells that give rise to all the other blood cells through the process of haematopoiesis. They are derived from mesoderm and located in red bone marrow in adults, particularly in the pelvis, femur, and sternum. HSCs are multipotent and self-renewing: a single HSC can reconstitute the entire blood system of a recipient, the basis of bone marrow transplantation.

Properties

HSCs divide asymmetrically and can enter long periods of quiescence in the bone marrow niche. Through successive progenitor stages they generate the myeloid lineage (red blood cells, platelets, granulocytes, monocytes) and the lymphoid lineage (B cells, T cells, NK cells). The existence of HSCs was demonstrated by Till and McCulloch's spleen colony-forming experiments in 1961.

Sources and transplantation

HSCs for transplantation can be collected from bone marrow, from mobilized peripheral blood after G-CSF treatment, or from umbilical cord blood. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation is used to treat leukaemias, lymphomas, immune deficiencies, and certain genetic blood disorders. Age-related clonal haematopoiesis — expansion of HSC clones carrying somatic mutations — increases with age and is associated with blood cancers and cardiovascular disease.
