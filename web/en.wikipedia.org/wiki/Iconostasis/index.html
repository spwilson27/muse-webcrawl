Iconostasis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An iconostasis (Greek: εἰκονοστάσιον) is a wall of icons and religious paintings, separating the nave from the sanctuary in a church. It is a defining feature of Byzantine, Eastern Orthodox and some Eastern Catholic churches. The iconostasis typically has three doors: the central Royal Doors (or Holy Doors) leading to the altar, flanked by deacon's doors.

Development

The iconostasis evolved from the Byzantine templon, a low barrier or beam bearing icons that separated the sanctuary in early churches. Between the 11th and 14th centuries, the templon developed into a screen with multiple rows of icons. In Russia the iconostasis grew into a tall wall of up to five tiers, reaching its most elaborate form in the 16th and 17th centuries.

Arrangement

Reading from the bottom, a classical iconostasis may include: the Sovereign tier, containing the Royal Doors with icons of Christ, the Theotokos, the patron saint of the church and the feast of the church; the Deesis tier, centred on Christ Pantocrator flanked by the Virgin and St John the Baptist interceding; the Feasts tier, depicting the great feasts of the liturgical year; and upper tiers of prophets and patriarchs, surmounted by a cross or icon of the Crucifixion.

Theology

The iconostasis is not understood as a barrier dividing clergy from laity so much as a visible meeting of heaven and earth: the icons depict the communion of saints surrounding the worshipping congregation. The Royal Doors are opened at specified moments of the services, and only clergy and servers pass through them.
