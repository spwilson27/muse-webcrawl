Activity coefficient

An activity coefficient is a factor used in thermodynamics to account for deviations from ideal behaviour in a mixture of chemical substances. In an ideal mixture, the microscopic interactions between each pair of chemical species are the same, so bulk properties can be expressed directly in terms of concentrations or partial pressures. In real mixtures these interactions differ, and the activity coefficient corrects the concentration to give the "effective concentration", the activity.

Definition

The chemical potential of a species i in solution is written μi = μi⊖ + RT ln ai, where the activity ai = γi xi for a mole-fraction scale (or γi ci/c⊖ on a concentration scale). The dimensionless factor γi is the activity coefficient; it approaches 1 as the solution becomes ideally dilute.

Ionic solutions

For electrolytes, long-range electrostatic interactions make activity coefficients deviate strongly from unity even at low concentrations. The Debye–Hückel theory (1923) predicts the limiting behaviour at high dilution, in which log γ depends on the square root of the ionic strength and the charges of the ions; extended equations and the Pitzer equations handle concentrated brines. Because individual ion activities cannot be measured independently, mean ionic activity coefficients are used in practice.

Applications

Activity coefficients enter equilibrium constants expressed in activities, solubility and pH calculations, electrochemistry (where the Nernst equation uses activities), colligative properties, and chemical engineering models of vapour–liquid equilibrium such as UNIFAC. In blood plasma and seawater chemistry they are essential for accurate speciation calculations.
