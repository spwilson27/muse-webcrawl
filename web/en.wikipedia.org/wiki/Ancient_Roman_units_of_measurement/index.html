Ancient Roman units of measurement

The ancient Roman units of measurement were primarily built on the Hellenic system, with Egyptian influences, and from them the later English and Imperial systems partly derive. Roman law required standards to be kept in the Temple of Juno Moneta in Rome, and the system was standardised across the empire to a degree unmatched in the ancient world.

The Roman mile of a thousand paces ("mille passus") was marked by milestones on the great roads of the empire. Land was measured in actus and iugera; a iugerum of 28,800 square feet was nominally the area a yoke of oxen could plough in a day.

Weight

The fundamental weight was the libra (pound) of about 328.9 g, divided into 12 unciae (ounces) — the origin of both the words "ounce" and "inch". Fractions of the uncia included the semuncia, sicilicus, sextula, and scrupulum, the smallest of which, the scruple, weighed about 1.14 g and lent its name to apothecaries' measure.

Volume

Liquid measure centred on the amphora (about 26 litres), divided into 8 congii and 48 sextarii; dry measure used the modius (about 8.7 litres), the standard grain measure of Rome's annona.
