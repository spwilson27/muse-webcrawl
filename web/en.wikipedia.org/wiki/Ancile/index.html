Ancile

In ancient Rome, the ancile (Latin ancile, plural ancilia) was the sacred shield of the god Mars, said to have fallen from heaven during the reign of Numa Pompilius, the second king of Rome. The plural term came to denote the twelve sacred shields kept in the Temple of Mars in the Regia and carried in procession by the Salii, the dancing priests of Mars.

Legend

According to the legend told by Livy, Plutarch, and Ovid, during a plague in Numa's reign a bronze shield of curious shape — curved inward on both sides like a figure of eight — fell from the sky into the king's hands. A divine voice declared that Rome would rule the world so long as the shield was preserved. Recognising the danger that it might be stolen, Numa commissioned the smith Mamurius Veturius to make eleven identical copies, so that the original could not be identified. Mamurius asked as his reward that his name be remembered in the hymn of the Salii, the Carmen Saliare.

Cult

The twelve ancilia were guarded by the Salii Palatini, a college of twelve patrician priests founded by Numa. Each March, at the opening of the campaigning season sacred to Mars, the Salii took the shields from their shrine and processed through Rome singing and performing their archaic armed dance, striking the shields with staffs; the ceremony was repeated in October to close the season. While the ancilia were in motion, it was considered unlucky to begin a war or marry.

Significance

The original ancile was counted among the pignora imperii, the sacred tokens guaranteeing Rome's dominion, alongside the Palladium and the ashes of Orestes. Its shape resembles the figure-eight shields depicted in Mycenaean art, and the myth may preserve a memory of great antiquity.
