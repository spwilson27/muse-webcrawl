Renin–angiotensin system

The renin–angiotensin system (RAS), or renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), is a hormone system that regulates blood pressure, body fluids, and electrolyte balance, as well as systemic vascular resistance.

Mechanism

When renal blood flow is reduced, juxtaglomerular cells in the kidneys convert the precursor prorenin into renin and secrete it into the circulation. Renin cleaves the liver protein angiotensinogen to form angiotensin I. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), found predominantly in the pulmonary circulation, converts angiotensin I into the potent vasoconstrictor angiotensin II.

Angiotensin II acts on AT1 receptors to constrict blood vessels, stimulate aldosterone secretion from the adrenal cortex (increasing sodium and water reabsorption in the kidneys), stimulate thirst and antidiuretic hormone release, and enhance sympathetic activity. A counter-regulatory axis involving ACE2, angiotensin-(1–7) and the Mas receptor produces vasodilator and anti-fibrotic effects that oppose the classic pathway.

Clinical significance

Overactivity of the system contributes to hypertension, heart failure, diabetic kidney disease and other cardiovascular disorders. Drugs that interrupt the cascade — ACE inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), direct renin inhibitors such as aliskiren, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists — are mainstays of cardiovascular and renal medicine. Local tissue renin–angiotensin systems have also been described in the heart, brain, vasculature and other organs.
