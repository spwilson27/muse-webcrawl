Oolite

Oolite (also oolitic limestone) is a sedimentary rock formed from ooids: small, spherical, concentrically layered grains, usually of calcium carbonate, that resemble tiny eggs — hence the name, from the Greek for "egg stone." Rocks composed predominantly of ooids cemented together are widespread in the geological record and are widely used as building stone.

Formation

Ooids form in warm, shallow, agitated marine waters supersaturated with calcium carbonate. A nucleus — a sand grain or shell fragment — is rolled about by currents and waves, accreting concentric layers of carbonate, typically aragonite or calcite, often with microbial involvement. Grains are usually 0.25 to 2 millimetres in diameter; larger similar grains are called pisoids. Modern ooid shoals occur in places such as the Bahamas and Shark Bay, Australia.

Distribution and use

Famous oolitic formations include the Jurassic limestones of England — Bath stone, Cotswold stone, and Portland stone are oolites used in many landmark buildings — and the Miami oolite of Florida. The rock is valued as a dimension stone because it is relatively soft to cut, hardens on exposure, and has a uniform texture. Oolitic ironstones and oolitic cherts also occur.
