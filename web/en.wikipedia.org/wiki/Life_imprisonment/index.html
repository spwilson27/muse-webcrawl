Life imprisonment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Life imprisonment is any sentence of imprisonment under which the convicted person is to remain in prison for the rest of their natural life or until pardoned, paroled or commuted. It is the most severe penalty in abolitionist countries and the principal alternative to the death penalty where that exists. Forms vary: life with parole eligibility after a minimum term ("tariff" — often 15–30 years), indeterminate life with review, and life without parole (LWOP), including mandatory whole-life orders in England and Wales for the most serious murders and "natural life" sentences elsewhere.

Use and debates

Life imprisonment is prescribed principally for murder and aggravated offences; the US holds a large share of the world's life-sentenced prisoners (including thousands sentenced as juveniles — a practice the US Supreme Court has progressively restricted since Graham (2010) and Miller (2012)). Human-rights courts confront its reducibility: the European Court of Human Rights held (Vinter, 2013) that irreducible whole-life sentences violate the prohibition of inhuman punishment unless review mechanisms exist in law and practice — a standard driving reforms and political argument. Gerontology of prisons, compassionate release and the penological purposes of decades-long confinement are parallel debates.
