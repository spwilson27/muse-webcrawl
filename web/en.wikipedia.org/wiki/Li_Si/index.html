Li Si (c. 280 – 208 BC) was a Chinese calligrapher, philosopher and politician of the Qin dynasty. A student of the Confucian-turned-Legalist thinker Xunzi, alongside Han Fei, he served as chancellor to Qin Shi Huang, the First Emperor, and was the chief architect of the policies that unified China in 221 BC — standardising script, law, measures and administration — as well as of the notorious burning of books in 213 BC.

Career

According to the Shiji, Li Si began as a minor clerk in Chu, where watching rats in the privy versus rats in the granary taught him that environment determines success; he therefore sought his fortune in Qin. There his famous memorial against the expulsion of foreign officers saved his career, and he rose steadily. As chancellor after unification, he abolished the feudal enfeoffment system in favour of centrally administered commanderies and counties, standardised the currency, weights, measures and axle widths, and oversaw the creation of the small seal script as the empire's standard writing.

Book burning and fall

In 213 BC, after a court scholar praised the old feudal ways, Li Si proposed the destruction of privately held copies of the histories, the Classic of Poetry and the writings of the Hundred Schools of Thought, sparing only practical works on medicine, divination and agriculture and the Qin state archives. On the First Emperor's death in 210 BC, Li Si conspired with the eunuch Zhao Gao to conceal the death and forge a succession edict, forcing the heir Fusu to suicide and installing the pliable Huhai as Second Emperor. The alliance destroyed him: Zhao Gao framed Li Si for treason, and in 208 BC he was executed by being cut in two at the waist in the marketplace of Xianyang, his family exterminated with him.
