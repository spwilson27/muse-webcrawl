Georgian folk dancing

Georgian folk dancing (Georgian: ქართული ცეკვა) is the traditional dance culture of the Georgian people, characterised by spectacular leaps, spins performed on the knees, dancing on the toes, and — for women — famously smooth, gliding steps in which the body appears to float. Each region of Georgia has its own dances reflecting local history, warfare, courtship, and work. Georgian dance is accompanied by polyphonic singing, drums (doli), accordions, and wind instruments such as the panduri and chonguri.

Principal dances

Kartuli, the romantic wedding dance, is performed by a couple: the man, proud and statuesque, never touches the woman except in the prescribed figures, while the woman keeps her eyes demurely lowered and glides as if on ice. Khorumi is a war dance from Achara, performed by men and culminating in acrobatic solos that imitate searching for and fighting the enemy. Acharuli is a light, flirtatious mixed dance from the same region, with colourful costumes. Mtiuluri is a competitive mountain dance featuring individual contests of technique, while Kintouri depicts the playful, wine-loving urban tricksters (kintos) of old Tbilisi. Samaia, performed by three women, evokes the frescoes of Queen Tamar, and Partsa and Kazbeguri are further regional forms.

Costume

Male dancers traditionally wear the chokha, a long woollen coat with ornamental cartridge holders (gazyrs), a dagger (khanjali), black leather soft boots, and sometimes a fur hat (papakha). Women's costumes are long, richly decorated dresses with elaborate headdresses and veils.

Ensembles

The Georgian National Ballet Sukhishvili, founded in 1945 by Iliko Sukhishvili and Nino Ramishvili, has toured worldwide and made Georgian dance famous internationally; the Erisioni ensemble is another leading company. Georgian folk dance and song are central to national celebrations and the supra (feast) tradition.
