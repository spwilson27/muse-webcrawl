Pink triangle

The pink triangle, reclaimed as a symbol of gay pride

The pink triangle has been a symbol for various LGBTQ identities since the 1970s, beginning as a symbol of gay liberation and gay pride. It originated as a badge worn by prisoners in Nazi concentration camps: men imprisoned for homosexuality under Paragraph 175 of the German criminal code were forced to wear an inverted pink triangle sewn onto their uniforms.

Nazi persecution

Between 1933 and 1945 the Nazi regime arrested an estimated 100,000 men as homosexuals; between 5,000 and 15,000 were sent to concentration camps, where those marked with the pink triangle endured especially brutal treatment and very high mortality. After liberation, some pink-triangle prisoners were returned to prison, since Paragraph 175 remained in force in West Germany until reforms beginning in 1969.

Reclamation

In the 1970s gay rights activists in Germany and the United States adopted the triangle — usually turned point-up — as a memorial to Nazi persecution and an emblem of pride and defiance. It became prominent during the AIDS crisis: the ACT UP activist coalition's 1987 "Silence = Death" poster paired an upright pink triangle with its famous slogan. Memorials to gay victims of the Holocaust, including Amsterdam's Homomonument (1987), built of pink granite triangles, have made it one of the most enduring symbols of LGBTQ remembrance.

Related symbols

The concentration camp badge system assigned other colours to other prisoner categories, including the black triangle worn by "asocial" prisoners, which some lesbian activists later adopted. The pink triangle's broad use declined somewhat as the rainbow flag, introduced in 1978, became the dominant emblem of LGBTQ pride.
