Giraffe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The giraffe (Giraffa camelopardalis, in the traditional single-species view) is the tallest living terrestrial animal — males stand 4.6–5.5 metres — and the largest ruminant, native to the savannas and open woodlands of Africa. Its extreme neck, contrary to intuition, contains the same seven cervical vertebrae as other mammals, each greatly elongated; its heart (up to ~11 kg) pumps blood two metres uphill against gravity, with tight skin and valved veins managing pressure when it stoops to drink.

Why the neck evolved so long is debated between the classic browsing-competition hypothesis and sexual selection — males fight by 'necking', swinging their ossicone-tipped heads like clubs. Its patchwork coat pattern is unique to each individual and now used by researchers for identification.

Taxonomy and conservation

Genetic work (2016 onward) argues the giraffe is really four species; conservation bodies have been catching up, and giraffes overall are listed Vulnerable after a decline of roughly 30% over three decades — a 'silent extinction' masked by their fame. Poaching, habitat fragmentation and civil unrest are the drivers.

Biology

Giraffes sleep little (often under two hours, in snatches, usually standing), ruminate like cattle, and use infrasound-range rumbles and even humming at night. Their calves drop nearly two metres at birth. Despite vocal-fold anatomy long thought limiting, giraffes do vocalise — mostly after dark.
