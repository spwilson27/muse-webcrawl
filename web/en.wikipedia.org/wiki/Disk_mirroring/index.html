Disk mirroring

In data storage, disk mirroring is the replication of logical disk volumes onto separate physical hard disks in real time to ensure continuous availability. It is the scheme standardized as RAID 1. A mirrored volume is a complete logical representation of separate volume copies; data written to one disk is simultaneously written to the other (or others), so if one physical disk fails, the data remains accessible from its mirror without interruption.

Operation

Mirroring may be implemented in hardware by a RAID controller, in software by the operating system (for example Linux mdraid, Windows dynamic disks, or ZFS mirror vdevs), or by a disk array. Reads can be served from either copy, sometimes doubling read throughput and reducing latency by choosing the disk whose heads are closer. After a disk is replaced, the mirror must be resilvered or rebuilt by copying the surviving data, a process that stresses the remaining disk and can take hours on large drives.

Advantages and disadvantages

Mirroring offers excellent fault tolerance and simple recovery, at the cost of half (or more) of raw capacity: usable space is that of a single disk. Disks are usually identical in size and model, and mirrors are often paired with hot spares. Synchronous remote mirroring extends the idea between sites for disaster recovery, though at the cost of latency limits. Mirroring is not a substitute for backup, because deletion, corruption, and ransomware are faithfully replicated to every copy.

Variants

Three-way mirrors tolerate two failures. Duplexing mirrors across separate controllers to remove the controller as a single point of failure. In ZFS and some enterprise arrays, mirrors are combined with striping (RAID 10) for both performance and redundancy.
