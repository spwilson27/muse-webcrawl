Architectural drawing

An architectural drawing or architect's drawing is a technical drawing of a building (or building project) that falls within the definition of architecture. Architectural drawings are used by architects and others to develop a design idea into a coherent proposal, to communicate ideas and concepts, to convince clients of the merits of a design, to assist a building contractor in constructing it, and to make a record of a completed building.

Standard views

Architectural drawings are produced in conventional orthographic projection, the principal views being the floor plan (a horizontal section viewed from above), the elevation (a flat view of one facade), the section (a vertical cut showing interior structure), and site plans showing the building in its plot. Perspective renderings and axonometric drawings supplement these to convey appearance. Each view is drawn to a stated scale, such as 1:50 or 1:100, with conventions for line weights, symbols, and dimensioning regulated by standards and office practice.

History

Surviving plans from ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia show that scaled ground plans are thousands of years old. Medieval master masons used full-size incised "tracing floors" for Gothic cathedrals. The Renaissance, with Brunelleschi's discovery of linear perspective and Alberti's treatises, established drawing as the architect's primary design medium, a role it kept through the age of ink-on-linen drafting into the twentieth century.

Modern practice

Hand drafting with drawing boards, T-squares, and parallel rules has largely been replaced by computer-aided design (CAD) and building information modeling (BIM), in which drawings are generated as views of a single digital model and automatically coordinated. A complete construction set may run to hundreds of sheets: architectural, structural, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing drawings, schedules, and details, all cross-referenced to written specifications.
