Lake Copais, also spelled Kopais (Greek: Κωπαΐς), was formerly a great shallow lake in the center of Boeotia, Greece, fed by the Boeotian Cephissus and Melas rivers. It was drained in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, converting its bed into one of the most fertile agricultural plains of Greece.

Ancient drainage

Remarkably, the lake had already been drained once in antiquity. During the Bronze Age, the Minyans of Orchomenus constructed an extensive system of dikes, canals, and sinkholes (katabothra) in the 2nd millennium BC that reclaimed much of the basin, associated with the great fortress of Gla. The system fell into ruin after the collapse of the Mycenaean palaces, and the lake re-formed. Strabo and Pausanias describe the lake and its drainage channels; Alexander the Great and later Roman engineers reportedly attempted restoration without lasting success.

Modern drainage

In 1882 a French company, and from 1887 the British Lake Copais Company, undertook drainage works. The Cephissus was diverted through canals toward Lake Yliki and Lake Paralimni. Final drainage was achieved by 1931, reclaiming roughly 21,000 hectares. The Kopais plain now produces cotton, rice, and other crops, administered after 1952 by a Greek state organization.
