The Golden Bough: A Study in Magic and Religion is a wide-ranging comparative study of mythology and religion by Scottish anthropologist Sir James George Frazer, first published in two volumes in 1890 and expanded in the third edition (1906–1915) to twelve volumes. A popular one-volume abridgement appeared in 1922.

Content

The work takes its name and starting point from the priest-king of Nemi — the Rex Nemorensis, priest of Diana at Lake Nemi in Italy, who held office until a challenger broke a golden bough from a sacred tree and slew him. From this puzzle Frazer built a grand theory of human thought evolving through three stages — magic, then religion, then science — and surveyed fertility rites, sacred kingship, dying-and-rising gods, scapegoats, and taboos across cultures worldwide.

Influence

Although its evolutionary theory has been largely rejected by later anthropologists, The Golden Bough had an enormous influence on modernist literature, shaping writers such as T. S. Eliot, Ezra Pound, and James Joyce, and remains a landmark of comparative mythology.
