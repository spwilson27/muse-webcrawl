Tardive dyskinesia

Tardive dyskinesia (TD) is a disorder characterised by involuntary, repetitive movements, most often of the face, lips, jaw and tongue, that develops as a side effect of long-term use of dopamine receptor blocking agents — principally antipsychotic (neuroleptic) medications, and also certain antiemetic drugs such as metoclopramide. "Tardive" refers to the delayed onset of symptoms, typically after months or years of treatment; the condition may persist after the drug is stopped.

Signs and mechanism

Classic features include lip smacking, puckering, tongue protrusion ("fly-catcher tongue"), chewing movements, grimacing, and choreiform movements of the limbs and trunk; severe cases can impair speech, eating and breathing. The leading explanation is dopamine-receptor supersensitivity: chronic blockade up-regulates D2 receptors in the striatum, producing hyperkinetic movements. Risk is highest with first-generation antipsychotics but exists with second-generation agents as well.

Management

Prevention focuses on using the lowest effective antipsychotic dose and monitoring with instruments such as the Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS). In 2017 the United States FDA approved the vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) inhibitors valbenazine and deutetrabenazine — the first drugs specifically approved for TD — which reduce dopaminergic transmission presynaptically and can substantially improve movements.
