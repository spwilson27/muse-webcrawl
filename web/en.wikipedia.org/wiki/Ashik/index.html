An ashik (Turkish: aşık, Azerbaijani: aşıq, Armenian: ashugh) is a singer-poet and wandering bard who accompanies his songs on the saz, a long-necked lute, in the folk traditions of the Turkic peoples of Anatolia, Azerbaijan, the Caucasus, and north-western Iran, and in Armenian and Georgian culture. The word derives from the Arabic ʿāshiq, "lover," reflecting the mystical love-poetry at the heart of the tradition.

History

The ashik tradition descends from the ozan, the shaman-bards of the pre-Islamic Turkic peoples of Central Asia who accompanied the armies of the Oghuz Turks westward into Anatolia and the Caucasus. From the sixteenth century, under the influence of Sufism and Shiʿa devotion, the ozan was transformed into the ashik, whose repertoire combined heroic epics — such as the tales of Köroğlu — with lyric love songs and mystical verse.

Performance and repertoire

Ashiks traditionally performed in coffeehouses, at weddings, and at festivals, improvising verse and competing in contests of wit and song (deyişmə) with rival bards. An ashik's training involves a long apprenticeship to a master, and legend often describes the calling as revealed in a dream in which the novice drinks a cup of divine love offered by a saint or by the beloved. Famous ashiks include Ashik Qarib, Ashiq Alasgar of Azerbaijan, the Armenian Sayat-Nova — who composed in Armenian, Azerbaijani, Georgian and Persian — and the Turkish folk poets Karacaoğlan and Âşık Veysel.

Recognition

In 2009 the art of the Azerbaijani ashik was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The tradition remains a living part of folk culture in Turkey, Azerbaijan and the Armenian and Azerbaijani diasporas.
