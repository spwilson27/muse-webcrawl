Patronage in ancient Rome

Patronage (clientela) was the distinctive social relationship in ancient Rome between a patron (patronus) and his clients (clientes). The bond was hereditary, reciprocal, and unequal: the patron offered protection, legal representation, and material support, while the client owed loyalty, political support, and services. Patronage structured much of Roman politics, law, and daily life from the early Republic into the Empire, and extended beyond individuals to whole communities and provinces.

Obligations

The patron was expected to represent his clients in court, give gifts and loans, invite them to dinner, and provide for their families; a regular morning dole (the sportula, originally food, later money) was distributed to clients. In return, clients gathered each morning at the salutatio to greet the patron at his home, escorted him to the Forum, canvassed for him in elections, and supported his public image. The relationship was governed by fides (good faith); tradition ascribed its foundation to Romulus, and a patron who defrauded a client was declared sacer (accursed) under the Twelve Tables.

Freedmen and patrons

A special form of patronage bound a freedman (libertus) to his former master, who became his patron automatically upon manumission. The freedman owed obsequium (respect) and operae (days of work or services), could not sue his patron, and the patron retained certain inheritance rights — obligations that could extend to the freedman's descendants.

Political role

Client armies of voters helped great families dominate elections, and candidates cultivated clients in the tribes and districts. Foreign cities and provinces also placed themselves under the patronage of Roman generals and senators who had conquered or governed them, expecting advocacy at Rome; Julius Caesar, for example, acted as patron of regions of Gaul he had conquered. Under the Empire, the emperor became the universal patron, and imperial patronage — appointments, favors, and benefactions — became central to the working of the state.

Cultural patronage

By extension, "patronage" also describes the support of poets, artists, and intellectuals by wealthy Romans: Maecenas, the adviser of Augustus, famously patronized Virgil and Horace, and his name became the generic word for a patron of the arts in many languages. Poets addressed patrons in dedications, seeking financial security and social standing in exchange for literary praise.
