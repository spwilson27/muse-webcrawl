Religion in Korea

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Buddhist temple in the mountains and a Christian church in Seoul — two of the peninsula's major traditions.

Religion in Korea encompasses a range of beliefs and practices, historically dominated by Korean shamanism, Buddhism and Confucianism, and in the modern period increasingly by Christianity. In contemporary South Korea a majority of the population reports no religious affiliation, while Christians form the largest identified religious bloc, followed by Buddhists. In North Korea the state is officially atheist, religion is heavily controlled, and reliable statistics are unavailable.

South Korea

According to the 2015 census in South Korea, 56.1% of the population had no religious affiliation, 27.6% were Christian (19.7% Protestant and 7.9% Catholic), and 15.5% were Buddhist. The remainder belonged to Won Buddhism, Confucianism, Korean shamanism, Cheondoism, Islam, and other traditions. The irreligious share has grown steadily, especially among younger South Koreans.

History

Shamanism and early traditions

The oldest stratum of Korean religion is shamanism (Muism), centred on the mudang (usually a woman) who mediates between humans and spirits through ritual (gut). Shamanic practice persists today, often alongside other faiths, in rituals for healing, fortune and ancestors. Ancestor veneration, later systematised by Confucianism, remains a powerful cultural norm.

Buddhism

Buddhism was introduced from China in the 4th century CE during the Three Kingdoms period and flourished under Silla and Goryeo, where it was effectively the state religion; the Tripitaka Koreana woodblocks are its most famous artefact. Under the Joseon dynasty (1392–1910), neo-Confucian scholars suppressed Buddhism, driving monks into mountain temples, where the tradition survived.

Confucianism

Joseon adopted Neo-Confucianism as its governing ideology, shaping Korean family structure, hierarchy, education and state ritual for five centuries. Although few Koreans have ever called themselves "Confucian" as an exclusive religion, its ethics — filial piety, respect for elders, emphasis on scholarship — still permeate Korean society in both North and South.

Christianity

Catholicism arrived in the late 18th century through Korean scholars who had encountered Jesuit texts in Beijing, and was persecuted in the 19th century, producing thousands of martyrs. Protestant missionaries arrived in the 1880s and founded schools and hospitals. After the Korean War, Christianity grew rapidly in the South; South Korea today has some of the world's largest churches, including the Yoido Full Gospel Church in Seoul, and sends out one of the world's largest missionary forces. New religious movements such as the Unification Church and Shincheonji also originated in Korea.

North Korea

The North Korean constitution nominally guarantees freedom of religious belief, but in practice the state demands absolute loyalty to the ruling Kim family and the ideology of Juche. Organised religion was largely eradicated after the Korean War; a small number of state-controlled religious federations and showcase churches and temples operate in Pyongyang, while underground Christian practice is reported to be severely punished. Cheondoism retains a nominal presence through the Chondoist Chongu Party.
