Barrel vault
TypeArchitectural element
Also calledTunnel vault, wagon vault
FormContinuous semicircular arch extruded in depth

A barrel vault, also known as a tunnel vault or wagon vault, is an architectural element formed by the extrusion of a single curve (typically a semicircular arch) along a given distance. The simplest of all vaults, it creates a tunnel-like interior space and exerts continuous outward thrust along the entire length of its supporting walls, which must therefore be thick or buttressed.

History

True barrel vaults were built by the ancient Mesopotamians and Egyptians in mudbrick, and were used extensively by the Romans in stone and concrete for bathhouses, basilicas, warehouses and substructures such as the Cloaca Maxina's channels. The huge groin vaults of the Baths of Caracalla and the Basilica of Maxentius are developments of the barrel vault intersected at right angles. After a long decline, barrel vaults returned to prominence in Romanesque church architecture of the 11th and 12th centuries, where stone vaulting replaced fire-prone timber roofs — at the cost of small windows and massive walls, since the vault's thrust had to be resisted. Pointed barrel vaults in Gothic architecture reduced the thrust and lightened the structure.

Engineering

A barrel vault behaves like a series of parallel arches: loads are carried down and outward along the whole springing line, requiring continuous support. Variants include the pointed barrel vault, the rampant vault (over staircases), and annular vaults over curved corridors such as amphitheatre passages. In modern construction the form reappears in thin-shell concrete and in cut-and-cover tunnels.
