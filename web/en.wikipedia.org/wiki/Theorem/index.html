Theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A blackboard filled with a chalk proof beneath a stated theorem]

In mathematics and formal logic, a theorem is a statement that has been proved on the basis of previously established statements — other theorems — together with generally accepted statements such as axioms. The proof of a mathematical theorem is a logical argument showing the statement is a necessary consequence of the axioms.

Thousands of results are titled theorems; the term (from Greek theorein, to look at) is reserved for statements considered important or interesting, while supporting results are lemmas, direct consequences corollaries, and minor statements propositions.

Famous examples

The Pythagorean theorem is the most widely known, with hundreds of published proofs; Fermat's Last Theorem waited 358 years between statement (1637) and proof (Wiles, 1995); Gödel's incompleteness theorems are theorems about the limits of theorem-proving itself.

Theorem status is social as well as logical: a claimed proof must survive communal scrutiny, a process occasionally long and contentious (the classification of finite simple groups spans thousands of pages; some recent claimed proofs of major conjectures remain disputed).

Formalisation

Proof assistants (Lean, Coq, Isabelle) increasingly verify theorems mechanically, with libraries like Lean's mathlib formalising large bodies of mathematics — a new standard of certainty, at the cost of much labour.
