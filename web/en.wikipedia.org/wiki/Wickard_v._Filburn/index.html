Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111 (1942), is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision that dramatically expanded the federal government's power under the Commerce Clause. The Court unanimously held that Congress could regulate wheat grown by an Ohio farmer purely for consumption on his own farm, because such activity, in aggregate, substantially affects interstate commerce.

Facts and holding

Roscoe Filburn was fined under the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 for harvesting 239 bushels of wheat above his allotment; the excess was grown for his own family's and livestock's consumption. Writing for the Court, Justice Robert H. Jackson reasoned that home-grown wheat competes with wheat in commerce because it supplies a farmer's needs that would otherwise be met by purchases, and that the aggregate effect of many farmers doing the same is far from trivial.

Legacy

The decision underpinned decades of expansive federal regulation and was described as the high-water mark of Commerce Clause jurisprudence, later tested in cases such as United States v. Lopez (1995), Gonzales v. Raich (2005), and the Affordable Care Act litigation in NFIB v. Sebelius (2012), which concerned the related taxing power rather than the commerce power analysis in Wickard.
