War wagon

A war wagon is a fortified wagon used in warfare, most famously by the Hussite armies of Bohemia in the 15th century. War wagons were typically heavy carts with armoured wooden sides containing loopholes, crewed by soldiers armed with crossbows, handguns, flails and other weapons. Chained together in a defensive formation called a wagenburg (wagon fort or laager), they provided mobile fortification that allowed infantry to defeat heavily armoured cavalry.

Hussite wars

The war wagon was developed to its classic form under the Hussite commander Jan Žižka during the Hussite Wars (1420–1434). Hussite armies, composed largely of peasant infantry, faced crusading armies of armoured knights. Žižka's wagons — each carrying a mixed crew of crossbowmen, handgunners and close-combat troops, with pavises and an underslung chain — could be drawn up in minutes into a wagon fort. Attacking cavalry, unable to break the formation, were shot down at close range; Hussite forces then counterattacked. The system won a string of victories, including at Sudoměř (1420), Kutná Hora (1422) and Tachov (1427), and wagon tactics spread the use of early firearms across Central European warfare.

Later use

Wagon forts were adopted widely after the Hussite example: Hungarian, Polish, German, Cossack and Ottoman forces all employed versions of the wagon laager, and the tactic persisted on the steppes and in colonial warfare into the 19th century — in the Boer laager and the circled wagons of North American frontier defence. Armoured trains and modern armoured vehicles are sometimes seen as conceptual descendants of the war wagon's combination of mobility, protection and firepower.
