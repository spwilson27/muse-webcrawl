Earth's rotation is the spinning of the planet around its own axis, once every 23 hours, 56 minutes and 4 seconds relative to the distant stars (the sidereal day). Because Earth also moves along its orbit, the interval between successive noons — the mean solar day — is slightly longer, at 24 hours. Viewed from above the North Pole, Earth rotates counterclockwise, from west to east, which is why the Sun appears to rise in the east.

Effects

Rotation causes the cycle of day and night, flattens Earth into an oblate spheroid (the equatorial diameter exceeds the polar diameter by about 43 km), and produces the Coriolis effect that deflects moving air and water — to the right in the Northern Hemisphere — shaping cyclones and ocean currents. The equatorial surface speed is about 1,670 km/h, decreasing with latitude to zero at the poles.

Changes over time

Friction from lunar tides is gradually transferring angular momentum from Earth to the Moon: the day lengthens by roughly 1.7–2.3 milliseconds per century, and the Moon recedes about 3.8 cm per year. In the distant past, days were much shorter — evidence from fossil corals and tidal sediments indicates days of about 22 hours some 600 million years ago. Shorter-term variations arise from winds, ocean currents, earthquakes and melting ice redistributing mass. Rotation is monitored by the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service (IERS), which also announces leap seconds to keep civil time aligned with Earth's angle.

Axis variations

The rotation axis slowly precesses over about 26,000 years and nods (nutation) with an 18.6-year period; the pole itself wanders by metres over the surface (polar motion, including the Chandler wobble). Historically, the Foucault pendulum (1851) gave the first direct, tabletop demonstration of the rotation.
