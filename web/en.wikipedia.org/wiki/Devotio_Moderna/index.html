Devotio Moderna

The movement produced influential devotional works, most famously The Imitation of Christ.

Devotio Moderna (Latin for "modern devotion") was a religious renewal movement within the Catholic Church in the late medieval Low Countries and northern Germany, flourishing from the late 14th century through the 16th century. It emphasised personal devotion, meditation on the life of Christ, humility, and inner piety over external ritual and scholastic theology.

Origins

The movement is traced to Gerard Groote (1340–1384), a wealthy Deventer scholar who, after a religious conversion, preached reform and founded communities for devout laypeople. His followers formed the Brethren of the Common Life, who lived communally without formal monastic vows, supporting themselves largely by copying manuscripts, and the Windesheim Congregation of Augustinian canons regular, founded in 1387.

Spirituality and works

Devotio Moderna spirituality practised methodical meditation, self-examination, and the disciplined "imitation" of Christ's humility. Its most famous product is The Imitation of Christ, attributed to Thomas à Kempis, a member of the movement — one of the most widely read devotional books in Christian history. Other figures include Florens Radewyns, Jan van Ruusbroec (an influence), and Geert Groote's disciple Gerard Zerbolt.

Influence

The movement ran schools that educated figures such as Erasmus of Rotterdam and possibly influenced the young Martin Luther indirectly through its emphasis on personal faith and Scripture. Historians debate the extent to which it prepared the ground for the Reformation and for Christian humanism.
