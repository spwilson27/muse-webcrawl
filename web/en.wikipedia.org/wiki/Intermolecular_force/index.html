Intermolecular force

Hydrogen bonding between water molecules is a strong type of intermolecular force.

An intermolecular force (IMF) (or secondary force) is the force that mediates interaction between molecules, including the electromagnetic forces of attraction or repulsion which act between atoms and other types of neighbouring particles, e.g. atoms or ions. Intermolecular forces are weak relative to intramolecular forces — the forces which hold a molecule together, such as covalent bonds.

Types of intermolecular forces

The main types of intermolecular attraction are:

  • London dispersion forces — weak attractions arising from instantaneous dipoles induced in neighbouring molecules; present in all substances and the only force between nonpolar molecules.
  • Dipole–dipole interactions — attractions between the permanent dipoles of polar molecules.
  • Hydrogen bonding — a particularly strong dipole interaction in which a hydrogen atom bonded to nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine is attracted to a lone pair on a neighbouring electronegative atom.
  • Ion–dipole and ion-induced dipole forces — attractions involving ions and polar or polarizable molecules, important in solutions of salts.

Collectively, the attractive forces between neutral molecules are often called van der Waals forces. At very short range, repulsion arising from the Pauli exclusion principle between overlapping electron clouds balances the attraction, a behaviour modelled by the Lennard-Jones potential.

Effects on physical properties

Intermolecular forces determine many bulk properties of substances. Stronger intermolecular forces raise boiling and melting points, increase viscosity and surface tension, and reduce vapour pressure. The unusually high boiling point, surface tension, and heat capacity of water, for example, are attributed to its extensive hydrogen-bond network. Intermolecular forces are also central to protein folding, DNA base stacking, solubility, and the action of many drugs at their binding sites.
