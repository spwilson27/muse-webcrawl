In fluid dynamics, a blast wave is the increased pressure and flow resulting from the deposition of a large amount of energy in a small volume — an explosion. It is a shock wave followed by a wind of compressed gas that expands outward from the source, decaying in strength with distance.

Structure

An idealised free-air blast wave shows a near-instantaneous rise to a peak overpressure, then an exponential decay through ambient pressure into a brief phase of negative pressure (suction) before returning to equilibrium — a profile described by the Friedlander equation. Reflected waves from the ground or walls can greatly amplify the pressure, and where incident and reflected shocks merge they form a Mach stem. Injury and structural damage correlate with overpressure and impulse: eardrums, lungs and buildings each have characteristic thresholds.

Scaling and the Sedov–Taylor solution

Blast effects obey Hopkinson–Cranz cube-root scaling: the same overpressure occurs at distances scaled by the cube root of the energy released. For a point explosion in a uniform gas, G. I. Taylor (and independently von Neumann and Sedov) derived a self-similar solution in which the shock radius grows as the two-fifths power of time; Taylor famously used declassified photographs of the Trinity test to estimate the bomb's yield.

Occurrence

Blast waves are produced by chemical and nuclear explosions, lightning (thunder), volcanic eruptions, meteor airbursts such as Tunguska and Chelyabinsk, and astrophysical events including supernovae, whose expanding blast waves sweep up the interstellar medium.
