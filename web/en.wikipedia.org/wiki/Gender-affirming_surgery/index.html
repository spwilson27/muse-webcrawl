Gender-affirming surgery

Gender-affirming surgical care is provided at specialist centres.

Gender-affirming surgery (also called sex reassignment surgery or gender confirmation surgery) is a surgical procedure, or series of procedures, by which a transgender or transsexual person's physical sex characteristics are altered to resemble those socially associated with their identified gender. It forms one component of gender-affirming care, alongside hormone therapy and psychological support, for some people with gender dysphoria.

Procedures

For trans women, procedures may include vaginoplasty, breast augmentation, facial feminization surgery, tracheal shave, and orchiectomy. For trans men, procedures may include mastectomy ("top surgery"), hysterectomy, metoidioplasty, and phalloplasty. Not all transgender people seek or undergo surgery; eligibility generally follows standards of care such as those of the World Professional Association for Transgender Health (WPATH), which typically require assessment and, for some procedures, a period of hormone therapy and living in the affirmed gender role.

History

Early operations were performed in Germany in the 1920s and early 1930s at Magnus Hirschfeld's Institut für Sexualwissenschaft in Berlin, including surgeries for Dora Richter and Lili Elbe. The field became widely known after the 1952 transition of Christine Jorgensen. University-based gender clinics opened in the United States in the 1960s, notably at Johns Hopkins, which later closed its programme in 1979 before it resumed decades later.

Outcomes and regulation

Studies and major medical associations generally report high satisfaction and low regret rates following gender-affirming surgery, and professional bodies including the American Medical Association and the Endocrine Society support access as part of evidence-based care. Legal availability, insurance coverage, and rules for minors vary widely by jurisdiction and remain politically contested in several countries and U.S. states.
