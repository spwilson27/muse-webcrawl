ß

ß, known in German as Eszett (IPA: [ɛsˈtsɛt], "ess-zett") or scharfes S ("sharp S"), is a letter in the Latin script used in the German alphabet. It represents the voiceless alveolar fricative /s/ in Standard German, following long vowels and diphthongs, and historically stood for a ligature of long s (ſ) and z.

The letter ß in lowercase and its capital form ẞ.

History

The letter originated in medieval handwriting as a ligature of the long s (ſ) and the letter z (or, in some accounts, of ſ and s). It appears in German-language printing from the late Middle Ages onward. For centuries the letter had no capital form, because words containing ß never normally begin a word; when capitalisation was unavoidable (in all-caps text), ß was replaced by SS or, less commonly, SZ.

A capital form, ẞ (U+1E9E, Latin capital letter sharp S), was added to Unicode in 2008 and was officially included in German orthography by the Council for German Orthography in 2017, making it an option alongside SS in all-caps writing (for example, STRAẞE).

Usage

In modern standard German spelling, ß is used after long vowels and diphthongs where the sound is /s/: Straße (street), Fuß (foot), groß (big), weiß (white), außen (outside). After short vowels, the double letter ss is written instead: Fluss (river), muss (must). This distinction was codified in the German spelling reform of 1996, which also changed some words from ß to ss (for example, daß became dass).

Switzerland and Liechtenstein do not use ß; Swiss Standard German always writes ss instead (for example, Strasse). Austria and Germany use ß in accordance with the reformed rules.

Related letters

ß should not be confused with the Greek letter beta (β), which it resembles in some typefaces, or with the letter B. In German dictionaries and telephone directories, ß is sorted as if it were "ss".
