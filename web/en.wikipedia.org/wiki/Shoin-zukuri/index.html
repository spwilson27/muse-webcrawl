Shoin-zukuri

Shoin-zukuri (書院造) is a style of Japanese residential architecture that developed in the Muromachi period (1336–1573) and formed the basis of the traditional Japanese house. The name derives from the shoin, a study or writing alcove with a built-in desk, originally adapted from the studies of Zen Buddhist monks.

Features

The mature shoin style, codified in the Azuchi–Momoyama and early Edo periods, is defined by a set of features that remain standard in Japanese rooms today:

  • Tatami mats covering the floor completely, in standardised sizes, with rooms measured by mat count.
  • Tokonoma — a recessed alcove for displaying a hanging scroll, flower arrangement, or art object.
  • Chigaidana — staggered shelves beside the tokonoma for displaying objects.
  • Shoin — a projecting study bay with a writing desk and shoji windows.
  • Shoji and fusuma — sliding paper screens and opaque sliding doors that subdivide space flexibly.
  • A formal reception arrangement (zashiki) in which the most honoured guest sits before the tokonoma.

Development and legacy

Emerging from the palaces of the military aristocracy, shoin-zukuri reached its classical form in buildings such as the guest hall of Nijō Castle and reached a refined, restrained perfection in the Katsura Imperial Villa in Kyoto (17th century). In the Edo period it became the prescribed style for samurai residences, and its spatial elements — tatami, tokonoma, sliding partitions — were absorbed into ordinary Japanese houses. The related sukiya-zukuri style applied shoin elements with the rustic aesthetic of the tea house.
