Step-growth polymerization

Step-growth polymerization refers to a type of polymerization mechanism in which bi-functional or multifunctional monomers react to form first dimers, then trimers, longer oligomers and eventually long chain polymers. Many naturally occurring and synthetic polymers are produced by step-growth polymerization including polyesters, polyamides, polyurethanes, etc.

Characteristics

Because monomers, oligomers and polymer chains can all react with one another at any stage, molecular weight builds slowly and high polymer is obtained only at very high conversions — a consequence expressed by the Carothers equation, which relates the number-average degree of polymerization to the extent of reaction and the stoichiometric ratio of functional groups. This contrasts with chain-growth polymerization, in which high polymer forms from the beginning while monomer is steadily consumed. Step-growth reactions are often condensation polymerizations, releasing a small molecule such as water, but addition-type step growth (e.g., polyurethane formation from diisocyanates and diols) releases none.

History and examples

The concept was formalized by Wallace Carothers at DuPont in the 1930s, whose work led to nylon, the first wholly synthetic fibre, made by polycondensation of diamines and dicarboxylic acids. Other important step-growth polymers include polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polycarbonates, epoxies, phenol-formaldehyde resins (Bakelite), and aramids such as Kevlar. When multifunctional monomers are used, branching and eventually gelation (network formation) occur, as described by Flory–Stockmayer theory.
