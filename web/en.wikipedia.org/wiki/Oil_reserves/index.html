Oil reserves denote the amount of crude oil that can be technically recovered at a cost that is financially feasible at the present price of oil. Oil reserves are therefore influenced by the price of oil, unlike oil resources, which include oil that could be recovered at some price. Reserves are commonly reported as proven reserves (1P) — quantities recoverable with reasonable certainty (often defined as a 90% probability) — alongside probable and possible categories used in the 2P and 3P figures.

Classification and estimation

Engineers estimate reserves from geological surveys, drilling results and production performance, applying a recovery factor to the oil in place in a reservoir; typical recovery ranges from under 10% to more than 50% with enhanced recovery techniques. Unconventional sources — Canadian oil sands, Venezuelan extra-heavy oil and tight (shale) oil — are counted as reserves when commercial projects exist to produce them.

Distribution

Proven reserves are heavily concentrated in the Middle East and in a small number of other countries. Venezuela's Orinoco Belt extra-heavy oil gives it the largest reported total, followed by Saudi Arabia and Canada. Reported figures are politically and commercially sensitive: several OPEC members sharply raised reported reserves in the 1980s when production quotas were tied to reserve size, and such numbers are rarely audited independently.

Reserves-to-production ratio

The reserves-to-production (R/P) ratio — current reserves divided by annual production — has hovered around 40–50 years for decades, since reserves are continually replenished by new discoveries, extensions and revisions as prices and technology change. Debate continues over peak oil: when global production will plateau, and how quickly demand may fall as energy systems decarbonise.
