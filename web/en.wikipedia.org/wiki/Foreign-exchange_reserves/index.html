Foreign-exchange reserves (also called forex reserves or international reserves) are assets held on reserve by a central bank in foreign currencies. These reserves are used to back liabilities and influence monetary policy, to pay for imports and service foreign debt, and to intervene in currency markets.

Purpose

Central banks accumulate reserves as insurance against external shocks and balance-of-payments crises, to maintain confidence in a managed or pegged exchange rate, and to ensure the country can meet international obligations. A common rule of thumb holds that reserves should cover at least three months of imports or the country's short-term external debt (the Guidotti–Greenspan rule).

Composition and scale

Reserves are held mainly as securities denominated in major currencies — the US dollar accounts for the largest share globally, followed by the euro, yen, sterling and renminbi — plus monetary gold, Special Drawing Rights and the reserve position at the IMF. Global reserves grew enormously after the 1997 Asian financial crisis, when many emerging economies self-insured by building large stockpiles; China has held the world's largest reserves, peaking near US$4 trillion in 2014.
