George Phillips Bond (May 20, 1825 – February 17, 1865) was an American astronomer, the son of William Cranch Bond, founder of the Harvard College Observatory. Working with his father, he was a pioneer of astronomical photography and, in 1848, co-discovered Saturn's moon Hyperion. The concept of the Bond albedo, a measure of a planet's total reflectivity, is named for him.

Career

Bond graduated from Harvard in 1845 and became his father's assistant at the Harvard College Observatory, whose 15-inch "Great Refractor" — then among the largest telescopes in the world — was installed in 1847. On September 16, 1848, the Bonds identified a new satellite of Saturn; the English astronomer William Lassell observed it independently two nights later, and the moon was named Hyperion. In 1850 father and son made the first successful photograph of a star — a daguerreotype of Vega — and in 1857 they photographed the double star Mizar, demonstrating the future of celestial photography. George also discovered a number of comets between 1847 and 1849 and observed the Great Comet of 1858 (Donati's comet).

Directorship and later work

On his father's death in 1859, Bond succeeded him as director of the observatory. His ambitious plans were curtailed by the Civil War and declining health and funds, but he continued photographic experiments, studied the structure of Saturn's rings (including notes relating to the dark Crepe Ring), and pursued the photometry of planets and satellites. His posthumous memoir on Donati's comet won the Gold Medal of the Royal Astronomical Society in 1865, the year he died of tuberculosis at 39. The lunar crater G. Bond and the Martian crater Bond commemorate him, as does the Bond albedo in planetary photometry.
