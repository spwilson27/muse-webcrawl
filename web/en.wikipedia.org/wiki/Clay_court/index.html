A clay court is a tennis court whose playing surface is made of crushed stone, brick or shale. The classic European red clay — finely crushed red brick dust spread over layers of stone and clinker, as at Roland Garros in Paris — is the slowest of the sport's main surfaces: the loose top layer grips the ball, slowing it and producing a high, heavy bounce. A green variant, Har-Tru, made of crushed metabasalt, is common in North America.

Play and technique

Clay rewards patience, fitness and heavy topspin. Rallies are long, points are constructed from the baseline, and players slide into their shots, arriving at the ball with a controlled skid rather than a stop. Because the surface leaves a clear mark where the ball lands, disputed line calls at the French Open are famously judged by the umpire inspecting the mark — Roland Garros remains the only Grand Slam event played without electronic line-calling review of the kind used elsewhere. Courts require constant upkeep: watering, sweeping and rolling between matches.

Clay-court tennis

The professional clay season runs through the European spring, culminating in the French Open. Rafael Nadal, whose 14 Roland Garros titles earned him the title "King of Clay", is the surface's defining champion; Björn Borg and Chris Evert were its earlier masters. Players raised on South American and European clay have historically dominated the surface, while serve-and-volleyers have traditionally struggled on it.
