Order of precedence

An order of precedence is a sequential hierarchy of nominal importance applied to individuals, groups, or organisations. Most often it is used in the context of people — for example by governments to rank diplomats, officials, royalty, nobility, and clergy for ceremonial purposes such as seating at state banquets, order of entry into a room, and placement in processions.

Usage

Orders of precedence exist for most countries and many institutions. In the United Kingdom, separate orders apply in England and Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland, placing the sovereign first, followed by the royal family, archbishops, and the great officers of state. In the United States, the order of precedence places the President first, then the Vice President, then the Speaker of the House, the Chief Justice, and former presidents, and it governs protocol at official functions and the line of presidential succession in part.

Diplomatic precedence

Among diplomats, precedence is regulated internationally by the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961): ambassadors rank by the length of their accreditation in the host country, a system designed to end the disputes over rank that once led to quarrels and even violence among envoys in early modern Europe.

Other applications

The term is also used for orders of precedence of military decorations and medals — the sequence in which ribbons are worn — and in heraldry and ecclesiastical processions.
