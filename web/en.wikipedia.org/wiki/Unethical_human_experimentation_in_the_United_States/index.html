Unethical human experimentation in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Unethical human experimentation in the United States encompasses numerous medical and psychological experiments conducted on human subjects — often prisoners, patients, soldiers, children, and marginalised communities — without informed consent, in violation of the ethical principles later codified in the Nuremberg Code (1947), the Declaration of Helsinki (1964), and the Belmont Report (1979). Many programmes were sponsored or funded by government agencies, universities, and hospitals during the 20th century, and revelations about them repeatedly spurred reforms in research oversight.

Notable cases

The Tuskegee Syphilis Study (1932–1972), run by the U.S. Public Health Service, observed hundreds of African American men with syphilis without treating them — even after penicillin became the standard cure — so researchers could study the disease's progression. The Guatemala syphilis experiments (1946–1948) deliberately infected prisoners, soldiers, and mental patients. At the Willowbrook State School (1956–1970), institutionalised children were deliberately infected with hepatitis. The CIA's Project MKUltra (1953–1973) administered LSD and other drugs to unwitting subjects in mind-control research. During the Cold War, government-funded radiation experiments injected patients with plutonium, fed radioactive material to children at the Fernald School, and exposed soldiers and civilians to fallout and open-air releases. Prisoners were used in dermatological and chemical-agent tests at Holmesburg Prison and elsewhere.

Reform

Public exposés — Henry Beecher's 1966 catalogue of unethical studies, the 1972 press revelation of Tuskegee — led to the National Research Act (1974), federal regulations at 45 CFR 46 (the "Common Rule"), institutional review boards, and formal informed-consent requirements. Presidential apologies followed for Tuskegee (1997) and Guatemala (2010). Historians note that many victims were chosen precisely because poverty, race, incarceration, or institutionalisation made them unlikely to object or be believed.
