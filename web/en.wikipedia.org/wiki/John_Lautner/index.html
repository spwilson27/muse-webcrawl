John Lautner (16 July 1911 – 24 October 1994) was an American architect whose dramatic, sculptural houses — mostly in Southern California — became icons of mid-century modernism and of Los Angeles itself. Influenced by Frank Lloyd Wright, his mentor, Lautner developed an "organic" architecture of concrete, glass and sweeping geometry that fused buildings into their sites and exploited spectacular views. Famous works include the Chemosphere (1960), the Goldstein House, the Elrod House in Palm Springs, and the Garcia House.

Early life and Taliesin

Lautner was born in Marquette, Michigan, where his father taught at Northern Michigan University; his childhood summers on Lake Superior shaped his feeling for landscape. At the University of Michigan he studied literature and philosophy rather than architecture. In 1933 he and his wife Mary joined Frank Lloyd Wright's Taliesin Fellowship in Wisconsin, where Lautner spent six years building and absorbing Wright's principles — and a lifelong resistance to academic fashion.

Career

Moving to Los Angeles in 1938, Lautner supervised Wright projects before opening his own practice; his own Lautner House (1939) announced his talent. Through the 1940s and 1950s he designed Googie coffee shops and stores (including the original Googie's and Henry's restaurants) and innovative houses, such as the Mauer House and the Foster Carling House with its moving wall. The Chemosphere (1960) — an octagonal, UFO-like house perched on a single concrete column above a Hollywood Hills slope — became his most recognisable work and was declared a Los Angeles Historic-Cultural Monument.

Later residences grew ever more monumental: the Elrod House (1968) with its conical concrete dome and pool running through the living room (immortalised in the Bond film Diamonds Are Forever); the Arango House (1973) in Acapulco; the undulating Garcia House (1962); the Sheats–Goldstein House with its coffered concrete ceiling and triangular skylights; and the vast Hope Residence in Palm Springs. Lautner called architecture "a basic human need" and insisted each building grow from its site and programme without predetermined style — total concepts he termed "real architecture."

Legacy

Long overshadowed by the glass-box International Style, Lautner was rediscovered by critics and filmmakers; his houses appear in films from Lethal Weapon 2 to The Big Lebowski. A major retrospective at the Hammer Museum in 2008 and the work of the John Lautner Foundation have secured his reputation as one of the twentieth century's most original architects. His archive is held by the Getty Research Institute.
