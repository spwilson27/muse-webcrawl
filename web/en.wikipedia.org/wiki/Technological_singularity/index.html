Technological singularity

The technological singularity — or simply the singularity — is a hypothetical future point in time at which technological growth becomes uncontrollable and irreversible, resulting in unforeseeable consequences for human civilisation. According to the most popular version of the hypothesis, the intelligence explosion, an upgradable intelligent agent, typically identified with artificial general intelligence (AGI), will eventually enter positive feedback loops of self-improvement cycles, each new and more intelligent generation appearing more rapidly, causing an "explosion" in intelligence and resulting in a powerful superintelligence that surpasses human intelligence.

Origins of the concept

The idea is often traced to mathematician John von Neumann, who in the 1950s was reported by Stanislaw Ulam to have spoken of "the ever accelerating progress of technology … approaching some essential singularity in the history of the race beyond which human affairs, as we know them, could not continue." In 1965 statistician I. J. Good described an "intelligence explosion" in which an ultraintelligent machine could design even better machines, leaving human intelligence far behind. Science fiction author Vernor Vinge popularised the term in his 1993 essay "The Coming Technological Singularity", predicting the event within 30 years. Futurist Ray Kurzweil extended the argument in The Age of Spiritual Machines (1999) and The Singularity Is Near (2005), projecting the singularity around 2045 on the basis of exponentially accelerating returns in computing.

Intelligence explosion and superintelligence

Philosopher Nick Bostrom's Superintelligence (2014) analysed possible paths to machine superintelligence and the control problem: how to ensure that a system vastly more capable than humans would act in accordance with human values. Concerns include instrumental convergence — the tendency of agents with diverse goals to pursue self-preservation, resource acquisition, and goal integrity — and the orthogonality thesis, the claim that intelligence level and final goals are independent axes.

Criticism

Sceptics question whether exponential trends in computing will translate into recursive self-improvement, whether intelligence is the sort of quantity that can "explode", and whether physical, economic, or cognitive limits will intervene. Cognitive scientist Steven Pinker has argued that superior intelligence does not imply the capability or motivation to dominate, while others, including roboticist Rodney Brooks, have criticised singularity timelines as implausibly confident extrapolations. Some researchers who accept the long-run possibility nevertheless regard alignment and governance of advanced AI as practical concerns independent of the singularity framing.

In popular culture

The singularity has been a recurring theme in science fiction, from Vinge's novels to works exploring posthuman futures, and in popular discourse it is sometimes described, half-jokingly, as "the rapture of the nerds".
