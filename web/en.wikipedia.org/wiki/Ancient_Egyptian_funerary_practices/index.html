Ancient Egyptian funerary practices

The ancient Egyptians developed an extraordinarily elaborate set of funerary practices, grounded in the belief that the soul — composed of elements including the ka (life force) and ba (personality) — survived death and required the preservation of the body, sustenance, and magical protection to reach and enjoy the afterlife in the Field of Reeds.

Mummification

Embalmers removed the internal organs, storing the lungs, liver, stomach and intestines in canopic jars guarded by the Four Sons of Horus; the brain was extracted and discarded, while the heart — seat of thought and conscience — was left in place. The body was desiccated with natron salt for about forty days, then anointed, packed, and wrapped in hundreds of metres of linen bandages, often with amulets placed between the layers. The full process took some seventy days. Cheaper methods were available for the less wealthy, and animal mummies were produced in vast numbers as votive offerings.

Funeral and burial

A procession conveyed the mummy to the tomb, accompanied by mourners and by priests who performed the Opening of the Mouth ceremony, ritually restoring the deceased's senses for the afterlife. Tombs ranged from simple pit graves to mastabas, rock-cut chambers in the Valley of the Kings, and the great pyramids. Graves were stocked with food, furniture, clothing, weapons and the small shabti figurines intended to labour for the deceased in the next world.

Judgment and texts

The dead were believed to face judgment before Osiris, their hearts weighed against the feather of Maat (truth); those who failed were devoured by the monster Ammit. Funerary literature evolved from the Pyramid Texts of the Old Kingdom through the Coffin Texts of the Middle Kingdom to the New Kingdom Book of the Dead, collections of spells guiding the soul through the underworld (Duat).
