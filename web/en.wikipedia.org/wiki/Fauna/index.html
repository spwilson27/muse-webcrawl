Fauna

The fauna of a region comprises all of its animal life.

Fauna (pl.: faunae or faunas) is all of the animal life present in a particular region or time. The corresponding term for plants is flora, and for fungi, funga. Flora, fauna, funga and other forms of life are collectively referred to as biota. Zoologists and palaeontologists use fauna to refer to a typical collection of animals found in a specific time or place, e.g. the "Sonoran Desert fauna" or the "Burgess Shale fauna".

Etymology

The name comes from Fauna, a Roman goddess of earth and fertility, and from the related fauns, gods of the forest in Roman religion and mythology. The term was first used in a biological sense by Carl Linnaeus in the title of his 1745 work Fauna Suecica ("Fauna of Sweden").

Subdivisions

Faunas are often classified by the size of the animals they include:

  • Cryofauna – animals living in or near ice
  • Cryptofauna – animals that live in protected or concealed microhabitats
  • Macrofauna – benthic or soil animals at least 1 mm in length
  • Megafauna – large animals of a region or period
  • Meiofauna – small benthic invertebrates of marine and freshwater environments
  • Mesofauna – soil invertebrates too small to be seen easily with the naked eye
  • Microfauna – microscopic animals

Study

The study of the fauna of a region is called faunistics; inventories and checklists compiled by biologists help document biodiversity, track invasive species, and guide conservation.
