Absurd literature is a body of fiction and drama rooted in the philosophy of absurdism — the conflict between humanity's search for inherent meaning and the apparent meaninglessness of the universe. Absurdist works characteristically feature illogical, circular, or dreamlike situations, purposeless action, and characters unable to communicate or understand their circumstances.

Origins and development

Precursors include the works of Franz Kafka, whose novels The Trial and The Castle depict protagonists trapped in incomprehensible bureaucracies. After the Second World War, writers such as Albert Camus — whose essay The Myth of Sisyphus and novel The Stranger articulated absurdist philosophy — and the dramatists of the Theatre of the Absurd, including Samuel Beckett and Eugène Ionesco, developed the mode.

Characteristics

Absurd literature often dispenses with conventional plot and character development, uses repetitive or nonsensical dialogue, and mixes tragedy with farce. Its works ask whether life has meaning and dramatize the failure of language and reason to supply one.
