Fire engine

A modern fire engine in service with a municipal fire department.

A fire engine (also known in some territories as a fire truck, fire tender, or fire appliance) is a road vehicle, usually a specially designed or modified truck, that functions as a firefighting apparatus. The primary purposes of a fire engine include transporting firefighters and water to an incident scene, as well as carrying equipment for firefighting operations. Some fire engines have specialized functions, such as wildland fire suppression and aircraft rescue and firefighting, and may also carry equipment for technical rescue.

Many fire engines are based on commercial vehicle chassis further upgraded with firefighting-specific components. They are generally equipped with audible and visual warnings, as well as communication equipment such as two-way radios and mobile data terminals.

Terminology

The terms "fire engine" and "fire truck" are often used interchangeably to mean the same thing; however, some fire departments or services distinguish fire engines from other vehicles. A fire engine is a vehicle that carries a pump, water tank, hose, and possibly an extendable ladder or other devices. A fire truck is typically a large vehicle that carries an extensive complement of ground ladders and possibly a turntable ladder or other aerial device.

History

Early devices

Early firefighting devices known as "hand tubs" were developed in the 17th century. These were tubs carried or mounted on wheels, fitted with a hand-operated pump worked by handles on either side, forcing water through a hose or nozzle. Newsham fire engines, developed by Richard Newsham in 18th-century London, became widely exported. The introduction of steam-powered pumps in the 19th century greatly increased the power and reliability of fire engines, and steam pumpers remained common into the early 20th century.

Motorisation

The first motorised fire engines appeared in the early 1900s, combining internal combustion engines for both propulsion and pump operation. By the 1930s, diesel engines and enclosed crew cabs had become standard. Modern engines are typically built on custom chassis purpose-designed for fire service use.

Types

Pumper

The conventional fire engine, often called a pumper, carries a fire pump, a water tank (typically 300 to 1,500 US gallons), and an assortment of hoses and ladders. Its primary function is to supply water to attack lines at a fire.

Aerial apparatus

Aerial devices include turntable ladders, platforms, and water towers, used for rescue and elevated water streams. A truck company typically operates aerial apparatus and carries tools for forcible entry, ventilation, and search and rescue.

Wildland engines

Wildland fire engines are smaller, often four-wheel-drive vehicles designed for off-road operation at wildfires, carrying smaller water tanks and portable pumps.

Airport crash tenders

Aircraft rescue and firefighting (ARFF) vehicles are specialized engines designed for rapid response to aircraft emergencies, carrying large quantities of water and foam.

Equipment

Fire engines carry a wide range of equipment, including:

  • Ground ladders of various lengths
  • Attack and supply hose lines
  • Self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA)
  • Hydraulic rescue tools (commonly called the "Jaws of Life")
  • Ventilation fans and saws
  • Medical equipment for emergency medical response
  • Thermal imaging cameras

Crewing

Staffing varies by jurisdiction. In the United States, a typical engine company consists of an officer, a driver/engineer responsible for pump operations, and one or more firefighters. Many departments cross-train firefighters as emergency medical technicians, as medical calls form the majority of responses.
