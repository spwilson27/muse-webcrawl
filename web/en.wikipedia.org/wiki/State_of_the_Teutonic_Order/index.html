The State of the Teutonic Order (German: Staat des Deutschen Ordens), also called Deutschordensstaat, was a crusader state formed in 1230 by the German Teutonic Order of knights during the Northern Crusades in the region of Prussia. It grew out of the order's conquest and Christianisation of the pagan Old Prussians and expanded to become one of the most powerful states on the southern Baltic coast.

History

Foundation and conquest

In 1226 the Polish duke Konrad I of Masovia, unable to defend his lands against Prussian raids, invited the Teutonic Knights to campaign against the Prussians in exchange for the Chełmno Land. Emperor Frederick II confirmed the grant in the Golden Bull of Rimini (1226), and Pope Gregory IX endorsed the crusade in the Golden Bull of Rieti (1234). Under Grand Master Hermann von Salza and Landmeister Hermann Balk, the knights began the systematic conquest of Prussia in 1230, building a chain of castles along the Vistula — Thorn (Toruń), Kulm (Chełmno), Marienwerder and Elbing among them — and founding towns under German law that attracted settlers from the Empire.

After decades of warfare, punctuated by the Great Prussian Uprising (1260–1274), the order completed the conquest by about 1283. In 1237 the order also absorbed the Livonian Brothers of the Sword, extending its rule into Livonia (Terra Mariana) in modern Latvia and Estonia.

Expansion and zenith

In 1308–1309 the order seized Gdańsk Pomerania (Pomerelia) from Poland, bringing the state to its greatest territorial extent and prompting the transfer of the Grand Master's seat from Venice to Marienburg Castle (Malbork) in 1309. The order bought the Neumark (1309), Estonia from Denmark (1346), and the island of Gotland (1398, held until 1408). Under Grand Masters such as Winrich von Kniprode (r. 1351–1382) the state reached its zenith: a wealthy, centralised administration financed by the grain and amber trades, a network of some 60 towns, and annual crusading campaigns (Reisen) against pagan Lithuania that drew knights from across Europe.

Decline and the Thirteen Years' War

The conversion of Lithuania and its union with Poland (1386) removed the crusade's rationale. At the Battle of Grunwald (Tannenberg) on 15 July 1410, a Polish–Lithuanian army crushed the order and killed Grand Master Ulrich von Jungingen. The First Peace of Thorn (1411) imposed a heavy indemnity. In 1454 the Prussian Confederation of cities and nobles rebelled and placed itself under the Polish crown, beginning the Thirteen Years' War. By the Second Peace of Thorn (1466) the order lost Pomerelia, Chełmno Land and Marienburg, retaining only eastern Prussia as a fief of the Polish king, with the capital moved to Königsberg.

Secularisation

In 1525 Grand Master Albert of Brandenburg-Ansbach converted to Lutheranism and, by the Treaty of Kraków, secularised the Prussian branch of the order, becoming the first Duke of Prussia as a Polish vassal. The Livonian branch survived until 1561. The Teutonic Order itself continued as a religious order within the Holy Roman Empire.

Government and society

The state was ruled by the Grand Master (Hochmeister), elected by the order's chapter, assisted by senior officers including the Grand Commander and the Marshal. Territory was divided into commandries (Komtureien) administered by knight-brothers. The population comprised German settlers in the towns and countryside, assimilated Prussians, and Polish-speaking communities in the south; serfdom bound much of the peasantry. Trade through Hanseatic ports such as Danzig (Gdańsk), Elbing and Königsberg, together with the order's monopoly on the amber trade, made the state prosperous.
