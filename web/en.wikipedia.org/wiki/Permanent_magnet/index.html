Permanent magnet

Permanent magnet
DefinitionObject producing its own persistent magnetic field
MaterialsFerrite, alnico, samarium–cobalt, neodymium–iron–boron
Key propertiesRemanence (Br), coercivity (Hc), energy product (BHmax)
ContrastElectromagnet (field only while current flows)

A permanent magnet is an object made from a material that is magnetised and creates its own persistent magnetic field. An everyday example is a refrigerator magnet used to hold notes on a refrigerator door. Materials that can be magnetised, and that are strongly attracted to a magnet, are called ferromagnetic (or ferrimagnetic); these include iron, nickel, cobalt and most of their alloys, and some alloys of rare-earth metals.

Properties

A permanent magnet material is characterised by high remanence — the magnetisation remaining after an external field is removed — and high coercivity — resistance to demagnetisation. Their product, the maximum energy product (BHmax), measures the magnet's strength per volume. Unlike an electromagnet, a permanent magnet requires no power supply, but its field cannot be switched off or readily adjusted, and it weakens above its Curie temperature or under strong opposing fields or shock.

Materials

  • Ferrite (ceramic) magnets — inexpensive, corrosion-resistant, moderate strength; used in refrigerator magnets and small motors.
  • Alnico — aluminium–nickel–cobalt alloys, early 20th century; good temperature stability, low coercivity.
  • Samarium–cobalt — rare-earth magnets (1970s) with high strength and excellent high-temperature performance.
  • Neodymium–iron–boron (NdFeB) — developed in 1984; the strongest commercially available type, used in hard-disk drives, headphones, wind turbines and electric-vehicle motors, though more corrosion-prone and temperature-sensitive.

Applications

Permanent magnets are used in electric motors and generators, loudspeakers and headphones, magnetic bearings and couplings, MRI scanners (in some designs), magnetic separators, sensors, latches and toys. Their strength is central to modern compact electronics and renewable-energy technology, which has made the supply of rare-earth elements a matter of industrial policy.

History

Naturally magnetised lodestone (magnetite) was known in antiquity and used in early compasses in China and medieval Europe. Artificial steel magnets followed, and the twentieth century saw successive improvements from carbon steel through alnico and ferrite to the rare-earth magnets.
