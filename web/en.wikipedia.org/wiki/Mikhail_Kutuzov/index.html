Mikhail Kutuzov

Prince Mikhail Illarionovich Golenishchev-Kutuzov (16 September 1745 – 28 April 1813) was a Russian field marshal, the hero of the Patriotic War of 1812. As commander-in-chief he preserved the Russian army through the long retreat before Napoleon's invasion, fought him at the Battle of Borodino, surrendered Moscow to save the army, and then harried the Grande Armée's retreat until it was annihilated — a strategy of patience that made him, in Russian memory, the saviour of the fatherland.

Career

Kutuzov made his name in the Turkish wars under Suvorov, distinguishing himself at the storming of Izmail (1790) despite wounds that cost him an eye and twice nearly killed him. In 1805 he commanded the Russian army in Austria and extricated it by retreat before being overruled into the disaster of Austerlitz. As commander against the Ottomans in 1811–12 he won the war and signed the advantageous Treaty of Bucharest weeks before Napoleon invaded.

1812

Public clamour forced Alexander I of Russia to appoint the ageing, one-eyed Kutuzov over the German-born Barclay de Tolly in August 1812. Kutuzov fought the great defensive battle at Borodino, then at the council of Fili took the agonising decision to abandon Moscow — "with the loss of Moscow, Russia is not lost; with the loss of the army, Russia is lost", as the saying attributed to him runs. From the Tarutino camp he rebuilt the army, blocked Napoleon's southern retreat at Maloyaroslavets, and forced him back onto the devastated Smolensk road, where Cossacks, partisans, hunger and cold completed the destruction. Kutuzov received the victory title Prince of Smolensk.

Death and memory

He died of illness at Bunzlau in April 1813, pursuing the French into Germany, and was buried in the Kazan Cathedral in Saint Petersburg. Tolstoy made him the wise, passive centre of War and Peace; in the Second World War the USSR instituted the Order of Kutuzov (1942) for skilful retreat and counterstroke — a uniquely apt decoration.
