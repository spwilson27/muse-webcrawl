Neanderthal genome project

The Neanderthal genome project is an international scientific effort to sequence the genome of the Neanderthals, the extinct archaic humans who lived across Europe and western Asia until about 40,000 years ago. It was established in 2006 by the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig, Germany, under the leadership of Swedish geneticist Svante Pääbo, working with the sequencing company 454 Life Sciences.

Findings

In May 2010 the team published a draft sequence of the Neanderthal genome, assembled from DNA extracted from bones of three individuals from Vindija Cave, Croatia, supplemented by material from other sites. The headline result was that Neanderthals interbred with the ancestors of modern humans: roughly 1–4 percent of the genomes of present-day people of non-African ancestry derives from Neanderthals. The project also produced a catalogue of genomic regions where modern humans differ from Neanderthals, including genes involved in skin, immunity, and metabolism.

Subsequent work produced a high-coverage genome from a toe bone from Denisova Cave (the "Altai Neanderthal", published 2013), revealing inbreeding in that population and further episodes of gene flow between Neanderthals, Denisovans, and modern humans. Comparisons identified Neanderthal-derived variants affecting modern traits and disease risks, including immune responses and, notably, a chromosome-3 region associated with severe COVID-19.

Significance

The project demonstrated that complete genomes could be recovered from ancient remains and transformed understanding of human evolution from a story of replacement to one of admixture. Pääbo received the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discoveries concerning the genomes of extinct hominins and human evolution, work rooted in the Neanderthal genome project.
