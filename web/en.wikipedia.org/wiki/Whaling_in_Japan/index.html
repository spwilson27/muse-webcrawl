Whaling in Japan

Japanese whaling, in terms of active hunting of whales, is estimated by the Japan Whaling Association to have begun around the 12th century; organised coastal whaling with nets dates from the 17th century in towns such as Taiji. Japan became a major pelagic whaling nation in the 20th century, and after the International Whaling Commission's 1986 commercial moratorium it continued whaling under a scientific research permit — a programme the International Court of Justice ruled in 2014 was not genuinely scientific. In 2019 Japan withdrew from the IWC and resumed commercial whaling within its territorial waters and exclusive economic zone.

Whaling has deep roots in some Japanese coastal communities.

History

Hand-harpoon and net whaling flourished in the Edo period, using nearly the whole animal for meat, oil and materials. Norwegian-style modern whaling arrived in the Meiji era, and factory ships began Antarctic expeditions in the 1930s. After World War II, whale meat became a vital protein source — at its 1960s peak supplying a large share of Japan's meat — and was common in school lunches.

Controversy

Consumption has since fallen sharply (to a few thousand tonnes a year), and polls show most Japanese rarely eat whale. Anti-whaling campaigns, notably by Sea Shepherd against Antarctic fleets, and disputes with Australia and other nations made Japan's programme a diplomatic flashpoint. Small-type coastal whaling and the Taiji dolphin drive hunt, governed separately, also draw international criticism. The government defends whaling as a cultural tradition and matter of sustainable resource use. See also International Whaling Commission.
