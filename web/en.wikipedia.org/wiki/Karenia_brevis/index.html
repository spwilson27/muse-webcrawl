Karenia brevis is a microscopic, single-celled dinoflagellate native to the Gulf of Mexico. It is best known as the organism responsible for the harmful algal blooms known as "Florida red tide", which can kill large numbers of fish and other marine animals and cause respiratory irritation in humans.

Description

K. brevis is an unarmoured dinoflagellate roughly 20–40 micrometres across, with two flagella that allow it to swim. It photosynthesises and can also use organic nutrients. Blooms typically begin offshore in late summer or autumn and are transported toward the coast by winds and currents. The species was formerly classified as Gymnodinium breve and Ptychodiscus brevis; the genus was renamed in honour of phycologist Karen A. Steidinger.

Toxins and effects

The organism produces a group of neurotoxins called brevetoxins. When cells break open in surf, the toxins become aerosolised and can cause coughing, sneezing and eye irritation in people onshore. Brevetoxins accumulate in filter-feeding shellfish, causing neurotoxic shellfish poisoning in humans who eat them, and they are a major cause of mortality in manatees, dolphins, sea turtles and fish during severe blooms. Blooms also impose heavy economic costs on coastal tourism and fisheries.

Monitoring

In Florida, K. brevis is monitored by the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission, which classifies bloom severity by cell counts per litre of seawater. Satellite imagery and forecast models are used to track bloom movement.
