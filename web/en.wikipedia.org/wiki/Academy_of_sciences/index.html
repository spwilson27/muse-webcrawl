Academy of sciences

An academy of sciences is a learned society dedicated to the advancement of science, typically at the national level. National academies usually elect their members from among distinguished scientists, advise governments, publish journals, award prizes and, in some countries, directly operate research institutes.

History

The model derives from early modern learned societies such as the Accademia dei Lincei (1603) and Accademia del Cimento in Italy. The Royal Society of London (1660) and the French Academy of Sciences (1666) set the pattern of state-recognised bodies of elected fellows. Peter the Great founded the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences in 1724, which developed a large network of directly administered research institutes — a model widely adopted in the Soviet Union and China.

Examples

  • National Academy of Sciences (United States, 1863)
  • French Academy of Sciences (1666), part of the Institut de France alongside the Académie Française
  • Russian Academy of Sciences (1724)
  • Chinese Academy of Sciences (1949)
  • German National Academy of Sciences Leopoldina (1652)
  • Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences (1739), which awards the Nobel Prizes in Physics and Chemistry

Functions

Academies commonly provide independent scientific advice, promote science education and public engagement, maintain international links through bodies such as the InterAcademy Partnership, and confer honours. In some systems — notably the former Soviet model — academies employ tens of thousands of researchers in their own institutes, while in the Anglo-American model they are chiefly honorific and advisory.
