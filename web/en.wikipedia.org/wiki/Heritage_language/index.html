Heritage language
Field: Linguistics, language education
Related terms: Heritage speaker; community language; immigrant language

Heritage language

A heritage language is a minority language learned by its speakers at home as children, but never fully developed because of insufficient input from the social environment. The term is commonly used for the languages of immigrant communities and Indigenous peoples: for example, Spanish spoken by second-generation immigrants in the United States, or Chinese in diaspora communities.

Heritage speakers

Heritage speakers typically acquire the language naturalistically in early childhood but shift to the dominant societal language upon starting school. Their proficiency is often strong in listening and pronunciation but incomplete in literacy, formal registers, and grammar — a profile that differs from both native speakers raised in the language's homeland and foreign-language learners. Researchers such as Guadalupe Valdés and Maria Polinsky have been influential in defining this population.

Education

Heritage language programmes — community weekend schools, bilingual education, and university courses designed specifically for heritage learners — aim to maintain and develop the language, which is often tied to cultural identity and family cohesion. From a policy perspective the concept is also applied to Indigenous languages undergoing revitalisation.

References: Valdés, G., "Learning and Not Learning English" (2001); Polinsky, M. & Kagan, O., "Heritage languages: In the 'wild' and in the classroom", Language and Linguistics Compass (2007).
