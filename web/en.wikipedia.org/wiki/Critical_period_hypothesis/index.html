Critical period hypothesis

Critical period hypothesis
Field: Linguistics, psycholinguistics
Proposed by: Eric Lenneberg (1967)
Related: Second-language acquisition

The critical period hypothesis is the proposal that there is a biologically determined window in early life during which language can be acquired fully and effortlessly, after which acquisition becomes markedly harder and typically less complete. It is most associated with neurologist Eric Lenneberg's Biological Foundations of Language (1967).

Origins

Earlier observations came from cases of children deprived of language, such as Victor of Aveyron, and from studies of brain injury showing children recover language better than adults. Lenneberg linked the period to cerebral lateralisation, suggesting it ran from about age two to puberty.

Evidence

Second-language learners who begin after puberty rarely achieve native-like pronunciation and often struggle with grammar, a pattern studied by Johnson and Newport (1989) among immigrants. Cases of isolated children such as Genie, discovered at age 13, who never acquired full syntax, are often cited, though abuse confounds interpretation.

Criticism and sensitive periods

Many researchers now prefer a sensitive period: a gradual decline rather than a sharp cutoff, differing by linguistic subsystem — phonology closes earliest, vocabulary remains learnable lifelong. Social, educational and motivational factors also explain adult outcomes.
