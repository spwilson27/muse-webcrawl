Edible seaweed

Edible seaweed
Common typesNori, kombu, wakame, dulse, sea lettuce
Main producing regionsEast and Southeast Asia; coastal Europe
NutrientsIodine, minerals, vitamins, soluble fibre, umami compounds

Edible seaweed, or sea vegetables, are marine algae that can be eaten and are used in cooking, especially in East and Southeast Asian cuisines and in coastal traditions of the British Isles, Iceland and the Pacific. They are harvested wild or farmed — seaweed aquaculture is a major industry, particularly in China, Indonesia, South Korea and Japan.

Common varieties

  • Nori (Pyropia species, red algae) — dried into sheets used for sushi rolls and snacks; in Korea called gim, in Wales processed into laverbread.
  • Kombu (kelps of the genus Saccharina) — used to make Japanese dashi stock; a principal source of glutamates and umami flavour.
  • Wakame (Undaria pinnatifida) — used in salads and miso soup.
  • Dulse (Palmaria palmata) — red alga eaten in Ireland, Iceland and Atlantic Canada, fresh, dried or as a snack.
  • Sea lettuce (Ulva species) — green alga used in salads and soups.
  • Hijiki, arame, mozuku and ogonori — other regional staples; Irish moss and agar- and carrageenan-producing red algae supply food additives.

Nutrition and safety

Seaweeds are rich in iodine (often very high), calcium, iron, magnesium, folate and soluble fibre, and low in calories. Their glutamate content gives them strong umami. Cautions include excessive iodine intake affecting the thyroid, variable accumulation of heavy metals such as inorganic arsenic (notably in hijiki), and high vitamin K content relevant to people on anticoagulants.

Uses beyond food

Hydrocolloids extracted from seaweeds — agar, carrageenan and alginates — are widely used as gelling and thickening agents in food, cosmetics and biotechnology. Seaweed farming is promoted as a relatively low-input aquaculture: seaweeds need no fresh water, fertiliser or land, and absorb nutrients and carbon as they grow.
