Engelbrekt Engelbrektsson

Engelbrekt Engelbrektsson (c. 1390 – 4 May 1436) was a Swedish nobleman and statesman who led the Engelbrekt Rebellion of 1434–1436 against King Eric of Pomerania, ruler of the Kalmar Union. He is regarded as a national hero in Sweden, and the rebellion he led helped lay the groundwork for Swedish nationhood.

Background

Engelbrekt came from the mining district of Dalarna, and was himself a mine owner (bergsman) of German-descended family. Grievances against the Danish bailiffs of the Union king — including heavy taxation and oppression of the mining communities — led him to travel to Denmark to present complaints to the king. When redress failed, he raised a force of miners and peasants.

The rebellion

In the summer of 1434 the rebels took castle after castle across central Sweden. The Swedish Council of the Realm, initially alarmed, moved to support the movement, and at a meeting in Arboga in 1435 — sometimes counted as the first Swedish Riksdag — Engelbrekt was proclaimed leader of the realm (rikshövitsman) alongside the nobleman Karl Knutsson (Bonde).

Death and legacy

Factional rivalry pushed Engelbrekt toward the margins of the leadership, and on 4 May 1436 he was murdered on an island in Lake Hjälmaren by the nobleman Måns Bengtsson (Natt och Dag). He was buried in Örebro, where his grave became a place of pilgrimage. Engelbrekt has been celebrated in Swedish literature, opera and public monuments as a champion of liberty and the common people; Örebro's Engelbrekt statue and the Engelbrekt Church in Stockholm commemorate him.
