Borderline personality disorder (BPD), also known as emotionally unstable personality disorder, is a personality disorder characterised by a long-term pattern of unstable interpersonal relationships, a distorted sense of self, and strong emotional reactions. Those affected often engage in self-harm and other dangerous behaviours, in part to relieve intense feelings of emptiness, fear of abandonment, stress or anger.

Signs and symptoms

Diagnosis under the DSM-5 requires five (or more) of nine criteria, including frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment; a pattern of unstable and intense interpersonal relationships alternating between idealisation and devaluation; identity disturbance; impulsivity in at least two potentially self-damaging areas; recurrent suicidal behaviour or self-mutilation; affective instability; chronic feelings of emptiness; inappropriate, intense anger; and transient, stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociative symptoms.

Causes

As with other mental disorders, the causes of BPD are complex and not fully understood. Genetic factors play a substantial role — twin studies suggest considerable heritability — and childhood adversity, including abuse and neglect, is reported at high rates among people with the diagnosis. Neurobiological research implicates circuits involved in emotion regulation, including the amygdala and prefrontal cortex.

Treatment

Psychotherapy is the mainstay of treatment. Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT), developed by Marsha Linehan specifically for BPD, has the strongest evidence base; mentalization-based treatment, transference-focused psychotherapy and schema therapy are also used. No medication is approved specifically for BPD, though drugs are commonly prescribed for co-occurring conditions such as depression and anxiety.

Prognosis and epidemiology

Long-term follow-up studies show that many people with BPD improve substantially over time, with high rates of remission over a decade. Point prevalence in the general population is commonly estimated at around 1–2%. The term "borderline" derives from an older psychoanalytic idea that the condition lay on the border between neurosis and psychosis; the name is widely criticised as stigmatising and misleading.
