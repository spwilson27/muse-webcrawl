Dame Alice Kyteler (c. 1263 – after 1325) was a wealthy noblewoman of Kilkenny, Ireland, who in 1324 became the first person recorded to stand trial for witchcraft in Ireland. The proceedings against her, driven by the Franciscan bishop of Ossory, Richard de Ledrede, ended with the burning of her servant Petronilla de Meath — the first recorded execution for heresy on the island — while Kyteler herself escaped.

Life

Kyteler was a member of the Anglo-Norman Kyteler family of Kilkenny. She was married four times: to William Outlaw, a banker; to Adam le Blund; to Richard de Valle; and lastly to Sir John le Poer. Each husband predeceased her, leaving her an increasingly wealthy widow — a fact that later formed part of the case against her.

The trial of 1324

In 1324 the children of her fourth husband accused Kyteler of poisoning their father and of witchcraft. Bishop de Ledrede investigated and laid charges of sorcery: that Kyteler denied Christ, sacrificed animals to a demon named "Robin, son of Art", brewed potions from the entrails of cocks, the hair and nails of dead men and other ingredients, and bewitched her husbands. A search of her house reportedly produced a pipe of ointment and a wafer bearing the devil's name.

Kyteler used her connections to resist: the Chancellor of Ireland, Roger Outlaw, was her former brother-in-law, and de Ledrede was himself briefly arrested when he pressed the case. After her patron's protection failed, Kyteler fled to England and disappears from the record. Her servant Petronilla de Meath was tortured, confessed, and was burned at the stake in Kilkenny on 3 November 1324 — the first such burning in Ireland.

Legacy

The case is notable as an early importation of continental ideas of diabolical witchcraft into Ireland, superimposed on older folk beliefs in fairies and charms. Kyteler's Inn in Kilkenny, traditionallly identified with her house, bears her name today.
