Unitarian Universalism (UU) is a liberal religious movement characterised by a "free and responsible search for truth and meaning". It has no creed: members are united by shared values and covenants rather than common doctrine, and may draw on Christianity, Judaism, Buddhism, humanism, earth-centred traditions and direct experience.

History

The movement arose from two American denominations: the Unitarians, who rejected the doctrine of the Trinity, organised through figures such as William Ellery Channing and Ralph Waldo Emerson; and the Universalists, who taught universal salvation, rejecting eternal damnation. The American Unitarian Association (1825) and the Universalist Church of America merged in 1961 to form the Unitarian Universalist Association, headquartered in Boston.

Principles and practices

Unitarian Universalists affirm seven principles adopted by the UUA, beginning with "the inherent worth and dignity of every person" and including justice, equity and compassion in human relations, acceptance of one another, a free search for truth, the democratic process, and respect for the interdependent web of all existence. Worship varies widely between congregations; common elements include the lighting of a flaming chalice — a symbol derived from the Unitarian Service Committee's aid work during the Second World War — sermons, hymns and readings from many sources.

The movement is congregational in polity: each local church owns its property and calls its own minister. UUs have been prominent in social justice causes including abolition, civil rights and marriage equality.
