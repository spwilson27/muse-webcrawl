Alopecia areata

Alopecia areata is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks hair follicles, causing hair to fall out, usually in small round patches on the scalp or beard. It affects about 2 per cent of people at some point in life, often beginning in childhood or young adulthood.

Forms and course

Most cases involve a few patches that regrow spontaneously within a year. More extensive forms include alopecia totalis (loss of all scalp hair) and alopecia universalis (loss of all body hair). Nail pitting may accompany the condition. The course is unpredictable, with relapses common, and the condition is associated with other autoimmune disorders such as vitiligo and thyroid disease. It is not contagious and does not destroy follicles permanently.

Causes and treatment

Genetic susceptibility combined with immune dysregulation of the hair follicle's immune privilege underlies the disease. Treatments include corticosteroids (topical, injected or systemic), contact immunotherapy, and, for severe disease, JAK inhibitors such as baricitinib, approved for adults with severe alopecia areata in 2022, followed by ritlecitinib and deuruxolitinib. Psychological support is important because of the condition's visible effects.
