Norse funerals, or the burial customs of Viking Age Scandinavians, involved elaborate rituals intended to equip the dead for the afterlife and to display the status of the deceased and their family. Practices varied by region, period and wealth, encompassing cremation, inhumation in mounds or stone ship settings, and the famous ship burial.

Ship burials

In the grandest funerals the dead were laid in a ship or boat with weapons, tools, furniture, food, horses and sometimes human sacrifices, then buried under a mound or cremated. The Oseberg ship (buried 834 CE) contained two women, a richly carved vessel, sledges, tapestries and sacrificed animals; the Gokstad ship (c. 890) held a high-status man. Anglo-Saxon Sutton Hoo in England reflects closely related customs.

Accounts

The most famous literary description is by the Arab traveller Ahmad ibn Fadlan, who witnessed the funeral of a Rus chieftain on the Volga in 921–922: the ship was drawn ashore, the chieftain laid in a pavilion with goods, a slave girl who "volunteered" was ritually killed and placed beside him, and the ship was burned. Norse sagas describe cremation on pyres (Baldr's funeral in the Prose Edda), grave goods, memorial feasts (sjaund) seven days after death, and runestones raised to the dead.

Beliefs

Burial provisioned the dead for a continuing existence — in Valhalla or Odin's hall for warriors, Hel, or within the mound itself as a draugr. Grave goods ranged from everyday tools and food to elaborate weapon sets, and sites were often reopened in antiquity, whether for ritual "retrieval" of swords or simple robbery.

Archaeology

Excavations of cemeteries such as Birka (Sweden), Hedeby and Lindholm Høje (Denmark) show cremation predominating in some regions and chamber-grave inhumation in others, with Christianisation in the 10th–11th centuries gradually replacing furnished burial with churchyard interment.
