Pattern 1913 Enfield

The Pattern 1913 Enfield (P13) was an experimental British bolt-action rifle developed at the Royal Small Arms Factory, Enfield, in response to lessons of the Boer War. It was intended to replace the Short Magazine Lee-Enfield, firing a powerful new rimless cartridge, the .276 Enfield, from a Mauser-style action with an integral five-round magazine.

Development

Experience against Boer marksmen armed with Mauser rifles led Britain to seek a flatter-shooting cartridge and a stronger action. The P13 combined a Mauser-type bolt with Enfield features, aperture sights and a distinctive stock shape. Trials in 1913 showed promise but also problems: the .276 cartridge produced excessive muzzle flash, recoil, barrel wear and overheating.

Fate and descendants

The outbreak of the First World War shelved the project; Britain retained the SMLE and .303 cartridge for mass production. The P13 design was adapted to .303 as the Pattern 1914 (P14), produced in the United States by Winchester, Remington and Eddystone and widely issued as a sniper and second-line rifle. In the United States the design, reworked for .30-06, became the M1917 Enfield, which armed more American troops in 1917–18 than the Springfield M1903.
