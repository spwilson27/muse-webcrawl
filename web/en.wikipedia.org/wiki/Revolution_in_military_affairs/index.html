Revolution in military affairs

A revolution in military affairs (RMA) is a major change in the nature of warfare brought about by the innovative application of new technologies which, combined with dramatic changes in military doctrine and operational and organisational concepts, fundamentally alters the character and conduct of military operations.

History of the concept

The term grew out of Soviet military theory of the 1970s and 1980s, where writers such as Marshal Nikolai Ogarkov described a "military-technical revolution" driven by precision-guided munitions, reconnaissance-strike complexes, and automated command and control. American analysts in the Pentagon's Office of Net Assessment, notably Andrew Marshall, adopted and developed the idea in the early 1990s, shortening it to "revolution in military affairs."

Characteristics

An RMA is generally understood to require four elements working together: technological change, the evolution of military systems, operational innovation, and organisational adaptation. Technology alone is not enough: the interwar development of the tank only transformed warfare when combined with new doctrine, as in German blitzkrieg, and with organisational changes such as the panzer division.

Historical examples

Historians have identified candidate RMAs across history, including the infantry revolution of the fourteenth century, the gunpowder revolution, the Napoleonic levée en masse and corps system, the mechanisation and radio of the Second World War, and the nuclear revolution. The most discussed modern case is the precision-strike and information revolution demonstrated by the United States in the 1991 Gulf War.

Debate

Critics argue the concept is overused, that many supposed revolutions are evolutionary, and that an excessive focus on technology and conventional warfare left Western militaries ill-prepared for the counterinsurgency campaigns in Iraq and Afghanistan. Supporters reply that the RMA framework usefully highlights how militaries must adapt organisations and doctrine, not merely buy new equipment.
