Progress (history)

Progress is the idea that history moves in a direction of improvement—that advances in technology, science, and social organization inevitably bring about an improvement in the human condition. The concept arose in the Age of Enlightenment and became one of the defining ideas of modernity, underlying liberalism, Marxism, and much of Western political thought.

Origins

Earlier Western thought tended to view history as cyclical or as a decline from a golden age. The idea of linear, cumulative progress developed in the 17th and 18th centuries alongside the Scientific Revolution. Thinkers such as Francis Bacon argued that knowledge could grow indefinitely, and the Marquis de Condorcet's Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind (1795) divided history into ten epochs of advancing reason, culminating in a future of equality and enlightenment.

Development

In the 19th century the idea of progress informed Hegelian and Marxist philosophies of history, evolutionary theory applied to society, and ideologies of colonial expansion. In the 20th century, two world wars, totalitarianism, and the threat of nuclear annihilation severely challenged confidence in inevitable progress, and postmodern thinkers questioned grand narratives of improvement altogether. Nevertheless, measurable advances in life expectancy, literacy, and material welfare continue to be cited in debates about whether progress is real and how it should be defined.
