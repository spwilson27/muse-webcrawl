The Group of 77 (G77) at the United Nations is a coalition of developing countries designed to promote its members' collective economic interests and create an enhanced joint negotiating capacity in the United Nations. The group was founded on 15 June 1964 by seventy-seven developing countries that signed the "Joint Declaration of the Seventy-Seven Developing Countries" issued at the end of the first session of the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) in Geneva.

Although the membership has grown — to 134 countries in recent years — the original name has been retained because of its historic significance. China participates in the group and associates itself with its statements (formally "the Group of 77 and China"), although it is not counted as a regular member.

History

The G77 emerged in the context of decolonisation and the widening economic gap between the industrialised North and the developing South. Its early work focused on trade and development; during the 1970s the group was a driving force behind calls for a New International Economic Order, adopted in declarations by the UN General Assembly in 1974, which demanded fairer terms of trade, increased development assistance, and greater control over natural resources. The group remains the largest intergovernmental organisation of developing countries in the United Nations.

Structure and activities

The group operates through chapters in New York (headquarters), Geneva, Nairobi, Paris, Rome, Vienna, and Washington, D.C. (the Group of 24, a G77 affiliate concerned with international monetary affairs, sits in Washington). The chairmanship rotates annually among the regional groups of Africa, Asia-Pacific, and Latin America and the Caribbean. The G77 coordinates positions on issues including development finance, climate change — where it has been a central bloc in UN climate negotiations — trade, and reform of the international financial institutions.

Members

Nearly all developing countries are members, including almost all of Africa, most of Asia and Latin America, and small island developing states. South Africa joined in 1994 after the end of apartheid. A small number of founding members, such as South Korea, Mexico, and Chile, later withdrew upon joining the OECD.
