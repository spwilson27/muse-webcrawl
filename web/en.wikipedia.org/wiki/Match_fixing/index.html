Match fixing

Match fixing occurs in organised sports when a match is played to a completely or partially pre-determined result, violating the rules of the game and often the law. Match fixing is generally motivated by gambling, in which players, officials, or outsiders profit from bets placed on the manipulated outcome. A related practice is spot fixing, in which a specific moment or aspect of a match — rather than the final result — is manipulated for betting purposes.

Motivations

The most common motivation is financial gain through gambling. Matches may also be fixed for competitive advantage: a team may deliberately lose (or arrange a result) to secure an easier opponent in a later round, a better draft position, or to help another team. Conversely, officials such as referees may be bribed to influence outcomes.

Notable incidents

Famous cases include the Black Sox Scandal, in which eight Chicago White Sox players were accused of conspiring to fix the 1919 World Series; the 2006 Italian football scandal (Calciopoli), which involved manipulation of referee appointments; the 2000 Hansie Cronjé cricket scandal in South Africa; spot-fixing by Pakistani cricketers in 2010; and recurring fixing scandals in professional tennis, snooker, and lower-tier football linked to Asian betting syndicates.

Prevention and law enforcement

Sporting bodies maintain integrity units that monitor betting patterns and educate players, while agencies such as Europol and Interpol coordinate cross-border investigations of fixing networks. In many countries match fixing is a criminal offence; the Council of Europe's Macolin Convention (2014) was the first international treaty dedicated to combating the manipulation of sports competitions.
