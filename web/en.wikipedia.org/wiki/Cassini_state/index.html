Cassini state

A Cassini state is a configuration of a rotating celestial body in which its spin axis, its orbit normal, and the normal to the Laplace plane remain coplanar while precessing together. Such configurations are described by Cassini's laws, formulated by Giovanni Domenico Cassini in 1693 for the Moon.

Cassini's laws

The laws state that (1) the Moon rotates uniformly about an axis whose period equals its orbital period, so it always shows the same face to Earth; (2) the Moon's equator maintains a constant inclination to the ecliptic; and (3) the ascending node of the lunar equator on the ecliptic coincides with the descending node of the lunar orbit, keeping the spin axis, orbit normal and ecliptic normal in one plane.

Dynamics

In modern terms, Cassini states are equilibria that arise when the precession of the spin axis, driven by torques from the central body, is resonant with the precession of the orbit. As orbital precession slows, a body can be captured into a Cassini state; which of the several possible states it occupies depends on parameters such as obliquity and orbital inclination. The planet Mercury, in its 3:2 spin–orbit resonance, and several moons and exoplanets are analysed in this framework, and Cassini states are important to studies of tidal heating and obliquity evolution.
