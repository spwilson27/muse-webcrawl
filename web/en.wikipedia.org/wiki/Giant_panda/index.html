The giant panda (Ailuropoda melanoleuca), also known simply as the panda, is a bear species endemic to China. It is characterised by its white coat with black patches around the eyes, over the ears, and across its body. Although classified in the order Carnivora, the giant panda's diet is over 99% bamboo, and it is one of the world's most recognisable and beloved animals, serving as the logo of the World Wide Fund for Nature (WWF).

Taxonomy

The giant panda was first described to Western science in 1869 by the French missionary and naturalist Armand David, who obtained a specimen in Sichuan. Together with the unrelated red panda, it gave rise to the family Ailuridae debate; molecular studies have confirmed that the giant panda is a true bear (family Ursidae), the most basal member of that family, having diverged roughly 19 million years ago. The subspecies A. m. qinlingensis, restricted to the Qinling Mountains, has a smaller skull and brown-and-white fur.

Description

Adults measure around 1.2 to 1.9 metres (3 feet 11 inches to 6 feet 3 inches) long and weigh 70 to 120 kilograms (150 to 260 lb). The giant panda has a "pseudo-thumb" — an enlarged, modified wrist bone (the radial sesamoid) — that helps it grip bamboo stems. Its powerful jaw muscles and large molar teeth are adapted to crushing bamboo, and its round face results from the massive muscles anchoring to the top of the head.

Diet and behaviour

Giant pandas spend 10 to 16 hours a day feeding, consuming 9 to 18 kilograms (20 to 40 lb) of bamboo shoots, stems and leaves daily. Because bamboo is low in nutrients, pandas conserve energy, move slowly, and avoid social interaction much of the time. They are generally solitary; adults maintain overlapping home ranges and communicate mainly through scent marking. Pandas are excellent climbers and capable swimmers.

Reproduction

Females are fertile only two to three days a year, usually in spring. Cubs are born blind and hairless after a gestation of 95 to 160 days, weighing only about 100 grams (3.5 oz) — roughly 1/900th of the mother's weight, the smallest placental mammal young relative to adult size. Twins are common, but the mother usually raises only one cub, which stays with her for up to three years.

Conservation

The giant panda inhabits a few mountain ranges in central China, mainly Sichuan, Shaanxi and Gansu. Habitat fragmentation by roads and railways isolates populations. The fourth national survey (2015) estimated about 1,864 adults in the wild, and in 2016 the IUCN downlisted the species from "endangered" to "vulnerable". China established Giant Panda National Park in 2021, linking 67 existing reserves. Captive breeding centres such as Chengdu Research Base of Giant Panda Breeding, and "panda diplomacy" loans to foreign zoos, have made the species a global conservation icon.
