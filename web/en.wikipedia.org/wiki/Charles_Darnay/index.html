Charles Darnay

Charles Darnay is a fictional character in Charles Dickens' 1859 novel A Tale of Two Cities. A French aristocrat by birth — born Charles St. Evrémonde — he renounces his family's title and wealth out of disgust at their cruelty, emigrates to England, and earns his living as a tutor of French language and literature. He marries Lucie Manette and is twice tried for his life, once in England and once in revolutionary Paris.

Role in the novel

Darnay is introduced as a prisoner in the Tower of London, tried for treason on the evidence of spies; he is acquitted after Sydney Carton points out his striking physical resemblance to the prisoner, undermining eyewitness identification. In France, the crimes of his father and uncle — who wronged the family of Dr Manette and of Madame Defarge — hang over him: when he returns to Paris during the Revolution to save his family's steward Gabelle, he is arrested, tried by a revolutionary tribunal and condemned.

The substitution

Darnay's double, the dissolute lawyer Sydney Carton, who also loves Lucie, exploits their resemblance to take Darnay's place in the Conciergerie and goes to the guillotine in his stead, in one of the most famous acts of self-sacrifice in English fiction: "It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done."

Characterisation

Critics often find Darnay honourable but comparatively passive — the novel's moral centre of decency rather than its most dynamic figure — with Dickens reserving his deepest psychological interest for Carton and Madame Defarge. Darnay embodies the possibility of an aristocrat who repudiates aristocratic guilt, yet cannot escape inherited responsibility.
