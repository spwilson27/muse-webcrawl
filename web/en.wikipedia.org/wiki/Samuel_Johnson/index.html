Samuel Johnson (1709–1784), often called Dr Johnson, was an English writer, poet, playwright, essayist, editor and lexicographer — arguably the most distinguished man of letters in English history. His Dictionary of the English Language (1755) was the standard English dictionary for over a century and remains famous for its wit as much as its scholarship: he defined a lexicographer as "a harmless drudge" and oats as a grain "which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people."

Life and works

Born in Lichfield, Staffordshire, Johnson was plagued by poverty and ill health (scrofula, and the involuntary movements now often attributed to Tourette syndrome). He left Oxford without a degree for lack of funds, moved to London in 1737 with the actor David Garrick, and ground out journalism for the Gentleman's Magazine before the Dictionary — produced in just nine years with a handful of assistants, where the French Academy had used forty scholars for forty years — made his name. Later works include the poem The Vanity of Human Wishes (1749), the periodical essays of The Rambler, the philosophical tale Rasselas (1759), an edition of Shakespeare (1765), A Journey to the Western Islands of Scotland (1775) and the Lives of the Poets (1779–81).

Legacy

Johnson is the most quoted English writer after Shakespeare, largely through James Boswell's Life of Samuel Johnson (1791), considered the greatest biography in English. His conversation, club (The Literary Club, with Reynolds, Burke, Goldsmith and Gibbon) and aphorisms — "When a man is tired of London, he is tired of life" — made him the emblem of 18th-century letters.
