Civil religion

Civil religion, also referred to as a civic religion, is the implicit religious values of a nation, as expressed through public rituals, symbols, and ceremonies on sacred days and at sacred places (such as monuments, battlefields, or national cemeteries). It is distinct from churches and specific religious faiths, though church officials and ceremonies are sometimes incorporated into the practice of civil religion.

Public ceremonies at national memorials are a common expression of civil religion.

History of the concept

The term was coined by Jean-Jacques Rousseau in The Social Contract (1762), where he proposed a minimal civic creed — belief in a deity, an afterlife with reward and punishment, and the sanctity of the social contract — to bind citizens together. Émile Durkheim later analyzed how societies sacralize collective symbols. In 1967 sociologist Robert Bellah's essay "Civil Religion in America" revived the concept, arguing that the United States possesses an elaborate, institutionalized civil religion — inauguration ceremonies, Memorial Day observances, the Pledge of Allegiance, and reverence for founding documents — alongside its denominational religions.

Examples and criticism

Scholars have identified civil-religious elements in many societies: France's cult of the Republic and its martyrs, state Shinto in imperial Japan, and the veneration of Lenin's mausoleum in the Soviet Union. Critics debate whether civil religion genuinely integrates societies or instead sanctifies nationalism, militarism, and exclusion, and Bellah himself later expressed ambivalence about its American form.
