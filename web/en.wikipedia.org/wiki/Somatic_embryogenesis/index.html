Somatic embryogenesis

Somatic embryogenesis is a process by which a plant or embryo is derived from a single somatic cell or group of somatic cells. Somatic embryos are formed from plant cells that are not normally involved in the development of embryos — that is, ordinary plant tissue rather than the fertilised egg. Plants derived from somatic embryogenesis are genetically identical clones of the donor plant, and no fertilisation is involved.

Process

In tissue culture, explants are typically induced to form an undifferentiated mass of cells called callus, using plant growth regulators — classically a high ratio of auxin (such as 2,4-D) to cytokinin. Under suitable conditions, cells within the callus or in suspension culture become embryogenic and develop through stages resembling zygotic embryogenesis: globular, heart-shaped, and torpedo stages in dicots, followed by maturation and germination into plantlets. The phenomenon demonstrates the totipotency of plant cells and was established in pioneering work on carrot cultures by F. C. Steward and by Jakob Reinert in the late 1950s.

Direct and indirect embryogenesis

Somatic embryogenesis may be direct, with embryos arising straight from explant tissue, or indirect, passing through a callus phase. The indirect route risks somaclonal variation — genetic or epigenetic changes accumulated in culture — which can be a drawback for cloning but is sometimes exploited in breeding.

Applications

The technique is used for mass propagation of elite genotypes, notably in conifer forestry and in crops such as coffee, oil palm, and banana; for producing artificial (synthetic) seeds by encapsulating embryos; for virus elimination, genetic transformation, and germplasm preservation; and as a model system for studying embryo development.
