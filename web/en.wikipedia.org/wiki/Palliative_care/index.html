Palliative care (from the Latin palliare, "to cloak") is an interdisciplinary approach to specialised medical and nursing care for people with life-limiting illnesses. It focuses on providing relief from the symptoms, pain, physical stress and mental stress of a terminal diagnosis, with the goal of improving quality of life for both the patient and their family. The World Health Organization defines palliative care as an approach that improves quality of life "through the prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification and impeccable assessment and treatment of pain and other problems, physical, psychosocial and spiritual".

Palliative care may be provided at any stage of a serious illness and can be given alongside curative or life-prolonging treatment. It is distinct from, but overlaps with, hospice care, which is generally reserved for the final months of life.

History

The modern movement grew out of the hospice tradition. Dame Cicely Saunders founded St Christopher's Hospice in London in 1967, developing the concept of "total pain" — physical, emotional, social and spiritual. The term "palliative care" was coined in 1974 by Balfour Mount, who established a hospital-based palliative care service in Montreal after visiting St Christopher's. In 1987 palliative medicine was recognised as a medical specialty in the United Kingdom, and other countries followed.

Principles and practice

Care is delivered by interprofessional teams that may include physicians, nurses, social workers, chaplains, pharmacists and therapists. Core elements include:

  • Assessment and management of pain and other symptoms such as breathlessness, nausea, fatigue and delirium;
  • Communication about prognosis and goals of care, including advance care planning;
  • Psychological, social and spiritual support for patients and carers;
  • Coordination of care across settings, and support through bereavement.

Opioids such as morphine are mainstays of analgesia and relief of breathlessness, titrated to need; fears of addiction and hastened death historically limited their use, though evidence shows appropriate use does not shorten life.

Settings

Palliative care is provided in hospitals (consultation teams and dedicated units), hospices, care homes and patients' own homes, as well as through outpatient clinics and telehealth. Children's palliative care is a related subspecialty addressing the needs of infants, children and young people with life-limiting conditions and their families.

Evidence and outcomes

Randomised trials, including a landmark 2010 study of early palliative care in metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer published in The New England Journal of Medicine, have found improved quality of life and mood, less aggressive end-of-life treatment, and in some cases longer survival. Palliative care is also associated with reduced hospital costs.

Access and barriers

The WHO estimates that worldwide only about 14% of people who need palliative care receive it, with access concentrated in high-income countries. Barriers include restrictive opioid regulations, lack of trained staff, late referral, and misconceptions equating palliative care with "giving up".
