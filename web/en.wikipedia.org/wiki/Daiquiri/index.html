Daiquiri

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The daiquiri (/ˈdaɪkəri/, /ˈdækəri/; Spanish: daiquirí) is a cocktail whose main ingredients are white rum, citrus juice (typically lime), and sugar or another sweetener. It belongs to the family of sour cocktails and is one of the six basic drinks listed in David A. Embury's classic The Fine Art of Mixing Drinks. The drink is named after Daiquirí, a beach and iron mine near Santiago de Cuba.

History

The daiquiri is generally credited to Jennings Cox, an American mining engineer working near the Daiquirí mine in Cuba, who around 1898–1902 combined local rum with lime juice and sugar, reputedly when he ran out of gin for visiting guests. The recipe was documented in print in the following decade and was popularised in the United States after Rear Admiral Lucius W. Johnson brought it to Washington, D.C. in 1909.

In Havana, bartender Constantino Ribalaigua at the El Floridita bar refined the drink, creating the blended "frozen daiquiri" style and the Hemingway Special variation. The daiquiri was a favourite of writer Ernest Hemingway and of President John F. Kennedy.

Preparation

The International Bartenders Association recipe combines 60 ml white rum, 20 ml fresh lime juice and two bar spoons of superfine sugar, shaken with ice and strained into a chilled cocktail glass. Variations include the Hemingway Special (with grapefruit juice and maraschino liqueur, no added sugar), the frozen daiquiri blended with crushed ice, and fruit versions such as the strawberry daiquiri.
