Surfboard

A surfboard is a narrow plank used in surfing, ridden standing (or lying, in the case of bodyboards) on the face of a moving wave toward shore. Modern surfboards are lightweight composite structures built around a foam core; their shapes — length, width, rocker, rails, and fin arrangement — are tuned to the rider and the waves being surfed.

Typical types
Shortboard: ~1.8 m
Funboard: ~2.1–2.4 m
Longboard: 2.7 m +
Gun: for big waves

History

Surfing originated in Polynesia; early Hawaiian boards (alaia and olo) were solid wood, sometimes over 4 metres long. In the early 20th century, boards were still heavy hollow or solid timber. The 1950s brought balsa cores and then polyurethane foam with fibreglass, dramatically reducing weight. Australian Bob Simmons and Californian shapers pioneered planing hulls; in 1957 Hobie Alter and others commercialised foam boards. Later innovations included the shortboard revolution of the late 1960s, Simon Anderson's three-fin "thruster" (1981), and computer-aided shaping.

Construction

A typical board is shaped from a polyurethane or expanded polystyrene foam blank reinforced by a wooden stringer, then laminated with polyester or epoxy resin and fibreglass cloth. Alternatives include epoxy-over-EPS construction, hollow wood, and soft-top foam boards for learners. A traction pad and a leash (leg rope) attach near the tail; wax applied to the deck provides grip.

Design elements

Rocker is the board's curve from nose to tail; more rocker suits steep waves. Rails, the edges, range from soft and forgiving to hard and biting. The bottom may be flat, vee, or concave. Fins provide grip and drive: single fins, twin fins, thrusters (three), quads (four), and 2+1 setups are common, often in removable fin systems. Board categories include shortboards, fish, funboards/mid-lengths, longboards, guns for very large surf, and stand-up paddle boards.
