Chronological dating

Stratigraphy is a foundation of relative dating

Chronological dating, or simply dating, is the process of attributing to an object or event a date in the past, allowing such object or event to be located in a previously established chronology. This usually requires what is commonly known as a "dating method". Several dating methods exist, depending on different criteria and techniques, and some very well known examples of disciplines using such techniques are, for example, history, archaeology, geology, palaeontology, astronomy and even forensic science. Dating methods are most commonly classified as relative dating and absolute dating.

Relative dating

Relative dating methods determine the relative order of past events without necessarily determining their absolute age. In geology, the principle of superposition holds that in undisturbed strata, lower layers are older than those above them. Techniques include stratigraphy, typology and seriation of artefacts, cross-dating between sites, fluorine absorption dating, and the use of index fossils in biostratigraphy. In archaeology, relative sequences can be constructed from changes in artefact styles and from the layered deposits of excavated sites.

Absolute dating

Absolute dating methods attempt to establish a specific calendar age for an object or event. Radiometric dating techniques measure the decay of radioactive isotopes: radiocarbon dating applies to organic material up to about 50,000 years old, while potassium–argon and uranium–lead dating can date rocks millions or billions of years old. Other absolute methods include dendrochronology (tree-ring dating), thermoluminescence, optically stimulated luminescence, electron spin resonance, fission-track dating, and archaeomagnetic dating. Ice-core layer counting and varve chronology also provide calendar-scale ages for climate records.

Calibration and presentation

Many absolute dates require calibration: radiocarbon ages, for example, are converted to calendar years using calibration curves derived from dendrochronology and other records, because atmospheric radiocarbon levels have varied over time. Dates are conventionally reported with their uncertainties and with conventions such as BP (before present, where "present" is 1950) and BCE/CE or BC/AD.
