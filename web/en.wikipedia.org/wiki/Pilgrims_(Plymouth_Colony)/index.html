Pilgrims (Plymouth Colony)

The Pilgrims, also known as the Pilgrim Fathers, were the English settlers who travelled to America on the Mayflower and established the Plymouth Colony in 1620. The Pilgrims' leadership came from a congregation of religious separatists who had fled persecution in England first to Amsterdam and then to Leiden in the Dutch Republic.

Background

The core group originated around Scrooby in Nottinghamshire, where, from about 1606, a congregation of Separatists — Christians who believed the Church of England was too corrupt to be reformed from within and who therefore separated from it — met under the leadership of figures including William Brewster and the pastor John Robinson. Facing persecution and arrest, they emigrated to the Netherlands in 1607–08, settling in Leiden in 1609. There they enjoyed religious freedom but worried about poverty, the assimilation of their children into Dutch culture, and the approaching end of the Twelve Years' Truce with Spain.

The voyage

The congregation decided to emigrate to America, obtaining a patent from the Virginia Company and financing from the Merchant Adventurers, a group of London investors. Not all of the Leiden congregation sailed; those who went were joined by other settlers recruited in England, whom the Leiden group called "Strangers". After delays and the abandonment of the leaky Speedwell, 102 passengers set sail from Plymouth, England, aboard the Mayflower on 6 September 1620 (Old Style).

After a difficult 66-day crossing, they sighted Cape Cod and, finding themselves outside the bounds of their Virginia patent, drew up the Mayflower Compact on 11 November 1620, an agreement to form a civil body politic and enact just and equal laws — often cited as a foundational document of American self-government. Exploring the coast, they chose Plymouth, the site of the abandoned Wampanoag village of Patuxet, and came ashore in December 1620.

First years

The first winter was devastating: roughly half the settlers died of disease, cold and malnutrition. In the spring of 1621 the survivors were aided by the Wampanoag people under the sachem Massasoit, and were taught to plant corn by Tisquantum ("Squanto"), a Patuxet man who had learned English. A treaty of mutual protection was concluded with Massasoit, and in the autumn of 1621 the colonists celebrated a harvest feast with the Wampanoag — the event remembered as the First Thanksgiving. William Bradford, elected governor after John Carver's death, chronicled the colony in Of Plymouth Plantation.

Legacy

The name "Pilgrims" derives from Bradford's description of the settlers as "pilgrims" (alluding to Hebrews 11:13) and became popular in the 19th century, when Plymouth Rock, Forefathers' Day and the Thanksgiving holiday elevated the Pilgrims into a central American founding myth, celebrated and critically reassessed ever since.
