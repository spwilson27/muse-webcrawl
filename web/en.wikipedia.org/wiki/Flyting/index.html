Flyting is a ritual, poetic exchange of insults between two rivals, practiced in Scotland, Scandinavia, and Anglo-Saxon England from roughly the 5th to the 16th century. The name derives from the Old English flītan, "to quarrel". Opponents traded extravagant, often obscene accusations—of cowardice, sexual misconduct, sorcery, and low birth—combining genuine aggression with display of verbal artistry. The winner was judged by the audience.

Germanic and Norse examples

Flyting appears in Old English heroic poetry, most famously when Unferth challenges Beowulf in Beowulf and Beowulf answers with a counter-account of his exploits. Norse literature preserves flytings between gods and heroes: in the Eddic poem Lokasenna, Loki insults each of the assembled gods in turn, and in Hárbarðsljóð Odin, disguised as a ferryman, taunts Thor. In Old Norse the exchange of insults is also called senna or mannjafnaðr ("man-comparison").

Scottish flyting

The tradition flourished at the Scottish court in the late 15th and early 16th centuries among the makars. The most celebrated example is The Flyting of Dunbar and Kennedie (c. 1500), in which William Dunbar and Walter Kennedy exchanged hundreds of lines of virtuosic abuse. James IV and James V enjoyed such contests, and Sir David Lyndsay also participated in flyting.

Legacy

Scholars compare flyting to later ritual insult traditions, including the African-American practice of the dozens and modern rap battles and diss tracks, in which ritualized boasting and insult are judged by audiences as displays of skill.
