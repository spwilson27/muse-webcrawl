Sergei Eisenstein

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Sergei Eisenstein in the 1930s at the editing table, strips of film in hand, in a portrait of the montage theorist at work]

Sergei Mikhailovich Eisenstein (22 January 1898 – 11 February 1948) was a Soviet film director and theorist, the father of montage cinema. His Battleship Potemkin (1925) — commissioned for the 20th anniversary of the 1905 revolution — is routinely ranked among the greatest films ever made, and his writings on montage (the collision of shots to produce ideas) founded film theory as a discipline.

Eisenstein's films are Strike (1925), Battleship Potemkin (1925), October: Ten Days That Shook the World (1928), the unfinished Que Viva México! (1931–32), Alexander Nevsky (1938, with Prokofiev's score) and Ivan the Terrible Parts I (1944) and II (1946, banned until 1958).

Montage and the Odessa steps

Trained as an engineer and formed in Meyerhold's theatre, Eisenstein theorised five kinds of montage, from metric cutting to the 'intellectual' juxtaposition of images to force conclusions in the viewer. The Odessa steps sequence in Potemkin — the Tsarist massacre of civilians that never happened as shown, intercutting boots, a pram, a woman's shattered face — became cinema's most imitated set piece, quoted by everyone from De Palma to the makers of cartoons.

Under Stalin

A trip to Hollywood (1930, a failed Paramount contract, the Upton Sinclair-financed Mexican film abandoned in recrimination) left him suspect at home. Nevsky's anti-German parable won him the Stalin Prize; Ivan Part I was praised, Part II denounced for making its tsar a Hamlet-like neurotic — Eisenstein, summoned to the Kremlin by night, promised corrections he did not live to make. He died of a heart attack at 50.
