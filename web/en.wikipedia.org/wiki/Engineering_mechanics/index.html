Engineering mechanics

Engineering mechanics is the application of mechanics to solve problems involving common engineering elements. The goal of this engineering field is to analyze and predict the accuracy and performance of physical systems under given forces and constraints. It forms a core part of the curriculum in mechanical, civil, aerospace and structural engineering education.

Branches

Engineering mechanics is traditionally divided into two broad areas:

  • Statics — the study of bodies at rest or moving with constant velocity, in which the sum of forces and the sum of moments acting on the body are zero. Static analysis determines reactions at supports, forces in trusses and frames, friction forces, and properties of cross-sections such as centroids and moments of inertia.
  • Dynamics — the study of bodies in motion under unbalanced forces. Dynamics is subdivided into kinematics, which describes motion (displacement, velocity, acceleration) without reference to its causes, and kinetics, which relates motion to the forces and masses involved, including work–energy and impulse–momentum methods.

A third major area, mechanics of materials (or strength of materials), applies the equilibrium results of statics to deformable bodies, computing stresses, strains and deflections in beams, columns, shafts and pressure vessels. Vibrations, rigid-body dynamics in three dimensions, and an introduction to continuum mechanics are commonly treated as advanced extensions.

Fundamental principles

The subject rests on Newton's laws of motion together with a small set of derived principles: the parallelogram law for vector addition of forces, the principle of transmissibility, Newton's law of gravitation, and the methods of free-body diagrams. Bodies are idealized as particles when their dimensions are irrelevant to the motion, or as rigid bodies when rotation and the distribution of forces matter.

Applications

Engineering mechanics underlies the design of structures, machines, vehicles and mechanisms. Typical calculations include the load capacity of a truss bridge, the torque required to drive a machine shaft, the trajectory and impact conditions of moving components, and the stability of bodies subject to friction and distributed loads.
