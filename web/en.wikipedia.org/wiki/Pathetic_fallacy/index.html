Pathetic fallacy

The pathetic fallacy is a literary device in which human emotions and feelings are attributed to nature, inanimate objects, or animals — for example, describing weather as angry or a landscape as melancholy to reflect a character's mood.

Origin of the term

The term was coined by the critic John Ruskin in the third volume of Modern Painters (1856). Ruskin used it critically, arguing that the fallacy — arising when strong feeling distorts perception — separated lesser poets, who indulge it, from the greatest, who see truly despite feeling. In modern usage the term is often neutral, denoting a common figurative technique related to personification and anthropomorphism.

Examples

Storm scenes mirroring turmoil in Shakespeare's King Lear, the gloomy settings of Gothic fiction, and weather reflecting mood in the Brontës' novels are standard examples.
