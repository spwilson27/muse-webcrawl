Proof of work

Proof of work (PoW) is a consensus mechanism and form of cryptographic proof in which one party (the prover) demonstrates to verifiers that a specified amount of computational effort has been expended. Verifying the proof must be cheap compared with producing it. Proof of work is best known as the consensus algorithm of Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies, where "miners" compete to find a hash below a target value — the process that orders transactions, secures the blockchain, and issues new coins.

History

The idea originated in anti-spam research: Cynthia Dwork and Moni Naor proposed "pricing via processing" in 1992, requiring email senders to perform a modest computation per message. Adam Back's Hashcash (1997) implemented hash-based partial-hash-inversion puzzles and coined the term "proof-of-work". Hal Finney's reusable proof of work (2004) anticipated cryptocurrency tokens, and Satoshi Nakamoto combined hashcash-style PoW with a peer-to-peer ledger and difficulty adjustment in Bitcoin (2009).

How it works in blockchains

Miners assemble candidate blocks and repeatedly vary a nonce (and other fields) until the block header's double-SHA-256 hash (in Bitcoin) falls below the current target. The protocol automatically retargets the difficulty — in Bitcoin every 2,016 blocks — to hold the average block interval near ten minutes. The first miner to find a valid block broadcasts it; others verify it instantly and build on the longest (most cumulative work) chain. Rewards of new coins plus transaction fees compensate miners for the real-world cost of computation and electricity, which is what makes rewriting history prohibitively expensive.

Criticism and alternatives

PoW systems are criticised for their very large energy consumption and electronic waste, and for mining centralisation in pools and ASIC hardware. These concerns motivated alternative consensus mechanisms, notably proof of stake, to which Ethereum migrated in 2022 ("the Merge"). Supporters argue that PoW's energy use buys uniquely objective, thermodynamically anchored security for a permissionless network.
