Ferroelectricity

Ferroelectricity is a characteristic of certain materials that have a spontaneous electric polarization that can be reversed by the application of an external electric field. All ferroelectrics are also piezoelectric and pyroelectric, with the additional property that their natural electrical polarization is reversible. The term is used in analogy to ferromagnetism, in which a material exhibits a permanent magnetic moment; ferroelectricity was discovered after ferromagnetism, when the prefix ferro-, meaning iron, was already associated with the concept of a permanent, switchable moment rather than with iron itself.

Discovery

Ferroelectricity was discovered in 1920 by Joseph Valasek in Rochelle salt (sodium potassium tartrate tetrahydrate), the first material in which a spontaneous, reversible polarization was observed. Valasek measured a hysteresis loop of polarization against field closely analogous to the magnetic hysteresis of iron. For many years Rochelle salt appeared to be a curiosity, but in the 1940s the discovery of strong ferroelectricity in the simple oxide barium titanate (BaTiO3) — a perovskite — showed the effect could occur in robust, easily produced ceramics and triggered intense research and commercial development.

Properties

In a ferroelectric material, the centers of positive and negative charge within the unit cell do not coincide, producing a permanent electric dipole. Below a critical temperature, the Curie temperature (or Curie point), the dipoles align spontaneously, giving the crystal a net macroscopic polarization. Above the Curie temperature the material becomes paraelectric, losing its spontaneous polarization. When an external electric field is applied, the polarization follows a hysteresis loop: it saturates at high fields, retains a remanent polarization at zero field, and switches sign only when the field exceeds a coercive field in the opposite direction. Ferroelectrics also exhibit very large dielectric constants (relative permittivities of hundreds to tens of thousands) near the transition.

Mechanism

In displacive ferroelectrics such as barium titanate, the transition is driven by the displacement of cations (e.g. Ti4+) relative to the oxygen octahedra in the perovskite lattice, coupled with a softening of a lattice vibrational mode. In order–disorder ferroelectrics, such as sodium nitrite or potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KDP), the dipoles exist above the transition but are randomly oriented, and order cooperatively below it. Domains of uniform polarization form within the crystal, separated by domain walls whose motion under an applied field produces the observed switching.

Materials

  • Barium titanate (BaTiO3) and lead zirconate titanate (PZT) — the most widely used ferroelectric ceramics.
  • Rochelle salt, triglycine sulfate (TGS), and potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KDP) — early-studied single crystals.
  • Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) — a ferroelectric polymer.
  • Lithium niobate and potassium niobate — used in nonlinear optics and surface-acoustic-wave devices.
  • Hafnia (HfO2)-based thin films — ferroelectricity discovered in doped hafnium oxide in 2011, important because it is compatible with CMOS processing.

Applications

Ferroelectric capacitors exploit the high permittivity of these materials. Non-volatile ferroelectric random-access memory (FeRAM) stores data in the two remanent polarization states. Piezoelectric applications include ultrasound transducers, sonar, actuators, and inkjet printer heads, while pyroelectric detectors built from ferroelectrics are used in passive infrared motion sensors and thermal imaging. Ferroelectric tunnel junctions and negative-capacitance field-effect transistors are active research topics for low-power and neuromorphic computing.
