The Texas annexation was the 1845 incorporation of the Republic of Texas into the United States of America as the State of Texas. The annexation, accomplished by a joint resolution of the U.S. Congress rather than a treaty, ended the Republic's nine years of independence and set the stage for the Mexican–American War.

Background

Texas declared independence from Mexico in 1836 and won it on the battlefield at the Battle of San Jacinto. The Republic of Texas sought annexation by the United States almost immediately, but the question of slavery and fears of war with Mexico delayed action in Washington. President John Tyler revived the issue in 1844; an annexation treaty was rejected by the Senate, and the issue became central to the presidential election of 1844, won by the expansionist Democrat James K. Polk.

Joint resolution

Failing to secure a treaty, supporters turned to a joint resolution, which required only simple majorities in both houses. The resolution passed the House in January 1845 and the Senate in February. Mexico had warned that annexation would be treated as an act of war and broke diplomatic relations. Texans approved annexation in a convention and referendum in 1845, and Texas was formally admitted to the Union on December 29, 1845.

Consequences

Disputes over the Texas–Mexico boundary — the United States claimed the Rio Grande, Mexico the Nueces River — led directly to the Mexican–American War (1846–1848). The Compromise of 1850 later settled Texas's western boundary claims in exchange for federal assumption of the Republic's debts. Annexation also deepened the sectional conflict over the expansion of slavery that culminated in the American Civil War.
