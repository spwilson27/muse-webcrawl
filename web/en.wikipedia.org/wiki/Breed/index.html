Breed

A breed is a specific group of breedable domestic animals having homogeneous appearance (phenotype), homogeneous behaviour, and/or other characteristics that distinguish it from other organisms of the same species. In literature, there exist several slightly deviating definitions — but a breed is generally developed and maintained through selective breeding by humans, and breeders' associations typically define a breed standard and keep a herdbook or studbook recording pedigrees.

Breeding and breed formation

Breeds arise when breeders isolate a population and select for desired traits — milk yield, speed, coat, temperament — over generations. Consistent selection, together with some degree of inbreeding and linebreeding, fixes the characteristic phenotype. The concept is most developed for livestock (cattle, sheep, pigs, goats, horses), dogs, and cats, but applies to any domesticated species, including poultry, rabbits, and even laboratory animals. Well-known examples include Holstein cattle, Merino sheep, Thoroughbred horses, and Labrador Retriever dogs.

Breed versus related terms

A breed is not a formal taxonomic rank. Related concepts include the cultivar in plants, the strain in laboratory animals and microorganisms, and the landrace — a locally adapted population shaped by environment and traditional husbandry rather than by a formal standard. Crossbreeding between breeds produces crossbreds; deliberate two-breed or multi-breed crosses are common in commercial meat and egg production to exploit hybrid vigour (heterosis).

Conservation

Many traditional breeds have declined as commercial agriculture concentrates on a few high-yielding types. Organisations such as the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations and national rare-breeds trusts monitor breed populations and promote conservation of animal genetic resources, regarding breed diversity as insurance against disease, climate change, and shifting markets.
