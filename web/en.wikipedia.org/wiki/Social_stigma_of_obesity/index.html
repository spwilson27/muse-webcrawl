Social stigma of obesity

Weight stigma is among the most pervasive forms of social stigma in modern societies.

The social stigma of obesity, commonly called weight stigma or weight bias, refers to negative attitudes, stereotypes, and discriminatory treatment directed at people because of their body weight, particularly people classified as overweight or obese. Stereotypes commonly attached to higher body weight include laziness, lack of willpower, low intelligence, and poor hygiene. Research documents weight stigma in employment, education, healthcare, interpersonal relationships, and media representation, and it is reported by affected individuals as one of the most common forms of discrimination, comparable in prevalence to racial discrimination in some studies.

Manifestations

In employment, studies using audit and experimental methods find that heavier applicants, especially women, are less likely to be hired and are offered lower wages. In healthcare, weight bias among providers is associated with shorter consultations, less health education, and attributing unrelated symptoms to weight ("diagnostic overshadowing"); patients who experience stigma may avoid or delay care. Children with obesity report high rates of teasing and bullying, including by peers, teachers, and family members. Media frequently depict larger people in stigmatizing ways — eating junk food, sedentary, or as comic relief — and images accompanying news stories about obesity often show headless torsos, a practice criticized by health advocates.

Health consequences

Weight stigma is itself associated with adverse health outcomes independent of body mass index: chronic stress, elevated cortisol, depression, anxiety, disordered eating, avoidance of physical activity in public, and increased mortality risk in longitudinal studies. These findings have led researchers and organizations such as the World Obesity Federation to argue that stigma is a barrier to effective obesity treatment and public policy, and that blaming approaches can be counterproductive.

Legal and social responses

Weight is rarely a protected characteristic in anti-discrimination law. In the United States, Michigan's Elliott-Larsen Civil Rights Act is a notable exception, and some cities prohibit weight discrimination; the European Union debated but has not adopted general protections, though some court rulings address severe obesity as a disability in employment contexts. Movements such as fat acceptance and body positivity, dating to the late 1960s, challenge weight stigma culturally and politically.
