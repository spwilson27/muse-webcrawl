Callimachus (Ancient Greek: Καλλίμαχος, romanized: Kallimachos; c. 310 – c. 240 BC) was an ancient Greek poet, scholar and librarian who was a native of Cyrene and a descendant of the Battiadae, the royal house of that city. He was noted for his short, highly polished poems and for the Pinakes, a bibliographic survey of the contents of the Library of Alexandria, which made him one of the most influential scholars of the Hellenistic age.

One of the most productive poets of the Hellenistic Age, Callimachus was the most influential figure of the age in poetry, and his work and aesthetic principles — favouring brevity, learning, refinement and allusiveness over the grand epic manner — profoundly influenced Roman poets such as Catullus, Propertius and Ovid, and through them later European literature.

Life

Little is known to be certain about Callimachus' life. Born in Cyrene in modern Libya, he claimed descent from Battus, the legendary founder of the city. He moved to Alexandria in Egypt, where he may have worked as a schoolteacher before being introduced to the court of Ptolemy II Philadelphus. He became associated with the Great Library of Alexandria and is sometimes said to have been its head librarian, though modern scholars generally assign that office to others, such as Apollonius of Rhodes, with whom Callimachus is said (on slender evidence) to have quarrelled.

For the Library, Callimachus compiled the Pinakes ("Tables of persons eminent in every branch of learning together with a list of their writings"), in 120 books — the first systematic catalogue of Greek literature, organising authors by genre and providing biographical and bibliographical details. The Pinakes became the foundation of later classical scholarship and bibliography.

Works

Callimachus is said to have written some 800 works, almost all of which are lost; his surviving poetry comes through medieval manuscripts, papyrus fragments (especially from Oxyrhynchus) and quotations by later authors.

  • Aetia ("Origins" or "Causes") — four books of elegiac poems explaining the origins of customs, festivals, names and cities. It opened with a famous prologue in which the poet defends his slighter, refined style against his critics (the "Telchines"), and included the celebrated stories of Acontius and Cydippe and the Lock of Berenice, the latter known chiefly through Catullus' Latin adaptation (poem 66).
  • Hecale — an epyllion (miniature epic) about Theseus' capture of the Marathonian bull and his stay with the poor old woman Hecale, whose humble hospitality the poem celebrates.
  • Hymns — six hymns to gods (Zeus, Apollo, Artemis, Delos, Athena and Demeter), written in the Homeric manner but full of learned and playful touches.
  • Epigrams — some 60 short poems preserved in the Greek Anthology, including the famous epitaph for his friend Heraclitus of Halicarnassus ("They told me, Heraclitus, they told me you were dead"), translated by William Johnson Cory.
  • Iambi and other works in varied metres, plus prose treatises on birds, rivers, winds and the names of months and tribes.

Style and influence

Callimachus championed the short, exquisitely crafted poem over the long, traditional epic. His watchword, attributed to him by later tradition, was "a big book is a big evil" (μέγα βιβλίον μέγα κακόν), and his prologue to the Aetia urges the poet to keep the Muse slender, to walk untrodden paths and to prefer the cicada's clear song to the braying of asses. His poetry is dense with rare words, local myths, etymologies and scholarship — the hallmark of Alexandrian learned poetry.

Roman poets of the first century BC, the poetae novi or neoterics such as Catullus and Cinna, took Callimachus as their model, styling themselves Latin Callimacheans; Propertius called himself the "Roman Callimachus." Through Ovid, Virgil and later writers, Callimachean aesthetics shaped the whole subsequent tradition of Western poetry.
