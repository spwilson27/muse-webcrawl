Pentti Eelis Eskola (8 January 1883 – 6 December 1964) was a Finnish geologist who profoundly influenced metamorphic petrology by creating the concept of metamorphic facies. He was professor of geology at the University of Helsinki from 1924 to 1953 and is regarded as one of Finland's most internationally influential scientists.

Metamorphic facies

Building on the work of the Swiss geologist Amanz Gressly and the depth-zone scheme of earlier petrologists, Eskola proposed in his doctoral work and a key 1915 paper, "On the relations between the chemical and mineralogical composition in the metamorphic rocks of the Orijärvi region," that metamorphic rocks formed under similar pressure–temperature conditions develop characteristic mineral assemblages regardless of their original bulk composition. He developed this into the facies classification published fully in 1920 and refined in his contribution to Barth, Correns and Eskola's Die Entstehung der Gesteine (1939), naming facies such as the sanidinite, pyroxene-hornfels, amphibolite, greenschist, blueschist, and eclogite facies. The facies concept eventually gave rise to the concept of paired metamorphic belts and remains fundamental to how metamorphic terranes are mapped and interpreted in terms of plate-tectonic setting.

Career

Eskola studied chemistry before turning to geology, and worked on the Precambrian rocks of Finland, including the origin of granites and migmatites, contributing to debates over "granitisation." He also wrote on the geology of Finland for a broad audience and served in scientific administration. His international honours included the Wollaston Medal of the Geological Society of London (1943) and the Penrose Medal of the Geological Society of America (1951) — the first recipient from outside the society's usual circles to receive both of geology's premier awards — and foreign membership of the Royal Society.
