Tanakh

The Tanakh (Hebrew: תָּנָ״ךְ, Tānāḵ), also known in Hebrew as the Mikra (מקרא, "Reading" or "that which is read"), is the canonical collection of Hebrew scriptures, comprising the Bible's foundational texts in Judaism. It is an acronym of its three divisions: the Torah ("Teaching"), the Nevi'im ("Prophets"), and the Ketuvim ("Writings"). The Tanakh's content substantially corresponds to the Protestant Old Testament, though the ordering of books differs.

Contents

Torah

The Torah, or Five Books of Moses, comprises Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy — narrating creation, the patriarchs, the Exodus from Egypt, and the giving of the law at Sinai.

Nevi'im

The Prophets are divided into the Former Prophets (Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings) and the Latter Prophets (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the Twelve Minor Prophets).

Ketuvim

The Writings include poetry (Psalms, Proverbs, Job), the Five Scrolls (Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther), and wisdom, historical, and apocalyptic books (Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles).

Textual history

The authoritative Hebrew text is the Masoretic Text, standardized by the Masoretes between the 7th and 10th centuries CE; the oldest complete surviving codex is the Leningrad Codex (1008/1009 CE). The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve much older copies of nearly all books.
