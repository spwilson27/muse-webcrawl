Hyphenation (typesetting)

Hyphenation is the process of breaking words between lines of text, marking the break with a hyphen. It is a standard component of justified typesetting, in which text is aligned to both the left and right margins: dividing long words at syllable boundaries allows the spacing between words to be kept more even, avoiding large gaps ("rivers") in narrow columns.

In modern desktop publishing and word processing software, hyphenation is performed automatically by algorithms that consult dictionaries and pattern tables. A classic and widely used approach is the algorithm developed by Franklin Mark Liang for the TeX typesetting system in the early 1980s, which uses hyphenation patterns derived from a dictionary rather than the dictionary itself, achieving a good balance of speed, compactness, and accuracy.

Rules and conventions

Style guides generally recommend that a word be broken only at a syllable boundary, that at least two or three letters remain on each side of the break, and that proper names and the last word of a paragraph not be hyphenated. Consecutive hyphenated lines are usually limited to two or three to preserve readability. In American English, hyphenation points typically follow pronunciation (as recorded in dictionaries such as Merriam-Webster), while British practice, following the Oxford style, tends to favour breaks based on word structure and etymology.

Soft and hard hyphens

A soft hyphen (discretionary hyphen) is a character inserted into a word to indicate where it may be broken if it falls at the end of a line; it is rendered only when a break actually occurs. In Unicode it is U+00AD. A hard hyphen, by contrast, is a permanent part of a compound word, as in "well-known", and is always printed.
