Refeeding syndrome is a potentially fatal metabolic disturbance that occurs when nutrition is restarted in a severely malnourished person. During starvation the body conserves phosphate, potassium and magnesium; when carbohydrate feeding resumes, insulin drives these electrolytes into cells, and blood levels — phosphate above all — can crash.

Features and management

The characteristic finding is hypophosphataemia, often with low potassium and magnesium and thiamine deficiency. Consequences include muscle weakness, respiratory failure, cardiac arrhythmia and heart failure, confusion and seizures. Prevention centres on identifying at-risk patients (including those with anorexia nervosa, chronic alcoholism, or prolonged fasting), starting feeding slowly ("start low, go slow"), replacing electrolytes aggressively, and giving thiamine and multivitamins before and during refeeding — principles set out in guidelines such as those of NICE.

History

The syndrome was recognised after the Second World War, when starved prisoners and famine victims died unexpectedly after apparently welcome refeeding; it remains a hazard in hospitals and famine relief whenever depleted patients are fed too quickly.
