Guanay cormorant

The Guanay cormorant or Guanay shag (Leucocarbo bougainvilliorum) is a member of the cormorant family found on the Pacific coast of South America, along the shores of Peru and northern Chile. It is a black-and-white seabird with a white breast and throat, a red patch of bare skin around the eye, and a crest on the head. Its name derives from "guano", for it is the most important producer of the fertiliser among the seabirds of the Humboldt Current.

Distribution and behaviour

The Guanay cormorant breeds in enormous colonies on offshore islands and rocky headlands of the Peruvian and Chilean coast, nesting on the accumulated guano deposits themselves. It feeds in the cold, nutrient-rich waters of the Humboldt Current, principally on anchoveta and other small schooling fish, diving from the surface and pursuing prey underwater. Flocks numbering in the hundreds of thousands travel between roosting islands and feeding grounds.

Guano production

Historically the species numbered in the tens of millions and was the principal contributor to the vast guano deposits of Peru's coastal islands, which were mined intensively in the nineteenth century and remained a major export. The Peruvian government eventually protected the birds and regulated harvesting, and the islands became one of the earliest examples of industrial wildlife management.

Population and threats

Populations fluctuate dramatically with El Niño events, which warm the coastal waters and disperse the anchoveta schools, causing mass starvation and breeding failure. Competition with industrial anchoveta fisheries and episodes of over-exploitation of guano reduced numbers sharply in the twentieth century. The species is classified as Near Threatened by the IUCN, with a population estimated in the low millions.
