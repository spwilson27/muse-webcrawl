Colour vision, a feature of visual perception, is the ability of an organism to distinguish objects based on the wavelengths (or frequencies) of the light they reflect, emit or transmit. Colour is not a property of objects themselves but a percept constructed by the visual system from the spectral composition of light reaching the eye.

Mechanism

Humans are trichromats: the retina contains three classes of cone photoreceptors, most densely packed in the fovea centralis, sensitive to short (blue), medium (green) and long (red) wavelengths. The brain compares their outputs — in opponent channels such as red–green and blue–yellow, as proposed by Ewald Hering — to yield perceived colour. Thomas Young and Hermann von Helmholtz developed the trichromatic theory in the nineteenth century.

Variation and deficiency

Most mammals are dichromats; many birds, fish and insects are tetrachromats and can see ultraviolet. In humans, inherited defects in cone pigments cause colour vision deficiency, most commonly red–green confusion affecting about 8% of men. Colour constancy mechanisms let perception remain stable despite changes in illumination, while phenomena such as metamerism — different spectra matching in colour — underpin colour reproduction in printing and displays.
