Direct oral anticoagulant

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), also called non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs), are a class of anticoagulant medications taken by mouth that act directly on a single target in the coagulation cascade. Unlike warfarin and other vitamin K antagonists, they have predictable pharmacokinetics, are given in fixed doses, and do not require routine blood monitoring of their anticoagulant effect.

Drugs in the class

  • Direct thrombin (factor IIa) inhibitor: dabigatran.
  • Direct factor Xa inhibitors: rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban, and betrixaban (the last withdrawn from the market in the United States).

The first DOAC, dabigatran, was approved in 2010, followed by rivaroxaban (2011), apixaban (2012) and edoxaban (2015), after large randomised trials (RE-LY, ROCKET-AF, ARISTOTLE and ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48) showed them to be at least as effective as warfarin for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation, with lower rates of intracranial haemorrhage.

Uses

DOACs are used for the prevention of stroke and systemic embolism in non-valvular atrial fibrillation, for the treatment and secondary prevention of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, and for thromboprophylaxis after hip and knee replacement surgery. They are not recommended in patients with mechanical heart valves or moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis, in whom warfarin remains standard.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages over warfarin include rapid onset and offset, fewer food and drug interactions, fixed dosing, and no routine INR monitoring. Disadvantages include dependence on renal function for clearance (especially dabigatran), lack of a simple routine test of effect, higher drug cost, and gastrointestinal bleeding risks with some agents. Specific reversal agents now exist: idarucizumab for dabigatran and andexanet alfa for factor Xa inhibitors, alongside non-specific measures such as prothrombin complex concentrate.
