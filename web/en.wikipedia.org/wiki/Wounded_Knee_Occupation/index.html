The Wounded Knee Occupation, also known as the Second Wounded Knee, was a 71-day standoff from 27 February to 8 May 1973 at the town of Wounded Knee on the Pine Ridge Indian Reservation in South Dakota, United States. It began when about 200 Oglala Lakota and followers of the American Indian Movement (AIM), including leaders Russell Means and Dennis Banks, seized the town to protest the alleged corruption of tribal chairman Richard Wilson, the failure of the US government to fulfil treaty obligations, and a pattern of violence against Native people.

Background

Wounded Knee was symbolically charged as the site of the 1890 massacre of Lakota by the US Army. On the reservation, opponents of Wilson accused his administration of nepotism and of using a private militia, the Guardians of the Oglala Nation ("GOONs"), to suppress dissent. A failed impeachment attempt against Wilson in February 1973 heightened tensions, and traditional chiefs and AIM supporters chose Wounded Knee for a stand.

Standoff

Federal marshals, FBI agents and military advisers surrounded the town; the occupiers, calling their position the Independent Oglala Nation, exchanged gunfire with federal forces on multiple occasions. Two occupiers were killed — Cherokee activist Frank Clearwater and Oglala Lakota Buddy Lamont — and a US marshal was seriously wounded. The occupation drew national media attention and support protests, including Marlon Brando's protest at the 1973 Academy Awards ceremony.

Aftermath

The standoff ended with an agreement providing for federal review of Wilson's administration and of the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie. Prosecutions of Banks and Means were dismissed in 1974 because of government misconduct. The occupation is remembered as a landmark of 20th-century Native American activism, though the reservation afterward experienced continuing political violence.
