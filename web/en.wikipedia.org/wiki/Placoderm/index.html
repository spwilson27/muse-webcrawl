Placoderm

Placoderms (class Placodermi, from Greek for "plate-skinned") were a diverse group of armoured prehistoric fish that lived from the Silurian to the end of the Devonian period (roughly 439–359 million years ago). They were among the first jawed vertebrates (gnathostomes) and the dominant marine predators of their age, including giants such as Dunkleosteus, which reached perhaps 4–6 metres in length.

Anatomy

Placoderms were encased in bony armour over the head and thorax, the two shields often joined by a ball-and-socket joint in the neck, while the tail remained scaly and flexible. They had true jaws but generally lacked true teeth; instead, many had sharp, self-sharpening bony plates that functioned as cutting beaks — in Dunkleosteus these produced one of the strongest bites known in any fish. Most were bottom-dwellers, but arthrodires like Dunkleosteus were active open-water predators.

Reproduction

Fossils of the antiarch Materpiscis and ptyctodonts from the Gogo Formation of Australia preserve embryos inside the mother, showing that some placoderms gave birth to live young by internal fertilisation — the earliest evidence of vertebrate viviparity and copulation, assisted in males by bony claspers.

Extinction and significance

Placoderms disappeared completely in the Late Devonian mass extinctions (the Kellwasser and Hangenberg events), making way for sharks and bony fish. Their fossils are central to understanding the origin of jaws, paired appendages and internal fertilisation in vertebrates; some recent analyses place placoderms as a paraphyletic grade on the stem of jawed vertebrates.
