Hebrew poetry

Hebrew poetry spans more than three thousand years, from the verse of the Hebrew Bible to medieval liturgical and secular poetry and the modern Hebrew poetry of the nineteenth- and twentieth-century revival.

Biblical poetry

Much of the Hebrew Bible is poetry, notably the Psalms, Job, Proverbs, the Song of Songs and large parts of the prophetic books. Its defining device, identified by Robert Lowth in the eighteenth century, is parallelism — the pairing of lines that restate, contrast or extend a thought — together with imagery, acrostics and refrains rather than regular metre or rhyme.

Medieval poetry

In the early medieval period, liturgical poets (paytanim) such as Eleazar ben Killir composed elaborate piyyutim for synagogue use. In Muslim and Christian Spain, poets including Solomon ibn Gabirol, Moses ibn Ezra and Judah Halevi adopted Arabic metres and rhyme to write both sacred and secular verse on love, wine, nature and philosophy.

Modern poetry

The revival of Hebrew as a spoken language produced a flourishing modern poetry, led by Hayim Nahman Bialik and Saul Tchernichovsky and later by figures such as Yehuda Amichai, Dahlia Ravikovitch and Nathan Alterman.
