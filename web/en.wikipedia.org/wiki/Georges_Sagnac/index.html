Georges Sagnac

Georges Sagnac (14 October 1869 – 26 February 1928) was a French physicist who lent his name to the Sagnac effect, a phenomenon encountered in interferometry that is the basis of ring laser gyroscopes and fibre optic gyroscopes. He was a contemporary and friend of Marie Curie, Pierre Curie, and Paul Langevin.

Life and work

Sagnac studied at the École Normale Supérieure and the Sorbonne in Paris. Early in his career he was among the first to study X-rays; he demonstrated that X-rays striking a metal produce secondary radiation, and in 1897–98 he investigated the retardation of light in moving media. He held posts at Lille and later at the Sorbonne.

The Sagnac effect

In 1913 Sagnac built an interferometer in which two beams of light travelled in opposite directions around a closed loop on a rotating turntable. He observed a shift in the interference fringes proportional to the rate of rotation, demonstrating that the time taken for light to complete the circuit depends on the direction of travel relative to the rotation. Sagnac interpreted the result as evidence for the luminiferous aether; in modern physics it is fully consistent with special relativity. The effect is used today in ring laser gyroscopes and fibre-optic gyroscopes for navigation, and underpins experiments testing relativistic effects, such as the Michelson–Gale–Pearson experiment of 1925.
