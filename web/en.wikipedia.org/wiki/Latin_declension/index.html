Latin declension

Endings of the five declensions

Latin declension is the set of patterns according to which Latin words are declined — that is, inflected in form — to reflect grammatical case, number, and gender. Latin nouns are divided into five declensions, identified by the ending of the genitive singular: first (-ae), second (-ī), third (-is), fourth (-ūs), and fifth (-eī). Adjectives and pronouns have their own declensional patterns, largely following those of nouns.

Cases

Latin has six main cases: nominative (subject), genitive (possession), dative (indirect object), accusative (direct object and motion toward), ablative (separation, means, or object of certain prepositions), and vocative (direct address). A seventh case, the locative, survives only in a restricted set of words, mainly names of towns and a few common nouns such as domus ("house") and rūs ("countryside").

Gender and agreement

Every Latin noun has one of three genders — masculine, feminine, or neuter — which determines the forms of adjectives and pronouns referring to it. Second-declension neuter nouns differ from masculines in having identical nominative and accusative forms, a pattern shared by neuter nouns of all declensions.
