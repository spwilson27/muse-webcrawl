Aegina (Greek: Αίγινα) is one of the Saronic Islands of Greece in the Saronic Gulf, some 27 kilometres (17 mi) from Athens. In classical antiquity it was a major maritime and commercial power and a bitter rival of Athens; today it is a popular excursion destination from the capital, famous for its Temple of Aphaea and for its prized pistachio nuts.

History

Aegina was inhabited from the Neolithic period and took part in the early Aegean trading networks. In the 7th and 6th centuries BC it became a leading naval and commercial state: its "turtle" coins were among the first minted in Europe and circulated widely as a standard currency. The island's rivalry with Athens culminated in the Peloponnesian War, when Athens besieged Aegina, expelled its population in 431 BC and installed Athenian settlers; the original inhabitants were restored by Sparta after Athens' defeat.

Temple of Aphaea

The Doric Temple of Aphaea, built around 500 BC on a wooded hill in the northeast of the island, is one of the best-preserved Greek temples. Together with the Parthenon in Athens and the Temple of Poseidon at Sounion it is said to form a "holy triangle" of temples. Its pedimental sculptures are now in the Glyptothek in Munich.

Modern Aegina

Aegina town served briefly as the first capital of the modern Greek state under Ioannis Kapodistrias in 1828–1829. The island's economy rests on tourism, sponge fishing historically, and agriculture — above all the PDO-protected Aegina pistachio, celebrated at an annual festival.
