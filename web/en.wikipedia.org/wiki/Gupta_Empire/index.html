Gupta Empire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A gold coin (dinara) of Chandragupta II showing the king as an archer]

The Gupta Empire (c. 320–550 CE) ruled northern India during a period celebrated as a golden age of Indian civilisation — an era of flourishing Sanskrit literature, sculpture, mathematics and astronomy. Founded by Chandragupta I, expanded by Samudragupta and Chandragupta II, the empire governed loosely through tributary kings rather than the Maurya's tight bureaucracy. The decimal place-value system and the concept of zero matured in this milieu, Aryabhata computed π and the length of the year, and Kalidasa wrote the classical Sanskrit drama and poetry by which the age is remembered.

Expansion and culture

Chandragupta I (r. c. 319–335) founded the era bearing his family's name; his son Samudragupta campaigned across the subcontinent, as the Allahabad pillar inscription boasts, and his son Chandragupta II (r. c. 375–415) destroyed the Western Satraps and made Ujjain a second capital. The Chinese pilgrim Faxian, travelling in 399–412, described a prosperous, lightly governed land. Gupta coinage, temple architecture (the beginnings of the Nagara style) and painting at Ajanta set classical standards.

Decline

From the 460s, invasions by the Hunas (Hephthalite-related peoples) drained the empire; Skandagupta repelled them at great cost, but after c. 500 the empire shrank to a regional power in Magadha and faded by the mid-6th century. Gupta institutions, art and learning shaped classical Indian culture and spread through Southeast Asia for centuries after.
