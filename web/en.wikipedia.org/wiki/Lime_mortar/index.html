Lime mortar or torching is a type of mortar composed of lime (slaked lime, calcium hydroxide), an aggregate such as sand, and water. It is one of the oldest known types of mortar, used since ancient times by Egyptians, Greeks and Romans, and remained the standard building mortar until it was largely displaced by Portland cement mortars in the late 19th and 20th centuries. Lime mortar is still prized in conservation work and increasingly in sustainable building because it is soft, vapour-permeable ("breathable") and compatible with historic masonry.

Production and the lime cycle

Limestone (calcium carbonate) is burned in a kiln at around 900 °C to produce quicklime (calcium oxide), releasing carbon dioxide. Quicklime is slaked with water — traditionally stored as a putty for months or years for high-quality work — to give calcium hydroxide. Mixed with sand (commonly 1 part lime to 2.5–3 parts sand by volume), the mortar slowly hardens by carbonation: the calcium hydroxide reabsorbs CO₂ from the air and reverts to calcium carbonate, completing the "lime cycle". Pure (non-hydraulic) lime mortars set only by this slow process; natural hydraulic limes, made from limestones containing clay, also set partly with water and are graded NHL 2, 3.5 and 5.

Properties

Compared with cement mortar, lime mortar is weaker but more flexible and permeable: it allows moisture to evaporate through the joints rather than through the bricks or stones (protecting them from frost and salt damage), accommodates slight movement, and can "self-heal" hairline cracks as dissolved lime recrystallises. It requires longer curing and protection from frost and rapid drying. These properties make it essential for repairing pre-20th-century buildings, where hard cement pointing is a common cause of masonry decay.

History and revival

Lime mortars and plasters were used in Neolithic settlements such as 'Ain Ghazal, in Egyptian pyramid construction, in Roman concrete and in medieval cathedrals; hot-mixed lime mortars have recently been shown by research to confer self-healing properties on Roman concrete. After a century of eclipse by Portland cement, lime has been revived by the building-conservation movement, heritage authorities and low-carbon construction, since its manufacture emits less CO₂ and reabsorbs a portion during carbonation.
