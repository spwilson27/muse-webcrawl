Dirac equation

[Image: The Dirac equation inscribed on Dirac's memorial in Westminster Abbey]

Overview

In particle physics, the Dirac equation is a relativistic wave equation derived by British physicist Paul Dirac in 1928. It describes massive spin-½ particles (fermions) such as electrons, consistently with both quantum mechanics and special relativity — the first theory to fully do so. It requires a four-component spinor wavefunction and matrices (the gamma matrices) satisfying a Clifford algebra.

Predictions

The equation's negative-energy solutions led Dirac to predict antimatter: the positron, discovered by Carl Anderson in cosmic rays in 1932. The equation also naturally yields electron spin and the correct fine structure of hydrogen, plus the electron's g-factor of 2. Dirac shared the 1933 Nobel Prize with Schrödinger. The equation is commemorated on Dirac's memorial stone in Westminster Abbey — one of very few formulae so honoured.
