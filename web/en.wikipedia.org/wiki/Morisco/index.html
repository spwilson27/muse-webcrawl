The Moriscos (Spanish: moriscos, "Moor-like") were former Muslims and their descendants in Spain who were forced to convert to Christianity between 1502 and 1526, following the completion of the Reconquista. Suspected of practising Islam in secret, they were subjected to discrimination, Inquisition scrutiny, and cultural suppression, before being expelled from Spain entirely between 1609 and 1614.

Forced conversion

After the fall of Granada in 1492, the capitulations had guaranteed Muslims freedom of worship, but under Cardinal Cisneros these guarantees were broken; revolts in the Alpujarras (1499–1501) were followed in 1502 by a royal decree requiring Muslims in Castile to convert or leave. Aragon followed in 1526. The converts and their children — "New Christians" of Muslim origin — numbered perhaps 300,000 or more, concentrated in Valencia (where they formed a large part of the agricultural labour force), Aragon, and Granada.

Culture and repression

Many Moriscos retained Arabic speech, dress, baths, music, and Islamic practice in private. They produced a distinctive literature in Aljamiado — Spanish written in Arabic characters. In 1567 a pragmatic sanction of Philip II banned the Arabic language, traditional dress, and baths; the result was the second Alpujarras revolt (1568–1571), after which the Moriscos of Granada were dispersed throughout Castile.

Expulsion

In 1609 Philip III's government, advised by the Duke of Lerma, decreed the expulsion of all Moriscos, beginning with Valencia. By 1614 roughly 300,000 people had been deported, mostly to North Africa, in one of the largest forced migrations of early modern Europe. The expulsion devastated the Valencian economy and left lasting marks on Spanish agriculture and demography.
