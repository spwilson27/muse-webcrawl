The Hussite Wars, also called the Bohemian Wars or the Hussite Revolution, were a series of civil wars fought between the Hussites — followers of the executed Bohemian reformer Jan Hus — and the combined forces of the Holy Roman Emperor Sigismund, the papacy and various Catholic leagues between 1419 and about 1434. The wars involved five successive crusades proclaimed against the Hussites, all of which failed. They were an early episode of religious warfare in Europe and are noted for innovative tactics, including the use of war wagons and early hand-held firearms.

Background

Jan Hus was burned at the stake at the Council of Constance in 1415 despite a safe conduct, provoking outrage in Bohemia. The First Defenestration of Prague on 30 July 1419, when a Hussite mob threw Catholic councillors from the windows of the New Town Hall, is conventionally taken as the start of the war.

Course of the war

Under the one-eyed (later blind) general Jan Žižka, the radical Taborites perfected the wagenburg — a mobile fortress of armoured wagons chained together and defended by crossbowmen, handgunners and flail-men. Hussite armies defeated crusading forces at Vítkov Hill (1420), Kutná Hora, Německý Brod, Ústí nad Labem and Tachov. Hussite raiding expeditions, the "beautiful rides" (spanilé jízdy), reached into Germany, Poland and Hungary.

End and settlement

The moderate Utraquist faction, which sought communion under both kinds (bread and wine) for the laity, eventually allied with the Catholics against the radicals. The Taborites and their allies were decisively defeated at the Battle of Lipany on 30 May 1434, where Prokop the Great was killed. The Compactata of Basel (1436) legally recognised Utraquism in Bohemia, a unique settlement that lasted until the seventeenth century.

Legacy

The wars are a cornerstone of Czech national memory; Žižka is celebrated as a national hero. Militarily, the Hussite combination of field fortification, firearms and disciplined infantry anticipated later developments in European warfare.
