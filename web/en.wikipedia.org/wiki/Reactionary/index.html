Reactionary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In political science, a reactionary is a person or ideology favouring a return to a previous political state of society that they believe possessed positive characteristics absent from contemporary society. The term originated in the French Revolution, where réaction described those who sought to reverse the Revolution and restore the Bourbon monarchy and the privileges of the ancien régime; its theorists included Joseph de Maistre and Louis de Bonald.

Reaction differs from conservatism in its object: the conservative seeks to preserve existing institutions and change slowly, while the reactionary seeks to undo changes already made and restore an earlier order — monarchy, established religion, aristocratic hierarchy — that has been lost or fatally weakened. Because the label is usually applied by opponents, and carries a pejorative charge, few movements adopt it proudly, though some thinkers (the Spanish traditionalist movement, and some contemporary integralist and neo-reactionary writers) have embraced versions of it.

Usage and analysis

Scholars distinguish reaction from other right-wing positions by its temporal direction — restoration rather than conservation or reform — while noting that “restoration” is always selective, since the past cannot literally return. The term is also used loosely in journalism as a synonym for extremely conservative, a usage historians caution against. Mark Lilla's The Shipwrecked Mind (2016) is a well-known study of the reactionary cast of mind across eras.
