Drum brake

A drum brake is a brake that uses friction caused by a set of shoes or pads that press outward against a rotating bowl-shaped part called a brake drum. The term drum brake usually means a brake in which shoes press on the inner surface of the drum. When shoes press on the outside of a drum, it is usually called a clasp brake.

History

The modern automobile drum brake was first used in a car made by Maybach in 1900, although the principle was used earlier by Louis Renault in 1902 with a mechanically operated system. Drum brakes were standard equipment on cars for most of the twentieth century. As disc brakes became cheaper and more common from the 1960s onward, drums were progressively relegated to the rear wheels of smaller and cheaper cars, and to parking brake applications.

Construction

A drum brake consists of a cylindrical drum that rotates with the wheel, a pair of curved brake shoes lined with friction material, a wheel cylinder in hydraulic systems, return springs, and a backing plate. When the brake pedal is pressed, hydraulic pressure in the wheel cylinder pushes the shoes outward against the inside of the drum, and the resulting friction slows the wheel. A self-energizing (servo) action, in which the rotation of the drum helps wedge the leading shoe more firmly against the drum, increases braking force.

Advantages and disadvantages

Drum brakes are cheap to manufacture, can incorporate a parking brake mechanism easily, and provide good braking force for their size. Their principal disadvantage is poor heat dissipation: the enclosed drum traps heat, which can cause brake fade under repeated heavy use. They are also more affected by water, and servicing the internal components is more laborious than changing disc pads. For these reasons, disc brakes are preferred on the front wheels of most modern vehicles, where the majority of braking effort occurs.

Applications

Drum brakes remain common on the rear axles of economy cars, on many motorcycles and bicycles (as hub brakes), on trailers, and in industrial machinery. Large trucks often use air-operated drum brakes with S-cam actuation.
