Transgenesis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transgenesis is the process of introducing a foreign gene — a transgene — into a living organism so that the organism acquires a new property and passes it to its offspring. The resulting organism is described as transgenic, and is a type of genetically modified organism (GMO). Transgenesis differs from cisgenesis, in which the introduced gene comes from the same or a cross-compatible species. Transgenic organisms are produced in research (especially mice, Drosophila, zebrafish and plants) and commercially (notably insect- and herbicide-resistant crops, and animals engineered to produce pharmaceuticals in milk).

Methods

Several techniques are used to deliver and integrate a transgene:

  • Pronuclear microinjection: purified DNA is injected directly into the nucleus of a fertilised egg; the egg is implanted in a foster mother. Used classically for transgenic mice and livestock, though integration is random and efficiency low.
  • Embryonic stem cell methods: DNA is introduced into cultured embryonic stem cells, selected, and injected into blastocysts, enabling targeted insertion (gene targeting) and knock-out animals.
  • Retroviral and lentiviral vectors: engineered viruses carry the transgene into cells or embryos.
  • Agrobacterium-mediated transformation and biolistics: the standard routes in plants, using the bacterium's natural T-DNA transfer system or a "gene gun".
  • Genome editing: nucleases such as CRISPR-Cas9 can insert transgenes at chosen sites with much greater precision than random integration.

Applications

Transgenic animals serve as models of human disease (for example, mice carrying human cancer genes — "oncomice"), as bioreactors producing therapeutic proteins, and in xenotransplantation research, where pigs have been engineered to reduce rejection of organs in humans. Fluorescent reporter transgenes (for example GFP) let researchers watch gene expression in living tissue. In agriculture, widely grown transgenic crops include soybean, maize, cotton and canola engineered for herbicide tolerance or insect resistance (Bt crops).

Concerns and regulation

Transgenesis raises ethical and safety questions: animal welfare, the risk of transgenes spreading to wild relatives, food safety assessment, and patenting of living organisms. Regulation varies widely — the United States regulates largely by product characteristics, while the European Union applies process-based rules to GMOs including transgenic organisms, with labelling and traceability requirements.
