Evangelicalism is a worldwide movement within Protestant Christianity holding that the essence of the gospel lies in salvation by faith in the atonement of Jesus Christ. Historian David Bebbington's influential "quadrilateral" defines evangelicalism by four marks: biblicism (the Bible as ultimate authority), crucicentrism (emphasis on Christ's death and resurrection), conversionism (the need to be "born again"), and activism (spreading the faith).

History

Evangelicalism emerged from the eighteenth-century revivals — British Methodism under John Wesley, the preaching tours of George Whitefield, and the First Great Awakening in colonial America associated with Jonathan Edwards. Nineteenth-century evangelicalism powered missionary expansion, abolitionism, and temperance; the Second Great Awakening reshaped American religion.

In the early twentieth century the movement split over theological modernism: fundamentalists withdrew from mainline denominations, while after 1945 a self-consciously moderate "neo-evangelicalism" — associated with Billy Graham, Fuller Seminary, and the National Association of Evangelicals — sought cultural engagement. From the late twentieth century, evangelical and Pentecostal Christianity grew explosively in Latin America, Africa, and Asia, and in the United States white evangelicals became a prominent political constituency.
