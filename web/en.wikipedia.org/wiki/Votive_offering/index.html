Votive offering

A votive offering or votive deposit is one or more objects displayed or deposited, without the intention of recovery or use, in a sacred place for broadly religious purposes. Such items are a feature of modern and ancient societies and are generally made in order to gain favor with supernatural forces.

Votive offerings are typically made in response to a vow (votum), in gratitude for a benefit received — such as recovery from illness, survival of a danger, or success in an undertaking — or in anticipation of a desired favor. The Latin term ex voto ("from the vow") gives its name to the ex-voto, an offering given in fulfillment of a vow.

History

Votive deposits are known from Neolithic and Bronze Age Europe, where weapons, tools, and ornaments were placed in rivers, bogs, and lakes. In ancient Egypt, Greece, and Rome, temples accumulated figurines, plaques, and models; healing sanctuaries such as those of Asclepius received anatomical votives representing the body part that had been healed. In the Etruscan and Roman worlds, thousands of terracotta body parts have been excavated from sanctuary sites.

The practice continues in many religions. In Roman Catholic tradition, ex-votos, milagros, candles, and crutches are left at shrines and pilgrimage churches. Buddhist and Hindu temples receive offerings of statues, money, and miniature objects, and in Japan, wooden ema plaques bearing wishes are hung at Shinto shrines.
