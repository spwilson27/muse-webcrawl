Joseph Larmor

Sir Joseph Larmor (11 July 1857 – 19 May 1942) was an Irish physicist and mathematician who held the Lucasian chair at Cambridge and made major contributions to the theory of electromagnetism and the electron. His name survives in the Larmor formula for the power radiated by an accelerating charge, in Larmor precession of magnetic moments, and in the Larmor radius of charged-particle orbits.

Life

Larmor was born at Magheragall, County Antrim, Ireland, and educated at the Royal Belfast Academical Institution, Queen's University Belfast, and St John's College, Cambridge, where he was Senior Wrangler. After fellowships and a professorship at Queen's College Galway, he returned to Cambridge, succeeding George Gabriel Stokes as Lucasian Professor of Mathematics in 1903, a chair he held until 1932. He served as a Unionist Member of Parliament for Cambridge University from 1911 to 1922, and was secretary of the Royal Society. He died at Holywood, County Down, in 1942.

Work

Larmor's treatise Aether and Matter (1900) developed an electron theory of matter in which he derived, before and independently of Lorentz, the transformations now called Lorentz transformations, and discussed length contraction and time dilation within an ether framework — work that anticipated aspects of special relativity, whose physical interpretation he resisted. In 1897 he published the formula (also derived by Liénard) giving the total power radiated by a non-relativistic accelerating charge, a foundation result of electrodynamics. His theorem on Larmor precession (1897) describes how the orbit of an electron, or a magnetic moment, precesses in a magnetic field; the associated frequency and the gyro-radius of ions in plasmas and magnetic fields — the Larmor radius — remain standard concepts in plasma physics and astrophysics.
