The Trikāya (Sanskrit: त्रिकाय, literally "three bodies") is a Mahayana Buddhist doctrine that a Buddha has three kāyas or "bodies": the Nirmāṇakāya (the created or emanation body, which appears in the physical world), the Saṃbhogakāya (the body of bliss or enjoyment, a celestial form), and the Dharmakāya (the truth body, which embodies the principle of enlightenment itself and is identical with ultimate reality).

The doctrine developed within Mahayana Buddhism as a way of explaining how the historical Buddha, Siddhartha Gautama, could simultaneously be a human being who lived and died, and also a transcendent, eternal presence accessible to devotees. It became a central teaching in East Asian Buddhism, Tibetan Buddhism, and in the interpretation of Pure Land traditions, where Amida (Amitābha) is understood as a Saṃbhogakāya Buddha and the Pure Land as his field of activity.

The three bodies

Dharmakāya

The Dharmakāya ("truth body" or "reality body") is the unmanifested, inconceivable aspect of a Buddha, out of which Buddhas arise and to which they return after death. It is equated with the Dharma itself, with emptiness (śūnyatā), and with the ground of all phenomena. Because it transcends form and conceptual thought, it cannot be depicted or described directly.

Saṃbhogakāya

The Saṃbhogakāya ("enjoyment body" or "bliss body") is a luminous, celestial form that a Buddha assumes in order to teach advanced bodhisattvas in pure realms. Buddhas such as Amitābha and the Medicine Buddha are commonly understood as Saṃbhogakāya manifestations. This body is adorned with the thirty-two major marks and eighty minor marks of a great being and is not subject to ordinary birth and death.

Nirmāṇakāya

The Nirmāṇakāya ("transformation body" or "emanation body") is the physical manifestation of a Buddha in the world of beings — a body that appears to be born, to age, to teach, and to pass away, while in reality being a skilful means (upāya) projected for the benefit of others. Śākyamuni Buddha, born as Siddhartha Gautama, is the paradigmatic Nirmāṇakāya. In Tibetan Buddhism, tulkus — recognised reincarnate lamas, including the Dalai Lamas — are also regarded as Nirmāṇakāya emanations.

Historical development

The roots of the doctrine lie in earlier Buddhist ideas distinguishing the Buddha's physical body (rūpakāya) from his "Dharma body" (dharmakāya), found in early texts that describe the Buddha as identical with his teaching: "Whoever sees the Dharma sees me." Mahayana schools, particularly the Yogācāra, elaborated these hints into a threefold scheme during roughly the 4th century CE, adding the Saṃbhogakāya as an intermediate category. The fully developed Trikāya system is expounded in texts such as the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra, the Suvarṇaprabhāsa Sūtra (Golden Light Sutra), and treatises attributed to Asaṅga and later commentators.

In East Asian and Tibetan Buddhism

In Chinese Buddhism, the three bodies were correlated with indigenous categories and became fundamental to schools such as Tiantai and Huayan. In the Japanese Shingon school, the Dharmakāya Buddha Mahāvairocana is held to preach the Dharma directly through the universe itself. In Tibetan Buddhism, the Trikāya underpins practices such as guru yoga, in which the teacher is visualised as inseparable from the three bodies, and the understanding of the bardo states between death and rebirth.
