Phosphorus (Greek: Φωσφόρος, Phōsphoros, "light-bringer") is the ancient Greek name for the Morning Star — the planet Venus as it appears in the eastern sky before sunrise. Venus seen in the evening was called Hesperus. For a long time the Greeks treated the two as different celestial objects; the recognition that morning and evening star are one and the same planet was traditionally credited to Pythagoras or Parmenides, and was known to the Babylonians much earlier.

Mythology

In Greek mythology Phosphorus was personified as a god, a son of the dawn goddess Eos and the Titan Astraeus (or of Cephalus), and father of Ceyx and Daedalion. His Roman equivalent, Lucifer ("light-bearer"), was a minor figure in classical myth; the name only later became associated, through the Vulgate translation of Isaiah 14:12, with the fallen angel Satan.

Philosophy

The identity of Phosphorus and Hesperus is a famous example in the philosophy of language: Gottlob Frege used "morning star" and "evening star" to illustrate the distinction between sense (Sinn) and reference (Bedeutung). The two expressions refer to the same object — Venus — yet differ in cognitive significance, which is why the statement "the morning star is the evening star" is informative while "the morning star is the morning star" is trivial.

The chemical element phosphorus, isolated by Hennig Brand in 1669, took its name from this "light-bringer" because the white form glows in the dark.
