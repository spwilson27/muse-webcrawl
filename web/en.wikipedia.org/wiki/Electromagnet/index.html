An electromagnet is a type of magnet in which the magnetic field is produced by an electric current. Electromagnets usually consist of wire wound into a coil. A current through the wire creates a magnetic field which is concentrated in the hole in the centre of the coil. The magnetic field disappears when the current is turned off. The wire turns are often wound around a magnetic core made from a ferromagnetic material such as iron, which greatly strengthens the field.

History

Danish scientist Hans Christian Ørsted discovered in 1820 that electric currents create magnetic fields. The British scientist William Sturgeon invented the electromagnet in 1824: a horseshoe-shaped piece of iron wrapped with bare copper wire that could lift many times its own weight. Joseph Henry in the United States improved the design by insulating the wire, allowing many layers of windings and far stronger magnets.

How it works

The magnetic field of a current-carrying coil (a solenoid) closely resembles that of a bar magnet, with a north and south pole. The field strength depends on the current, the number of turns, and the permeability of the core. Inserting a soft-iron core concentrates the field lines and can increase the field by hundreds or thousands of times, until the core saturates.

Applications

Electromagnets are fundamental to electric motors, generators, transformers, relays, loudspeakers, hard disk drives and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanners. Powerful industrial electromagnets lift scrap metal in junkyards, and superconducting electromagnets bend particle beams in accelerators and levitate maglev trains. Unlike permanent magnets, their strength can be switched on and off and adjusted through the current, which makes them indispensable in control systems.

Types

Resistive electromagnets use ordinary conducting wire and dissipate energy as heat. Superconducting magnets use coils cooled below their critical temperature, carrying large currents with no resistive loss and producing the strongest steady fields. Pulsed magnets achieve extreme fields for very short durations.
