Edict of Torda

DateJanuary 1568
LocationTorda (Turda), Principality of Transylvania
Issued byDiet of Transylvania under John Sigismund Zápolya
SubjectReligious toleration

The Edict of Torda (also called the Edict of Turda) was a decree of the Diet of Transylvania, issued in January 1568 under Prince John Sigismund Zápolya, that granted a remarkable degree of religious freedom for its time. It is often called the first edict of religious toleration in Christian Europe.

Content

The edict declared that faith is a gift of God and forbade anyone to coerce or persecute others for their religion. Preachers could expound the Gospel according to their own understanding, and each community could choose its own preacher. In a Europe torn by wars of religion, the edict gave legal protection to Catholic, Lutheran, Calvinist and — uniquely — Unitarian communities in Transylvania, whose "received religions" were formally recognised.

Context

Transylvania, a semi-independent principality under Ottoman suzerainty, was religiously diverse, and its court under John Sigismund — advised by figures such as Giorgio Biandrata and Ferenc Dávid — increasingly favoured the anti-Trinitarian (Unitarian) movement. The edict did not establish full modern freedom of religion: it primarily protected communities' choice among recognised confessions, and later rulers restricted its scope, but toleration of the four received religions remained a feature of Transylvanian law.

Legacy

The Edict of Torda is celebrated by Unitarians as a foundational document of religious liberty, predating the Edict of Nantes (1598) by three decades and modern human-rights instruments by centuries.
