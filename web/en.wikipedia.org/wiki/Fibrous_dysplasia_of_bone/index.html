Fibrous dysplasia of bone

Fibrous dysplasia
Specialty: Medical genetics, orthopaedics
Cause: Activating GNAS mutation (mosaic)
Forms: Monostotic (one bone), polyostotic (many bones)
Onset: Childhood or adolescence

Fibrous dysplasia is a rare, non-inherited developmental disorder of bone in which normal bone and marrow are replaced by fibrous tissue and irregular, poorly mineralised trabecular bone, leading to weak, deformed, and fracture-prone bones. It is caused by a post-zygotic activating mutation in the GNAS gene, which encodes the Gsα subunit of a G protein; because the mutation arises after fertilisation, it is present in a mosaic pattern, and the severity of disease depends on how widely the mutant cells are distributed.

Forms and presentation

When only one bone is affected, the disease is called monostotic fibrous dysplasia (about 70–80% of cases), commonly involving the ribs, femur, tibia, or skull. Polyostotic disease affects multiple bones, often on one side of the body, and can cause leg-length discrepancy, limp, and the classic "shepherd's crook" deformity of the proximal femur. Lesions often appear in childhood, grow during adolescence, and stabilise in adulthood. On X-ray, affected bone shows a characteristic homogeneous "ground-glass" appearance.

Associated syndromes

Polyostotic fibrous dysplasia combined with café-au-lait skin macules with irregular ("coast of Maine") borders and endocrine hyperfunction — such as precocious puberty, hyperthyroidism, growth hormone excess, and phosphate wasting — constitutes McCune–Albright syndrome. Fibrous dysplasia combined with intramuscular myxomas is known as Mazabraud syndrome.

Treatment

There is no cure. Management is directed at symptoms and complications: orthopaedic surgery for fractures and deformity, and bisphosphonates (such as pamidronate) which may relieve bone pain. Malignant transformation into osteosarcoma or other sarcomas is rare (well under 1% in most series) but recognised, particularly in polyostotic disease and after radiation, which is avoided for this reason.
