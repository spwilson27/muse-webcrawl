Alfred Pritchard Sloan Jr. (23 May 1875 – 17 February 1966) was an American business executive in the automotive industry, best known as the long-time president and chief executive officer of General Motors (GM). He is credited with creating the modern decentralised corporate structure and the strategy of marketing distinct car brands to successive rungs of buyers — "a car for every purse and purpose".

Career

Sloan graduated from MIT in 1895 and became president of the Hyatt Roller Bearing Company, a supplier of bearings to the young automobile industry. When Hyatt was acquired by William C. Durant's United Motors, which in turn merged into General Motors, Sloan rose within GM and became its president in 1923, succeeding Pierre S. du Pont. He remained chief executive until 1946 and chairman until 1956.

Under Sloan, GM overtook Ford as the largest automobile manufacturer. His innovations included annual model changes (a form of planned obsolescence), a brand hierarchy running from Chevrolet through Pontiac, Oldsmobile and Buick to Cadillac, consumer credit through GMAC, and a management system of decentralised divisions coordinated by central policy committees and financial controls — the "Sloan model" studied by management theorists ever since. His memoir, My Years with General Motors (1963), is regarded as a classic of management literature.

Philanthropy

Through the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation, established in 1934, he funded scientific and educational causes, including the Sloan Kettering Institute for cancer research in New York and the Sloan School of Management at MIT, which bears his name.
