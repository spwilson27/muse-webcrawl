Virtual particle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Feynman diagram showing a virtual photon exchanged between two electrons]
The internal line of a Feynman diagram is a virtual particle.

A virtual particle is a theoretical transient particle that appears in the intermediate stages of a scattering process in Quantum field theory, represented by the internal lines of Feynman diagrams. Virtual particles need not satisfy the usual relation between energy, momentum and mass ('off mass shell'), need not be directly observable, and their properties are constrained by the uncertainty principle — roughly, the further they stray from real-particle behaviour, the shorter their lifetime.

Forces, in this picture, are mediated by virtual particles: a virtual photon exchanged between electrons produces the Coulomb and magnetic forces; virtual gluons and sea quarks fill the proton; vacuum fluctuations are virtual particle–antiparticle pairs appearing and vanishing (the physical reality of the vacuum's activity is attested by the Casimir effect and Lamb shift). Physicists stress that virtual particles are a computational feature of perturbation theory — real processes can be computed other ways — while attempts to 'observe' them directly blur into the real particles they become when a process supplies enough energy.

Mediated forces

The range of a force is set by its virtual carrier's mass: massless photons give electromagnetism infinite range, while the 80–91 GeV W and Z bosons confine the weak force to ~10⁻¹⁸ m, and Yukawa's 1935 prediction of the meson from the nuclear force's range is the classic success of this reasoning. Virtual particles also carry the theory's precision: every loop correction to the electron's Anomalous magnetic dipole moment is a statement about virtual processes.
