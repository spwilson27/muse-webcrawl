Intellectual giftedness

Intellectual giftedness is an intellectual ability significantly higher than average. It is a characteristic of children, variously defined, that motivates differences in school programming. It is thought to persist as a trait into adult life, with various consequences studied in longitudinal studies of giftedness over the last century.

There is no generally agreed definition of giftedness for either children or adults, but most school placement decisions and most longitudinal studies over the course of individual lives have followed people with IQs in the top 2.5 percent of the population — that is, IQs above 130. Definitions of giftedness also vary across cultures.

Longitudinal studies

The best-known longitudinal study is the Genetic Studies of Genius begun by Lewis Terman in 1921, which followed 1,528 Californian children with IQs of 135 or higher throughout their lives. The "Termites", as the participants became known, were found to be generally healthier, taller, and better adjusted than stereotypes suggested, though the study also showed that high IQ alone did not guarantee exceptional creative achievement. Later studies include the Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth founded by Julian Stanley in 1971.

Characteristics and controversies

Gifted children may display asynchronous development, intense curiosity, and advanced vocabulary, and some experience social or emotional difficulties, particularly where their abilities are not accommodated. Concepts such as twice-exceptionality — giftedness combined with a disability — and debates over equity in identification remain active areas of research and policy dispute.
