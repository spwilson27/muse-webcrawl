Titan

In Greek mythology, a Titan (Greek: Τιτάν, plural Τιτᾶνες, Titanes) was a member of the elder generation of powerful deities who preceded the Olympian gods. The Titans were the children of the primordial Uranus (Sky) and Gaia (Earth); the word is used both for that first generation and, more loosely, for their descendants who fought on their side.

Usage of the term

Hesiod's Theogony, the principal source, names twelve original Titans — six sons (Oceanus, Coeus, Crius, Hyperion, Iapetus, Cronus) and six daughters (Theia, Rhea, Themis, Mnemosyne, Phoebe, Tethys). Later authors extended the name to children of these Titans, such as Prometheus, Atlas, Helios, and Leto. Hesiod folk-etymologises "Titanes" as "strainers", those who strained and did a fearful deed; modern scholars connect the name with pre-Greek origins.

The Titanomachy

Under Cronus the Titans ruled the cosmos during the mythical Golden Age. When Zeus and the Olympians rose against them, the ten-year war called the Titanomachy ended with the Titans defeated and imprisoned in Tartarus, guarded by the Hecatoncheires, while Atlas was condemned to hold up the sky. The myth belongs to a widespread ancient Near Eastern pattern of succession among divine generations.
