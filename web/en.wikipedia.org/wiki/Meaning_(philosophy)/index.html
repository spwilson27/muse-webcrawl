Meaning (philosophy)

In philosophy — particularly in the philosophy of language, semiotics and hermeneutics — meaning concerns what words, signs, symbols and sentences express, signify or stand for, and how they can be understood. The study of meaning overlaps with semantics, pragmatics and the theory of reference, and raises questions about the relation between language, thought and the world.

Sense and reference

Gottlob Frege distinguished the reference (Bedeutung) of an expression — the thing it picks out — from its sense (Sinn), the mode of presentation of that thing. "The Morning Star" and "the Evening Star" refer to the same planet, Venus, but differ in sense, which explains why the statement of their identity can be informative. Bertrand Russell's theory of descriptions and later causal theories of reference associated with Saul Kripke and Hilary Putnam offered competing accounts of how names and natural-kind terms attach to the world.

Theories of meaning

Major approaches include: ideational theories, descending from Locke, which identify meaning with associated mental ideas; verificationism, championed by the logical positivists, which tied a sentence's meaning to its method of verification; truth-conditional semantics, developed by Donald Davidson, which explains meaning through the conditions under which sentences are true; use theories, inspired by the later Wittgenstein's dictum that meaning is use in the language; and intentional theories such as Paul Grice's, which analyse meaning in terms of speakers' communicative intentions. J. L. Austin and John Searle added the dimension of speech acts — what utterances do as well as what they say.

Related senses

"Meaning" also names significance in a broader existential register — the "meaning of life" — and in hermeneutics the meaning of a text, which thinkers from Schleiermacher to Gadamer debated whether to anchor in authorial intention or in the reader's historically situated understanding.
