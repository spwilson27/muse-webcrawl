The Unknown Warrior is an unidentified British serviceman killed in the First World War whose body was brought from France and buried in Westminster Abbey, London, on 11 November 1920. The grave, in the west nave of the Abbey, represents all British and Commonwealth service personnel who have no known grave, and has become one of the most revered war memorials in the United Kingdom; it is the only grave in the Abbey over which visitors are not permitted to walk.

History

The idea was proposed in 1916 by army chaplain David Railton, who had seen a grave in France marked "An Unknown British Soldier." In November 1920, the bodies of four unidentified British soldiers were exhumed from different battlefields in France and Belgium; one was chosen at random at Saint-Pol-sur-Ternoise by Brigadier General Louis Wyatt, and the others were reburied. The chosen body was escorted to London with full honours, drawn in procession past the newly unveiled Cenotaph, and buried in the Abbey in the presence of King George V, with earth from the battlefields and a slab of black Belgian marble inscribed with text by the Dean of Westminster, Herbert Ryle.

Legacy

The concept inspired similar tombs worldwide, including the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier at Arlington National Cemetery in the United States and the Arc de Triomphe tomb in France, both established in 1921. In 1923 Queen Elizabeth, later the Queen Mother, began a royal tradition by laying her wedding bouquet on the grave in memory of her brother killed at Loos; royal brides have followed the custom since.
