Thubten Gyatso, the 13th Dalai Lama (1876–1933)

Ngawang Lobzang Thubten Gyatso Jigdral Chokley Namgyal (12 February 1876 – 17 December 1933), commonly known as the 13th Dalai Lama, was the spiritual and temporal ruler of Tibet who declared and defended Tibet's de facto independence during the collapse of the Qing dynasty. Remembered in Tibet as the "Great Thirteenth", he was a reforming moderniser who sought to strengthen his country between British India, Russia and China.

Early life

He was born to a peasant family in Dagpo, south of Lhasa, and recognised as the reincarnation of the 12th Dalai Lama in 1877, being enthroned at the Potala Palace in 1879. He assumed full political power in 1895.

Exiles and independence

During the British invasion of Tibet under Francis Younghusband in 1904, the Dalai Lama fled to Mongolia and later Beijing; he returned to Lhasa in 1909 but fled again, this time to British India, when Qing troops occupied the capital in 1910. After the Chinese revolution, Tibetan forces expelled the Qing garrison, and in 1913 he returned and issued a proclamation asserting Tibet's independence. He concluded a treaty with Mongolia and pursued recognition, though Tibet remained unrecognised internationally.

Reforms

The 13th Dalai Lama modernised Tibet's army with British assistance, created police and postal services, introduced paper currency and postage stamps, curtailed the power of monastic officials, and sent students to be educated in England. In his political testament of 1932 he warned Tibetans of the dangers of complacency and of the communist threat — a warning remembered as prophetic after 1950.
