Liu Bei (Chinese: 劉備; 161 – 10 June 223), courtesy name Xuande, was a Chinese warlord in the late Eastern Han dynasty who founded the state of Shu Han in the Three Kingdoms period and became its first emperor (as Emperor Zhaolie of Han). A distant descendant of the Han imperial house, Liu Bei rose from humble origins — in his youth he wove straw mats and sandals for a living — to become one of the principal contenders for power as the Han dynasty collapsed.

Rise

Liu Bei first gained notice suppressing the Yellow Turban Rebellion (184), famously alongside his sworn brothers Guan Yu and Zhang Fei, with whom tradition says he took the Oath of the Peach Garden. Through two decades of shifting alliances and defeats he served, and was sheltered by, a succession of warlords — Gongsun Zan, Tao Qian, Cao Cao, Yuan Shao and Liu Biao — acquiring a reputation for benevolence and for attracting talented followers. In 207 the recluse strategist Zhuge Liang joined him and set out the Longzhong Plan for a tripartite division of China. Allied with Sun Quan, Liu Bei defeated Cao Cao at the Battle of Red Cliffs (208), then seized Jing Province and, in 214, Yi Province from Liu Zhang.

Emperor of Shu Han

In 219 Liu Bei's general Guan Yu was captured and executed by Sun Quan's forces, and Jing Province was lost. When Cao Pi usurped the Han throne in 220, Liu Bei proclaimed himself emperor in 221, claiming to continue the Han line. To avenge Guan Yu and recover Jing, he launched a great campaign against Sun Quan's Eastern Wu, but was disastrously defeated by Lu Xun at the Battle of Xiaoting (Yiling) in 222. He died the following year at Baidicheng, entrusting his young heir Liu Shan and the state to Zhuge Liang with the famous words that if the heir proved unfit, Zhuge might take the throne himself.

Legacy

In the 14th-century novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms by Luo Guanzhong, Liu Bei is portrayed as the paragon of Confucian virtue — benevolent, loyal and rightful — and his cause framed as the legitimate one against the "usurping" Cao Wei. This sympathetic image has dominated East Asian popular culture ever since, though historians drawing on Chen Shou's Records of the Three Kingdoms see a more pragmatic and ambitious leader.
