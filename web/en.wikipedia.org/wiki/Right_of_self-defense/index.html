Right of self-defense

For self-defence by states under international law, see Self-defence in international law. For the martial art concept, see Self-defense.

Self-defense (law)
TypeLegal defence (justification)

The right of self-defense (also called self-defence) is the right for people to use reasonable or defensive force, for the purpose of defending one's own life (self-defense) or the lives of others, including, in certain circumstances, the use of deadly force. If a defendant uses defensive force because of a threat of death or serious bodily harm (or, in some jurisdictions, a serious felony), the act is considered justified and the defendant is not criminally liable.

General principles

Across legal traditions, self-defense generally requires that the defender face an unlawful threat that is imminent, that force be necessary to repel it, and that it be proportionate to the threat. The defender typically must not have been the initial aggressor (though an aggressor may regain the right by withdrawing). Philosophically, the right is grounded in natural-law accounts of self-preservation (Locke, Aquinas) and in the state's monopoly on violence being unavailable at the moment of attack. Most systems also allow the defence to be mistaken, if the belief in the threat was honest and (in many jurisdictions) reasonable.

Deadly force

Lethal force is generally justified only against threats of death, serious bodily injury, or certain grave crimes such as armed robbery or rape. Jurisdictions differ on whether non-deadly threats can ever justify deadly response and on the evidentiary burdens: some place the burden of disproving self-defense on the prosecution once the issue is raised.

Duty to retreat

Legal systems diverge on retreat:

  • Duty-to-retreat jurisdictions (including England and Wales, and a minority of US states) require a person outside the home to retreat if it can be done safely before using deadly force.
  • Stand-your-ground laws (in a majority of US states, by statute or case law) remove any duty to retreat where a person is lawfully present; Florida's 2005 law popularised the model and remains controversial.
  • Castle doctrine — widely adopted — treats the home specially: occupants generally have no duty to retreat from an intruder and may enjoy a presumption that defensive force was reasonable.

Defence of others and property

Most systems extend the right to defending third parties under similar conditions. Defence of property alone justifies only non-deadly force in most jurisdictions; mechanical traps set to injure intruders (e.g. spring guns) are generally unlawful.

International law

At the level of states, Article 51 of the UN Charter preserves the "inherent right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs", subject to necessity, proportionality and reporting to the Security Council. The customary criteria are often traced to the Caroline test (1837/1842): defensive action must be "instant, overwhelming, leaving no choice of means, and no moment for deliberation".
