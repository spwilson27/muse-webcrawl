Ferrite (magnet)

Ferrite magnet
Type: Ceramic permanent magnet
Main compositions: SrFe12O19, BaFe12O19
Remanence: ~0.2–0.4 T
Developed: 1930s–1950s

A ferrite is a type of ceramic compound composed of iron oxide (Fe2O3) combined chemically with one or more metallic elements such as strontium, barium, manganese, nickel, or zinc. Ferrites are ferrimagnetic, meaning they can be magnetised, but they are electrically non-conductive, which makes them especially useful in high-frequency applications where eddy currents in metallic magnets would cause losses.

Hard and soft ferrites

Ferrites are divided into two families. Hard ferrites have high coercivity and are used to make permanent magnets; the commonest are strontium ferrite (SrFe12O19) and barium ferrite (BaFe12O19), the dark grey ceramic magnets found in refrigerator magnets, loudspeakers, and small electric motors. Soft ferrites, such as manganese–zinc and nickel–zinc ferrites, have low coercivity and are used for transformer and inductor cores, ferrite beads for noise suppression, and the ferrite rods inside AM radio aerials.

Production

Ferrite magnets are manufactured by powder metallurgy: iron oxide is mixed with strontium or barium carbonate, calcined, milled to a fine powder, pressed into shape (often in a magnetic field to produce an anisotropic magnet), and sintered at high temperature. The finished ceramic is hard and brittle and can only be machined by diamond tools.

Properties and uses

Ferrite magnets are much weaker than rare-earth magnets such as neodymium magnets, but they are cheap, corrosion-resistant, and stable at relatively high temperatures, with Curie temperatures around 450 °C. By mass they account for the great majority of permanent magnets produced worldwide, used in motors, generators, magnetic separators, loudspeakers, and magnetic resonance imaging accessories. Soft ferrites are essential in switch-mode power supplies and telecommunications.

History

The magnetic properties of ferrites were investigated by Yogoro Kato and Takeshi Takei in Japan in the 1930s, and Philips developed commercial hexagonal ferrites (marketed as Ferroxdure) in the 1950s, greatly expanding their industrial use.
