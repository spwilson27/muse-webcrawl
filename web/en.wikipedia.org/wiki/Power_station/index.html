Power station

A power station, also referred to as a power plant or powerhouse and sometimes a generating station or generating plant, is an industrial facility for the generation of electric power. Power stations are generally connected to an electrical grid.

Many power stations contain one or more generators, rotating machines that convert mechanical power into three-phase electric power. The relative motion between a magnetic field and a conductor creates an electric current. The energy source harnessed to turn the generator varies widely; most power stations in the world burn fossil fuels such as coal, oil and natural gas to generate electricity, while others use nuclear power, solar, wind, geothermal or hydroelectric sources.

History

Early central stations used reciprocating steam engines. The development of the steam turbine allowed larger, more efficient central stations to be built, and by the early 20th century large turbo-alternators had become standard. The Pearl Street Station in New York, opened by Thomas Edison in 1882, is often cited as the first central power station, supplying direct current to light bulbs in lower Manhattan.

Thermal power stations

In thermal power stations, mechanical power is produced by a heat engine that transforms thermal energy, often from combustion of a fuel, into rotational energy. Most thermal power stations produce steam, so they are sometimes called steam power stations. Not all thermal energy can be transformed into mechanical power; the lost heat is discharged to the environment through cooling towers, flue gas or cooling water.

Classification by fuel

  • Fossil-fuel power stations burn coal, natural gas or petroleum. They provide most of the world's electricity but are major sources of carbon dioxide emissions.
  • Nuclear power stations use heat from nuclear fission in a reactor to generate steam.
  • Geothermal power stations draw on heat from beneath the Earth's surface.
  • Solar thermal power stations concentrate sunlight to heat a working fluid.
  • Waste incineration and biomass stations burn municipal or agricultural material.

Power from renewable energy

Hydroelectric stations capture the energy of falling water; they range from small run-of-the-river schemes to huge dams such as the Three Gorges Dam in China, the largest power station in the world by installed capacity. Wind turbines and photovoltaic solar plants convert wind and sunlight directly or mechanically into electricity without a thermal cycle.

Prime movers

The prime mover may be a steam turbine, gas turbine, reciprocating engine, water turbine or wind turbine. Combined-cycle stations use both a gas turbine and a steam turbine to improve efficiency, reaching thermal efficiencies above 60 per cent in the best modern plants.
