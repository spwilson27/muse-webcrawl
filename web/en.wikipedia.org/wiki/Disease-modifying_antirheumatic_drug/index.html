Disease-modifying antirheumatic drug

Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) comprise a category of otherwise unrelated drugs used in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis and other inflammatory arthritides and autoimmune diseases. They are defined by their use in slowing or halting disease progression — reducing joint damage and preserving function — rather than merely relieving symptoms, which distinguishes them from nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and corticosteroids.

Classification

  • Conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs) — small molecules developed without a specific molecular target in mind. The anchor drug is methotrexate, taken weekly and used for most patients. Others include hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine, leflunomide, and historically gold salts, penicillamine, and azathioprine.
  • Biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs) — engineered proteins introduced from the late 1990s, such as the TNF inhibitors etanercept, infliximab, and adalimumab; the IL-6 receptor blocker tocilizumab; rituximab; and abatacept.
  • Targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs) — small molecules aimed at specific intracellular pathways, principally the Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors such as tofacitinib, baricitinib, and upadacitinib.

Use

Because DMARDs take weeks to months to act, patients are often bridged with corticosteroids or NSAIDs. Early, aggressive treatment with methotrexate, escalating to combination therapy or a biologic if targets are not met ("treat-to-target"), substantially improves long-term outcomes in rheumatoid arthritis. Most DMARDs are immunosuppressants to varying degrees, so treatment requires monitoring for infection and for drug-specific toxicities — liver and blood monitoring for methotrexate, eye screening for hydroxychloroquine, and tuberculosis screening before TNF inhibitors.
