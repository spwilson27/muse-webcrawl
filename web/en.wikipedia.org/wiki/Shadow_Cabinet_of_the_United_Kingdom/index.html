Shadow Cabinet of the United Kingdom

The Shadow Cabinet is a group of senior spokespeople of His Majesty's Official Opposition in the United Kingdom who, led by the Leader of the Opposition, form an alternative cabinet mirroring the government's Cabinet. Each member — a shadow secretary of state — scrutinises the corresponding government minister, questions them in the House of Commons, and develops alternative policies for the portfolio.

Role and practice

The shadow cabinet meets regularly, typically weekly, while Parliament is sitting. Its members receive a special salary in addition to their MP's pay, and the Leader of the Opposition is entitled to government-provided support, including "Short money" to fund the opposition's parliamentary work. Shadow ministers have no access to departmental civil servants beyond limited factual briefings, and by convention outgoing governments permit pre-election contacts between the opposition and the civil service so that a shadow cabinet can be ready to govern if it wins.

Development

The practice of a formalised opposition front bench grew during the nineteenth century and became established after the First World War. The official status of the Opposition was recognised in 1937, when the Leader of the Opposition first received a statutory salary. The term "shadow cabinet" is credited in parliamentary usage to the mid-20th century; in the Labour Party, the shadow cabinet was for many years elected by Labour MPs (as the Parliamentary Committee) when the party was in opposition.
