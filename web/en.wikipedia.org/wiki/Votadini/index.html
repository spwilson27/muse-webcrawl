Votadini

The Votadini were a Celtic people of the Iron Age and Roman period in what is now southeastern Scotland and northeastern England. Their territory stretched from the Firth of Forth and the Lammermuir Hills south through Lothian and the Borders to the River Tyne, including the great hillfort of Traprain Law in East Lothian, a major center of settlement and metalworking.

Roman period

The Votadini appear in Ptolemy's Geography (2nd century AD), which lists their towns. Unlike many northern peoples, they seem to have maintained friendly relations with Rome: Traprain Law continued to flourish through the Roman period, receiving large quantities of Roman goods, and a famous hoard of hacked late Roman silver (the Traprain Treasure) was buried there in the early 5th century. Historians generally regard the Votadini as a client or buffer people guarding the frontier approaches south of the Antonine Wall and later Hadrian's Wall.

Gododdin

After Roman rule ended, the Votadini's Brythonic kingdom was known in Welsh as Gododdin — the name being the Brythonic development of Votadini. Its territory, centered perhaps on Din Eidyn (Edinburgh), survived into the 6th and 7th centuries. The kingdom is immortalized in the early Welsh poem Y Gododdin, attributed to Aneirin, which laments a warband of Gododdin warriors destroyed at the Battle of Catraeth (c. 600) fighting the Angles of Deira and Bernicia. The celebrated ruler Cunedda is said in Welsh genealogies to have come from Manaw Gododdin to north Wales, founding the dynasty of Gwynedd. The kingdom was ultimately absorbed by the expanding Angles of Northumbria, with Edinburgh falling in the 7th century.
