Car-free city

Venice, the largest entirely car-free urban area in Europe. (Image placeholder)

A car-free city is an urban area in which private motor vehicles are prohibited or severely restricted, so that movement relies on walking, cycling and public transport. The term can describe entire settlements — such as Venice, whose canals and narrow streets never accommodated cars — or designated car-free zones and developments within otherwise car-oriented cities, such as the Vauban district of Freiburg, Germany.

Examples

  • Venice, Italy — the largest car-free city in Europe, where transport is by foot and boat.
  • Mackinac Island, Michigan, USA — motor vehicles have been banned since 1898; transport is by bicycle, horse-drawn carriage and foot.
  • Zermatt, Switzerland — a car-free resort town served by rail, with electric taxis.
  • Ghent and central Brussels, Belgium — large low-traffic zones introduced in recent decades.
  • Halifax and other ecodistricts — car-free or car-reduced neighbourhoods built as planning experiments.
  • Fes el-Bali, Morocco — a medieval medina effectively closed to cars by its street pattern.

Rationale and effects

Advocates cite reduced air and noise pollution, lower greenhouse-gas emissions, safer streets, reclaimed public space, and health benefits from active travel. Urban planners argue that car-free areas encourage denser, mixed-use development and more efficient land use, since large areas devoted to roads and parking can be repurposed. Car-free development typically requires excellent public transport, safe cycling infrastructure, and arrangements for deliveries, emergency vehicles and residents with limited mobility.

Criticism and challenges

Critics question accessibility for disabled people, the effect on businesses dependent on car-borne customers, and the practicality of extending car-free policies beyond historic centres to suburbs built around the automobile. Many cities therefore adopt partial measures — pedestrian zones, congestion charging and low-emission zones — rather than complete bans.

Annual events such as World Car-Free Day (22 September) temporarily extend car-free arrangements in many cities. The concept is closely related to the 15-minute city, transit-oriented development and the broader car-free movement.
