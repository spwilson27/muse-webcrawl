Thomism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Thomas Aquinas, whose thought defines the Thomist school

Thomism is the philosophical and theological school that arose from the thought of St Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), the Dominican friar whose Summa Theologiae and other works synthesised Christian doctrine with the philosophy of Aristotle. Thomism is characterised by the distinction between essence and existence, the doctrine of act and potency, hylomorphism, a realist theory of universals, natural law ethics, and the "five ways" of demonstrating God's existence.

History

Defended by the Dominican order after Aquinas's death and his canonisation in 1323, Thomism contended with rival scholastic schools, notably Scotism and nominalism. It flourished in the sixteenth-century School of Salamanca, whose thinkers such as Francisco de Vitoria applied Thomist natural law to questions of international law and economics. After a period of decline, Pope Leo XIII's encyclical Aeterni Patris (1879) launched a great revival — neo-Thomism — making Aquinas's philosophy normative in Catholic seminaries and universities. Twentieth-century Thomists as different as Jacques Maritain, Étienne Gilson, and the analytic Thomists influenced by Elizabeth Anscombe have kept the tradition in dialogue with modern philosophy.
