Fifty-move rule

The fifty-move rule in chess states that a player can claim a draw if no capture has been made and no pawn has been moved in the last fifty moves by each player (that is, the last hundred half-moves). Its purpose is to prevent a player from avoiding losing indefinitely or attempting to win by exhausting the opponent.

Rules

Under the FIDE Laws of Chess, the game is drawn upon a correct claim by the player having the move if the last 50 moves by each player have been completed without a pawn move or capture. Separately, the game is automatically drawn — with no claim required — once 75 such moves by each player have been completed (the seventy-five-move rule), unless the final move produces checkmate.

History and exceptions

Rules limiting play without progress date back centuries, with the move count varying over time. For a period in the twentieth century, FIDE extended the limit to 75 or 100 moves for certain endgames that endgame tablebases later showed could require many precise moves to win, but the exceptions were abolished in 1992, restoring a uniform fifty-move rule. The longest possible chess game under the rule is finite, and theoretical studies of tablebase endgames have found forced mates requiring more than 500 moves without a capture or pawn move — wins that would be draws under the rule in over-the-board play.
