Stereoisomerism

Stereoisomerism is a form of isomerism in which molecules have the same molecular formula and the same sequence of bonded atoms (constitution), but differ in the three-dimensional arrangement of their atoms in space. Stereoisomers contrast with structural (constitutional) isomers, which differ in the connectivity of their atoms.

Enantiomers

Enantiomers are stereoisomers that are related as non-superimposable mirror images, like left and right hands. They arise most commonly when a carbon atom bears four different substituents, making the molecule chiral. Enantiomers have identical physical properties except that they rotate polarised light in opposite directions and react differently with other chiral substances — a fact of great importance in pharmacology, where one enantiomer of a drug may be therapeutic and the other inactive or harmful.

Diastereomers

Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images of each other. They occur in molecules with two or more stereocentres, and in compounds with double bonds that give rise to cis–trans (or E–Z) isomerism: in cis-2-butene the methyl groups lie on the same side of the double bond, in trans-2-butene on opposite sides. Unlike enantiomers, diastereomers generally have different melting points, solubilities and reactivities, and can be separated by ordinary laboratory methods.

Conformation

Arrangements interconvertible by rotation about single bonds — such as the staggered and eclipsed forms of ethane, or the chair and boat forms of cyclohexane — are called conformations. They are sometimes treated as a special class of stereoisomer (conformational isomers or rotamers), though they usually interconvert too rapidly to be isolated at room temperature.
