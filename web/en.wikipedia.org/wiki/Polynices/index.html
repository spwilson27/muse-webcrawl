Polynices

In Greek mythology, Polynices (also Polyneices; Ancient Greek: Πολυνείκης, "manifold strife") was the son of Oedipus and Jocasta and the brother of Eteocles, Antigone, and Ismene. His fratricidal war against his brother Eteocles for the throne of Thebes is the subject of Aeschylus's tragedy Seven Against Thebes, and his sister Antigone's defiance of the decree forbidding his burial is the subject of Sophocles' Antigone.

Mythology

When Oedipus discovered that he had killed his father and married his mother, he was driven into exile (in some versions cursing his sons for failing to aid him). Eteocles and Polynices agreed to rule Thebes in alternate years, but after the first year Eteocles refused to surrender the throne. Polynices withdrew to Argos, where he married Argeia, daughter of King Adrastus, and persuaded his father-in-law to mount an expedition to restore him. Seven champions, including Adrastus, Tydeus, Capaneus, and Amphiaraus, led the army of the Seven against Thebes.

The expedition failed disastrously: all the seven champions died except Adrastus. Eteocles and Polynices met in single combat and killed each other. The new ruler, Creon, decreed that Eteocles should be buried with honour but that the body of Polynices, as a traitor who had attacked his own city, should be left unburied for the dogs and birds. Antigone defied the edict and performed burial rites for her brother, an act for which she was condemned to death — the central conflict of Sophocles' play and one of the most enduring episodes in Greek tragedy.

Polynices' son Thersander later joined the Epigoni, the sons of the Seven, in their successful second assault on Thebes.

In literature

Polynices appears in Aeschylus's Seven Against Thebes (467 BCE), Sophocles' Antigone and Oedipus at Colonus, Euripides' The Phoenician Women, and Statius's Latin epic the Thebaid. In Dante's Inferno, the mutual hatred of the brothers is evoked among the sowers of discord, and their father's story frames the flame of Ulysses and Diomedes. The quarrel of Eteocles and Polynices was also a stock example in ancient rhetoric of unjust war between kin.
