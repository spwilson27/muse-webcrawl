Erdős–Kac theorem

The Erdős–Kac theorem in number theory, named after Paul Erdős and Mark Kac, establishes that the number of distinct prime factors of a natural number has a normal distribution when suitably normalized. Proved in 1940, it is regarded as a founding result of probabilistic number theory, because it shows that arithmetic functions can behave like sums of independent random variables.

Statement

Let ω(n) denote the number of distinct prime divisors of n. For n chosen uniformly from the integers up to N, ω(n) has mean approximately log log N and variance approximately log log N. The Erdős–Kac theorem states that, as N → ∞, the distribution of the normalized quantity (ω(n) − log log N) / √(log log N) converges to the standard normal distribution. In other words, log log N acts like both the expected value and the variance, much as in the central limit theorem for a sum of independent Bernoulli variables.

History

Erdős and Kac announced the result in 1939 and published the proof in 1940. Their argument made essential use of the sieve of Eratosthenes and moment computations, linking the distribution of prime divisors to the behavior of random variables. The theorem was later generalized: for a wide class of strongly additive arithmetic functions, the limiting distribution is normal provided a certain variance condition holds, a refinement due to Erdős, Kac, and others.

Significance

The theorem provided the first rigorous bridge between classical number theory and probability theory. It motivated the development of probabilistic number theory, including results of Turán, Kubilius, and the later work on mean values of arithmetic functions. The typical size of ω(n) is log log n, so a "random" integer near N has about log log N distinct prime factors with Gaussian fluctuations.
