Glycobiology


A branched N-linked glycan, the kind of structure studied in glycobiology.

Glycobiology, in its broadest sense, is the study of the structure, biosynthesis, biology and evolution of saccharides — sugar chains called glycans — that are widely distributed in nature. It combines carbohydrate chemistry, enzymology, molecular and cell biology, and the study of glycan-binding proteins to understand how glycans mediate biological recognition.

Scope

Glycans occur free or attached to proteins (glycoproteins and proteoglycans) and lipids (glycolipids). They coat the surface of every cell in a dense layer called the glycocalyx and are the most abundant and structurally diverse class of biopolymers. Unlike DNA and proteins, glycans are not directly template-encoded: their structures are determined by the coordinated action of glycosyltransferases and glycosidases in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, making them sensitive readouts of a cell's physiological state. Major classes include N-linked and O-linked glycans, glycosaminoglycans such as heparin and hyaluronan, and the glycan head-groups of glycosphingolipids.

Biological roles

Glycan–protein interactions, mediated by lectins, govern cell–cell recognition, immune surveillance, pathogen attachment (influenza viruses, for example, bind sialic acid), fertilisation, and the trafficking and half-life of proteins. The ABO blood group antigens are glycans. Altered glycosylation is a hallmark of cancer cells, and inherited congenital disorders of glycosylation demonstrate the pathways' essential functions. Modern glycobiology employs mass spectrometry, glycan microarrays and chemoenzymatic synthesis, and it underpins the engineering of therapeutic antibodies and the production of glycoprotein drugs whose efficacy depends on their glycan structures.
