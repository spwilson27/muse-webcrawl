Hung Ga

Hung Ga (洪家), also known as Hung Ga Kuen (洪家拳) or Hung Gar, is a southern Chinese martial art, one of the "five great schools" of southern China and a member of the Southern Shaolin family of styles. It is strongly associated with the legendary folk hero Wong Fei-hung, whose father Wong Kei-ying was counted among the Ten Tigers of Canton.

History

Hung Ga traces its origins to the 17th-century Shaolin monk Jee Sin (Gee Sim), said to have survived the burning of the Shaolin Monastery and taught the style at the southern Shaolin temple in Fujian. The founder of the Hung family style is generally identified as Hung Hei-gun (洪熙官, 1745–1825), a tea merchant who trained under Jee Sin and combined Shaolin techniques with elements learned from his wife, Fong Wing-chun, a crane-style expert. The style developed in Guangdong province and became one of the most popular martial arts of southern China. The lineage was later popularized worldwide by Lam Sai-wing, whose books and photographs codified the forms, and in the 20th century by the Kwan Tak-hing film series about Wong Fei-hung.

Characteristics

Hung Ga is famous for its deep, low stances — especially the horse stance (sei ping ma) — strong forearms developed through "bridge arm" conditioning, and hard-hitting hand techniques. Its core empty-hand forms include Gung Ji Fook Fu Kuen (Taming the Tiger), Fu Hok Seung Ying Kuen (Tiger and Crane Double Form), Sei Ying Kuen / Ng Ying Kuen (Five Animal Form), and Tit Sin Kuen ("Iron Wire Fist"), the most advanced form, which develops internal strength (qi) through dynamic tension and breathing exercises. Weapons forms include the butterfly swords, pole, broadsword, and kwan dao. The system's twelve bridges (splintering hands) summarize its hand techniques: hard, soft, pressing, straight, dividing, stabilizing, inch, lifting, flowing/delivering, controlling, setting, and intercepting.
