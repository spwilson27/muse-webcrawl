The Oseberg ship (Norwegian: Osebergskipet) is a well-preserved Viking ship discovered in a large burial mound at the Oseberg farm near Tønsberg in Vestfold county, Norway. This ship is commonly acknowledged to be among the finest artifacts to have survived from the Viking Era. The ship and some of its contents are displayed at the Viking Ship House at Bygdøy on the western side of Oslo, Norway (moving to the new Museum of the Viking Age).

Discovery and excavation

The mound was excavated in 1904 under the direction of Gabriel Gustafson, with Haakon Shetelig leading much of the work. The ship had been buried in 834 in blue clay, which preserved the oak timbers extraordinarily well. Grave robbers had broken into the burial chamber in antiquity and removed precious metals, but the ship, its carved fittings, and most grave goods survived. Excavation and conservation took years; the ship was reconstructed from thousands of fragments.

The ship and burial

The vessel is a graceful karvi-type ship, 21.58 m long and 5.1 m wide, with 12 pairs of oars and a side rudder. Its prow ends in an elegant spiral rather than a dragon head, and the hull and fittings carry carving in an early Viking style that takes its name — the "Oseberg style" — from this find. Buried with the ship were two women: an older woman of about 80, long identified (speculatively) with Queen Åsa, mother of Halfdan the Black, and a younger woman of about 30, possibly a high-ranking attendant. Grave goods included sledges, a richly carved cart, beds, tapestries (the Oseberg tapestry fragments), household equipment, and the skeletons of 15 horses, an ox, and several dogs.

Significance

Together with the Gokstad and Tune ships, the Oseberg find transformed scholarly understanding of Viking shipbuilding, art, and burial custom. The Oseberg style — gripping beasts and interlace carved in shallow relief — became the first phase in the standard sequence of Viking art styles.
