The East–West Schism, also known as the Great Schism or the Schism of 1054, is the break of communion between the Roman Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church, which has remained in place ever since. The schism was the culmination of theological and political differences that had developed during the preceding centuries between the Christian East and West.

Causes

Long-standing disputes included the Filioque clause added in the West to the Nicene Creed, the authority and primacy of the Bishop of Rome, differing practices such as the use of unleavened bread in the Eucharist and clerical celibacy, and the growing linguistic and cultural divide between the Latin West and the Greek East.

The events of 1054

In 1054 a delegation from Pope Leo IX, led by Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida, travelled to Constantinople. On 16 July 1054 Humbert placed a bull of excommunication against Patriarch Michael I Cerularius on the altar of Hagia Sophia. Cerularius responded by excommunicating the legates. Although the mutual excommunications applied formally only to individuals, they symbolised a definitive rupture.

Aftermath

The sack of Constantinople by Western crusaders in 1204 deepened the estrangement, and reunion councils at Lyon (1274) and Florence (1438–45) failed to heal it. In 1965 Pope Paul VI and Ecumenical Patriarch Athenagoras I lifted the mutual excommunications, but full communion between the churches has not been restored.
