Möbius strip

Möbius strip
Discovered1858, independently by August Ferdinand Möbius and Johann Benedict Listing
PropertiesOne side, one boundary component; non-orientable
Euler characteristic0

The Möbius strip, or Möbius band, is a surface that can be formed by attaching the ends of a strip of paper together with a half-twist. As a mathematical object, it was discovered by August Ferdinand Möbius and Johann Benedict Listing in 1858, though it had appeared in Roman mosaics centuries earlier.

Properties

The strip is the canonical example of a non-orientable surface: it has only one side and one edge. An ant walking around the band returns to its starting point mirrored, having traversed both "sides" without ever crossing an edge. Cutting a Möbius strip along its centre line yields, surprisingly, a single longer strip with a full twist rather than two separate bands; cutting a third of the way in produces two linked strips.

Applications and culture

Möbius geometry appears in conveyor belts designed to wear evenly, electronic resistors, and molecular chemistry — chemists have synthesized Möbius aromatic molecules. The recycling symbol's twisted-loop form is inspired by the strip, and M. C. Escher famously depicted ants traversing one in Möbius Strip II. In topology, the strip is the total space of the non-trivial line bundle over the circle, and gluing a disc to its boundary yields the real projective plane.
