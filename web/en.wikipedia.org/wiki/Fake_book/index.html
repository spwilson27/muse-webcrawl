Fake book

A fake book of lead sheets

A fake book is a collection of musical lead sheets intended to help a performer learn new songs quickly and "fake" an accompaniment. Each lead sheet gives the essentials of a song — melody line, chord symbols and sometimes lyrics — typically condensed onto a single page, in contrast to a full score or arrangement.

History

Fake books emerged in the mid-20th century for working dance-band and jazz musicians. The earliest famous example was the collection of "Tune-Dex" cards and bootleg volumes circulating from the 1940s. Because most early fake books reproduced copyrighted songs without licences or royalties, they were illegal and sold semi-clandestinely; the FBI investigated fake-book rings, and musicians referred to the books discreetly.

The Real Book

The most influential fake book is The Real Book, compiled in the 1970s by students at the Berklee College of Music in Boston, which gathered lead sheets for jazz standards and compositions by modern jazz composers. Widely copied for decades as an underground publication, it was eventually issued legally by Hal Leonard in 2004 in a corrected, licensed sixth edition. Numerous genre-specific fake books — standards, Latin, blues, Broadway, folk — are now published legally, and digital successors such as tablet apps and the iReal Pro format have largely replaced printed volumes among gigging musicians.
