William Stokoe

William C. Stokoe Jr.
Born: 21 July 1919, Lancaster, New Hampshire
Died: 4 April 2000 (aged 80)
Known for: Linguistics of American Sign Language

William C. Stokoe Jr. (July 21, 1919 – April 4, 2000) was an American linguist and a long-time professor at Gallaudet University. His research on American Sign Language (ASL) revolutionized understanding of sign languages: at a time when ASL was widely dismissed as a pantomime or a simplified manual code for English, Stokoe demonstrated that it is a complete, natural language with its own phonology, morphology, and syntax.

Research

Stokoe arrived at Gallaudet in 1955 to teach English and Chaucer. Observing his deaf students signing among themselves, he began to analyze ASL using the methods of structural linguistics, identifying its sublexical building blocks—handshape, location, and movement—which he termed "cheremes" in analogy to phonemes. His 1960 monograph Sign Language Structure and the 1965 Dictionary of American Sign Language on Linguistic Principles, co-authored with Dorothy Casterline and Carl Croneberg, laid the foundation of sign language linguistics.

Impact

Initially resisted by both linguists and educators of the deaf, Stokoe's work eventually transformed deaf education, linguistics, and public attitudes, contributing to the recognition of sign languages worldwide and to the cultural self-confidence of the Deaf community. He founded the journal Sign Language Studies and the Linstok Press to promote the field.
