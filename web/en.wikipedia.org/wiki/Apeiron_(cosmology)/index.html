Apeiron (cosmology)

The apeiron (Greek: ἄπειρον, "unlimited", "boundless", "indefinite") is a cosmological concept proposed by the pre-Socratic philosopher Anaximander of Miletus (c. 610 – c. 546 BC). Anaximander held that the origin and first principle (archē) of everything that exists is not any definite element such as water or air, but an indefinite, ageless, indestructible substance — the apeiron — from which all things arise and to which they return.

Anaximander's doctrine

According to Aristotle and Theophrastus, Anaximander reasoned that if the first principle were one of the known elements it would overwhelm the others; the source must therefore be something neutral and without limits. From the apeiron the opposites — hot and cold, wet and dry — were separated out, giving rise to the ordered cosmos, the heavenly bodies and innumerable worlds. Things perish back into the apeiron "according to necessity", paying "penalty and retribution to each other for their injustice according to the assessment of Time", in the earliest surviving fragment of Western philosophy.

Interpretation

Scholars debate whether Anaximander conceived the apeiron as spatially infinite, temporally eternal, qualitatively indeterminate, or all three. The concept is a landmark in the move from mythological to abstract, naturalistic explanation, and it influenced later thinkers from Anaximenes to Plato and Aristotle, who discussed it at length in the Physics.
