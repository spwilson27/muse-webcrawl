Displaced persons camps in post–World War II Europe

Displaced persons camps were temporary camps established by the Allied authorities in Germany, Austria and Italy at the end of World War II to house the millions of displaced persons (DPs) found in Europe: liberated concentration camp prisoners, former forced labourers, prisoners of war, and refugees fleeing westward. An estimated 11 million people had been displaced outside their home countries by the German war machine, the great majority of whom were repatriated by the end of 1945.

Background

Planning for the displaced population began before the war ended, with the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration (UNRRA) founded in 1943 to administer relief. Supreme Headquarters Allied Expeditionary Force (SHAEF) made the military commands responsible for DP operations in their zones of occupation.

Population

After the first great wave of repatriation in 1945, roughly one million DPs remained in the western zones: Poles, Balts (Estonians, Latvians, Lithuanians), Ukrainians, Yugoslavs, and others who refused to return to Soviet-controlled homelands, together with some 200,000–250,000 Jewish survivors — many of them Polish Jews who had fled renewed antisemitic violence such as the Kielce pogrom of 1946 and entered the camps from the east (the Beriha movement).

Life in the camps

Camps were frequently located in former concentration camps, military barracks, schools, and apartment blocks. Jewish DPs organized an active communal life through the Central Committee of Liberated Jews, publishing newspapers, running schools and kibbutzim training for emigration to Palestine, and founding sports clubs, orchestras, and theatres. The largest Jewish DP camp was Bergen-Belsen; other major camps included Föhrenwald, Landsberg am Lech, Feldafing, and Zeilsheim.

Resettlement and closure

From 1947 the International Refugee Organization (IRO) took over from UNRRA and shifted policy from repatriation to resettlement overseas. The United States Displaced Persons Act of 1948 admitted some 200,000 DPs (later amended to about 400,000); other major destinations included Canada, Australia, the United Kingdom, and, after 1948, the new State of Israel, which received most Jewish DPs. By 1952 nearly all camps had closed; Föhrenwald, the last, closed in 1957.
