Luddite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Luddites were members of a 19th-century movement of English textile workers who opposed the use of certain types of cost-saving machinery, often by destroying the machines in organised raids, because they considered the machines a threat to their jobs and way of life. The movement arose during the harsh economic climate of the Napoleonic Wars and culminated in a region-wide rebellion that lasted from 1811 to 1816.

Mill and factory owners took to shooting protesters, and the movement was ultimately suppressed by legal and military force, including execution and penal transportation of accused and convicted Luddites. The word "Luddite" has since come to describe anyone opposed to technological change or new technology in general; the term neo-Luddism is applied to modern movements critical of technology.

Origins

The movement was said to have been named after Ned Ludd, an apprentice who allegedly smashed two stocking frames in 1779 in a "fit of passion"; the story may be apocryphal. The apocryphal "General Ludd" or "King Ludd" became a figurehead whose name was signed to threatening letters sent to mill owners, warning them to remove labour-replacing machinery or face destruction of their property.

The disturbances

Luddite activity began in Nottinghamshire in March 1811, directed against wide stocking frames that produced cheap, inferior goods and undercut skilled workers. The unrest spread to the woollen districts of Yorkshire, where croppers attacked gig mills and shearing frames, and to the cotton mills of Lancashire. In April 1812, an attack on Rawfolds Mill near Huddersfield was repelled by its owner William Cartwright, and the assassination of mill owner William Horsfall later that month intensified the government response.

Government response and trials

Parliament passed the Frame Breaking Act in 1812, making machine-breaking a capital offence, and deployed thousands of troops to the affected districts — at one point more soldiers than Wellington had under his command in the early Peninsular War. Mass trials at York in January 1813 led to seventeen executions and several transportations, effectively breaking the movement, although sporadic Luddite activity continued until about 1816.

Legacy

Historians such as E. P. Thompson have argued that the Luddites were not blind opponents of progress but skilled workers resisting the loss of customary rights, wages and status brought by unregulated industrial capitalism. The term remains in everyday use for technophobes, and the Luddites are frequently invoked in modern debates about automation and artificial intelligence displacing workers.
