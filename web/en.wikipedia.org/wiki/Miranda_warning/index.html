Miranda warning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Miranda warning is a type of notification customarily given by police to criminal suspects in police custody in the United States, advising them of their right to silence and to counsel. It derives from Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), which held that statements obtained from a suspect subjected to custodial interrogation are inadmissible unless the state demonstrates the use of procedural safeguards — the warnings — securing the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination.

Content and law

The familiar formulation advises: you have the right to remain silent; anything you say can be used against you in court; you have the right to an attorney; if you cannot afford an attorney, one will be provided. No canonical wording is required, so long as the rights are reasonably conveyed; invocation must be unambiguous, and exceptions exist (public-safety questions, New York v. Quarles, 1984; routine booking questions). Statements taken in violation trigger the exclusionary rule for the prosecution's case-in-chief (with impeachment and derivative-evidence doctrines litigated ever since). Congress's attempt to overrule Miranda by statute was itself struck down in Dickerson v. United States (2000), holding Miranda a constitutional rule. The warning is among the most globally recognised artefacts of American law — and a template several other systems have adapted to their own caution formulas.
