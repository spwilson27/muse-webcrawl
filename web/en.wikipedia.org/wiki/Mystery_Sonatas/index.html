Mystery Sonatas

The Mystery Sonatas (also called the Rosary Sonatas, German: Rosenkranzsonaten) are a collection of sixteen sonatas for violin and basso continuo by the Bohemian-Austrian composer Heinrich Ignaz Franz Biber, composed around 1676 but not published until 1905. The sonatas are renowned for their extensive use of scordatura — each sonata after the first requires the violin to be retuned to a different, unusual tuning.

Each sonata in the surviving manuscript is prefaced by a copperplate engraving depicting a mystery of the Rosary.

Structure

The fifteen numbered sonatas correspond to the fifteen Mysteries of the Rosary: five Joyful Mysteries (the Annunciation, the Visitation, the Nativity, the Presentation, and the Finding in the Temple), five Sorrowful Mysteries (the Agony in the Garden, the Scourging, the Crowning with Thorns, the Carrying of the Cross, and the Crucifixion), and five Glorious Mysteries (the Resurrection, the Ascension, Pentecost, the Assumption, and the Coronation of the Virgin). A final, unnumbered Passacaglia in G minor for solo violin — the "Guardian Angel" — concludes the collection; its 65 repetitions of a descending four-note ground bass make it one of the earliest and greatest works of its kind.

Scordatura

Only the first sonata and the Passacaglia use the standard tuning. The other sonatas retune the violin's strings — sometimes drastically, in the "Resurrection" sonata the two middle strings are crossed — to produce particular resonances, open-string sonorities, and symbolic sonorities appropriate to each mystery. The notation is written as fingered (sounding differently from what is played), which makes the set technically and mentally demanding for performers.

History and dedication

The surviving manuscript, preserved in the Bavarian State Library in Munich, suggests the sonatas were dedicated to Biber's employer, Maximilian Gandolph von Khuenburg, Prince-Archbishop of Salzburg, a centre of Rosary devotion. One engraved image for each mystery faces the corresponding sonata in the manuscript. Although unpublished during Biber's lifetime, the sonatas have become among the most admired works of the Baroque violin repertoire and are frequently recorded complete.
