Growth mindset

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A growth mindset is an implicit belief that one's abilities and intelligence can be developed through dedication, effort, and learning. The concept was developed by Stanford psychologist Carol Dweck, who contrasted it with a fixed mindset — the belief that intelligence and talent are innate traits that cannot change much. Dweck popularised the framework in her 2006 book Mindset: The New Psychology of Success.

Research

In Dweck's studies, students who believed intelligence was malleable were more likely to embrace challenges, persist after setbacks, and interpret failure as information rather than as evidence of low ability. A well-known 1998 experiment by Mueller and Dweck found that children praised for intelligence ("you must be smart") after success later avoided difficult tasks and performed worse after failure than children praised for effort ("you must have worked hard"). Mindset interventions typically teach students about neuroplasticity — that the brain forms new connections with practice.

Effects and criticism

Meta-analyses have found the average effect of growth mindset interventions on achievement to be small, though effects may be larger for low-achieving and disadvantaged students. A large 2019 national study in the United States (Yeager and colleagues, published in Nature) found a brief online intervention improved grades among lower-achieving students and increased enrolment in advanced mathematics. Critics caution against oversimplified popular versions of the theory, note replication difficulties, and warn that emphasising mindset can divert attention from structural barriers. Dweck herself has warned against a "false growth mindset" — merely praising effort without effective strategies.
