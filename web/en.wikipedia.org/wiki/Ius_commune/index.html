Ius commune

Ius commune or jus commune is Latin for "common law" in certain jurisdictions. It is, or historically was, the common law across a number of legal systems, most notably the law of continental Europe from the High Middle Ages until its displacement by national codifications.

History

The ius commune developed from the rediscovery and scholarly study of Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis at the University of Bologna from the late 11th century, combined with canon law. Glossators and later commentators such as Bartolus of Saxoferrato systematized Roman law and adapted it to contemporary practice.

Reception

Received to varying degrees across Europe, the ius commune served as a subsidiary law where local statutes and customs were silent, and supplied a shared legal vocabulary and method for jurists. Its influence was particularly strong in Germany, where the reception was consolidated with the establishment of the Reichskammergericht in 1495.

Decline

From the 18th century, natural-law codifications and later national codes — such as the French Civil Code of 1804 and the German BGB of 1900 — replaced the ius commune as a directly applicable body of law, though its concepts continue to shape European private law.
