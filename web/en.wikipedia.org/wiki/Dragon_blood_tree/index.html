The dragon blood tree (Dracaena cinnabari) is a distinctive umbrella-shaped tree endemic to the Socotra archipelago in the Arabian Sea, part of Yemen. It is named for its dark red resin — "dragon's blood" — which exudes from wounds in its bark and has been traded since antiquity as a dye, varnish, medicine and incense. The tree is an icon of Socotra, one of the most isolated and biologically unique island groups on Earth, and appears on Yemeni currency.

Description

Unlike most monocots, Dracaena species undergo secondary thickening, producing a genuine woody trunk. The dragon blood tree branches dichotomously, each branch repeatedly forking after flowering to build the dense, mushroom-like canopy — an adaptation thought to harvest moisture from mist, drizzle and dew and shade the ground beneath in Socotra's arid limestone plateaus. The stiff, sword-shaped leaves cluster at branch tips and are shed and replaced every three to four years. Whitish flowers appear in panicles around February, followed by fleshy orange-red berries containing one to three seeds, eaten and dispersed by birds such as the Socotra starling.

Dragon's blood

The crimson resin was prized by the Romans, mentioned by Dioscorides and the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, and carried via Arabian trade routes to Europe, where it served as a pigment and varnish (including, reputedly, for Cremonese violins), a styptic and astringent in folk medicine, and a colourant for toothpastes and tinctures. Resin is still harvested on Socotra by local people, who cut the bark and collect the hardened "tears" the following year. Other "dragon's blood" products come from unrelated plants, including Daemonorops rattans and South American Croton species.

Conservation

The IUCN lists the species as Vulnerable. Regeneration is poor across much of its range: seedlings are browsed by goats, the mist and dew inputs the adult trees depend on may be declining with regional climate change, and cyclones in 2015 uprooted many mature trees. Modelled projections suggest major contractions of suitable habitat by 2080. Fenced nurseries and protected areas in the Dixam plateau and Firmihin forest — the densest remaining stand — aim to secure the next generation of this Tertiary relict species.
