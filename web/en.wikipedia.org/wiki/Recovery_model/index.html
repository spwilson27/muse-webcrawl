Recovery model

The recovery model, also called the recovery approach, is an approach to mental disorder and substance use disorder that emphasises supporting a person in building a meaningful life for themselves, whether or not their symptoms fully remit. It emerged from the psychiatric survivor and consumer movements of the 1970s–1990s and has been adopted as guiding policy for mental health services in many countries, including the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia and New Zealand.

Principles

Rather than defining recovery solely as the absence of symptoms (clinical recovery), the model focuses on personal recovery: a process, described by researcher William Anthony in 1993 as "a deeply personal, unique process of changing one's attitudes, values, feelings, goals, skills and/or roles." Commonly cited elements, summarised in the CHIME framework, are Connectedness, Hope and optimism, Identity, Meaning in life, and Empowerment. Hope and self-determination are regarded as foundational; services aim to be person-centred, strengths-based and peer-supported.

Applications

Practical expressions of the model include peer support workers employed within clinical teams, recovery colleges offering co-produced education, Wellness Recovery Action Plans (WRAP) developed by Mary Ellen Copeland, and supported employment and housing-first programmes. In addiction services, recovery-oriented systems of care extend the model beyond acute treatment to long-term community support.

Criticism

Critics argue that "recovery" can be co-opted by services to justify funding cuts or the withdrawal of support, that it may understate the severity of conditions that require long-term treatment, and that its vagueness makes outcomes hard to measure. Proponents respond that the model challenges low expectations and institutional pessimism rather than denying the reality of illness.
