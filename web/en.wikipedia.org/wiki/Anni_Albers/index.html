Anni Albers

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anni Albers (born Annelise Elsa Frieda Fleischmann; 12 June 1899 – 9 May 1994) was a German-American textile artist and printmaker. Trained at the Bauhaus, where she later taught, she is regarded as one of the most influential textile artists of the twentieth century, credited with elevating weaving to the status of a modern art form and with pioneering the idea of textiles as a flexible form of architecture.

Early life and Bauhaus

Albers was born in Berlin and studied art before enrolling at the Bauhaus in Weimar in 1922. Like most women students, she was channelled into the weaving workshop, where she developed a rigorous, geometric approach to woven structure. She married fellow Bauhaus artist Josef Albers in 1925. Her diploma work was a sound-absorbing, light-reflecting wall hanging for an auditorium. In 1931 she became head of the weaving workshop at the Bauhaus in Dessau, one of the few women to hold such a post.

Career in the United States

After the Nazis closed the Bauhaus in 1933, the Alberses emigrated to the United States and joined the faculty of Black Mountain College in North Carolina, where Anni taught weaving and textile design until 1949. In 1949 she became the first textile artist to receive a solo exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art in New York. She later worked from Connecticut, travelled extensively in Mexico and South America — whose ancient textiles deeply influenced her — and in her later years concentrated on printmaking.

Writings and legacy

Her books On Designing (1959) and On Weaving (1965) remain foundational texts in textile and design education. Her characteristic works combine grid structures, interlaced threads in restrained palettes, and an experimental curiosity about materials, including cellophane and synthetic fibres. Major retrospectives, including at Tate Modern in 2018, have confirmed her standing as a central figure of modernism.
