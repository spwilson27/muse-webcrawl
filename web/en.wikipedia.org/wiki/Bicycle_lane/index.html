Bicycle lanes (bike lanes) are a type of bikeway (cycleway) with lanes on the roadway for cyclists only. In the United Kingdom, a cycle lane is a lane marked on the carriageway for use by cyclists, sometimes with legal exclusivity. Bicycle lanes are usually separated from motorised traffic by a painted line, although physically protected variants — using kerbs, bollards, or parked cars — are commonly called protected bike lanes or cycle tracks.

Design

Lanes are typically designated by a solid or dashed white line, a bicycle symbol, and directional arrows. Design guidelines, such as those from NACTO in the United States and the Dutch CROW manual, recommend widths of roughly 1.5–2 metres, with additional buffer space beside parked cars to reduce "dooring" collisions. Coloured surfacing — often green in the US and red in the Netherlands — is frequently applied at intersections and conflict points. Contraflow lanes allow cyclists to travel against one-way motor traffic on designated streets.

Safety and debate

Research generally finds that marked lanes reduce cyclist injuries compared with unmarked roads, and that protected lanes offer further reductions, though results vary by design and context. Some vehicular-cycling advocates have historically argued that separate lanes can increase turning conflicts at junctions; mainstream transport planning now generally treats continuous, protected networks as the key to increasing cycling rates and safety, as in Dutch and Danish cities where modal share for cycling is high.

Legal status

Rules differ by jurisdiction: motor vehicles are generally prohibited from driving or parking in mandatory cycle lanes, while advisory lanes may be entered when necessary. E-scooters and powered mobility devices may or may not be permitted, depending on local law.
