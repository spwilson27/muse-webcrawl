Integral

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The area under a curve shaded between two vertical bounds on an axis]

In mathematics, an integral assigns a number to a function that can be interpreted as an area under its graph, an accumulated quantity, or the reverse of differentiation. The two main types are the definite integral, computed over an interval, and the indefinite integral, or antiderivative.

The fundamental theorem of calculus — the subject's centrepiece — states that differentiation and integration are inverse operations, linking the derivative and the integral. Newton and Leibniz both grasped the connection in the 17th century; Bernhard Riemann put the definite integral on a rigorous footing in the 19th.

Computation

Integrals are approximated numerically by sums (the trapezoid and Simpson rules) and computed exactly by substitution, integration by parts, and tables. The Lebesgue integral generalises Riemann's to handle wilder functions and underpins modern probability and analysis.

Applications range from volumes and arc lengths to work, probability masses over intervals, and the solutions of differential equations.
