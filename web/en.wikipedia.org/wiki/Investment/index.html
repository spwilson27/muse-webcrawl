Investment

[Image: a steadily rising line graph of a long-term investment portfolio on a tablet screen]

Investment is the allocation of money (or other resources) with the expectation of future benefit — income, appreciation or both. In finance it means buying assets: shares, bonds, property, or funds that hold them; in Economics it also means firms' spending on plant, machinery and buildings that adds to the capital stock and future output (the I in the GDP identity).

Risk, return and strategy

Investment returns compensate for risk, illiquidity and time: expected return rises along a risk spectrum from insured deposits and government bonds to corporate bonds, equities, Private equity and Venture capital. Modern portfolio theory (Markowitz, 1952) shows diversification can reduce risk for a given return, underpinning the rise of diversified funds and low-cost index investing championed by John Bogle. Investors divide into long-term savers (pension funds, insurers), active managers seeking to beat the market, and speculators trading on short horizons — a distinction John Maynard Keynes drew sharply, warning that markets can become a 'casino'. Compound growth of returns, fees, tax treatment and Inflation together determine real outcomes; investment differs from saving (holding safe liquid assets) chiefly in the risk deliberately accepted.
