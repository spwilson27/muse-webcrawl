Catalan's conjecture (aliquot sequences)

In number theory, Catalan's conjecture on aliquot sequences (not to be confused with the Catalan conjecture on consecutive powers, now Mihăilescu's theorem) states that every aliquot sequence eventually terminates with a prime number, a perfect number or a set of amicable or sociable numbers. The conjecture is named after the Belgian mathematician Eugène Charles Catalan, who stated it in 1888.

Aliquot sequences

An aliquot sequence is generated by repeatedly replacing a positive integer by the sum of its proper divisors. For example, starting from 12: 12 → 16 → 15 → 9 → 4 → 3 → 1 → 0. A sequence may terminate at 1, enter a cycle of length 1 (a perfect number, e.g. 6 or 28), a cycle of length 2 (an amicable pair, e.g. 220 and 284), or a longer cycle (a sociable cycle). Catalan conjectured that no aliquot sequence increases indefinitely.

Status

The conjecture remains unproven and undisproven. Extensive computation has shown that most starting values below large bounds resolve as predicted, but the fate of a handful of stubborn sequences — famously those beginning at 276, 552, 564, 660 and 966 (the so-called "Lehmer five") — is unknown despite being computed to hundreds of digits. Such sequences could, in principle, grow forever, settle into cycles, or terminate after enormous length, and resolving them is a popular target for distributed computing projects.
