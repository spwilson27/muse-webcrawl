Tel Aviv-Yafo, usually called simply Tel Aviv, is a city on Israel's Mediterranean coast and the country's economic and technological centre. It was founded in 1909 by members of the Jewish community of neighbouring Jaffa, who purchased sand dunes north of the ancient port and divided them by lottery — a scene captured in the famous photograph of the founders standing in the dunes.

History

Named after Theodor Herzl's novel Altneuland (in its Hebrew translation, Tel Aviv — "Hill of Spring"), the garden suburb grew rapidly with waves of immigration, becoming a municipality under the British Mandate. After the 1948 war and the founding of Israel, Tel Aviv and Jaffa were united in 1950. Jerusalem is Israel's capital and seat of government, but most foreign embassies historically remained in Tel Aviv.

Architecture and culture

The "White City" preserves some 4,000 buildings in the International Style, designed in the 1930s by German-Jewish architects influenced by the Bauhaus, and was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2003. The city is known for its beaches, the Carmel Market, Rothschild Boulevard, a major start-up economy ("Silicon Wadi"), and a famously liberal, round-the-clock cultural life.

Geography

Tel Aviv sits on the coastal plain at the mouth of the Yarkon River, with a warm Mediterranean climate. It is the core of the Gush Dan metropolitan area, Israel's largest.
