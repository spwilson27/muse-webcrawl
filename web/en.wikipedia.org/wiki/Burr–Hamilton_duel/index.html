Burr–Hamilton duel

The Burr–Hamilton duel took place in Weehawken, New Jersey, on July 11, 1804, between Aaron Burr, the sitting Vice President of the United States, and Alexander Hamilton, the former Secretary of the Treasury. Burr shot Hamilton, who died the following day. The duel ended Hamilton's life, destroyed Burr's political career, and became one of the most famous episodes in early American history.

Background

Burr and Hamilton had been political rivals for more than a decade. Hamilton had thwarted Burr's ambitions repeatedly: in the deadlocked presidential election of 1800 he urged the House of Representatives to choose Thomas Jefferson over Burr, and in 1804 he opposed Burr's campaign for governor of New York. During that campaign, a letter by Dr. Charles D. Cooper was published reporting that Hamilton had expressed a "despicable opinion" of Burr at a dinner party. Burr demanded an apology or explanation under the code of honour; Hamilton's responses were evasive, and negotiations between their seconds failed to produce a settlement.

The duel

On the morning of July 11, 1804, the parties rowed across the Hudson River to the duelling grounds at Weehawken — the same spot where Hamilton's son Philip had been killed in a duel in 1801. Shots were exchanged. Hamilton fired first, by most accounts deliberately missing or firing into the air; his shot struck a tree above Burr's head. Burr's shot struck Hamilton in the lower abdomen, fracturing a rib and lodging in his spine after passing through his liver. Hamilton died the next afternoon in Manhattan.

Aftermath

Burr was indicted for murder in both New York and New Jersey but never tried; the New Jersey indictment was later quashed. He completed his term as vice president, even presiding over the Senate impeachment trial of Justice Samuel Chase, but his reputation never recovered, and he soon became entangled in the western intrigues known as the Burr conspiracy. Public revulsion at the killing also accelerated the decline of duelling in the northern United States.

Legacy

The duel has been retold countless times, most recently in the musical Hamilton (2015). The pistols used, owned by Hamilton's brother-in-law John Barker Church, survive and were long displayed by JPMorgan Chase.
