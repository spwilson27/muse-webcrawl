Antibiotic

[Image: a petri dish with bacterial colonies and paper discs, clear zones around the discs showing where the antibiotic inhibits growth]

An antibiotic is a type of antimicrobial substance active against Bacteria. It is the most important type of antibacterial agent for fighting bacterial infections, and antibiotic medications are widely used in the treatment and prevention of such infections. They may either kill or inhibit the growth of bacteria. Antibiotics are not effective against viruses — a persistent source of public misunderstanding — nor, in general, against fungi.

The modern era of antibiotics began with Alexander Fleming's discovery of penicillin in 1928, from the mould Penicillium, and its development into a medicine in the early 1940s by Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and their team — a breakthrough that transformed medicine and saved hundreds of millions of lives.

Use and resistance

Antibiotics are prescribed for infections such as pneumonia, tuberculosis, urinary tract infections, sepsis and many sexually transmitted infections, and are used prophylactically in surgery and chemotherapy. Penicillins, cephalosporins, macrolides, tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones are major classes, differing in spectrum and mechanism.

Antimicrobial resistance — bacteria evolving resistance through natural selection, accelerated by overuse in medicine and agriculture — is described by the World Health Organization as one of the greatest threats to global health; resistant infections are estimated to have been directly responsible for over a million deaths in 2019. Stewardship programmes and the search for new antibiotics (few new classes have reached the clinic in decades) are the main responses.
