Autopsy

An autopsy (also post-mortem examination, necropsy, or obduction) is a surgical procedure consisting of a thorough examination of a corpse by dissection to determine the cause, mode, and manner of death, or to evaluate disease or injury for research or education.

Types

Forensic autopsies are performed for legal purposes, often ordered by a coroner or medical examiner in cases of sudden, violent, or unexplained death. Clinical or academic autopsies are performed in hospitals with family consent to confirm diagnoses and improve medical knowledge. Autopsy rates have declined in many countries despite evidence they reveal major diagnostic discrepancies in a significant share of cases.

Procedure

The pathologist records external findings, then opens the body — commonly with a Y-shaped incision — examines organs in situ, removes and weighs them (en masse by the Rokitansky method or individually), takes tissue and fluid samples for histology and toxicology, and examines the brain. Findings are compiled into a report giving cause and manner of death: natural, accident, homicide, suicide, or undetermined.

History

Systematic human dissection advanced with Morgagni's clinicopathological correlations in the 18th century and Virchow's cellular pathology in the 19th. Modern alternatives include virtopsy — post-mortem CT and MRI imaging.
