Quality circle

A quality circle meeting on the shop floor.

A quality circle or quality control circle is a group of workers who do the same or similar work, meeting regularly to identify, analyse, and solve work-related problems. Normally small in size, the group is usually led by a supervisor or team leader and presents its solutions to management, which reviews and decides on implementation. Quality circles are a participative management technique within the wider framework of total quality management (TQM) and continuous improvement (kaizen).

History

Quality circles were developed in Japan in the early 1960s under the leadership of Kaoru Ishikawa and the Union of Japanese Scientists and Engineers (JUSE), drawing on the statistical quality control teachings of W. Edwards Deming and Joseph M. Juran. The first circles were registered in 1962, and the movement grew rapidly in Japanese industry, where circle members were trained in simple analytical tools such as Pareto charts, cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagrams, and histograms. The concept spread to the United States and other countries in the 1970s and 1980s, with mixed success, often failing where management support or worker training was lacking.

Characteristics

  • Voluntary participation by workers from the same work area
  • Small size — typically three to twelve members
  • Regular meetings, often one hour per week
  • Problems chosen by the circle itself, not assigned by management
  • Use of structured problem-solving and statistical tools
  • Presentation of solutions to management for approval
