Muscogee

The Muscogee, also known historically as the Creek, are a group of related Indigenous peoples of the Southeastern Woodlands of North America. Their historical confederacy — the Creek Confederacy — comprised towns speaking Muscogee and other languages (including Hitchiti, Alabama, and Koasati) across what is now Alabama, Georgia, and northern Florida. The name "Creek" was applied by British traders.

History

Muscogee towns were organized as autonomous talwa, bound by kinship, ceremony such as the Green Corn Ceremony (Busk), and alliance rather than a central government. In the early 19th century, conflict between traditionalist Red Sticks and accommodationist factions led to the Creek War (1813–1814), after which large land cessions were imposed. Under the Indian Removal Act, most Muscogee were forcibly removed to Indian Territory (now Oklahoma) in the 1830s on a journey remembered as their Trail of Tears; some Muscogee remained in the Southeast, including the ancestors of the Poarch Band of Creek Indians in Alabama.

Modern nations

Federally recognized Muscogee governments include the Muscogee (Creek) Nation in Oklahoma — one of the largest tribal nations in the United States, with its capital at Okmulgee — the Poarch Band of Creek Indians in Alabama, and several smaller Creek tribal towns and communities. The 2020 U.S. Supreme Court decision in McGirt v. Oklahoma affirmed that the Muscogee reservation in Oklahoma had never been disestablished.

Language and culture

The Muscogee language (Mvskoke) is a Muskogean language with ongoing revitalization efforts. Cultural life continues around ceremonial grounds, stomp dances, and clan and town identities maintained since before removal.
