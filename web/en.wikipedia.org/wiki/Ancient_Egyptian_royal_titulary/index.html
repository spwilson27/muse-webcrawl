Ancient Egyptian royal titulary

The royal titulary or royal protocol of an ancient Egyptian pharaoh was the standard set of names by which the king was known. By the Middle Kingdom (c. 2055–1650 BCE) the complete titulary consisted of five names: the Horus name, the Nebty name, the Golden Horus name, the prenomen (throne name), and the nomen (birth name). These "fivefold titulary" proclaimed the king's divine nature and his relationship to the gods.

The five names

  • Horus name — the oldest royal name, written inside a serekh, a stylised palace façade surmounted by the falcon of Horus, identifying the king as the earthly embodiment of that god.
  • Nebty name — the "Two Ladies" name, placing the king under the protection of Nekhbet, vulture goddess of Upper Egypt, and Wadjet, cobra goddess of Lower Egypt.
  • Golden Horus name — written with the falcon of Horus seated on the hieroglyph for gold (nebu); its precise significance is debated, but gold was associated with divinity and eternity.
  • Prenomen — the throne name assumed at accession, written in a cartouche and preceded by the title nswt-bjtj, "King of Upper and Lower Egypt" (literally, "He of the Sedge and the Bee"). It usually incorporated the name of the sun god Ra, as in Thutmose III's prenomen Menkheperre.
  • Nomen — the king's birth name, also in a cartouche, preceded by the title sꜣ Rꜥ, "Son of Ra", introduced in the Fourth Dynasty.

Use

The full titulary appeared in formal inscriptions, coronation proclamations, and temple reliefs. Modern Egyptological convention usually refers to pharaohs by their nomen (e.g. Ramesses, Thutmose), sometimes distinguishing kings of the same name by their prenomen. Queens and royal women had their own titles, and the titulary evolved across three millennia, with archaic forms retained long after their origins were obscure.
