Disorder of sex development

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Disorders of sex development (DSDs), also known as differences in sex development, are congenital conditions in which development of chromosomal, gonadal, or anatomical sex is atypical. The term was introduced in 2006 following the International Consensus Conference on Intersex, and it covers many of the conditions also described by the term intersex, though usage and preferred terminology remain debated among clinicians and affected people.

Classification

DSDs are commonly grouped by karyotype:

  • Sex chromosome DSD – including Turner syndrome (45,X) and Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY).
  • 46,XY DSD – including androgen insensitivity syndrome, 5-alpha-reductase deficiency, and disorders of gonadal development such as Swyer syndrome.
  • 46,XX DSD – most commonly congenital adrenal hyperplasia, which can virilise the external genitalia of a genetic female.

Causes

Sex development depends on a cascade of genetic and hormonal events. The SRY gene on the Y chromosome normally initiates testis development; the testes then produce testosterone and anti-Müllerian hormone. Variations in genes such as SRY, SOX9, NR5A1, and the androgen receptor, or in hormone synthesis and sensitivity, can alter this pathway at many points, producing a wide spectrum of presentations ranging from ambiguous genitalia at birth to infertility or delayed puberty in adolescence.

Management and debate

Management typically involves a multidisciplinary team and may include hormone therapy, surgery, and psychological support. Historically, early "normalising" genital surgery on infants was standard practice, but it has become controversial: intersex advocates and human rights bodies argue such irreversible procedures should be deferred until the person can consent, while some clinicians point to medical or psychosocial indications. The word "disorder" itself is contested, and "differences of sex development" is often preferred.
