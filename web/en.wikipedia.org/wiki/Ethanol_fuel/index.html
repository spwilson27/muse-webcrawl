Ethanol fuel

Ethanol fuel is ethyl alcohol used as a motor fuel, mainly as a biofuel additive for gasoline. It is produced by fermenting sugars from crops such as sugarcane, corn (maize), wheat and sugar beet, or from cellulosic biomass. Common blends include E10 (10% ethanol, standard in the United States), E15, and E85 (51–83% ethanol) for flex-fuel vehicles; Brazil, the world's second-largest producer, uses hydrous ethanol (E100) and a mandatory blend of about 27% in gasoline.

Production and use

The United States and Brazil together account for the great majority of world production — the US chiefly from corn, Brazil from sugarcane, which yields a much more favourable energy balance. Brazil's Proálcool programme, begun in 1975 in response to the oil crises, built the world's most mature ethanol fuel market, and most Brazilian cars are flex-fuel. Ethanol has about 30% less energy per litre than gasoline, so fuel economy falls as blend levels rise, partly offset by ethanol's high octane rating.

Environmental debate

Supporters argue that fuel ethanol reduces dependence on petroleum and can lower net carbon dioxide emissions because the feedstock absorbs CO2 while growing; sugarcane ethanol is typically credited with substantial savings. Critics point to land-use change, deforestation pressure, fertiliser and water use, competition with food crops ("food vs fuel"), and studies suggesting corn ethanol's climate benefit is modest once indirect land-use effects are counted. Advanced cellulosic ethanol, made from residues and grasses, aims to avoid these trade-offs but has been slow to reach scale. See also biofuel and ethanol.
