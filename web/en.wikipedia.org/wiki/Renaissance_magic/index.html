Renaissance magic

Renaissance magic refers to the revival and development of magical thought and practice in Europe during the Renaissance (roughly the 14th to 17th centuries), closely intertwined with Renaissance humanism, Neoplatonism, Hermeticism, and the emerging natural sciences.

History

The recovery of ancient texts, above all the Corpus Hermeticum translated by Marsilio Ficino in 1463 at the request of Cosimo de' Medici, transformed European attitudes toward magic. Ficino and his younger contemporary Giovanni Pico della Mirandola sought to reconcile magic with Christianity, distinguishing natural magic — the manipulation of hidden ("occult") sympathies in nature — from demonic magic, which they condemned.

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa's De occulta philosophia (1533) systematized Renaissance magical theory into three worlds — elemental, celestial, and intellectual — while Paracelsus applied magical and alchemical ideas to medicine. John Dee, astrologer to Elizabeth I, combined mathematics, angelic conversations, and alchemy. Giordano Bruno extended Hermetic ideas into cosmology before his execution in 1600.

Concepts

Central ideas included the doctrine of signatures, cosmic sympathy and antipathy, astrological influence, the power of images and talismans, and the belief that the magus could draw down celestial virtues into material objects. Renaissance magic blurred into early science: figures such as Dee and later Isaac Newton studied alchemy alongside mathematics and natural philosophy.

Decline

During the 17th century, the mechanical philosophy of Descartes and the experimental program of the Scientific Revolution progressively displaced magical explanations, though Hermetic and occult traditions persisted in Rosicrucianism and Freemasonry.
