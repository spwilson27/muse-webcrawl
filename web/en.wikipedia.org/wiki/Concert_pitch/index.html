Concert pitch

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Concert pitch is the pitch reference to which a group of musical instruments are tuned for a performance. It is most commonly defined by the frequency of the A above middle C: the modern international standard, A = 440 Hz, was adopted by treaty in 1939 and confirmed by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO 16) in 1955.

History

Historically, pitch varied enormously by place and era — by as much as a minor third. Surviving Baroque instruments and tuning references suggest values from below A = 392 Hz (French Baroque pitch) to above A = 460 Hz in some Northern Italian churches; Handel's tuning fork (1751) gives about A = 422.5 Hz. Through the 19th century, pitch tended to creep upward ("pitch inflation") as players sought a brighter sound, straining singers' voices. France legislated the diapason normal of A = 435 Hz in 1859, and an 1885 conference in Vienna adopted 435 Hz internationally, before the shift to 440 Hz in the 20th century.

Modern practice

Most ensembles tune to A = 440 Hz, but many symphony orchestras tune slightly sharp — A = 441–445 Hz is common in parts of Europe — for brilliance. Period-instrument ensembles often use A = 415 Hz for Baroque repertoire (approximately a semitone below 440) and A = 430 Hz for Classical repertoire. In an orchestra, the reference A is usually given by the principal oboist, the oboe's pitch being relatively stable and penetrating.

Transposing instruments

"Concert pitch" also names the actual sounding pitch as distinct from the written pitch of a transposing instrument: when a B♭ clarinet plays a written C, the concert pitch is B♭. Scores and rehearsal directions ("give me concert B♭") use the term in this sense.
