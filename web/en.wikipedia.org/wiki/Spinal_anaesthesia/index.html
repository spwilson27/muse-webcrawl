Spinal anaesthesia

Spinal anaesthesia (also called spinal block or subarachnoid block) is a form of regional anaesthesia involving the injection of a local anaesthetic into the subarachnoid space, generally through a fine needle, usually 9 cm (3.5 in) long. It allows the patient to remain awake, or to be lightly sedated, while the lower part of the body is rendered insensible to pain, and is widely used for surgery below the umbilicus, including caesarean sections, orthopaedic surgery on the lower limbs, and urological and gynaecological procedures.

Technique

The needle is inserted between the lumbar vertebrae, typically at the L3/L4 or L4/L5 interspace — below the termination of the spinal cord (the conus medullaris) to avoid cord injury — passing through the ligaments and the dura mater until cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flows back, confirming placement in the subarachnoid space. A small dose of local anaesthetic such as bupivacaine (often "heavy", i.e. hyperbaric, bupivacaine made denser than CSF with glucose) is injected, and its spread can be influenced by the patient's posture. Opioids such as fentanyl or morphine may be added to improve analgesia.

Effects and risks

Blockade of sympathetic fibres causes vasodilation and commonly hypotension, managed with fluids and vasopressors such as phenylephrine. Other complications include post-dural-puncture headache (from continued CSF leakage), urinary retention, nausea, and, rarely, high or total spinal block, infection, bleeding with spinal haematoma, or nerve injury. Contraindications include patient refusal, coagulopathy, infection at the insertion site, raised intracranial pressure, and severe hypovolaemia.

History

The first planned spinal anaesthesia was performed in 1898 by the German surgeon August Bier, who used cocaine on a patient and then, famously, on himself and his assistant, with both suffering severe post-dural-puncture headaches afterwards.
