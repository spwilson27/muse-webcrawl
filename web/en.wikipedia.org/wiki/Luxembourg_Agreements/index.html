The Luxembourg Agreements (German: Luxemburger Abkommen), formally the Reparations Agreement between Israel and the Federal Republic of Germany, were signed on 10 September 1952. Under the agreements, West Germany undertook to pay reparations to Israel and to the Conference on Jewish Material Claims Against Germany in recognition of the persecution and murder of Jews during the Holocaust and the costs of resettling survivors.

Terms

The Federal Republic agreed to pay Israel three billion Deutsche Marks over twelve to fourteen years, largely in goods and services — including ships, machinery, and raw materials — to support the absorption of hundreds of thousands of Jewish refugees. A further 450 million DM went to the Claims Conference for the relief and rehabilitation of survivors outside Israel. Two additional protocols addressed restitution and compensation legislation within Germany. Chancellor Konrad Adenauer pushed the agreements through the Bundestag despite opposition.

Reactions and legacy

In Israel the negotiations provoked fierce controversy. In January 1952, debate in the Knesset was accompanied by riots led by Menachem Begin's Herut movement, which opposed accepting "blood money" from Germany; the government of David Ben-Gurion prevailed, holding that the funds were vital to the young state's survival. The agreements were a milestone in German Wiedergutmachung ("making good again") and paved the way for diplomatic relations between West Germany and Israel, established in 1965.
