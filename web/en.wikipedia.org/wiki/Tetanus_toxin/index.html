Tetanus toxin, or tetanospasmin, is an extremely potent neurotoxin produced by the vegetative cells of Clostridium tetani in anaerobic conditions, causing the spastic paralysis of tetanus. It is among the most toxic substances known, with an estimated lethal dose for humans of less than 2.5 nanograms per kilogram of body weight, exceeded in potency only by the related botulinum toxins.

Structure

The toxin is synthesised as a single ~150 kDa polypeptide, later cleaved into a heavy chain (~100 kDa) and a light chain (~50 kDa) linked by a disulfide bond — an "AB" toxin architecture homologous to botulinum toxin. The heavy chain mediates binding to neuronal membranes (via polysialogangliosides such as GT1b and GD1b, and protein receptors) and translocation; the light chain is the active enzyme.

Mechanism of action

Toxin released at a wound is taken up at peripheral motor nerve terminals and transported retrogradely within axons to the spinal cord and brainstem. There it is transcytosed into inhibitory interneurons (glycinergic and GABAergic). In the cytosol, the light chain — a zinc-dependent endopeptidase — cleaves the SNARE protein synaptobrevin (VAMP2), preventing fusion of neurotransmitter vesicles. Blockade of inhibitory transmission removes the normal restraint on motor neurons, causing the sustained muscle contractions, rigidity and reflex spasms characteristic of tetanus. The same cleavage mechanism in botulinum toxin acts peripherally instead, producing flaccid paralysis.

Genetics and production

The toxin gene (tetX) is carried on a large plasmid (pE88 in the reference genome) and its expression is regulated by the alternative sigma factor TetR. Non-toxigenic strains of C. tetani lack the plasmid.

Medical uses and immunisation

Formaldehyde-inactivated toxin (toxoid), developed in the 1920s, is the basis of tetanus vaccines and induces protective antitoxin antibodies. Pre-formed antibody — human tetanus immunoglobulin — is used therapeutically and for post-exposure prophylaxis. In research, tetanus toxin fragments (notably the non-toxic C fragment, TTC) are used as neuronal tracers and as carriers in vaccine and drug-delivery studies.
