Sequence of tenses

Backshifting: matching the tense of a subordinate clause to a past-tense matrix clause.

The sequence of tenses (Latin: consecutio temporum) is, in grammar, the set of rules governing the choice of verb tense in a subordinate clause according to the tense of the verb in the main (matrix) clause. The term is especially used in describing Latin grammar, where the sequence is strict, and in describing the backshift of tenses in reported speech in languages such as English.

In Latin

In Latin, subordinate clauses in the subjunctive mood follow either the primary sequence (after present, future, and perfect-with-present-force tenses) or the historic sequence (after imperfect, perfect, and pluperfect tenses). Present and perfect subjunctives serve the primary sequence, expressing action simultaneous with or prior to the main verb; imperfect and pluperfect subjunctives serve the historic sequence. The rule governs purpose clauses, result clauses, indirect questions and commands, and clauses after verbs of fearing.

In English reported speech

English exhibits a similar pattern known as backshift: when a reporting verb is in the past, tenses in the reported clause typically move one step back — "I am tired" becomes "She said she was tired"; "I will come" becomes "He said he would come". Backshift is optional when the reported statement remains true at the time of reporting, and formal analyses treat English sequence-of-tense as a semantic agreement phenomenon rather than a purely morphological one.

In other languages

Languages differ widely. Russian and other Slavic languages generally preserve the original tense in indirect speech, while Greek shows its own sequence rules for the optative mood. Some languages, such as Japanese, use relative tense in subordinate clauses, so no sequence rule of the Latin type applies.
