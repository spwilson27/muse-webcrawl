Acid mine drainage

Acid mine drainage (AMD), also called acid rock drainage, is the outflow of acidic water from metal mines or coal mines. It forms when sulfide minerals — chiefly pyrite — exposed by mining are oxidised by air and water, generating sulphuric acid. Drainage can continue for centuries after a mine closes and is one of the most persistent forms of water pollution associated with mining.

Chemistry

The overall process begins with the oxidation of pyrite (FeS2) to ferrous iron and sulphuric acid. Ferrous iron is further oxidised to ferric iron, which itself attacks more pyrite, creating a self-sustaining cycle greatly accelerated by acidophilic bacteria such as Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans. As the acid water later mixes and its pH rises, dissolved iron precipitates as orange ferric hydroxide — the conspicuous "yellow boy" coating of affected streams — while metals such as aluminium, copper, zinc and cadmium may remain in solution and toxic to aquatic life.

Environmental impact

Affected waters can reach pH values below 3, killing fish and invertebrates and sterilising stream ecosystems for long distances. Famous problem sites include the Rio Tinto in Spain, mined since antiquity, Iron Mountain Mine in California, and the Berkeley Pit in Montana. Tens of thousands of kilometres of streams in the Appalachian coalfields of the United States are impaired by drainage from abandoned mines.

Treatment and prevention

Active treatment neutralises acidity with lime or limestone and precipitates metals, but must often continue indefinitely. Passive systems — anoxic limestone drains, constructed wetlands and sulfate-reducing bioreactors — aim for lower maintenance. Prevention focuses on excluding water and oxygen from sulfide waste, for example by flooding workings or capping waste under water or engineered covers.
