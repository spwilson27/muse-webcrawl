Historically black colleges and universities

Historically black colleges and universities (HBCUs) are institutions of higher education in the United States established before the Civil Rights Act of 1964 with the intention of serving African-American students. There are 107 HBCUs, public and private, enrolling a disproportionate share of Black graduates, doctors, lawyers and PhDs despite chronic underfunding.

HBCUs
Number: 107
Oldest: Cheyney University (1837)
Notable: Howard, Spelman, Morehouse, Hampton, Tuskegee, Fisk, Xavier (Louisiana), North Carolina A&T

History

Cheyney (1837), Lincoln (1854) and Wilberforce (1856) were founded before emancipation, mostly by religious groups. After the Civil War, the Freedmen's Bureau, churches and Black communities founded scores of schools; the Second Morrill Act (1890) created public Black land-grant colleges in segregating states. Booker T. Washington (Tuskegee) and W.E.B. Du Bois (Fisk, Atlanta University) debated industrial vs liberal education.

Role and outcomes

HBCUs educated the Black professional class under Jim Crow, were centres of the civil rights movement, and today — about 3% of US colleges — produce roughly 17% of Black bachelor's degrees and a quarter of Black STEM graduates. Homecoming culture, marching bands and Divine Nine fraternities/sororities are central traditions.

Challenges

Studies document billions in state funding gaps for land-grant HBCUs (e.g. Tennessee State, Maryland's settlement with its HBCUs in 2021). Enrolment and donations surged after 2020, including large gifts from MacKenzie Scott.
