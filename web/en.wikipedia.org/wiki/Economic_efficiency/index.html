Economic efficiency

Economic efficiency
FieldMicroeconomics, welfare economics
Key conceptsPareto efficiency, allocative efficiency, productive efficiency
RelatedProduction–possibility frontier, deadweight loss

Economic efficiency is a state in which resources are allocated in a way that maximises the production of goods and services and overall welfare, given available resources and technology. In an efficient economy, it is impossible to make someone better off without making someone else worse off (in the strict Pareto sense) or to produce more of one good without producing less of another.

Types of efficiency

  • Productive efficiency — goods are produced at the lowest possible average cost; firms operate on their production-possibility frontier, minimising waste of inputs.
  • Allocative efficiency — resources are directed to producing the mix of goods and services most valued by society; in competitive markets this occurs where price equals marginal cost.
  • Pareto efficiency — an allocation from which no Pareto improvement is possible: no individual can be made better off without making another worse off.
  • Dynamic efficiency — the economy's capacity to improve efficiency over time through innovation, investment and technological progress.
  • X-efficiency — Harvey Leibenstein's term for the degree to which firms actually minimise costs given competitive and motivational pressures.

Market efficiency and failure

The fundamental theorems of welfare economics show that, under ideal conditions, competitive market equilibria are Pareto efficient. In practice, market failures — monopoly power, externalities, public goods, asymmetric information and incomplete markets — drive a wedge between market outcomes and efficiency, creating deadweight losses. Government intervention may improve efficiency but can itself be inefficient. The weaker Kaldor–Hicks criterion deems a change efficient if the winners could in principle compensate the losers, even if compensation does not occur.

Measurement

Empirical analysis uses tools such as data envelopment analysis and stochastic frontier analysis to estimate the efficiency of firms, hospitals, schools and whole economies relative to a best-practice frontier.
