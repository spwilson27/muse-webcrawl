Fermi–Dirac statistics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Graph of the Fermi–Dirac distribution: occupancy falling from 1 to 0 across the Fermi energy, sharpening at low temperature]
The Fermi–Dirac distribution at several temperatures.

In quantum statistics, Fermi–Dirac statistics describes the distribution of identical, indistinguishable particles with half-integer spin (fermions) over energy states in thermal equilibrium, subject to the Pauli exclusion principle: no state may hold more than one particle. Formulated independently by Enrico Fermi and Paul Dirac in 1926, it governs electrons in metals and semiconductors, nucleons in nuclei, and the degenerate matter of white dwarfs and neutron stars.

The occupancy of a state of energy E is 1/(e^{(E−μ)/kT} + 1) — the sign difference from Bose–Einstein statistics encodes exclusion. At zero temperature, states are filled up to the Fermi energy and empty above it, producing the degeneracy pressure that supports dead stars and the Fermi surfaces that organise the physics of metals.

Applications

Fermi–Dirac statistics explains why the electrons in a metal contribute so little to its heat capacity (only those near the Fermi surface can move), the operation of semiconductor devices (doping shifts the chemical potential μ), thermionic emission, and the structure of stellar remnants up to the Chandrasekhar limit. Together with Bose–Einstein statistics it brackets the classical Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, which both approach at high temperature and low density.
