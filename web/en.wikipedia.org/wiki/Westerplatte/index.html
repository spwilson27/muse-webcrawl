Westerplatte is a peninsula at the entrance to the port of Gdańsk (Danzig) on the Baltic coast of Poland. It is famous as the site of the first battle of the Second World War in Europe, where a small Polish garrison held out for seven days against overwhelming German forces in September 1939.

The battle

Under League of Nations arrangements Poland maintained a Military Transit Depot on Westerplatte, in the territory of the Free City of Danzig, garrisoned by about 180 men with one 75 mm gun, two anti-tank guns and machine guns in five guardhouses. At 04:47 on 1 September 1939 the German pre-dreadnought Schleswig-Holstein, supposedly on a courtesy visit, opened fire at point-blank range — among the first shots of the war. Supported by Stuka dive-bombers, naval guns and SS and police assault units, the Germans expected the depot to fall within hours; instead the defenders under Major Henryk Sucharski and Captain Franciszek Dąbrowski repelled repeated attacks, surrendering only on 7 September when ammunition, water and medical supplies ran out. Polish losses were about 15–20 killed; German losses were several hundred. The defence became a national symbol of resistance — "the Polish Thermopylae".

Memorial

The ruins of the guardhouses are preserved as an open-air museum. In 1966 Poland erected the 22-metre Monument of the Coast Defenders on an artificial mound, and Westerplatte remains an official national memorial where state commemorations of the war's outbreak are held each 1 September.
