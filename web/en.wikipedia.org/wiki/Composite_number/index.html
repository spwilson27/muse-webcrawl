Composite number

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A composite number is a positive integer that can be formed by multiplying two smaller positive integers other than 1 and the number itself. Equivalently, it is a positive integer that has at least one divisor other than 1 and itself. Every positive integer is composite, prime, or the unit 1, so the composite numbers are exactly the numbers that are neither prime nor a unit.

Examples

The smallest composite number is 4 = 2 × 2. The first few composite numbers are 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 20, 21, 22, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, … The number 1 is neither prime nor composite; 0 is conventionally excluded as well, since it is divisible by every integer.

Factorisation

By the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, every composite number can be written as a product of prime numbers in exactly one way (up to order). For example, 42 = 2 × 3 × 7. Determining this factorisation for very large composite numbers is computationally hard and underpins the security of the RSA cryptosystem.

Types

Composite numbers are sometimes classified by the number of prime factors: a product of two primes is a semiprime; a product of three is a sphenic number. Other named subclasses include highly composite numbers, which have more divisors than any smaller positive integer, and roughly composite categories based on the size of the smallest prime factor. A composite number whose prime factors are all distinct from one another raised to the first power in a certain pattern can also be discussed via the divisor function.

Density

Although there are infinitely many composite numbers and infinitely many primes, the proportion of composite numbers among the integers approaches 1 as numbers grow, because the density of primes falls to zero (by the prime number theorem, the fraction of integers ≤ n that are prime is about 1 / ln n).
