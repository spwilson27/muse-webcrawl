John Donne (22 January 1572 – 31 March 1631) was an English poet, scholar, soldier and secretary born into a recusant Catholic family, who later became a cleric in the Church of England. Under the patronage of James I he became Dean of St Paul's Cathedral in London (1621–1631). Considered the pre-eminent representative of the metaphysical poets, his work is celebrated for its inventive conceits, dramatic immediacy and mastery of both erotic and devotional verse.

Life

Donne studied at Oxford and Cambridge without taking a degree (as a Catholic he could not swear the Oath of Supremacy), then read law at Lincoln's Inn. He sailed on expeditions against Spain with Essex and Ralegh, and became secretary to Sir Thomas Egerton, the Lord Keeper. His secret marriage in 1601 to Egerton's niece, Anne More, destroyed his career prospects; he spent years in poverty with a growing family, writing (anonymously or in manuscript) the satires, elegies and love lyrics — the Songs and Sonnets — for which he is famous.

After converting to Anglicanism, and urged by James I, Donne took holy orders in 1615 and rose rapidly, becoming Dean of St Paul's in 1621. His sermons and his prose Devotions upon Emergent Occasions (1624) — written during a near-fatal illness, and source of the phrases "No man is an island" and "never send to know for whom the bell tolls; it tolls for thee" — made him the most renowned preacher of his age.

Poetry and legacy

Donne's poetry, published posthumously in 1633, ranges from the witty love poems ("The Flea", "The Sun Rising", "A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning" with its famous compasses conceit) to the tormented Holy Sonnets ("Death, be not proud"; "Batter my heart, three-person'd God"). Neglected in the 18th and 19th centuries, he was championed by T. S. Eliot and the New Critics, and is now among the most studied of English poets.
