Spanish Road

The Spanish Road from Milan to the Low Countries

The Spanish Road (Spanish: Camino Español) was a military supply and troop-movement route used by Habsburg Spain from 1567 to 1633 to move soldiers from Italy to the Spanish Netherlands. It ran overland from the Duchy of Milan, through Savoy or Swiss passes, Franche-Comté, and Lorraine to Luxembourg and Flanders. The route was created because the sea passage through the English Channel and Bay of Biscay was exposed to French, English, and Dutch naval attack.

Creation and use

The road was pioneered in 1567 when the Duke of Alba led some 10,000 troops from Italy to Brussels to suppress the Dutch Revolt — the opening of the Eighty Years' War. Thereafter, the Spanish Army of Flanders was continuously fed with veteran Italian and Spanish tercios marching north along the corridor of mutually allied or neutral territories Spain had negotiated along the way.

A typical march covered roughly 1,000 km (620 miles) in about six to eight weeks. Along the route, towns were obliged to provide staging posts (étapes) with food, lodging, and forage, for which Spain paid — an early system of contracted military logistics. Politically, the corridor underpinned Spanish power in northern Europe and encircled France, making its disruption a permanent French strategic aim.

Decline

France progressively cut the route during the Thirty Years' War: seizing the Valtellina passes, occupying Lorraine in the 1630s, and annexing Franche-Comté (Treaties of Nijmegen, 1678). With the defeat at Rocroi (1643) and the Peace of Westphalia (1648), Spanish reliance on the road ended, and the route survives in historiography as a classic example of pre-modern strategic logistics, notably in Geoffrey Parker's The Army of Flanders and the Spanish Road (1972).
