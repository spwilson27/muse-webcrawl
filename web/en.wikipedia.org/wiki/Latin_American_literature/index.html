Latin American literature

Latin American literature comprises the oral and written literature of Latin America, in Spanish, Portuguese, and (to a lesser extent) indigenous and other languages. It achieved worldwide prominence in the second half of the 20th century with the literary "Boom" and the style of magic realism.

Early and colonial periods

Pre-Columbian traditions include Maya codices, Aztec poetry, and Quechua oral literature. Colonial-era writing includes chronicles of the conquest, such as Bernal Díaz del Castillo's True History of the Conquest of New Spain, and the poetry of Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz in Mexico. The 19th century produced the gaucho epic Martín Fierro by José Hernández and the modernismo movement led by the Nicaraguan poet Rubén Darío.

The Boom and beyond

In the 1960s the Latin American Boom brought international fame to novelists including Gabriel García Márquez (One Hundred Years of Solitude, 1967), Julio Cortázar, Mario Vargas Llosa, and Carlos Fuentes. García Márquez (1982), Gabriela Mistral (1945), Miguel Ángel Asturias (1967), Pablo Neruda (1971), Octavio Paz (1990), and Vargas Llosa (2010) have received the Nobel Prize in Literature. Later generations — the "post-Boom" and McOndo writers — reacted against magic realism with urban, globalised themes, while authors such as Roberto Bolaño achieved major posthumous recognition.
