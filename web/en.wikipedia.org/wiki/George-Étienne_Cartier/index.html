Sir George-Étienne Cartier, 1st Baronet (September 6, 1814 – May 20, 1873) was a Canadian statesman and a Father of Confederation. The leading French Canadian politician of his era, he was co-premier of the Province of Canada with John A. Macdonald (1857–1858 and 1858–1862) and was instrumental in bringing French Canada, Manitoba, and British Columbia into Confederation.

Early life

Born in Saint-Antoine-sur-Richelieu, Lower Canada, Cartier was educated at the Collège de Montréal and called to the bar in 1835. As a young man he sympathised with the Patriote movement and fought at the Battle of Saint-Denis in 1837, after which he briefly fled to the United States.

Political career

Elected to the Legislative Assembly of the Province of Canada in 1848, Cartier became the dominant political figure in Canada East. As a Bleu (conservative) leader allied with Macdonald's Liberals-Conservatives, he promoted railway development, the codification of the civil law of Lower Canada (the Civil Code of 1866), and the abolition of seigneurial tenure.

Confederation

Cartier persuaded French Canadians that Confederation would protect their language, religion and civil law, and he delivered Canada East's support for the Quebec Resolutions. At the London Conference of 1866–67 he helped finalise the British North America Act. In the first federal government he served as Minister of Militia and Defence. He later negotiated Manitoba's entry (1870) and brought British Columbia into Confederation (1871) on the promise of a transcontinental railway. Defeated in the 1872 election amid the Pacific Scandal, he died in London in 1873 while seeking treatment for Bright's disease.
