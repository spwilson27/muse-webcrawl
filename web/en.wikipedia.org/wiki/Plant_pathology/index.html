Plant pathology

Plant pathology (or phytopathology) is the scientific study of diseases in plants caused by pathogens and environmental conditions. Plant pathologists investigate the organisms that cause infectious disease — fungi, oomycetes, bacteria, viruses, viroids, phytoplasmas, nematodes, and parasitic plants — as well as disorders caused by nutrient deficiencies, pollution, and other abiotic factors, and develop methods to control them.

The disease triangle

Disease occurs only when three conditions coincide: a susceptible host plant, a virulent pathogen, and an environment favourable to infection (for many fungi, leaf wetness and warmth). This "disease triangle" organises the discipline's approach: resistance breeding changes the host, sanitation and fungicides attack the pathogen, and irrigation or spacing practices alter the environment. Pathogens infect through wounds, natural openings such as stomata, or by direct penetration, and may be biotrophs (feeding on living tissue), necrotrophs (killing tissue first), or hemibiotrophs that switch strategy.

Historical importance

Plant diseases have shaped human history: the Irish Great Famine of the 1840s followed the arrival of potato late blight (Phytophthora infestans), whose study by Anton de Bary founded modern plant pathology; coffee rust destroyed Ceylon's coffee industry in the nineteenth century; and Dutch elm disease, chestnut blight, and citrus greening have transformed landscapes and agriculture. Plant diseases are estimated to destroy 10–16% of the world's harvest annually, so pathology underpins food security, quarantine law, and crop breeding — including the deployment of resistance genes and, increasingly, molecular diagnostics and genomic surveillance.
