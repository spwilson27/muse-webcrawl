Aid to Families with Dependent Children (AFDC) was a federal assistance programme in the United States in effect from 1935 to 1997, created by the Social Security Act and administered by the states. It provided financial assistance to children whose families had low or no income — the programme most commonly meant by the term "welfare" in American debate.

History

The programme began in 1935 as Aid to Dependent Children, designed to support children in single-parent homes so that mothers would not need to leave them to work. It expanded over the following decades to cover more families, and in 1962 its name was changed to Aid to Families with Dependent Children. Enrolment grew sharply in the 1960s and early 1970s as eligibility rules were liberalised by court decisions and greater take-up.

Administration

AFDC was a matching grant programme: the federal government reimbursed states for a share of payments, while states set benefit levels and administered eligibility. Benefits varied widely by state, being far more generous in some northern states than in the South. Because extra children brought extra payments, critics argued the programme encouraged dependency and non-marital childbearing; supporters pointed to the low value of benefits and its role in keeping children out of destitution.

Abolition

AFDC was abolished by the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996, signed by President Bill Clinton, and replaced in 1997 by Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF), a block grant with work requirements and time limits.
