Siege of Antwerp (1584–1585)

The fall of Antwerp on 17 August 1585 is often called the siege of Antwerp during the Eighty Years' War. Following the siege, which lasted from July 1584 until August 1585, the city of Antwerp, at that time one of the largest and richest cities in Europe and the centre of the Dutch revolt, was taken by Spanish forces under Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma. The siege is notable for the great fortified bridge Farnese built across the river Scheldt to cut off the city from relief by sea, and for the defenders' use of explosive "hellburner" ships against it.

Background

At the start of the Eighty Years' War, Antwerp was the richest trading city in northern Europe and a stronghold of the Dutch revolt against Spanish rule. After the Spanish Fury of 1576 and years of campaigning, Farnese methodically reconquered the southern Netherlands; Antwerp was the greatest prize. The city's defence was directed by its burgomaster, Philips of Marnix, Lord of Saint-Aldegonde.

The siege

Farnese encircled the city with a ring of forts and, in the winter of 1584–85, completed a fortified bridge roughly 800 metres long across the Scheldt below the city, sealed with chains, rafts and floating obstacles. Cut off from supply, the starving city attempted to break the blockade with fireships — the famous "hellburners" designed by the Italian engineer Federigo Giambelli — which exploded against the bridge on the night of 4–5 April 1585, killing many Spanish soldiers but failing to destroy the bridge permanently. A final relief attempt at the Kowenstyn dyke in May also failed, and Antwerp surrendered on 17 August 1585.

Aftermath

The Protestant population was given four years to convert or leave; most emigrated north, carrying Antwerp's wealth and skills to cities such as Amsterdam. Antwerp's population fell from around 100,000 to about 42,000, and the closure of the Scheldt to trade, confirmed at the Peace of Münster in 1648, ended its golden age.
