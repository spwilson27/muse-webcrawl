Samuel J. Tilden

Samuel Jones Tilden
Born: February 9, 1814, New Lebanon, New York
Died: August 4, 1886 (aged 72), Yonkers, New York
Office: 25th Governor of New York (1875–1876)
Party: Democratic

Samuel Jones Tilden (February 9, 1814 – August 4, 1886) was an American politician who served as the 25th governor of New York and was the Democratic nominee in the disputed 1876 United States presidential election. Tilden is the only person to win an outright majority of the popular vote in a United States presidential election but lose the election itself, a distinction he shares in part with other popular-vote winners who lost the Electoral College.

Early life and legal career

Tilden was born in New Lebanon, New York. He studied at Yale and New York University and was admitted to the bar in 1841. As a corporate lawyer, notably for railroads, he amassed a large fortune. He became a leader of the Democratic Party's reform wing, the "Bourbon Democrats," committed to free trade, hard money, and clean government.

Fight against Tammany

Tilden rose to national prominence by prosecuting the corrupt Tweed Ring of Tammany Hall in the early 1870s, helping to convict "Boss" William M. Tweed and break the ring's grip on New York City. Elected governor in 1874, he pursued the Canal Ring and other corrupt contractors, cementing his reputation as a reformer.

The election of 1876

Nominated for president in 1876 on a platform of civil service reform, Tilden won the popular vote against Republican Rutherford B. Hayes by roughly 3.0 million to 4.0 million votes — about 50.9 percent to 47.9 percent — and appeared to have won 184 electoral votes, one short of a majority, with 20 votes disputed from Florida, Louisiana, South Carolina, and Oregon. A special Electoral Commission awarded all disputed votes to Hayes, and the Compromise of 1877 — under which Republicans conceded an end to Reconstruction — secured his inauguration. Tilden accepted the outcome, declining to encourage resistance, saying he could "retire to private life without bitterness."

Later life and legacy

Tilden declined renomination in 1880 on grounds of health. He died at his estate, Greystone, in Yonkers in 1886, leaving much of his fortune to establish a free public library in New York City; his bequest helped found the New York Public Library, whose main branch bears the names of its benefactors Astor, Lenox, and Tilden.
