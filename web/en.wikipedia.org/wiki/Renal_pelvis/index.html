Renal pelvis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The renal pelvis or pelvis of the kidney is the funnel-like dilated part of the ureter in the kidney. In humans, the renal pelvis is the point where the two or three major calyces join together. It is lined by mucous membrane and is covered by a transitional epithelium and an underlying lamina propria of loose-to-dense connective tissue. Together with the calyces it forms the central collecting cavity of the kidney, from which urine passes into the ureter and onward to the bladder.

Structure

The renal pelvis is situated within the renal sinus. Several minor calyces, each cupping a renal papilla, merge into major calyces, which in turn unite to form the renal pelvis. At the ureteropelvic junction the pelvis narrows and continues as the ureter. Smooth muscle in its walls generates peristaltic waves that propel urine toward the bladder.

Clinical significance

The renal pelvis can become dilated (hydronephrosis) if urine flow is obstructed, for example by a kidney stone lodged at the ureteropelvic junction, or in congenital ureteropelvic junction obstruction. It is also a site where transitional cell carcinoma of the urinary tract can arise, and where larger "staghorn" calculi may form. Imaging such as ultrasound, CT urography and renal scintigraphy is used to evaluate its size and drainage.
