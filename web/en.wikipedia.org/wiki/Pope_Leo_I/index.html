Pope Leo I (c. 400 – 10 November 461), known as Leo the Great, was bishop of Rome from 440 to his death. He is regarded as one of the most important popes of late antiquity, asserting papal authority based on Peter's primacy and defining orthodox Christology.

Tome and Chalcedon

Leo's Tome (449), a letter to Patriarch Flavian of Constantinople, set out the doctrine that Christ has two natures, divine and human, united in one person. Though rejected at the 'Robber Council' of Ephesus (449), it was acclaimed at the Council of Chalcedon (451), where delegates reportedly cried 'Peter has spoken through Leo'. Chalcedon's definition became orthodox doctrine.

Attila and the Vandals

In 452 Leo met Attila near the Mincio River during the Huns' invasion of Italy after the Catalaunian Plains; Attila withdrew, for reasons debated (diplomacy, famine, disease, eastern pressure). In 455 Leo negotiated with the Vandal king Genseric, limiting the sack of Rome.

Legacy

Venerated as a saint and Doctor of the Church, Leo left many sermons and letters and strengthened the Roman see amid western imperial collapse.
