Delta hedging

Delta hedging is an options strategy that aims to reduce, or hedge, the directional risk associated with price movements in the underlying asset. It involves taking an offsetting position in the underlying such that the overall portfolio is delta neutral — its value is insensitive to small changes in the price of the underlying.

Delta

Delta (Δ) is one of the "Greeks": it measures the rate of change of an option's price with respect to changes in the underlying asset's price. A call option delta ranges from 0 to 1, and a put option delta from −1 to 0. To hedge a position of options, a trader holds Δ shares of the underlying per option, adjusted so the portfolio delta sums to zero.

Dynamic hedging

Because delta itself changes as the underlying price moves (an effect measured by gamma), a delta-neutral position must be rebalanced continually — bought or sold as the market moves — in a practice known as dynamic hedging. The Black–Scholes model assumes continuous, costless rebalancing; in practice transaction costs and discrete intervals leave residual risk.

Use by market makers

Options market makers routinely delta hedge their books so that their profit comes from the bid–ask spread rather than from bets on market direction. Large-scale dynamic hedging was implicated in the 1987 stock market crash in the context of portfolio insurance programmes that replicated put options by selling into falling markets.
