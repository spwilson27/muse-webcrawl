Unified field theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Portrait of Albert Einstein in his later Princeton years, when he pursued unified field theory]
Einstein spent his last thirty years seeking a unified field theory.

In physics, a unified field theory (UFT) is a type of field theory that allows all fundamental forces and elementary particles to be written in terms of a single field or a single geometric structure. The term is associated chiefly with Albert Einstein's long, unsuccessful programme (roughly 1925–1955) to unify gravitation and electromagnetism into one classical geometry — extending General relativity with asymmetric metrics, distant parallelism, or five-dimensional Kaluza–Klein theory.

Einstein's quest, pursued in Princeton while quantum mechanics — whose probabilistic character he rejected — triumphed without him, isolated him from the mainstream; correspondence with colleagues (Pauli among them, amused and sceptical) tracked each rise and fall of hope. Modern unification proceeds differently: quantum gauge unification (Electroweak interaction, Grand Unified Theory) succeeded where classical geometry failed, and the remaining problem — quantising gravity — is the Theory of everything problem. The historical UFT episode is studied both as a cautionary tale about aesthetic guidance and as a source of mathematical ideas (Kaluza–Klein geometry lives on in String theory's compactified dimensions).

Kaluza–Klein and after

Theodor Kaluza (1921) showed that general relativity in five dimensions contains electromagnetism in the fifth component; Oskar Klein (1926) made the extra dimension quantum and curled it invisibly small. Forgotten for decades, the idea was revived by supergravity and string theory, where six or seven compact dimensions are required. Einstein himself flirted with Kaluza's approach before abandoning it — the pattern of his UFT years: brilliant geometry, no contact with the quantum, no predictions.
