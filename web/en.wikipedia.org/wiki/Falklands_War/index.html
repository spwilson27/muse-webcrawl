The Falklands War (Spanish: Guerra de las Malvinas) was a ten-week undeclared war between Argentina and the United Kingdom in 1982 over two British dependent territories in the South Atlantic: the Falkland Islands and its territorial dependency, South Georgia and the South Sandwich Islands. The conflict began on Friday, 2 April 1982, when Argentina invaded and occupied the Falkland Islands, followed by the invasion of South Georgia the next day — an attempt by the Argentine military junta, led by General Leopoldo Galtieri, to assert sovereignty over the territories, which Argentina had claimed since the nineteenth century.

The British government, led by Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher, dispatched a naval task force to engage the Argentine Navy and Air Force before making an amphibious assault on the islands. The conflict lasted 74 days and ended with the Argentine surrender on 14 June 1982, returning the islands to British control.

Background

Sovereignty over the Falklands had been disputed since Britain re-established control in 1833; Argentina maintained that the islands (which it calls the Malvinas) were inherited from Spain. Negotiations between 1966 and 1981 failed to resolve the dispute. Facing domestic unrest and economic crisis, the Argentine junta under Galtieri — who had deposed his predecessor in December 1981 — invaded, apparently expecting Britain not to respond militarily. A March 1982 incident involving Argentine scrap-metal merchants on South Georgia provided the immediate catalyst.

Course of the war

Britain assembled a task force of over 100 ships, including the aircraft carriers HMS Hermes and HMS Invincible, sailing some 8,000 miles with logistic support from Ascension Island. British forces retook South Georgia on 25 April (Operation Paraquet), during which the Argentine submarine Santa Fe was disabled. On 1 May the air war began; on 2 May the British submarine HMS Conqueror torpedoed and sank the Argentine cruiser ARA General Belgrano, killing 323 — the most controversial British action of the war, as the ship was outside the declared Total Exclusion Zone. Argentine jets responded by sinking HMS Sheffield with an Exocet missile on 4 May.

British troops landed unopposed at San Carlos Water on 21 May, enduring heavy air attacks that sank or damaged several ships, including HMS Ardent, Antelope, and Coventry, and the transport Atlantic Conveyor. Land battles followed at Goose Green (28–29 May), Mount Kent, and in the final assault on the hills around Stanley — Mount Longdon, Two Sisters, Mount Harriet, Tumbledown, and Wireless Ridge. On 8 June Argentine aircraft attacked landing ships at Bluff Cove, killing 48 aboard RFA Sir Galahad. Argentine commander Mario Menéndez surrendered to Major General Jeremy Moore on 14 June 1982.

Aftermath

In total, 649 Argentine military personnel, 255 British military personnel, and three Falkland Islanders were killed. The war restored the popularity of Thatcher's government, which won the 1983 general election, and precipitated the fall of the Argentine junta, paving the way for the return of democracy in 1983. Argentina continues to claim the islands, and its 1994 constitution reaffirms the claim; in a 2013 referendum, Falkland Islanders voted almost unanimously to remain a British Overseas Territory. Britain retains a substantial garrison at RAF Mount Pleasant and the islands' war dead are commemorated at cemeteries including the Argentine cemetery near Darwin and the British cemetery at San Carlos.
