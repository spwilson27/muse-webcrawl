The New Guinea singing dog (Canis lupus hallstromi or Canis hallstromi) is a rare dog native to the highlands of New Guinea. It is named for its distinctive, melodious howl, which rises and falls in pitch in a manner compared to birdsong.

Description

Singing dogs are small, flexible, primitive-looking dogs with short legs, broad heads and erect ears. They are exceptionally agile climbers, with flexible joints and spines that let them squeeze through narrow gaps and climb trees and cliffs in their mountain habitat.

Vocalisation

The breed's howl is its signature trait: a single note that increases in pitch and ends in a series of harmonics, often performed in chorus by several animals. Unlike domestic dogs, singing dogs rarely bark repetitively.

History and status

Only a few hundred individuals existed in captivity worldwide in the late 20th century, mostly descended from a small number of animals captured in the 1950s and 1970s. Wild populations were long thought possibly extinct until photographic evidence and DNA sampling in 2016–2020 confirmed that wild highland dogs in Indonesian Papua are the original population from which the captive singing dogs derive.

The singing dog is closely related to the Australian dingo, and both are thought to descend from early dogs brought to the region thousands of years ago.
