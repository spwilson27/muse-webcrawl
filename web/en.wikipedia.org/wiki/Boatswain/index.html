A boatswain (pronounced "bosun"), formerly and often still written bos'n, is the senior crew member of the deck department on a ship. The boatswain supervises the deck crew and is responsible for the maintenance of the ship's hull, rigging, anchors, cables and boats. In navies, the boatswain was traditionally a warrant officer; today the title survives in ratings such as boatswain's mate in the United States Navy and as a rank/appointment in other fleets.

Duties

The boatswain plans and supervises deck work: painting and preserving the hull, maintaining rigging and deck machinery, handling mooring lines, operating anchors and windlasses, and launching and recovering boats. On sailing ships the boatswain was responsible for the sails, rigging and ground tackle, and "pipped" orders to the crew using a boatswain's call — a high-pitched whistle whose different calls conveyed commands in the noise of wind and sea. The bosun's mates and deckhands (ordinary and able seamen) carry out the work under the boatswain's direction, reporting through the chief mate.

History

The word derives from late Old English "bātswegen" (boat's man/servant). In the Royal Navy the boatswain was one of the original warrant officers, ranking with the master, purser, surgeon, chaplain and others who held warrants rather than commissions. The boatswain was responsible for the ship's rigging, sails, anchors and boats, and was famously associated with discipline, sometimes carrying a starter (a short rope) to encourage slow workers — a practice that gave the bosun a feared reputation in the age of sail.

In merchant service

On merchant vessels the bosun is the senior unlicensed deck rating, working under the chief officer. The role remains standard on cargo ships and on traditional sailing and training ships, where the bosun maintains the rig and supervises sail handling.
