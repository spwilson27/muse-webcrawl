The Guadalquivir is the fifth-longest river in the Iberian Peninsula and the longest in Andalusia, southern Spain. Its name derives from the Arabic al-wādī l-kabīr ("the great river valley"); the Romans knew it as the Baetis, from which the province of Baetica took its name. It rises in the Sierra de Cazorla in the province of Jaén and flows westward some 657 km past Córdoba and Seville to the Gulf of Cádiz at Sanlúcar de Barrameda.

Course and navigation

The river drains a basin of nearly 57,000 km² between the Sierra Morena to the north and the Baetic ranges to the south. Its main tributaries include the Genil, the Guadalimar, and the Guadiana Menor. The Guadalquivir is navigable from the sea to Seville — historically as far as Córdoba — making Seville an inland port of great importance. Ocean-going vessels still reach Seville through a dredged channel and lock.

History

The valley was a heartland of the ancient Tartessian culture and later of Roman Baetica, an oil- and wine-exporting province. Under Muslim rule, Córdoba became one of the largest cities in Europe with the river at its centre. After the Christian conquest, Seville's monopoly on trade with the Americas in the 16th and 17th centuries made the Guadalquivir one of the world's great commercial arteries; the fleets of the Spanish Empire departed from and returned to its banks. Today the lower valley is intensively farmed, and the river borders the wetlands of Doñana National Park near its mouth.
