Irish Draught

The Irish Draught is a breed of draught horse developed in Ireland, and the national horse breed of Ireland. Although the breed's traditional role was as an all-purpose farm horse, it is now best known as a foundation for the Irish Sport Horse when crossed with Thoroughbreds and warmbloods.

History

The breed evolved from the small Irish Hobby and the horses of tenant farmers, who needed an animal strong enough to plough and pull carts yet fast and economical enough to ride and hunt. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Thoroughbred and Clydesdale blood was introduced. The Irish government took an interest from 1906, and a stud book was opened in 1917. Numbers declined sharply with mechanisation after the Second World War, prompting conservation efforts; the Irish Horse Board and breed societies in Britain, North America, and elsewhere now maintain registers.

Characteristics

Irish Draughts typically stand 15.1 to 16.3 hands (61 to 67 inches, 155 to 170 cm) and are strongly built, with a well-set neck, deep chest, short strong cannon bones, and notably clean legs free of feathering. Any solid colour is accepted, including grey. They are prized for soundness, jumping ability, and an exceptionally sensible, trainable temperament.

Uses

Purebreds are shown in hand and under saddle and used for riding, driving, and hunting. The breed's greatest influence is as a cross: the Irish Draught × Thoroughbred cross, the Irish Sport Horse, has produced many of the world's top eventers and show jumpers.
