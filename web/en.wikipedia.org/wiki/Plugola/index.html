Plugola

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Plugola is the illicit business practice of endorsing a product or service on radio or television for personal consideration, without disclosing that the endorser is being paid or receiving something of value. The term is a portmanteau of "plug" (in the sense of a favorable mention or advertisement) and "payola", coined in the wake of the payola scandals that shook the American broadcasting industry in the late 1950s.

Distinction from payola

Whereas payola involves payments made to a disc jockey or program producer in exchange for airplay of a particular recording, plugola involves a broadcast personality promoting an outside product, business, or interest of their own — such as a nightclub appearance, a book, a film, or a company in which they hold a financial stake — during program time, without informing the audience or station management of the arrangement. Both practices undermine the principle, enshrined in United States communications law, that broadcast matter paid for by an interested party must be identified as such.

Legal status

Under Section 317 of the Communications Act of 1934, as amended in 1960 following congressional hearings on payola and plugola, broadcast stations must announce when matter has been paid for or furnished in exchange for consideration. Section 507 requires persons who pay or are paid to furnish program matter to disclose that fact. Employees who accept undisclosed consideration may face fines and imprisonment, and stations risk sanctions from the Federal Communications Commission. Undisclosed paid endorsements in modern media — including social media influencing — are treated under analogous sponsorship-identification rules enforced by the FCC and the Federal Trade Commission.

History

Investigations in 1959–1960 by the House Legislative Oversight Subcommittee exposed widespread payola in record promotion and also brought plugola to public attention, including celebrity hosts who used their programs to promote businesses in which they had undisclosed interests. The resulting 1960 amendments to the Communications Act criminalized undisclosed pay-for-play arrangements and tightened sponsorship disclosure across broadcasting.
