Iomante

An iomante (イオマンテ), sometimes spelled iyomante, is a traditional ceremony of the Ainu people of northern Japan in which a brown bear is raised from a cub and then ritually sacrificed so that its spirit (kamuy) may return to the world of the gods laden with gifts. The word is commonly translated as "sending-off" or "sending ceremony": i ("that"), oman ("to go"), and te (causative).

Description

In the traditional practice, a bear cub captured in winter was brought to the village, raised in a cage, and treated with great respect as a visiting deity. After one or two years, the community held the iomante: the bear was adorned, prayed over, offered food and sake, and then killed by arrows — ideally by a shot from a honoured guest. A feast followed, and the bear's meat and fur were shared as gifts from the god. The skull was placed on a ritual altar (nusa) beside the house.

Beliefs

In Ainu religion, the bear — kim-un-kamuy — is among the most important deities. The ceremony was understood not as killing but as releasing the god from its earthly "disguise" (the bear's body), allowing it to report the humans' hospitality to the other gods and return with blessings. Similar sending ceremonies (kamuy iomante) were held for other animals, particularly Blakiston's fish owl.

Modern status

Iomante ceremonies were discouraged under Japanese assimilation policies in the Meiji era and became rare in the twentieth century. Small community ceremonies have occasionally been revived, and the practice is now presented mainly in cultural and museum contexts, while animal-welfare concerns make full traditional performances rare.
