Mahabhashya

The Mahābhāṣya ("Great Commentary"), attributed to Patañjali, is a commentary on selected rules of Sanskrit grammar from Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī and the Vārttika of Kātyāyana. Composed around the middle of the 2nd century BCE, it is one of the foundational texts of the Indian grammatical tradition (vyākaraṇa) and of Indian philosophy of language.

Content

The Mahābhāṣya comments on 1,713 of Pāṇini's roughly 4,000 rules. Patañjali examines Kātyāyana's criticisms and suggested emendations, accepting some and rejecting others, so that the final doctrine of the school emerges from the layered discussion. Tradition calls Pāṇini, Kātyāyana, and Patañjali the munitraya, the "three sages" of grammar. The text is written as a lively dialogue, full of everyday examples, puzzles, and occasional humour, and it incidentally preserves precious historical information about 2nd-century BCE India — including references to the Mauryas, the Indo-Greek (Yavana) invasions, and the worship of deities such as Vāsudeva.

Philosophy of language

Beyond grammar, the Mahābhāṣya develops influential ideas about the relation of word and meaning, the eternity (nityatva) of language, and the concept of sphoṭa — the indivisible linguistic unit that bears meaning — later elaborated by Bhartṛhari. Its discussion of whether words denote universals or individuals shaped centuries of debate in Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, and Buddhist logic.

Transmission

The text fell into relative neglect for a period in the first millennium CE before being revived and commented on by scholars such as Kaiyaṭa (Pradīpa, 11th century) and Nāgeśa Bhaṭṭa (18th century).
