Moral nihilism

Moral nihilism (also known as ethical nihilism) is the meta-ethical view that nothing is morally right or wrong — that there are no objective moral facts, values, duties, or virtues. It holds that moral judgements, when taken as claims about the world, categorically fail.

Forms

The most developed form of moral nihilism is the error theory, defended systematically by J. L. Mackie, who argued that ordinary moral judgements presuppose objectively prescriptive values, but that such values do not exist, so the judgements are all false. Mackie's arguments — the argument from relativity and the argument from queerness — remain influential. Other versions are closer to non-cognitivism, denying that moral sentences express propositions at all.

Consequences and responses

A common worry is that moral nihilism licenses any behaviour. Nihilists reply that without objective values, people's preferences, conventions, and legal systems still structure conduct, and that many adopt compensating positions such as moral fictionalism, abolitionism about moral discourse, or pragmatic conservation of morality as a useful fiction.
