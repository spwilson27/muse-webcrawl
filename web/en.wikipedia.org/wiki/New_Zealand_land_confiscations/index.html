New Zealand land confiscations

Date1863–1867 (principal confiscations)
LocationNorth Island, New Zealand
AreaOver 3 million acres (about 1.2 million hectares)
Legal basisNew Zealand Settlements Act 1863

The New Zealand land confiscations took place during the 1860s under the New Zealand Settlements Act 1863, in which the colonial government seized more than three million acres of Māori land in the North Island. Known in Māori as raupatu, the confiscations followed the New Zealand Wars and affected districts including Waikato, Taranaki, Tauranga, the eastern Bay of Plenty, and Mohaka-Waikare in Hawke's Bay.

Background and legislation

During the Waikato War of 1863–64, Governor George Grey's government invaded the Waikato to suppress the Māori King Movement (Kīngitanga). The Settlements Act, passed in December 1863, authorized the Governor to declare districts where Māori were deemed to be "in rebellion" and to confiscate land within them for European settlement, ostensibly to punish rebellion and defray war costs while establishing military settlements as a buffer. In practice, confiscation fell on loyal and dissenting Māori alike, and the boundaries took in far more land than could plausibly be settled.

Effects and aftermath

The raupatu stripped iwi (tribes) such as Waikato-Tainui, Ngāti Awa, and Taranaki iwi of their economic base and caused lasting poverty, grievance, and social disruption; some land was later returned or compensation paid, but usually only a fraction and often to individuals rather than collectives. Commissions of inquiry, beginning with the Sim Commission of 1926–27, criticized the confiscations, and the settlements acts of the 1940s provided annuities regarded as inadequate. From the 1990s, Treaty of Waitangi settlements — notably the Waikato Raupatu Claims Settlement Act 1995, which included a formal Crown apology acknowledging that the confiscations were unjust — provided financial and cultural redress to several affected iwi.
