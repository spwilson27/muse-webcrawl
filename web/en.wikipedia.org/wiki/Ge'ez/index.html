Ge'ez (ግእዝ, Gəʾəz) is an ancient South Semitic language of Ethiopia and Eritrea. It originated in the region encompassing most of modern-day Eritrea and northern Ethiopia, and was the language of the Kingdom of Aksum. Ge'ez is no longer spoken as a mother tongue, but remains the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and Ethiopian Catholic and Beta Israel communities — comparable in role to Latin in Western Christianity.

History

The earliest Ge'ez inscriptions date to the pre-Aksumite period, written first in the South Arabian consonantal script and then in the dedicated Ge'ez script, which by the 4th century CE had developed vowel markings, becoming an abugida. When Christianity was adopted under King Ezana in the 4th century, the Bible was translated into Ge'ez, producing one of the earliest complete Bible translations. As a spoken vernacular, Ge'ez gave way to Amharic in the south and Tigrinya and Tigre in the north by around the 10th–13th centuries, but written Ge'ez literature — chronicles, hagiographies, theology and poetry (qene) — continued to be produced into the modern era.

Script

The Ge'ez script (fidäl) is an abugida: 26 consonant characters each appear in seven "orders" modified to indicate the following vowel, yielding well over 180 core syllabic symbols, plus labialised forms. It is written left to right and historically used a word-divider of two vertical dots and its own numeral system. The script is now used to write Amharic, Tigrinya, Tigre, Blin and several other languages.

Linguistics

Ge'ez is a typical Semitic language in morphology: consonantal roots (usually triconsonantal) interdigitate with vowel patterns to form words, and verbs inflect for person, number and gender. It preserves archaic features lost in its modern descendants, and its phonology included emphatic consonants and a richer sibilant system. Modern liturgical pronunciation differs between the Ethiopian and Eritrean traditions.
