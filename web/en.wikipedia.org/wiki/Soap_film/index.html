Soap film

A soap film is a thin film of soapy water, a few micrometres to nanometres thick, that forms a surface of minimal area between boundary wires or frames. Soap films are classical physical models of minimal surfaces in mathematics.

A soap film consists of a layer of water sandwiched between two layers of soap molecules, whose hydrophilic heads face the water and hydrophobic tails face the air. Surface tension drives the film to adopt the configuration of least area consistent with its boundaries, which is why soap films solve Plateau's problem experimentally — work systematised by Joseph Plateau in the 19th century and later studied mathematically by Jessie Douglas and Tibor Radó.

Colours

The shimmering colours of a soap film arise from thin-film interference: light reflected from the front and back surfaces interferes constructively or destructively depending on film thickness and wavelength. As the film drains under gravity it thins at the top, producing horizontal colour bands; a fully drained region appears black shortly before the film ruptures.

Applications

Soap-film experiments informed architecture — notably Frei Otto's tensile roof structures such as the Munich Olympic Stadium — and remain a tool in fluid dynamics research and science education.
