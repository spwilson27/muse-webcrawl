Bibliometrics

Bibliometrics is the quantitative analysis of books, articles, and other publications, using statistical methods to measure patterns in scholarly communication. It is widely used to evaluate research output, journal influence, and the impact of individual researchers and institutions.

History

The term "bibliometrics" was popularised by Alan Pritchard in 1969. Early foundations include Lotka's law of scientific productivity (1926), Bradford's law of scattering (1934), and Zipf's law. In the 1960s Eugene Garfield founded the Institute for Scientific Information and created the Science Citation Index, enabling systematic citation analysis.

Methods and indicators

Common bibliometric indicators include citation counts, the journal impact factor, the h-index (proposed by Jorge Hirsch in 2005), co-citation and bibliographic coupling, and altmetrics based on social media attention. Methods analyse publication and citation networks to map the structure of scientific fields.

Uses and criticism

Bibliometrics informs library collection management, funding decisions, and research assessment. Critics note that citation counts can be gamed, differ across fields, and do not directly measure quality; initiatives such as the San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA) and the Leiden Manifesto urge responsible use of metrics.
