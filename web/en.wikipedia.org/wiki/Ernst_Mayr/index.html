Ernst Mayr

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ernst Mayr (1904–2005) was a German-American evolutionary biologist, one of the architects of the modern evolutionary synthesis and among the most influential biologists of the 20th century. His book Systematics and the Origin of Species (1942) explained speciation in terms of genetics and geography, and his 'biological species concept' — species as groups of interbreeding populations — became standard.

Career and ideas

As a young ornithologist Mayr collected birds in New Guinea and the Solomon Islands, then spent most of his career at Harvard's Museum of Comparative Zoology. He championed allopatric speciation — new species arising when populations are geographically separated — studied the founder effect with Sewall Wright's ideas in view, and later wrote extensively on the philosophy and history of biology, arguing for biology's autonomy from physics-style reductionism.

Honours

Mayr described numerous bird species and received biology's triple crown of major prizes (the Balzan, Crafoord and International Biology prizes). He lived to 100, publishing into his final years, and is often called 'the Darwin of the 20th century'.
