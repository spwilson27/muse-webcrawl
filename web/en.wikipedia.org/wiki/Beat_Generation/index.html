Beat Generation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A 1950s New York bar booth: three young writers over coffee and manuscripts, neon rain on the window behind them]

The Beat Generation was a literary subculture movement started by a group of authors in New York in the late 1940s whose work explored and influenced American culture and politics in the post-war era. Its core figures — Jack Kerouac (who coined the name), Allen Ginsberg and William S. Burroughs — met around Columbia University and Times Square's demimonde, united by a hunger for experience (jazz, drugs, travel, sex, Eastern religion) and a rejection of materialist respectability.

The movement's canonical texts arrived in a rush: Kerouac's On the Road (1957, written on a scroll in 1951), Ginsberg's Howl (1956 — its obscenity trial a landmark for free expression) and Burroughs's Naked Lunch (1959). 'Beat' carried Kerouac's double sense: beaten down, and beatific.

The circle

Around the three principals gathered Gregory Corso, Lawrence Ferlinghetti (whose City Lights published Howl), Neal Cassady (On the Road's Dean Moriarty, the movement's holy fool), and later the West Coast and Black Mountain poets; women of the circle — Diane di Prima, Hettie Jones, Joyce Johnson — long under-credited, have been restored by later scholarship. The 1940s murders and madhouses in the circle's past (the Lucien Carr killing, Ginsberg's asylum stay) supplied its founding myths of damage and vision.

Influence

The Beats were the 1960s counterculture's advance guard: Dylan, the hippies, punk and the confessional century all read them. Critics remain divided — Norman Mailer and Truman Capote's jibes at typing-versus-writing stand for the prosecution — but the movement's opening of American literature to vernacular speech, Buddhism, homosexuality and first thought is irreversible.
