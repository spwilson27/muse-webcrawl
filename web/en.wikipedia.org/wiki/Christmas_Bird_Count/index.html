Christmas Bird Count

The Christmas Bird Count (CBC) is a census of birds in the Western Hemisphere, performed annually in the early Northern Hemisphere winter by volunteer birdwatchers and administered by the National Audubon Society. The purpose is provided by the bird counts and the information they give about population trends of birds.

History

The count grew out of a tradition of Christmas "side hunts", in which hunters competed to shoot the most birds and animals. Ornithologist Frank M. Chapman, an early officer of the Audubon Society, proposed instead a "Christmas Bird Census" in which birds would be counted rather than hunted. The first count was held on Christmas Day 1900, with 27 observers at 25 locations in North America, tallying about 90 species.

Methodology

Each count is carried out within a circle 15 miles (24 km) in diameter on a single day between 14 December and 5 January. Volunteers count every bird seen or heard, and also record the time spent and distance travelled by each party, so that results can be corrected for effort. More than 2,000 count circles are active across the Americas and the Pacific, involving tens of thousands of participants.

Scientific use

With over a century of data, the CBC is one of the longest-running citizen science projects in the world. Its data have been used by researchers and agencies such as the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to track shifts in winter bird ranges, including poleward movements associated with climate change.
