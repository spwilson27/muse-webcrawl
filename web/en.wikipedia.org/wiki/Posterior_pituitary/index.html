Posterior pituitary

The posterior pituitary (or neurohypophysis) is the posterior lobe of the pituitary gland, which is part of the endocrine system. Unlike the anterior pituitary, it does not synthesize its own hormones; instead, it stores and releases two hormones produced by neurons of the hypothalamus — oxytocin and vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone, ADH) — which travel down axons through the infundibulum (pituitary stalk) and are released into the systemic circulation from axon terminals.

Anatomy

The posterior pituitary consists mainly of unmyelinated axons of magnocellular neurons whose cell bodies lie in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus, plus specialized glial cells called pituicytes. It includes the pars nervosa and the infundibular stalk connecting it to the median eminence. Its blood supply comes from the inferior hypophyseal arteries, and its fenestrated capillaries allow rapid hormone release into the general circulation.

Hormones

Vasopressin (ADH) regulates water reabsorption in the kidney collecting ducts and causes vasoconstriction at high concentrations; deficiency causes diabetes insipidus. Oxytocin triggers uterine contractions in childbirth and milk ejection during breastfeeding, and is implicated in social bonding. Both are nine-amino-acid peptides differing at only two residues.

Clinical significance

Damage to the stalk or hypothalamus can cause central diabetes insipidus, while inappropriate excess ADH secretion (SIADH) leads to dilutional hyponatremia. The bright spot of the posterior pituitary on T1-weighted MRI, reflecting stored hormone granules, disappears in central diabetes insipidus.
