Music archaeology

Music archaeology is an interdisciplinary field combining musicology and archaeology that studies the music of past cultures through their material remains: surviving instruments, iconography, written sources and acoustic spaces. It overlaps with ethnomusicology and organology, and is also called archaeomusicology or, in earlier scholarship, "the archaeology of sound".

Evidence and methods

Researchers analyse excavated instruments — from the bone and ivory flutes of Geissenklösterle and Divje Babe, dated to around 40,000 years ago and among the oldest known instruments, to the bronze lur horns of Scandinavia, the Celtic carnyx, Greek auloi and Roman organs. Because organic materials rarely survive, iconography (paintings, reliefs, vase art) and texts such as the cuneiform tablets of Mesopotamia — including the Hurrian Hymn to Nikkal (c. 1400 BC), the oldest substantially complete notated song — are essential sources. Experimental archaeology reconstructs instruments and attempts to play them; modern work also uses CT scanning, 3D printing and acoustic measurement to study fragile originals without damaging them. The International Study Group on Music Archaeology (ISGMA) has coordinated the field since 1998.

Interpretive challenges

Reconstructing how ancient music actually sounded is difficult: scales, tunings, playing techniques and performance contexts must be inferred from incomplete evidence, and replicas may reflect modern assumptions. Music archaeologists therefore emphasise documenting the physical evidence rigorously before speculating on performance.
