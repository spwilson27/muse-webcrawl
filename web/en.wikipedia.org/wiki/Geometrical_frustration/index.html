Geometrical frustration

Frustrated spins on a triangular lattice

In condensed matter physics, geometrical frustration occurs when a system cannot simultaneously satisfy all of its local interaction preferences because of the geometry of its lattice. The term is most often used for magnetic systems: on a triangular lattice, antiferromagnetic interactions between neighbouring spins cannot all be satisfied at once, since if two spins point oppositely, a third spin cannot be opposite to both.

Consequences

Frustration suppresses conventional magnetic ordering, often down to very low temperatures, and can produce highly degenerate ground states with extensive residual entropy — as in the water-ice-like "spin ice" compounds such as dysprosium titanate. Frustrated magnets are leading candidates for hosting quantum spin liquid states, in which spins remain dynamically disordered even at absolute zero and excitations carry fractional quantum numbers.

Examples

  • The triangular-lattice antiferromagnet
  • The kagome lattice, a network of corner-sharing triangles
  • Pyrochlore lattices of corner-sharing tetrahedra, including spin ices
  • Artificial spin ice: lithographed arrays of nanomagnets

Beyond magnetism

The concept extends to structural frustration in crystals and glasses, protein folding, and packing problems — anywhere that local ordering rules are incompatible with global geometry, giving rise to complex, degenerate or glassy behaviour. The idea was crystallised by Gérard Toulouse in 1977, borrowing from the earlier notion of frustration in spin glasses.
