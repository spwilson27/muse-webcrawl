Conditional probability

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Venn diagram illustrating conditional probability: the probability of A given B is the share of B's area also covered by A]

In probability theory, conditional probability is the probability of an event occurring given that another event has already occurred. It is written P(A|B) and defined, when P(B) is greater than zero, as the probability of both events occurring divided by the probability of B.

Conditional probability is the foundation of Bayesian inference, medical testing, and much of statistics: a positive test result, for example, means something very different depending on how common the disease is in the population being tested.

Definition and examples

Two events are independent when the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other, in which case P(A|B) = P(A). The multiplication rule, the law of total probability and Bayes' theorem all follow from the definition of conditional probability.

Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A) is a common reasoning error, sometimes called the prosecutor's fallacy in legal contexts.
