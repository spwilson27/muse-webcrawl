Patriation

Patriation is the political process that led to full Canadian sovereignty, culminating with the Constitution Act, 1982. The word (from the Latin patria, "homeland") was coined to describe "bringing home" the Canadian constitution: before 1982, amendments to Canada's principal constitutional document, the British North America Act 1867, could only be enacted by the Parliament of the United Kingdom, and Canada had no domestic amending formula.

Queen Elizabeth II signed the proclamation of the Constitution Act, 1982 on Parliament Hill, 17 April 1982.

Background

The Statute of Westminster 1931 had granted Canada and the other Dominions legislative independence, but at Canada's request the British North America Act was exempted because federal and provincial governments could not agree on a domestic amending procedure. Repeated attempts to patriate the constitution — in 1927, 1950, 1960–61, 1964 (the Fulton–Favreau formula), and 1971 (the Victoria Charter) — all failed over the amending formula, language rights, or the division of powers.

The 1980–82 process

After winning the 1980 Quebec referendum on sovereignty, Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau pledged a renewed federalism and moved to patriate the constitution unilaterally when provincial agreement again proved elusive. The Supreme Court of Canada ruled in the Patriation Reference (1981) that unilateral action was legal but contrary to constitutional convention, which required a "substantial degree" of provincial consent. Negotiations resumed, and on 5 November 1981 the federal government and nine provinces (all but Quebec) reached agreement in what became known as the "Kitchen Accord." The United Kingdom Parliament then passed the Canada Act 1982, renouncing its power to legislate for Canada, and the Constitution Act, 1982 was proclaimed on 17 April 1982.

Content and legacy

Patriation gave Canada a domestic amending formula (the general "7/50" rule plus areas requiring unanimity), entrenched the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, recognized existing Aboriginal and treaty rights, and included a notwithstanding clause allowing legislatures to override certain Charter rights. Quebec's exclusion from the 1981 accord — the night of negotiations is remembered in Quebec as the "Night of the Long Knives" — fuelled two failed attempts at reconciliation, the Meech Lake Accord (1987–90) and the Charlottetown Accord (1992), and remains a grievance in Quebec politics.
