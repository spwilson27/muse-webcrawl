Flue-gas desulfurization

Flue-gas desulfurization (FGD) is a set of technologies used to remove sulfur dioxide (SO₂) from exhaust flue gases of fossil-fuel power plants and other sulfur-oxide-emitting processes. Removing SO₂ from emissions reduces acid rain and respiratory health hazards; FGD systems were installed on a large scale in response to clean-air legislation from the 1970s onward.

A wet limestone scrubber tower

Methods

The most widely used approach is wet scrubbing with an alkaline sorbent, usually limestone or lime slurry, in a spray tower or absorber. The SO₂ reacts with calcium carbonate to form calcium sulfite, which is commonly oxidised further to calcium sulfate (gypsum), a saleable by-product used in wallboard. Wet systems typically remove 90% or more of the SO₂.

Alternative processes include spray-dry scrubbing, in which a sorbent slurry is atomised into the hot gas and the dry product is collected with the fly ash, and dry sorbent injection, in which powdered sorbent is blown directly into the furnace or duct. Seawater scrubbing is used at some coastal plants, exploiting the natural alkalinity of seawater.

History

Early FGD experiments took place in England in the 1930s around London power stations, but large-scale deployment came with the US Clean Air Act of 1970 and its 1990 amendments, and with equivalent legislation in Europe and later China. FGD is credited with much of the dramatic decline in SO₂ emissions and acid-rain damage in North America and Europe.
