Dividend tax

A dividend tax is a tax imposed on the dividends received by shareholders of a company. Because dividends are paid from corporate profits that have already borne corporation tax, taxing them again in shareholders' hands produces what critics call double taxation of corporate income; many tax systems therefore relieve the overlap in one way or another.

Systems

Under a classical system, corporate profits and dividends are taxed separately with no relief (historically the United States model). Under an imputation system (used in Australia and formerly in much of Europe), shareholders receive credits (such as Australian franking credits) for tax already paid at the company level. Other countries exempt part of the dividend, tax dividends at reduced "qualified" rates, or levy a withholding tax at source, commonly creditable under tax treaties — cross-border dividend withholding typically ranges from 0% to 30% and is a central topic of international tax planning.

By country

In the United States, "qualified dividends" have since 2003 been taxed at the lower capital-gains rates (0%, 15% or 20%, plus a possible net investment income tax), while non-qualified dividends are taxed as ordinary income. The United Kingdom taxes dividends above a small allowance at rates lower than those on employment income, within its own bands. Many continental European countries apply flat withholding rates of roughly 25–30%.

Debate

Economists debate whether dividend taxes distort corporate payout policy (encouraging retention of earnings or share buy-backs over dividends), raise the cost of equity capital, or simply fall on shareholders as owners of the underlying capital. The "new view" of dividend taxation argues that for investment financed from retained earnings, dividend taxes are capitalised into share prices and do not affect marginal investment decisions.
