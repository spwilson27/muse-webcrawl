Cayley's theorem

In group theory, Cayley's theorem, named after Arthur Cayley, states that every group G is isomorphic to a subgroup of a symmetric group. In particular, every finite group of order n is isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group Sn of permutations of n elements.

Proof

The proof lets G act on itself by left multiplication: each element g defines a permutation of the set G sending x to gx. This action gives a homomorphism from G into the symmetric group on G, and the homomorphism is injective because the permutation associated with g moves the identity element to g itself. Hence G is isomorphic to its image, a subgroup of permutations.

Significance

The theorem justifies studying groups abstractly through permutation groups, and historically it helped establish that the abstract and concrete (permutation) notions of a group coincide. Its downside is that the embedding into Sn may be far from economical, motivating the study of smaller faithful permutation representations.
