Newton's laws of motion

Newton's laws of motion are three physical laws that form the basis of classical mechanics. Published by Isaac Newton in his Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687), they describe the relationship between the motion of an object and the forces acting on it.

First law

An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion continues in motion at constant velocity in a straight line, unless acted upon by a net external force. This is the law of inertia, previously articulated by Galileo.

Second law

The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass, summarised as F = ma. More generally, force equals the rate of change of momentum.

Third law

For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction: when one body exerts a force on a second body, the second body simultaneously exerts a force of equal magnitude in the opposite direction on the first.

Scope and limits

The laws apply in inertial reference frames and describe everyday phenomena with great accuracy. They are superseded at very high speeds by special relativity, at atomic scales by quantum mechanics, and in strong gravitational fields by general relativity, but remain an excellent approximation for most engineering purposes.
