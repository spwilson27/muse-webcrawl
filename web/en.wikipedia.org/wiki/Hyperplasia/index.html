Hyperplasia

Hyperplasia (from Greek hyper, "over" + plasis, "formation"), or hypergenesis, is an enlargement of an organ or tissue caused by an increase in the amount of organic tissue that results from cell proliferation. It may lead to the gross enlargement of an organ, and the term is sometimes confused with benign neoplasia or benign tumours.

Distinguishing features

Hyperplasia differs from hypertrophy, in which cells increase in size rather than number, though the two processes often occur together. Unlike neoplasia, hyperplastic proliferation remains under physiological control, preserves normal tissue architecture, and regresses when the stimulus is removed. It is nevertheless a possible precursor of neoplastic growth in some settings, such as atypical endometrial hyperplasia.

Examples

Physiological hyperplasia includes the proliferation of the breast epithelium during pregnancy and the regeneration of liver tissue after partial hepatectomy. Pathological examples include benign prostatic hyperplasia, endometrial hyperplasia caused by unopposed estrogen, gingival hyperplasia induced by certain drugs, and the skin thickening seen in psoriasis and callus formation.

Mechanism

Hyperplasia results from growth factors acting on mature cells or from activation of tissue stem cells. Only tissues containing cells capable of division can undergo it; permanent cells such as cardiac muscle respond by hypertrophy instead.
