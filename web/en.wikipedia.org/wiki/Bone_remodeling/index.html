Bone remodeling (or bone metabolism) is a lifelong process in which mature bone tissue is removed from the skeleton (a process called bone resorption) and new bone tissue is formed (a process called ossification or new bone formation). These two processes, normally tightly coupled, maintain bone strength, repair microdamage, and regulate calcium homeostasis. In the first year of life, almost 100% of the skeleton is replaced; in adults, roughly ten percent of skeletal mass is remodeled annually.

Physiology

Remodeling occurs in discrete packets called basic multicellular units (BMUs). The cycle proceeds through phases: activation, in which osteoclast precursors are recruited; resorption, in which multinucleated osteoclasts dissolve mineral and degrade matrix over about two weeks; reversal; and formation, in which osteoblasts lay down osteoid that later mineralizes over several months. Osteocytes, former osteoblasts embedded in the matrix, sense mechanical strain and microdamage and direct the process.

Regulation

The RANK/RANKL/osteoprotegerin (OPG) axis is the central regulator: osteoblast-lineage cells express RANKL, which activates osteoclasts via RANK, while OPG acts as a decoy receptor that inhibits the interaction. Systemic hormones — parathyroid hormone, calcitriol (active vitamin D), calcitonin, and estrogens — modulate remodeling, as do local growth factors including BMPs and IGFs. Mechanical loading suppresses sclerostin, permitting Wnt signaling and bone formation (an application of Wolff's law).

Clinical significance

An imbalance in which resorption exceeds formation leads to osteoporosis and increased fracture risk, particularly after menopause when estrogen falls. Excessive remodeling characterizes Paget's disease of bone. Antiresorptive drugs such as bisphosphonates and denosumab, and anabolic agents such as teriparatide, target remodeling pathways.
