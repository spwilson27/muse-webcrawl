Intelligentsia

The intelligentsia is a social class or status group consisting of intellectuals — writers, academics, artists, teachers and other cultural workers — who are engaged in shaping the culture and politics of their society and who typically see themselves as guides to social progress. The word derives from Russian intelligentsiya and Polish inteligencja, and came into wide use in the 19th century in the Russian Empire, where it denoted educated people alienated from the autocratic state and often committed to radical reform.

Origins

In partitioned Poland, the intelligentsia emerged from impoverished gentry and urban professionals who preserved national culture under foreign rule. In Russia, the term was popularised in the 1860s–1870s and described a stratum defined less by occupation than by a moral and political outlook: critical of the existing order, devoted to the "people", and inclined to discuss literature and philosophy as matters of urgent social significance. Figures such as Vissarion Belinsky, Alexander Herzen and the populist (Narodnik) movement typified this role. Unlike the Western European notion of "intellectuals", the Russian intelligentsia implied a quasi-missionary self-consciousness and estrangement from both state and commercial society.

In the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe

After 1917 the Bolsheviks both relied on and repressed the intelligentsia; the 1922 "philosophers' ships" expelled prominent thinkers. Soviet sociology later classified the intelligentsia as a stratum of mental workers between classes rather than a class itself. In postwar Eastern Europe, intellectuals were central to dissident movements — Charter 77 in Czechoslovakia and Solidarity in Poland, where intellectuals allied with workers — and to the transitions of 1989.

Usage and critique

In English the word often carries a slightly ironic or pejorative tone, suggesting a self-regarding cultural elite. Sociologists such as Alvin Gouldner analysed intellectuals as a "new class" wielding cultural capital, while critics have accused intelligentsias of abstraction, dogmatism and contempt for ordinary life. The concept remains useful in discussing the political role of educated strata in societies where they form a distinct, self-aware group.
