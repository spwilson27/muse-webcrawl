Special administrative regions of China

The special administrative regions (SAR) of the People's Republic of China are one of four types of province-level divisions of China. They are directly under the control of the Central People's Government and enjoy a high degree of autonomy. There are two special administrative regions: Hong Kong and Macau.

Constitutional basis

Article 31 of the Constitution of China authorizes the National People's Congress to establish special administrative regions and prescribe the systems to be instituted there. The arrangement is commonly summarized in the principle of "one country, two systems", under which the SARs maintain their own legal, economic and political systems distinct from mainland China for a period of fifty years after their establishment.

Hong Kong

Hong Kong became a special administrative region on 1 July 1997, when sovereignty was transferred from the United Kingdom to China. Its mini-constitution is the Basic Law of Hong Kong. It maintains its own currency, the Hong Kong dollar, its own legal system based on English common law, and separate customs and immigration controls.

Macau

Macau became a special administrative region on 20 December 1999, when sovereignty was transferred from Portugal to China. Governed by its own Basic Law, Macau retains the pataca as its currency and is best known for its tourism and casino gaming industries.

Autonomy

The SARs are responsible for their internal affairs, including executive, legislative and judicial powers, with the Court of Final Appeal in Hong Kong and the Court of Final Appeal in Macau serving as the highest courts. The central government retains responsibility for foreign affairs and defence.
