Paper crane

The paper crane (Japanese: 折鶴, orizuru) is a traditional Japanese origami design depicting a crane. It is the most famous of all origami models and a symbol of peace, hope, healing, and longevity in Japanese culture, where the crane itself is regarded as a mystical creature said to live for a thousand years.

History and construction

Origami folding in Japan dates to the Edo period, and illustrated instructions for the paper crane appear in the Hiden Senbazuru Orikata (1797), the oldest known origami book, which explains how to fold connected strings of cranes from a single sheet. The classic crane is folded from one square sheet of paper using the bird base, without cuts or glue, forming a body, neck, tail, and two wings.

One thousand cranes and Sadako Sasaki

Japanese tradition holds that folding one thousand paper cranes (senbazuru) grants a wish, long life, or recovery from illness. The practice became known worldwide through Sadako Sasaki, a girl exposed to radiation from the atomic bombing of Hiroshima who began folding cranes during her illness with leukemia and died in 1955 at age twelve. Her story made the paper crane an international symbol of peace; the Children's Peace Monument in Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park is topped by a statue of Sadako holding a crane, and visitors leave millions of origami cranes there each year. Strings of a thousand cranes are also given as wedding gifts and to the seriously ill.
