National Committee for a Free Germany

The National Committee for a Free Germany (German: Nationalkomitee Freies Deutschland, NKFD) was an anti-Nazi organisation founded in the Soviet Union on 12–13 July 1943, composed of German Communist exiles and German prisoners of war captured after the Battle of Stalingrad. Sponsored by the Soviet government, it published the newspaper Freies Deutschland and broadcast appeals urging German soldiers to overthrow Hitler and end the war.

Foundation and aims

The NKFD was established at Krasnogorsk near Moscow, with the exiled Communist writer Erich Weinert as president. Its founding manifesto, addressed to the Wehrmacht and the German people, demanded the removal of Hitler, an immediate end to the war, withdrawal to Germany's borders, and a free, democratic Germany — language deliberately pitched to appeal beyond Communist sympathisers. In September 1943 captured senior officers founded the allied League of German Officers (Bund Deutscher Offiziere) under General Walther von Seydlitz-Kurzbach; Field Marshal Friedrich Paulus, captured at Stalingrad, eventually lent his voice to NKFD radio broadcasts in 1944.

Activities

The committee's propaganda included newspapers and leaflets dropped over German lines, loudspeaker appeals at the front, and radio programmes in which POW officers urged surrender. Members such as Heinz Keßler and Markus Wolf worked in the organisation. Front-line results were modest — few soldiers defected because of the appeals, and Nazi propaganda denounced the members as traitors — but the NKFD had considerable symbolic value, and the Nazi regime retaliated by arresting members' families under Sippenhaft.

Dissolution and legacy

The NKFD was dissolved on 2 November 1945. Many of its Communist cadres, including Wilhelm Pieck and Walter Ulbricht's groups of returnees, went on to build the Soviet occupation zone and the German Democratic Republic, and a number of its officer-members served in East Germany's National People's Army. West German opinion long viewed the committee with suspicion as a Soviet instrument; historians generally see it as both a genuine anti-Nazi resistance effort and a tool of Soviet policy.
