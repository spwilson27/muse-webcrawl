Finery forge

A finery forge is a forge used to produce wrought iron from pig iron by decarburisation. In the traditional ironworks of early modern Europe, the finery was paired with a chafery: pig iron from the blast furnace was melted and oxidised in the finery hearth to burn out carbon, then the resulting pasty mass of iron was reheated in the chafery and hammered under a water-powered trip hammer to expel slag and consolidate it into bar iron.

Process

In the finery hearth, a charcoal fire was blasted with air from bellows. The finer melted pig iron in front of the tuyère; oxygen from the blast oxidised carbon (and silicon and manganese) out of the metal, raising its melting point so that it gradually collected as a spongy, semi-solid lump — the "loop" — at the bottom of the hearth. The ironmaster worked and turned the mass with a long bar to expose it evenly to the blast. Because the process worked by oxidation in an open hearth, the iron was refined but the yield was lossy and consumption of charcoal high.

The loop was then taken to the chafery, a second hearth where it was reheated to welding heat and shingled under the hammer into a bloom and then drawn out into bars of wrought iron, the standard raw material for blacksmiths, nailers, and other iron trades.

History

The finery forge was the key refining technology of the indirect process of ironmaking that spread across Europe from the late medieval period: ore was smelted to high-carbon pig iron in the blast furnace, then converted to malleable iron in the forge. In England, finery forges were integral to ironworks in the Weald, the Forest of Dean, and the West Midlands; the Walloon variant of the process was introduced to Sweden, where it produced prized "oregrund" iron. In the 1780s, Henry Cort's puddling process, using coal-fired reverberatory furnaces and grooved rolls, began to displace finery forges in Britain, though charcoal fineries survived into the 19th century in Sweden (the Lancashire hearth being a refined descendant) and elsewhere where charcoal remained cheap.

Archaeology

Surviving and excavated finery forges — with their hearths, wheel pits, and hammer bases — are important monuments of industrial archaeology, documenting the organisation and water-power systems of pre-industrial ironmaking.
