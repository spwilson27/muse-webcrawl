Secret Service code name

Secret Service code names are pseudonyms assigned by the United States Secret Service to individuals and locations under its protection, as well as to certain other prominent figures. The names were originally adopted for security purposes when sensitive communications were transmitted over insecure or easily monitored channels; today they persist largely as a matter of tradition, since modern encrypted communications have removed most of the original security rationale.

Secret Service agents use code names such as "Eagle" and "Evergreen" to refer to protectees in radio communications.

History

The practice dates to the era when radio communications could readily be intercepted. Code names gave agents a quick, unambiguous way to refer to protectees without using their real names. During the Second World War, for example, President Franklin D. Roosevelt was assigned code names, and the custom was later formalized for presidents, vice presidents, their families, and other protectees.

Presidential code names

Well-known examples include "Eagle" for Bill Clinton, "Renegade" for Barack Obama, "Mogul" for Donald Trump, and "Celtic" for Joe Biden. First ladies and family members receive related names, often beginning with the same letter: Michelle Obama was "Renaissance," Melania Trump was "Muse," and Jill Biden was "Capri." John F. Kennedy was "Lancer," Lyndon B. Johnson was "Volunteer," Richard Nixon was "Searchlight," Gerald Ford was "Passkey," Jimmy Carter was "Lockmaster" (he had earlier been "Deacon"), Ronald Reagan was "Rawhide," and George H. W. Bush was "Timberwolf," while George W. Bush was first "Tumbler" and later "Trailblazer."

Locations and objects

Code names are also assigned to places and things: the White House has been known as "Castle" and later "Olympus," Air Force One's presidential aircraft and Camp David have had their own designations, and the presidential state car is nicknamed "The Beast." Vice-presidential and cabinet protectees, foreign dignitaries on visits, and major events also receive names under the system.

Selection

Protectees are typically offered a list of names cleared for use and may choose among them; names are checked to avoid duplication or confusion with other protectees. Some protectees keep a code name for life as former presidents retain protection.
