Quadrangle (architecture)

In architecture, a quadrangle (or quad) is a courtyard or open space enclosed by buildings on all four sides, typically rectangular or square in plan. The form descends from the Roman atrium and the medieval monastic cloister, and became a defining feature of collegiate and institutional architecture.

History

The cloisters of medieval monasteries — covered walks around a square garth — provided the model for the colleges of Oxford and Cambridge, where ranges of rooms for scholars were arranged around a court entered through a gatehouse. Famous examples include the Great Court of Trinity College, Cambridge, and the Tom Quad of Christ Church, Oxford. Renaissance palaces and country houses adopted the courtyard plan, and the quadrangle later spread to schools, hospitals, barracks, prisons and civic complexes.

Collegiate use

At Oxford and Cambridge the quad traditionally houses student and fellows' rooms on staircases, a chapel, hall and library, with a lawn that in many colleges may be walked on only by fellows. American universities adopted the type widely — the "residential quadrangles" of Yale and Harvard, and the great lawns of Jefferson's Academical Village at the University of Virginia, owe much to the Oxbridge model.
