Ottawa Treaty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ottawa Treaty, formally the Convention on the Prohibition of the Use, Stockpiling, Production and Transfer of Anti-Personnel Mines and on Their Destruction, is an international treaty that comprehensively bans anti-personnel landmines, requiring parties to destroy stockpiles within four years and clear mined areas within ten (extensions granted). Adopted in Oslo on 18 September 1997 and opened for signature in Ottawa in December 1997, it entered into force on 1 March 1999 and has more than 160 parties.

The treaty was the product of the “Ottawa process” — a fast-track negotiation outside the deadlocked UN machinery, driven by the International Campaign to Ban Landmines (a coalition founded in 1992; Nobel Peace Prize 1997, shared with Jody Williams) and middle-power states led by Canada (Foreign Minister Lloyd Axworthy). Major military powers remain outside — the United States, Russia, China, India and Pakistan among them — though the US has largely aligned its policy with the treaty outside the Korean peninsula. In 2025, several European states bordering Russia (the Baltic states, Poland and Finland) announced or began withdrawal procedures citing changed security conditions — the treaty's first major reversals, taking effect six months after deposit under the Convention's terms.
