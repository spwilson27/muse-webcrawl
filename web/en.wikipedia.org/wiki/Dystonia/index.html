Dystonia is a neurological movement disorder characterised by sustained or intermittent involuntary muscle contractions that cause twisting and repetitive movements, abnormal postures, or both. The movements are typically patterned and may be accompanied by tremor. Dystonia is thought to result from dysfunction of the basal ganglia and related sensorimotor networks.

Classification

Dystonia is classified by the body regions affected: focal dystonia involves a single region (for example cervical dystonia of the neck, blepharospasm of the eyelids, or writer's cramp of the hand); segmental dystonia involves adjacent regions; multifocal involves non-contiguous regions; and generalized dystonia involves the trunk and at least two other sites. It is also divided into primary (isolated, often hereditary) and secondary or combined forms associated with other neurological features or acquired causes.

Causes

Several genes are associated with inherited dystonia, including TOR1A (DYT1), which causes early-onset generalized dystonia, and THAP1 (DYT6). Dopa-responsive dystonia results from defects in dopamine synthesis and responds dramatically to levodopa. Acquired dystonia can follow stroke, head injury, infections, or exposure to dopamine-blocking drugs (tardive dystonia). Task-specific dystonias, such as musician's dystonia, affect highly trained movements.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms often begin subtly and worsen with fatigue, stress or voluntary movement. A characteristic feature is the geste antagoniste, a sensory trick — such as touching the chin in cervical dystonia — that temporarily reduces the abnormal posture. Pain is common, particularly in cervical dystonia. Cognition is usually preserved in isolated dystonia.

Treatment

There is no cure, but several treatments reduce symptoms. Botulinum toxin injections into affected muscles are the mainstay for focal dystonia. Oral medications include anticholinergics (such as trihexyphenidyl), baclofen and benzodiazepines. For severe generalized dystonia, deep brain stimulation of the globus pallidus internus can be effective. Physiotherapy, occupational therapy and speech therapy support function.

History

The term dystonia was introduced by German neurologist Hermann Oppenheim in 1911, describing children with what he called dystonia musculorum deformans. For much of the twentieth century dystonia was wrongly considered psychiatric in origin, until clinical and genetic research established its neurological basis.
