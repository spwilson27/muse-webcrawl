Exodus

Exodus (from Ancient Greek éxodos, "exit" or "departure") most commonly refers to the Exodus, the founding myth of the Israelites in which Moses leads the enslaved Israelites out of Egypt, through the wilderness, and toward the Promised Land of Canaan. The narrative is told principally in the Book of Exodus, the second book of the Hebrew Bible and of the Christian Old Testament.

The narrative

According to the biblical account, the Israelites, descendants of Jacob, were enslaved in Egypt under a Pharaoh who feared their numbers. Moses, raised in the Pharaoh's household, was called by God at the burning bush to demand their release. When Pharaoh refused, God sent ten plagues upon Egypt; the final plague, the death of the firstborn, spared Israelite households marked with lamb's blood — the origin of Passover. Pharaoh relented, then pursued the fleeing Israelites, whose escape was completed when the sea parted for them and closed over the Egyptian army. At Mount Sinai, Moses received the Ten Commandments and the Law; after forty years of wandering in the desert, the generation of the Exodus died and their children prepared to enter Canaan under Joshua.

Historicity

Mainstream scholarship regards the Exodus as a foundational cultural memory rather than a documented historical event on the biblical scale: no direct Egyptian records of such a mass departure exist, and archaeological surveys of the Sinai have not found evidence of a large migrating population. Many scholars hold that the story crystallised around smaller historical kernels — such as Semitic groups entering and leaving Egypt (compare the Hyksos) — and reached its written form during the first millennium BCE.

Legacy

The Exodus is central to Jewish identity and liturgy, and its themes of liberation from bondage have been taken up in Christian theology, African-American spirituals and civil-rights rhetoric, and liberation theology.
