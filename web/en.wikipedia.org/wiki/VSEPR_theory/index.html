VSEPR theory

Valence shell electron pair repulsion (VSEPR) theory is a model used in chemistry to predict the geometry of individual molecules from the number of electron pairs surrounding their central atoms. It is also called the Gillespie–Nyholm theory, after its two principal developers, Ronald Gillespie and Ronald Nyholm.

Overview

The central premise of VSEPR is that pairs of electrons in the valence shell of a central atom repel one another and adopt an arrangement that minimises that repulsion, thereby determining the molecular geometry. Both bonding pairs and lone pairs count, although lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs and so compress bond angles. Double and triple bonds are treated as a single electron domain for the purpose of predicting shape.

AXE method

Molecules are classified in the AXE notation: A is the central atom, X the number of bonded atoms (sigma bonds), and E the number of lone pairs. Common outcomes include linear (AX2), trigonal planar (AX3), tetrahedral (AX4), trigonal pyramidal (AX3E, as in ammonia), bent (AX2E2, as in water), trigonal bipyramidal (AX5), and octahedral (AX6) geometries.

History and limitations

The idea of electron-pair repulsion was anticipated by Sidgwick and Powell in 1940 and developed by Gillespie and Nyholm in 1957. VSEPR is a qualitative model: it predicts shapes remarkably well for main-group compounds but has exceptions, particularly among transition-metal complexes and some alkaline-earth halides, and more sophisticated treatments such as ligand close-packing and molecular orbital theory are used where it fails.
