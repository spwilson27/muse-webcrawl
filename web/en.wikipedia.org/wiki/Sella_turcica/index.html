Sella turcica

The sella turcica (Latin for "Turkish saddle") is a saddle-shaped depression in the sphenoid bone of the human skull, on the upper surface of the body of the sphenoid. Its bowl, the hypophyseal fossa, holds the pituitary gland (hypophysis), which is covered above by a fold of dura mater, the diaphragma sellae. The name comes from its resemblance to a Turkish saddle with its raised front and back.

The pituitary gland sits in the hypophyseal fossa of the sella turcica.

Anatomy

The sella is bounded in front by the tuberculum sellae and the anterior clinoid processes, and behind by the dorsum sellae, a plate of bone terminating above in the posterior clinoid processes. On either side run the cavernous sinuses, containing the internal carotid artery and several cranial nerves. The optic chiasm lies just above and in front of the diaphragm of the sella, above the pituitary stalk (infundibulum), which passes through the diaphragm to connect the gland to the hypothalamus.

Clinical significance

Because the pituitary gland occupies the sella, its enlargement on skull radiographs or MRI is a key sign of pituitary tumors (adenomas). A sella that appears enlarged while containing only cerebrospinal fluid and a flattened gland is called an empty sella, which may be an incidental finding or associated with raised intracranial pressure and hormonal disturbance (empty sella syndrome). Fractures and erosions of the sella also occur with raised intracranial pressure.
