The Scottish Reformation was the process by which Scotland broke with the papacy and developed a predominantly Calvinist national church, the Church of Scotland. It culminated in the Reformation Parliament of 1560, which adopted a Protestant confession of faith and abolished papal jurisdiction, though the church's final Presbyterian settlement was not achieved until later in the century.

Background

Lutheran ideas circulated in Scotland from the 1520s; the martyrdoms of Patrick Hamilton (1528) and the preacher George Wishart (1546) spread sympathy for reform, and Wishart's bodyguard John Knox became the movement's leader. Protestant nobles, the Lords of the Congregation, rose against the regent Mary of Guise in 1559; iconoclastic riots swept towns including Perth, St Andrews and Scone, where Scone Abbey was burned.

The settlement of 1560

With English help under the Treaty of Berwick, the French garrison withdrew by the Treaty of Edinburgh (1560). The Reformation Parliament adopted the Scots Confession, drafted by Knox and five other ministers (the "six Johns"), and outlawed the Mass. The First Book of Discipline proposed using old church wealth to fund ministers, schools in every parish and poor relief — ambitions only partly realised because nobles kept much of the land.

Aftermath

Mary, Queen of Scots returned from France in 1561 as a Catholic monarch ruling a Protestant kingdom; her deposition in 1567 secured the Reformation. Under Andrew Melville the Kirk adopted Presbyterian government, and disputes between crown and Kirk over episcopacy shaped Scottish politics through the Covenanters and beyond, leaving Presbyterianism central to Scottish identity.
