Dekatron

[Image: A glowing Dekatron counter tube]
A Dekatron with its glow resting on one cathode

A Dekatron (also called a Decatron) is a gas-filled decade counting tube that was used in early electronic counters and computers in the 1950s and 1960s. The name, a trademark of the Ericsson Telephones company of the United Kingdom, derives from its function of counting in tens. A Dekatron contains a central anode surrounded by ten cathode pins arranged in a circle, usually filled with neon or another inert gas.

Operation

A glow discharge rests on one cathode at a time, visibly marking the count through the glass envelope. Properly phased pulses applied to guide electrodes between the cathodes transfer the glow step by step around the circle, in either direction, at rates up to tens of thousands of counts per second (and about 1 MHz in hydrogen-filled high-speed types). When the glow completes a full revolution it generates a carry pulse for the next tube, so chains of Dekatrons form decade counters with a direct visual readout. Variants called selector Dekatrons had separate connections to each cathode, allowing the tube to act as a rotary switch or distributor.

Uses

Dekatrons were famously used as the visible registers of the Harwell Dekatron Computer (also known as WITCH, 1951), the world's oldest working digital computer, and in frequency counters, Geiger counters and early business machines, before being displaced by transistor counters and later by integrated circuits and seven-segment displays.
