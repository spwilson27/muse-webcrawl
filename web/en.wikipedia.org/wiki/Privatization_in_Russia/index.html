Privatization in Russia

Privatization in Russia was the transfer of state-owned assets to private ownership following the dissolution of the Soviet Union, principally during the 1990s under President Boris Yeltsin. It was one of the largest and most consequential privatization programmes in history, transferring the bulk of Russian industry to private hands within a few years, and it remains deeply controversial for its chaotic execution and the concentration of wealth it produced.

Voucher privatization

The first mass phase, led by Anatoly Chubais, ran from 1992 to 1994. Every Russian citizen received a privatization voucher with a face value of 10,000 roubles, which could be invested in shares of state enterprises, sold for cash, or placed in voucher investment funds. In practice, hyperinflation destroyed the vouchers' value, many funds proved fraudulent, and enterprise insiders — managers and workers given preferential share options — accumulated controlling stakes in many firms.

Loans for shares

In 1995 the government adopted the notorious loans-for-shares scheme. A small group of commercial banks lent money to the cash-strapped state in exchange for temporary stakes in crown-jewel companies in oil, metals and telecommunications — including Yukos, Norilsk Nickel and Sibneft. When the state predictably defaulted, the banks kept the shares, which they had effectively auctioned to themselves at prices far below market value. The scheme created the class of politically powerful business owners known as the oligarchs and helped finance Yeltsin's 1996 re-election campaign.

Consequences

Privatization coincided with a catastrophic fall in output: Russian GDP contracted by roughly 40% between 1991 and 1998, inequality soared, and public trust in markets and reformers collapsed. Defenders argue that privatization, however flawed, was irreversible-proof against a communist return and eventually enabled more efficient private ownership; critics regard the 1990s transfers — especially loans-for-shares — as essentially theft of national wealth. The renationalization of major assets such as Yukos in the 2000s partially reversed the process.
