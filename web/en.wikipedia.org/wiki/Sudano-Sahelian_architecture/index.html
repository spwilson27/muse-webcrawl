Sudano-Sahelian architecture

Sudano-Sahelian architecture refers to a range of similar indigenous architectural styles common to the African peoples of the Sahel and Sudanian savanna regions of West Africa, south of the Sahara and north of the fertile forest belt. It is characterised by the use of mudbrick (adobe) and adobe plaster, with large wooden-log support beams (toron) that protrude from the wall face and serve both as scaffolding for replastering and as structural reinforcement.

Characteristics

Buildings in this style — including mosques, palaces, and communal houses — feature flat roofs, engaged vertical buttress-like projections, and conical or pyramidal minarets and towers. Because mud structures erode in the rains, communities hold annual replastering festivals, most famously for the Great Mosque of Djenné in Mali, the largest mud-brick building in the world, built in its present form in 1907 on the site of a 13th-century mosque.

Regional variants

Variants are associated with the great Sudanic empires and trading cities: the mosque architecture of Timbuktu (Djinguereber, Sankore, and Sidi Yahya mosques), the Hausa architecture of northern Nigeria, and the fortified tata somba houses of the Batammariba in Togo and Benin. Several Sudano-Sahelian sites are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.
