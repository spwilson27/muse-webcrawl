A Vindication of the Rights of Woman

AuthorMary Wollstonecraft
Full titleA Vindication of the Rights of Woman: with Strictures on Political and Moral Subjects
Published1792 (Joseph Johnson, London)
GenrePolitical philosophy, feminism

A Vindication of the Rights of Woman: with Strictures on Political and Moral Subjects (1792) is a work of political and moral philosophy by the British writer Mary Wollstonecraft. It is one of the earliest works of feminist philosophy, arguing that women are not naturally inferior to men but appear so only because they lack education, and that women should receive a rational education equal to that of men.

Context and argument

Wollstonecraft wrote the Vindication partly in response to Talleyrand's 1791 report to the French National Assembly, which proposed that women receive only domestic schooling, and as a sequel to her A Vindication of the Rights of Men (1790) defending the French Revolution against Edmund Burke. She attacks conduct-book writers such as James Fordyce and John Gregory, and Rousseau's portrayal of Sophie in Émile, for training women in artificial weakness and "cunning." Her positive program calls for a national system of coeducational day schools, arguing that educated women would be better citizens, wives, and mothers and could enter professions.

Reception

The book was a commercial success and was translated and praised in Britain, France, and America, though conservative reviewers recoiled; Horace Walpole dismissed Wollstonecraft as a "hyena in petticoats." After her death in 1797, her husband William Godwin's candid Memoirs damaged her reputation for a century. The Vindication was revived by suffragists and twentieth-century feminists and is now a canonical text in women's studies and political theory.
