Ion trap

An ion trap is a combination of electric and/or magnetic fields used to capture charged particles — known as ions — often in a system for mass analysis, spectroscopy, or quantum information processing. Ion traps work by using oscillating electric fields (radio frequency, RF) and static potentials to confine ions in a small region of space for extended periods.

Types

  • Paul trap (quadrupole ion trap): uses RF and DC potentials applied to ring and endcap electrodes. The three-dimensional version underlies many mass spectrometers; the two-dimensional linear Paul trap is the workhorse of trapped-ion quantum computing.
  • Penning trap: uses a homogeneous magnetic field plus an electrostatic quadrupole field; widely used for precision measurements of particle properties, such as the electron magnetic moment, and for antimatter research.
  • Orbitrap: an electrostatic trap in which ions orbit a central spindle-shaped electrode; used in high-resolution mass spectrometry.

Applications

Ion traps are used in mass spectrometry, atomic clocks (where trapped ions provide extremely stable frequency references), precision spectroscopy, fundamental tests of quantum mechanics, and trapped-ion quantum computers, in which chains of ions serve as qubits manipulated by lasers. Wolfgang Paul and Hans Georg Dehmelt shared part of the 1989 Nobel Prize in Physics for developing the ion trap technique.

Principle

Because a static electric field alone cannot confine a charge in three dimensions (Earnshaw's theorem), traps either oscillate the field rapidly — so the ion experiences a time-averaged restoring "pseudopotential" — or combine electric fields with a magnetic field, as in the Penning trap. Once trapped, ions are typically laser-cooled to millikelvin or microkelvin temperatures to reduce motion and enable precise control.
