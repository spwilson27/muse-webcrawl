Adverse pressure gradient

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In fluid dynamics, an adverse pressure gradient is a pressure gradient in which the static pressure increases in the direction of the flow (dp/dx > 0). It is called "adverse" because it acts against the motion of fluid near a solid surface, decelerating the boundary layer and tending to cause flow separation.

Mechanism

By Bernoulli's principle, a region of decelerating external flow corresponds to rising pressure. Fluid particles in the outer flow have enough momentum to proceed against this gradient, but particles within the boundary layer — already slowed by viscosity — lose momentum, come to rest, and may reverse direction. At the point where the wall shear stress falls to zero, the flow separates from the surface, forming a separated region of recirculating flow.

Consequences

Separation under an adverse pressure gradient is responsible for the aerodynamic stall of wings at high angles of attack, for much of the pressure (form) drag on bluff bodies, and for performance losses in diffusers, compressors and turbines. A favourable pressure gradient (pressure falling in the flow direction) has the opposite effect, thinning the boundary layer and delaying separation.

Control

Designers mitigate adverse-gradient separation by streamlining bodies, limiting diffuser angles, energising the boundary layer with vortex generators or blowing, and removing low-momentum fluid through suction — techniques used extensively in aircraft and turbomachinery design.
