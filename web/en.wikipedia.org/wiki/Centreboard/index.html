Centreboard

A centreboard pivots down through a slot in the hull of a small sailing boat.

A centreboard (often spelled centerboard in American English) is a retractable hull appendage used on sailing vessels. It pivots out of a slot in the hull, known as a centreboard trunk or case, down through the centreline of the boat. Its retractability allows the boat to operate in shallow water, to be easily hauled out, and to be trailered.

Function

When lowered, the centreboard provides lateral resistance that prevents the boat from sliding sideways (making leeway) under the pressure of wind on the sails, converting sideways force into forward motion. Unlike a fixed keel, a centreboard usually provides little or no ballast, so centreboard boats rely on the weight of the crew and the width of the hull for stability. A related device, the daggerboard, slides vertically rather than pivoting, while a leeboard is mounted externally on the side of the hull.

History

Pivoting centreboards were patented in the early 19th century; Henry R. Worthington and others developed early designs, and the device was popularised in the United States aboard shallow-draught schooners and small racing craft. Famous centreboard yachts include Joshua Slocum's Spray, which carried a centreboard for part of its circumnavigation. Many classic daysailers and dinghies, such as the Sunfish (which uses a daggerboard) and the Laser, use retractable foils of this family.

Design

Centreboards are typically made of wood, fibreglass, steel or lead-weighted materials. The board is raised and lowered by a line or pennant led to the cockpit. Because the trunk must be open at the bottom, centreboard boats can take on water through the slot and require careful sealing when the board is up.
