Geometry of numbers

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The geometry of numbers is the part of number theory which uses geometry for the study of algebraic numbers. Typically, a ring of algebraic integers is viewed as a lattice in real or complex vector space, and the study of these lattices provides important information about the arithmetic of the underlying number field.

The subject was founded by Hermann Minkowski, whose 1896 book Geometrie der Zahlen systematised earlier ideas. Its simplest and most famous result, Minkowski's theorem on convex bodies, states that any convex set in n-dimensional space that is symmetric about the origin and has volume greater than 2n times the determinant of a lattice must contain a non-zero lattice point.

Main results

  • Minkowski's convex body theorem — guarantees lattice points in sufficiently large symmetric convex sets.
  • Minkowski's theorem on linear forms — a system of linear inequalities with determinant condition has a non-trivial integer solution.
  • Minkowski's second theorem — bounds the product of the successive minima of a convex body with respect to a lattice.
  • Blichfeldt's principle — a set of volume greater than the lattice determinant contains two points differing by a lattice vector.
  • Pick's theorem and Ehrhart theory, relating lattice points in polygons and polytopes to area and volume.

Applications

Minkowski used the convex body theorem to prove that every finite extension of the rationals has discriminant greater than one, hence must be ramified — a deep result inaccessible to purely algebraic methods of the time. The geometry of numbers also gives finiteness results for the class group and Dirichlet's unit theorem. In modern mathematics it connects with sphere packing, coding theory, Diophantine approximation, and the study of lattice-based cryptography.

Later developments

Twentieth-century work by Mahler, Cassels, Siegel, and others developed the reduction theory of lattices and the geometry of numbers over adeles. Mahler's compactness criterion characterised when a sequence of lattices has a convergent subsequence. More recently, the subject has seen renewed activity through its role in the theory of sphere packings in high dimensions and in post-quantum cryptography based on hard lattice problems such as the shortest vector problem.
