Direct reference theory

On the direct reference view, a proper name contributes its bearer itself to what is said.

A direct reference theory (also called referentialism or Millianism) is a theory of meaning in the philosophy of language holding that the meaning of a proper name — and on some versions, of other referring expressions — is simply its referent: the object it stands for. A name does not abbreviate a description, nor does it express a Fregean "sense" that determines reference.

Origins

The view descends from John Stuart Mill, who held that proper names denote but do not connote: "Dartmouth" names a town without implying anything about it. In the 1970s the theory was revived and developed through the causal-historical picture of reference in Saul Kripke's Naming and Necessity and in work by Keith Donnellan, David Kaplan and others. Kripke argued that names are rigid designators, picking out the same object in every possible world in which it exists, and that reference is fixed by an initial "baptism" and a causal chain of uses rather than by descriptions speakers associate with the name.

Consequences and objections

Direct reference implies that co-referring names are interchangeable in content: "Hesperus is Phosphorus" expresses the same proposition as the trivial "Hesperus is Hesperus", even though the former is informative — Frege's puzzle. Millians typically explain the difference in cognitive significance pragmatically rather than semantically. The theory also faces challenges from empty names (such as "Vulcan" or "Sherlock Holmes") and from attitude reports, where substitution of co-referring names appears to change truth value. Kaplan's extension of direct reference to indexicals and demonstratives, treated in his work on "Demonstratives", distinguishes the character of an expression from the content it contributes in a context.
