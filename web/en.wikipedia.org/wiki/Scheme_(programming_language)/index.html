Scheme (programming language)

The lambda, symbol of Scheme

Scheme is a dialect of the Lisp family of programming languages, created in the 1970s by Guy L. Steele and Gerald Jay Sussman at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory. Scheme is known for its minimalist design: a small core of primitives from which the rest of the language can be built, and for its pioneering support of lexical scoping, first-class continuations, and proper tail recursion.

History

Scheme grew out of attempts to understand Carl Hewitt's Actor model; Sussman and Steele discovered that a suitably extended lambda calculus could implement actors, leading to a series of papers known as the "Lambda Papers" (1975–1980). The language has been standardised through the "Revised Reports on the Algorithmic Language Scheme"; the sixth edition (R6RS) appeared in 2007, and the seventh edition (R7RS small) in 2013.

Features

Scheme uses fully parenthesised prefix notation (S-expressions) and treats code as data, supporting powerful macros — traditionally syntax-rules and the more powerful syntax-case. It guarantees proper tail-call optimisation, requires support for continuations (call/cc), and uses lexical (static) scoping, unlike earlier Lisp dialects.

(define (factorial n)
  (if (= n 0)
      1
      (* n (factorial (- n 1)))))

Implementations

Notable implementations include Guile (the GNU extension language), Racket (originally a Scheme, now its own language family), Chicken, Gambit, Chez Scheme, and MIT/GNU Scheme.

Influence

Scheme has been influential in programming-language research and education; it was long the language of the classic textbook Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs. JavaScript's designer Brendan Eich drew on Scheme ideas, and features such as closures and first-class functions trace part of their lineage to it.
