Frederick Walton (1834 – 16 May 1928) was a British inventor and manufacturer best known for inventing linoleum, the resilient floor covering made from oxidised linseed oil, which he patented in 1860 and which became one of the most widely used flooring materials of the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Early life

Walton was born in Halifax, Yorkshire, into a family connected with the manufacture of wire cards used in the textile industry. He joined his father's firm but soon turned his attention to experiments with paints, varnishes and India rubber substitutes.

Invention of linoleum

While experimenting with linseed oil, Walton observed that a skin of solidified, rubber-like material — which he called linoxyn — formed on the surface of oil paint left exposed to the air. He reasoned that this oxidised oil, combined with cork dust and other fillers and pressed onto a canvas or jute backing, could make a durable and cheap floor covering. He took out his first linoleum patent in 1860 and further patents followed in 1863 and 1864 for the manufacturing process.

In 1864 Walton established the Linoleum Manufacturing Company at Staines, Middlesex, and the product — its name derived from the Latin linum (flax) and oleum (oil) — became a commercial success. After his early patents expired, rival manufacturers entered the market; Walton sued competitors over the use of the word "linoleum", but the courts held that the term had become generic — one of the earliest recorded cases of a trademark becoming a generic name.

Lincrusta

In 1877 Walton patented Lincrusta, a deeply embossed wall covering made from a similar oxidised-oil mixture, which became popular as a washable, durable substitute for ornate plasterwork and embossed leather hangings in Victorian interiors.

Later life and death

Walton continued to experiment with floor and wall coverings and held numerous patents. He spent his later years in France and died at Nice on 16 May 1928, aged 93 or 94.
