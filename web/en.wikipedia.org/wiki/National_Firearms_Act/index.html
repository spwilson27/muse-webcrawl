The National Firearms Act (NFA) is an Act of Congress in the United States that, in general, imposes an excise tax on the manufacture of, and registration of, certain firearms and devices. Enacted on June 26, 1934, in the wake of Prohibition-era gang violence (including the Saint Valentine's Day Massacre and crimes associated with figures such as John Dillinger), it was the first major federal gun control law in the United States.

Regulated items

The NFA regulates: machine guns; short-barreled rifles (barrel under 16 inches) and short-barreled shotguns (barrel under 18 inches); suppressors (silencers); destructive devices (such as many explosives and large-bore weapons); and disguised or improvised firearms classed as "Any Other Weapon" (AOW). Firearms meeting these definitions are informally called "NFA items" or "Title II weapons", by reference to Title II of the Gun Control Act of 1968, which amended the NFA scheme.

Registration and tax

Rather than banning the weapons outright, Congress used its taxing power: manufacture and transfer of NFA items require payment of a tax (historically $200, then a prohibitive sum) and registration in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record maintained by the ATF. Transfers require prior ATF approval, fingerprints and photographs. Unlawful possession of an unregistered NFA item carries penalties of up to ten years' imprisonment.

Later changes

The Firearm Owners Protection Act of 1986 froze the civilian machine-gun registry, prohibiting transfer or possession of machine guns manufactured after May 19, 1986, which fixed the pool of transferable machine guns and greatly increased their prices. The Supreme Court upheld the Act's framework in cases touching its registration requirement, and the NFA remains a central part of U.S. federal firearms law.
