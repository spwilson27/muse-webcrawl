Harquebusier

Harquebusier
RoleMounted infantry / heavy cavalry
PeriodLate 16th – late 17th century
ArmamentHarquebus or carbine, sword, pistols
ArmourBuff coat, cuirass, lobster-tailed pot helmet

A harquebusier was a soldier armed with a harquebus or carbine, a form of long firearm. In the warfare of the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the term came to describe a type of cavalryman who combined firepower with a substantial degree of armour protection, and who formed the dominant kind of mounted soldier in many European armies by the time of the English Civil War.

Equipment

The classic harquebusier of the 1640s wore a thick leather buff coat, often with a back-and-breast cuirass worn over it, and a three-barred "lobster-tailed pot" helmet (a form of zischägge). He was armed with a carbine or harquebus carried on a swivel snap-hook attached to a shoulder belt, a pair of pistols, and a stout broad-bladed sword suited to cutting in the melee. Because his equipment was cheaper than the full three-quarter armour of the cuirassier, harquebusiers could be raised in much larger numbers.

Tactics

Harquebusiers were expected both to skirmish with firearms and to charge home with the sword. In English service during the Civil War they were the standard troopers of regiments such as those commanded by Sir Arthur Haselrig — whose heavily armoured "Lobsters" were an unusually cuirassier-like exception — and they were used to great effect by Oliver Cromwell's Ironsides, who charged at a fast trot in close order after delivering a volley at close range.

Decline

By the end of the seventeenth century the harquebusier's armour steadily shrank — first the cuirass disappeared for most troopers, then the helmet — and the type merged into the generic dragoon and line cavalry of the eighteenth century.
