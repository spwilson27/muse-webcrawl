Home recording

Home recording is the practice of recording sound in a private home or other non-commercial space instead of a professional recording studio. Once limited to crude single-track machines, home recording was transformed by affordable multitrack equipment in the 1970s and 1980s and then by computer-based digital audio workstations, so that by the 21st century recordings of commercial quality could be produced in bedrooms and home studios.

History

Enthusiasts recorded at home from the earliest days of phonograph and wire recorders, but the modern era began with reel-to-reel tape machines and, later, cassette decks. A turning point was TEAC/Tascam's Portastudio (1979), which integrated a four-track cassette recorder and mixer in one portable unit and let musicians overdub parts at home; artists such as Bruce Springsteen, whose album Nebraska (1982) was recorded on a Portastudio, demonstrated its creative potential. In the 1980s, MIDI and affordable synthesisers, drum machines and samplers fed genres from synth-pop to hip hop and house music, while ADAT and other digital multitrack formats arrived in the 1990s.

The digital era

From the late 1990s, personal computers running digital audio workstation software (such as Pro Tools, Cubase, Logic Pro, FL Studio and GarageBand), combined with inexpensive audio interfaces, condenser microphones and plug-in effects and instruments, made a complete studio available for a few hundred dollars. Artists including Billie Eilish, whose Grammy-winning album When We All Fall Asleep, Where Do We Go? (2019) was recorded with her brother Finneas O'Connell in a bedroom at their family home, exemplify the professional results now achievable. Home recording has reshaped the music industry, lowering the cost of demos and releases, enabling podcasting and streaming creators, and shifting professional studios toward mixing, mastering and specialised work.

Challenges

Home recordists must manage poor room acoustics, background noise and limited monitoring, typically compensating with close miking, acoustic treatment, headphones and software correction. Legal questions also arose historically over home taping of broadcasts and records — the subject of the 1980s "Home Taping Is Killing Music" campaign.
