Variable gauge

Variable gauge systems allow railway vehicles to travel between tracks of different track gauges without transshipment of passengers or freight. The wheels of a variable gauge axle can be unlocked, moved sideways to the new gauge, and relocked as the vehicle passes slowly through a gauge changer installation at a break of gauge.

Technology

In most systems the wheels are mounted on a sliding sleeve or on independent half-axles rather than a fixed axle. As the train traverses the gauge changer at low speed (typically 10–30 km/h), guide rails bear the vehicle's weight, locking pins are withdrawn, the wheels slide along to positions determined by the guide rails of the new gauge, and the pins re-engage. The process takes a few minutes for a whole train, far faster than bogie exchange or transshipment.

History and use

Spain pioneered practical variable gauge technology: Talgo introduced its RD system in 1969 for through services between the Iberian gauge (1,668 mm) network and standard gauge France. CAF's BRAVA system followed. In Spain the technology became strategically important as the standard-gauge high-speed AVE network was built alongside the legacy Iberian network, letting trains serve both.

Poland's SUW 2000 system is used at borders with Lithuania, Belarus and Ukraine, where standard gauge meets the 1,520 mm broad gauge. Japan developed the Gauge Change Train (free-gauge train) to run between its standard-gauge Shinkansen and narrow-gauge conventional lines, though it has not entered commercial service. Experimental systems also exist for freight wagons.

Limitations

Variable gauge axles are heavier and more expensive than fixed axles, require specialised maintenance, and add mechanical complexity. For freight, where wagons are numerous and cheap, bogie exchange or transshipment often remains more economical.
