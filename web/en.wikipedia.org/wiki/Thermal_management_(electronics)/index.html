Thermal management (electronics)

All electronic devices and circuits generate excess heat and thus require thermal management to improve reliability and prevent premature failure. The amount of heat output is equal to the power input, if there are no other energy interactions. Techniques for heat dissipation include heat sinks and fans for air cooling, forced convection, and liquid cooling; thermal interface materials and good board layout reduce the thermal resistance between a component and its cooling path.

Thermal resistance

Engineers model a component's temperature rise with thermal resistance, measured in kelvin per watt, analogous to electrical resistance: the junction-to-case and case-to-ambient resistances combine to determine how hot a semiconductor junction will run at a given power. Device datasheets specify maximum junction temperatures, and derating or throttling protects parts that exceed them.

Cooling methods

  • Heat sinks — finned metal masses, usually aluminium or copper, that increase surface area for natural or forced convection.
  • Fans and forced air — standard in computers and power supplies.
  • Heat pipes and vapour chambers — sealed phase-change devices that spread heat efficiently.
  • Liquid cooling — cold plates and loops used in high-performance computing and power electronics.
  • Thermoelectric (Peltier) coolers — solid-state heat pumps for niche applications.
