Chumash people

A tomol, the Chumash sewn-plank canoe

The Chumash are a Native American people of the central and southern coastal regions of California, in parts of what are now San Luis Obispo, Santa Barbara, Ventura and Los Angeles counties, extending from Morro Bay in the north to Malibu in the south. They also occupied the northern Channel Islands.

The Chumash were among the most complex hunter-gatherer societies in North America. They built large villages, crafted finely made baskets and bead money from shells, and navigated the Santa Barbara Channel in plank canoes called tomols, which enabled trade between the mainland and the islands.

History

Archaeological evidence suggests the Chumash and their ancestors occupied the region for thousands of years before European contact. Spanish explorers reached the area in 1542, and missions were established among the Chumash beginning in the late eighteenth century. Missionization, disease, and displacement devastated the population; the Chumash revolt of 1824 was one of the largest uprisings against Spanish rule in California.

Culture

Chumash society was organised into chiefdoms with hereditary chiefs, craft specialists, and an extensive trade network. Rock art sites in the interior, painted with elaborate polychrome designs, are attributed to Chumash shamans. The Chumash languages, a small family of related tongues, are no longer spoken as first languages, though revitalisation efforts are under way.

The Santa Ynez Band of Chumash Indians is the only federally recognised Chumash tribe, based in Santa Barbara County.
