Samarium is a chemical element with the symbol Sm and atomic number 62. It is a moderately hard silvery metal that slowly oxidises in air. As a member of the lanthanide series, samarium usually assumes the oxidation state +3. It is named after the mineral samarskite, from which it was first isolated, and which was itself named after the Russian mining official Vasili Samarsky-Bykhovets.

History

Samarium was spectroscopically identified in 1879 by the French chemist Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran in the mineral samarskite, obtained from a mine in the Ural Mountains, and he isolated the oxide the same year. The pure metal was not produced until much later, with lanthanum reduction methods in the early twentieth century.

Properties

Samarium is reasonably stable in dry air but tarnishes and can ignite in moist air at elevated temperatures. It has a rhombohedral crystal structure at room temperature and a Curie temperature of about 85 K. Natural samarium is slightly radioactive, owing mainly to the alpha decay of the isotope samarium-147 (half-life about 1.07×1011 years), a property exploited in samarium–neodymium dating of rocks and meteorites. Samarium-149 is a strong neutron absorber, and samarium isotopes figure in reactor physics as fission products ("samarium poisoning").

Applications

The principal use of samarium is in samarium–cobalt magnets, which retain their magnetism at high temperatures and are used in precision motors, aerospace equipment and high-quality headphones and guitar pickups. Other uses include samarium oxide as a catalyst and in infrared-absorbing glass, samarium-153 in the radiopharmaceutical Quadramet for treating bone cancer pain, and neutron-control applications in nuclear reactors.

Occurrence

Samarium occurs in rare-earth minerals such as monazite and bastnäsite at concentrations of up to a few percent. China dominates world production of rare-earth elements, including samarium.
