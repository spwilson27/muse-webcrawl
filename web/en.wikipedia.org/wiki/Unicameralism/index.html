Unicameralism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Unicameralism is the practice of having a single legislative chamber. Many countries, including most unitary states and smaller democracies, have unicameral legislatures, as do most subnational governments such as the American state of Nebraska and the Canadian provinces. Unicameralism is the opposite of bicameralism, in which bills must pass two houses.

Advocates argue that one chamber is simpler, cheaper and more accountable: responsibility for legislation cannot be shifted to another house, and in a unitary state there is no federal logic requiring a states' house. Opponents reply that a single chamber may legislate hastily and that an upper house provides valuable revision — a “sober second thought”. Where upper houses were abolished, functions such as committee scrutiny were usually strengthened instead.

Examples

Unicameral national legislatures include those of New Zealand, Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Norway, Portugal, Greece, Israel (the Knesset) and South Korea. Several countries moved from bicameral to unicameral systems in the twentieth century; others, such as Ireland (2013) and the United Kingdom periodically, have debated abolition and rejected it in referendums or in practice. In federations unicameralism is rare at national level, because the upper house typically embodies the federal bargain, though subnational units within federations are often unicameral.
