Debt brake

The Bundestag in Berlin, whose budgets are constrained by Germany's constitutional debt brake.

A debt brake (German: Schuldenbremse) is a constitutional fiscal rule that limits the ability of a government to borrow by capping the structural budget deficit. Debt brakes are most closely associated with Switzerland, which adopted one at the federal level in 2003, and Germany, which wrote a debt brake into its Basic Law (constitution) in 2009.

Germany

The German debt brake was enacted in 2009 as part of the second federalism reform. It requires the federal government to limit its structural net borrowing to no more than 0.35 per cent of GDP, and requires the Länder (states) to run structurally balanced budgets. Cyclical fluctuations are accommodated through a business-cycle component, and an escape clause permits additional borrowing in natural disasters or extraordinary emergencies beyond the government's control, subject to a repayment plan.

The rule applied fully to the federal government from 2016 and to the Länder from 2020. It was suspended from 2020 to 2022 during the COVID-19 pandemic and the energy crisis. In November 2023, the Federal Constitutional Court ruled that the government's repurposing of €60 billion in unused pandemic-era credit authorisations for a climate fund violated the debt brake, triggering a budget crisis and intensifying debate about reforming the rule to permit greater investment in infrastructure and defence.

Switzerland

The Swiss debt brake, approved by referendum in 2001 and effective from 2003, caps federal expenditure at the level of expected revenues adjusted for the state of the business cycle. Revenues above forecast in good years build a compensation account that offsets shortfalls in recessions. It is widely credited with reducing Swiss federal debt substantially during the 2000s.

Criticism and debate

Supporters argue that debt brakes enforce fiscal discipline, protect future generations and lower borrowing costs. Critics contend that rigid rules can starve public investment, deepen recessions by forcing pro-cyclical cuts, and push spending into off-budget vehicles that evade democratic scrutiny.
