Tymbal

The tymbal (or timbal) is the sound-producing organ of cicadas and certain other insects. It consists of a ribbed, buckling membrane on the sides of the abdomen, driven by a powerful tymbal muscle. When the muscle contracts, the membrane clicks inward, producing a pulse of sound; as it relaxes and snaps back, further clicks are generated.

Mechanism

Successive ribs of the tymbal buckle in sequence, so a single muscle contraction produces a train of pulses at frequencies of hundreds of cycles per second. The abdomen acts as a resonant chamber, and in many species opercula (flaps) cover and help project the sound. Only male cicadas possess functional tymbals, and their songs — among the loudest sounds produced by any insect, exceeding 100 decibels in some species — serve to attract females and advertise species identity.

Other uses of the term

Sound production by buckling tymbal-like structures also occurs in some moths, which produce ultrasonic clicks that may jam bat echolocation or advertise unpalatability. The name derives from the Greek tympanon, a hand drum.
