The Franck–Hertz experiment, performed by James Franck and Gustav Hertz in 1914, was the first experiment to demonstrate directly that atoms absorb energy only in discrete amounts, confirming the quantised energy levels proposed in Niels Bohr's model of the atom. Franck and Hertz received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1925 for the work.

The experiment

Electrons from a heated filament were accelerated through mercury vapour in a vacuum tube. As the accelerating voltage increased, the collected current rose — until the electrons gained exactly the energy needed to excite a mercury atom (about 4.9 electronvolts), whereupon they lost their energy in inelastic collisions and the current dropped sharply. The pattern repeated at multiples of 4.9 V, and the tube emitted ultraviolet light at the wavelength corresponding to that energy.

Significance

The experiment gave direct, non-spectroscopic evidence for stationary states in atoms and remains a standard undergraduate laboratory demonstration, usually performed with mercury or neon.
