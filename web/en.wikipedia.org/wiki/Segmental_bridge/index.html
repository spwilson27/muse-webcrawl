Segmental bridge

A segmental bridge is a bridge built in short sections (called segments), i.e., one piece at a time, as opposed to traditional methods that build a bridge in very large sections. The bridge is made of concrete that is either cast-in-place (constructed fully in its final location) or precast in a casting yard and transported to the site. Segmental construction is most often used with prestressed concrete box girders.

Methods

In the balanced cantilever method, segments are added alternately on either side of a pier, so that the structure remains balanced during erection; the process advances outward until two cantilevers meet at mid-span. Precast segments are typically joined with epoxy and stressed together with post-tensioning tendons. In span-by-span construction, a launching gantry or crane assembles a full span of precast segments on temporary supports before tensioning. Cast-in-place segmental construction uses travelling formwork (form travellers) that move along the completed portion of the deck.

History

The technique was pioneered in France by Eugène Freyssinet and developed extensively by Jean Muller, whose designs from the 1960s onward established precast segmental construction worldwide. Notable examples include many long causeways and viaducts, such as the Seven Mile Bridge in Florida and the Confederation Bridge in Canada, the latter built from large precast components.

Advantages

Segmental construction minimises disruption below the bridge, suits curved and long-span alignments, benefits from factory quality control of precast elements, and can be rapid because segment casting proceeds while foundations are built. It is widely used for urban viaducts, transit guideways, and river crossings.
