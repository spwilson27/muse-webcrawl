The gusle (Serbian Cyrillic: гусле) is a traditional single-stringed musical instrument of the Dinaric region of Southeast Europe, played with a bow and used above all to accompany the singing of epic poetry. The instrument has been central to Serbian, Montenegrin, Bosnian, Croatian and Albanian heroic song traditions for centuries, and in 2018 "singing to the accompaniment of the gusle" was inscribed by UNESCO on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Construction

The body of the gusle is carved from a single piece of wood, traditionally maple, covered with a thin animal-skin soundboard. A long neck rises from the body and usually ends in a carved head — most iconically a horse's head, but also goat, ram or eagle motifs. The single string, classically of horsehair (in modern instruments often nylon or steel), runs from the bottom of the body over a small bridge to a tuning peg. The bow is a simple arched stick strung with horsehair.

Performance

The player, called a guslar, holds the instrument upright on the knee and fingers the string without pressing it fully to the neck, producing a rough, droning tone close to the voice. The gusle does not so much carry a melody as shadow the singer's line, punctuating the decasyllabic verses of epic songs recounting battles, heroes and hajduks — figures such as Prince Marko and the heroes of the Kosovo cycle. Performances could last for hours or resume over several evenings.

Historical significance

In the nineteenth century the epic songs sung to the gusle were collected by the language reformer Vuk Stefanović Karadžić, whose publications made them famous across Europe and influenced poets including Goethe and the Grimms. In the 1930s the Homeric scholars Milman Parry and Albert Lord recorded gusle singers in Yugoslavia, using the living tradition to develop the oral-formulaic theory of how the Iliad and Odyssey were composed.
