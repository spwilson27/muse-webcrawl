Decantation

Decantation is a process for the separation of mixtures of immiscible liquids, or of a liquid and a solid mixture such as a suspension. The layer closer to the top of the container — the less dense component — is poured off, leaving the denser liquid or the solid sediment behind. The process is typically preceded by allowing the mixture to stand so that gravity separates its components.

Process

In the simplest laboratory decantation, a suspension is left undisturbed until the solid settles (sedimentation). The supernatant liquid is then carefully poured from the vessel, often down a glass rod to prevent splashing, leaving the solid and a small amount of liquid behind. Separation is quick but imperfect: some solid particles usually remain suspended or are carried over, so decantation is generally less precise than filtration, but requires no filter medium and is useful when a filter would clog.

Examples

Decanting wine is the best-known everyday use: older red wines throw a sediment of tartrates and tannins, and pouring the wine into a decanter separates it from the sediment while also exposing the wine to air. Decantation is also used to obtain clear water from muddy water, to separate cream from milk after standing, to wash precipitates in gravimetric analysis, in oil-and-water separation, and in the rinsing of grains and legumes. Industrial versions include decanter centrifuges, which accelerate sedimentation mechanically, and continuous decanters used in wastewater treatment and olive-oil production.
