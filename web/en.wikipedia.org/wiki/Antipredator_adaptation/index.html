Antipredator adaptation

Antipredator adaptations are mechanisms developed through evolution that assist prey organisms in their constant struggle against predators. They operate at every stage of an encounter: avoiding detection, preventing or deterring attack, surviving capture, and escaping.

Avoiding detection

Prey use camouflage (crypsis), nocturnality, subterranean habits, and masquerade — resembling inedible objects such as twigs or leaves — to avoid being noticed. Some species reduce telltale signs such as noise, scent, or shadows.

Deterring attack

Once detected, prey may advertise unprofitability through aposematism — bright warning coloration associated with toxins or stings — and harmless species may mimic these signals (Batesian mimicry). Other defenses include chemical sprays, venom, spines, shells, startling displays (deimatic behavior), bluffing, playing dead (thanatosis), and mobbing of predators by groups. Physical escape adaptations include speed, agility, and autotomy (shedding a tail or limb).

Coevolution

Antipredator traits evolve in an arms race with predator counter-adaptations, a dynamic formalized in the "life–dinner principle": the prey runs for its life while the predator runs only for its dinner, so selection on prey is often the stronger.
