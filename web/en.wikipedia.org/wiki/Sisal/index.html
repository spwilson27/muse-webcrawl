Sisal (/ˈsaɪsəl/, Spanish: [siˈsal]; Agave sisalana) is a species of flowering plant native to southern Mexico but widely cultivated and naturalised in many other countries. It yields a stiff fibre used in making rope and twine, and has many other uses, including paper, cloth, footwear, hats, bags, carpets, geotextiles and dartboards. The term "sisal" may refer either to the plant's common name or the fibre, depending on the context. It is sometimes referred to as "sisal hemp", because hemp was for centuries a major source of fibre, and other fibre sources were named after it.

Cultivation

Sisal plants consist of a rosette of sword-shaped leaves 1.5–2 metres (5–6.5 ft) long growing from a thick stem. The plant has a lifespan of 7–10 years and typically produces 200–250 commercially usable leaves, each containing around 1,000 fibres. Propagation is traditionally by bulbils or rhizome suckers. Major producers include Brazil, Tanzania, Kenya, Madagascar, China and Mexico; the plant takes its name from the port of Sisal in Yucatán.

Uses

Traditionally used for rope and twine, sisal has applications as an environmentally friendly strengthening agent to replace asbestos and fibreglass in composite materials, in the automobile industry, and in cat scratching posts and dartboards. Sisal farming is considered ecologically beneficial because the crop stores carbon and requires few chemical inputs.
