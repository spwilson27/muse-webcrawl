Coffee preparation

Coffee preparation is the process of turning coffee beans into a beverage. While the particular steps vary with the type of coffee and with the raw materials, the process includes four basic steps: raw coffee beans must be roasted, the roasted coffee beans must then be ground, and the ground coffee must then be mixed with hot or cold water (depending on the method of brewing) for a specific time (brewed); the liquid coffee extraction must be separated from the used grounds, and finally, if desired, the extracted coffee is combined with various flavourings and presentation elements such as milk and sugar.

Roasting

Coffee roasting transforms the chemical and physical properties of green coffee beans. Roasts are commonly graded from light to dark: lighter roasts retain more of the bean's origin character and acidity, while darker roasts develop roasted, caramelised, and smoky flavours as the bean's sugars and oils break down. Roasted beans are ground shortly before brewing to preserve their volatile aromatics.

Grinding

The fineness of the grind is matched to the brewing method: coarse for the French press, medium for drip filters, fine for espresso, and powder-fine for Turkish coffee. Burr grinders give a more uniform particle size than blade grinders, producing more even extraction.

Brewing methods

  • Boiling / decoction — Turkish coffee, in which powder-fine coffee and water are boiled together in a cezve; also cowboy coffee and Scandinavian egg coffee.
  • Steeping — French press (cafetière), cold brew, and coffee bags, in which grounds soak in water and are then separated.
  • Filtration / drip — pour-over brewers such as the Chemex and V60, automatic drip machines, and percolators, in which hot water passes through a bed of grounds and a filter.
  • Pressure — espresso, forced through finely ground coffee at around nine bars of pressure, and its derivatives such as the moka pot and AeroPress.

Extraction and serving

Brewing is a balancing act of variables: water temperature (typically 90–96 °C), contact time, turbulence, and the coffee-to-water ratio. Under-extraction tastes sour and salty; over-extraction tastes bitter and hollow. The finished brew may be served black, with milk or cream, sweetened, or as the base of drinks such as the cappuccino, caffè latte, and flat white. Instant coffee, a dried extract of brewed coffee, requires only the addition of hot water.
