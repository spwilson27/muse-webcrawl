Baroque dance

Baroque dance is dance of the Baroque era (roughly 1600–1750), closely associated with Baroque music, theatre, and the court of Louis XIV of France. It encompasses both the social dancing of the ballroom — above all the minuet — and the theatrical dancing performed in operas and ballets, from which classical ballet developed.

Development

Dance flourished at the French court, where Louis XIV, himself an accomplished dancer who appeared as Apollo, the sun god, in court ballets, founded the Académie Royale de Danse in 1661. Dancing masters such as Pierre Beauchamps, dancing master to the king, codified the five positions of the feet and the turnout that remain fundamental to ballet. Choreography was recorded in the notation system published by Raoul-Auger Feuillet in 1701, known as Beauchamps–Feuillet notation, which preserved hundreds of dances.

Style and dances

Baroque dance is characterized by an upright, elegant carriage, precise footwork, graceful arm movements (port de bras), and intricate floor patterns traced by couples. Common dance types, many of which also became movements of the Baroque suite, include the allemande, courante, sarabande, gigue, gavotte, bourrée, passepied, and the minuet, the dominant social dance of the 18th century. In the theatre, dances were integrated into opéra-ballets by composers such as Lully and Rameau.

Revival

Since the late 20th century, historically informed performance groups have reconstructed Baroque dance from surviving notation, and it is now taught and performed alongside early music.
