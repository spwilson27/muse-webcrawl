Thomas Burberry

Thomas Burberry (27 August 1835 – 4 April 1926) was an English draper and the founder of Burberry, the international luxury fashion house. He is credited with inventing gabardine, a hardwearing, water-resistant yet breathable fabric that revolutionised outdoor clothing.

Early life and business

Born in Brockham Green, Surrey, Burberry was apprenticed to a draper before opening his own outfitter's shop in Basingstoke, Hampshire, in 1856. The business, T. Burberry and Sons, prospered by clothing local gentry, sportsmen and farmers, and by the 1870s had expanded to an emporium.

Gabardine and the trench coat

In 1879 Burberry invented gabardine, a tightly woven worsted or cotton fabric waterproofed before weaving. Patented in 1888, it was adopted by explorers and aviators: Roald Amundsen wore Burberry gabardine on his 1911 South Pole expedition, and Ernest Shackleton used it on his Antarctic journeys. During the First World War the company supplied officers' raincoats — the "trench coat" — to the British Army, cementing the garment as a fashion classic.

Later life

Burberry retired to Abbotts Court in Dorset and died in 1926. The firm he founded remained family-influenced for decades and became one of Britain's most recognisable luxury brands, famous for its check lining and equestrian knight logo.
