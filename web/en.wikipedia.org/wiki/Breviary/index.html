Breviary

A breviary (Latin: breviarium) is a liturgical book in the Roman Catholic Church and other Western Christian traditions that contains the public prayers constituting the Liturgy of the Hours (Divine Office) for each day of the year — psalms, hymns, readings from Scripture and the Church Fathers, antiphons, and prayers arranged for the canonical hours from Matins through Compline. The name derives from its character as a condensed, portable compendium of texts that had originally been spread across several books (psalter, lectionary, antiphonary, collectar).

History

The breviary took shape in the 11th–13th centuries as friars and travelling clergy needed the whole Office in a single volume; the Franciscans adopted and spread the breviary of the Roman curia across Europe. With printing, breviaries multiplied in local ("uses") editions. After the Council of Trent, Pope Pius V issued a standardised Roman Breviary in 1568, suppressing most local uses less than two centuries old. Successive revisions followed under Clement VIII, Urban VIII (who rewrote many hymns), Pius X (1911, a major rearrangement of the psalter), and Pius XII. Following the Second Vatican Council, Paul VI promulgated the reformed Liturgia Horarum (1971), with a four-week psalter and simplified structure, in English published as the Liturgy of the Hours and in Britain as the Divine Office.

Use

Clergy and religious in the Latin Church are obliged to pray the Liturgy of the Hours daily; the breviary is the book that makes this possible. Eastern Orthodox practice uses an analogous set of books centred on the Horologion. Anglican and Lutheran traditions developed their own daily-office books, most famously the Church of England's Book of Common Prayer, which condensed the eight medieval hours into Morning and Evening Prayer. Illuminated breviaries, such as the Belleville Breviary and the breviary of Queen Isabella of Castile, rank among the finest late medieval manuscripts.
