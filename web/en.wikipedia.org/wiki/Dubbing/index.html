Dubbing

A voice actor performing dialogue in a dubbing session

Dubbing is a post-production process in film and television in which additional or replacement dialogue is recorded and mixed with the original soundtrack. The term covers two main practices: replacing the dialogue of a work with a translation into another language, and re-recording dialogue in the original language — the latter known in Hollywood as automated dialogue replacement (ADR) or looping.

Foreign-language dubbing

Dubbing is the dominant method of translating audiovisual works in many large markets — including Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Brazil and much of Latin America and Eastern Europe — while smaller markets more often rely on subtitling. In a foreign-language dub, dialogue is translated, adapted so that the new lines roughly match the actors' lip movements and the timing of the original, and performed by voice actors directed to match the original performances. Countries develop strong dubbing industries with recognised voice actors; in some markets a particular foreign star is consistently dubbed by the same local voice.

Dubbing policy has historically had political dimensions: in Fascist Italy and Francoist Spain, dubbing was promoted (and used as a tool of censorship and linguistic nationalism) partly to discourage subtitled versions.

ADR

Dialogue is re-recorded in the original language when location sound is unusable because of noise, when lines are changed after filming, or to improve performance. Actors watch the scene on a loop and re-deliver lines in sync. ADR is also used to record dialogue for animated films, where the voices are created before or alongside the picture.

Dubbing in music

In recording, "dubbing" also refers to copying a recording from one medium to another, and overdubbing — adding further performances to an existing multitrack recording — is the standard method of building up popular-music arrangements. The Jamaican genre of dub takes its name from this copying process.
