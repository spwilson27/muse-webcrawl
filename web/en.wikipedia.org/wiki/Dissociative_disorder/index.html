Dissociative disorder

Dissociative disorders (DDs) are a group of mental disorders characterised by significant disruptions or discontinuities in the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception, body representation, motor control, and behaviour. Dissociative symptoms may potentially disrupt many areas of psychological functioning and are often associated with experiences of trauma, particularly in childhood.

Classification

The DSM-5 lists the following dissociative disorders:

  • Dissociative identity disorder (DID)
  • Dissociative amnesia
  • Depersonalization-derealization disorder
  • Other specified and unspecified dissociative disorders

The ICD-11 similarly groups dissociative disorders, including partial dissociative identity disorder and dissociative neurological symptom disorder.

Causes

Dissociative disorders are strongly associated with overwhelming stress and trauma, such as chronic childhood abuse or neglect, although not every person with such experiences develops a disorder. Dissociation is often described as a defensive response that compartmentalises traumatic experience.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is made clinically, sometimes aided by structured interviews and screening instruments such as the Dissociative Experiences Scale. Treatment is primarily psychotherapeutic and typically phase-oriented: establishing safety and stabilisation, processing traumatic memories, and integrating personality and functioning. No medication specifically treats dissociation, though drugs may be used for co-occurring depression, anxiety, or PTSD.
