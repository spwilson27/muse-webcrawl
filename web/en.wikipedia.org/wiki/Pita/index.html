Pita

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Pita breads puffed into pockets, stacked in a basket]

Pita (or pitta) is a family of yeast-leavened round flatbreads common across the Middle East, the Levant, the Balkans and the Mediterranean. Baked at very high temperature, the dough puffs up dramatically and collapses on cooling to form the characteristic internal pocket — perfect for stuffing with falafel, kebabs, salads and dips. Pocketless versions (as for Greek souvlaki wraps) are rolled around fillings instead.

History

Flatbreads are among the oldest breads, and pita-like breads descend from the ancient breads of the Fertile Crescent. The word "pita" is Greek, and the bread is known as khubz arabi ("Arab bread") in much of the Levant. Commercial bakeries with continuous ovens made pita an everyday staple across the Middle East and, through immigration, a supermarket standard in Europe and North America.

Making

A simple dough of flour, water, yeast and salt is rolled into rounds and baked at 230–260 °C or hotter for a few minutes. Steam generated inside the dough separates the layers, creating the pocket; the bread must be kept covered so it stays soft rather than crisping. Whole-wheat versions are common.

Uses

Pita pockets hold falafel and shawarma; torn pita scoops up hummus and baba ghanoush; pieces are toasted into chips or layered in the Levantine salad fattoush and the dish fatteh. In Greece, thicker pocketless pita wraps gyros and souvlaki with tzatziki, tomato and onion.
