Fraktur

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fraktur (German: [fʁakˈtuːɐ̯]) is a calligraphic hand of the Latin alphabet and any of several blackletter typefaces derived from it. Originating in the early 16th century in the circle of Emperor Maximilian I, Fraktur became the dominant printed type for the German language and was used extensively in Germany and neighbouring countries until the early 20th century. Its letterforms are characterised by sharp, broken strokes — the name derives from the Latin fractura, "a break" — and by elaborate capitals, a distinctive long s (ſ), and ligatures such as ß and ch.

History

Fraktur emerged from late medieval cursive and textualis hands; early forms appear in printed works from about 1513, including Maximilian's prayer book. Over the following centuries it developed into numerous regional and stylistic variants and became strongly identified with German national culture, in contrast to the "Latin" Antiqua typefaces used for other European languages. In the 19th century the German-speaking world debated fiercely between Fraktur and Antiqua (the Antiqua–Fraktur dispute), with Bismarck famously refusing to read German books printed in Latin type.

Abolition

In a surprising reversal, the Nazi regime — which had earlier celebrated Fraktur as quintessentially German — decreed in January 1941 that blackletter typefaces were to be abandoned in favour of Antiqua, officially (and baselessly) denouncing them as "Jewish letters" (Schwabacher Judenlettern). The practical motive was legibility for readers in occupied Europe. Fraktur never regained its place after the Second World War and survives today mostly in mastheads, signage, ceremonial documents, and decorative use.

In mathematics and Unicode

Fraktur letters are used as symbols in mathematics — for example for Lie algebras (𝔤) and ideals in ring theory — and Unicode encodes a set of "Mathematical Fraktur" symbols. The general blackletter style is also colloquially known as "Gothic script".
