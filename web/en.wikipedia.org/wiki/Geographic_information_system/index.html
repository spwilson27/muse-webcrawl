A geographic information system (GIS) is a computer system for capturing, storing, checking, analysing and displaying data related to positions on Earth's surface. By relating seemingly unrelated data through location, GIS helps users understand patterns, relationships and geographic context, and underpins modern cartography, urban planning, environmental management, logistics and epidemiology.

Data representation

GIS integrates many kinds of data layers. Spatial information is stored either as vector data — points, lines and polygons with associated attributes — or as raster data, grids of cells such as satellite imagery and elevation models. Each layer is georeferenced to a coordinate system and map projection, allowing layers to be overlaid and queried together.

History

Thematic map overlay has roots in nineteenth-century work such as John Snow's 1854 cholera map of London. Computerised GIS began with the Canada Geographic Information System, developed in the 1960s under Roger Tomlinson, often called the "father of GIS". Commercial software from firms such as Esri spread GIS through government and industry in the 1980s and 1990s, and open-source tools and web mapping later brought it to a mass audience.

Applications

Uses include land-use planning, disaster response, precision agriculture, utility network management, market analysis, archaeology and public health. Spatial analysis techniques — buffering, overlay, network analysis, interpolation — let users model scenarios such as flood risk or optimal facility locations.
