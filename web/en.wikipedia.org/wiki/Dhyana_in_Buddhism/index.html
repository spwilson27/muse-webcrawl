Dhyana in Buddhism

Dhyana meditation posture, with legs crossed and hands resting in the lap.

In the oldest texts of Buddhism, dhyāna (Sanskrit: ध्यान) or jhāna (Pāli: ཛྙ་) is the training of the mind, commonly translated as meditation, to withdraw the mind from automatic responses to sense impressions and attain a state of perfect equanimity and awareness (upekkha-sati-parisuddhi). It is a central element of Buddhist practice and the eighth limb of the Noble Eightfold Path as "right concentration" (sammā samādhi).

The four jhānas

The canonical texts describe four progressive states of absorption (the rūpa jhānas). In the first jhāna, discursive thought remains but the five hindrances are suppressed, accompanied by rapture and pleasure born of seclusion. In the second, thought subsides and inner confidence and unification of mind arise. In the third, rapture fades, leaving equanimity and bodily pleasure. In the fourth, even pleasure is abandoned, leaving only equanimity and purified mindfulness. Beyond these lie four formless attainments (the āruppa states), based on infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception.

Role in the path

Scholars debate whether jhāna is indispensable for liberation or one tool among others. The Pali Canon portrays the Buddha as mastering the formless attainments under Āḷāra Kālāma and Uddaka Rāmaputta but finding them insufficient, then rediscovering jhāna as part of the path to awakening. In later traditions dhyāna gave its name to the Chan (China), Zen (Japan), and Seon (Korea) schools — all transliterations of "dhyāna."

Interpretation

Modern teachers and scholars differ on how "deep" canonical jhāna is, with some describing profound absorption in which the body and senses disappear entirely, and others a lighter state of embodied awareness. The term is also used more loosely in later Mahayana texts for various forms of meditation.
