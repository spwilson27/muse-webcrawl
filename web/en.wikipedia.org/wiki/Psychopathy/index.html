Psychopathy, sometimes informally called sociopathy, is a personality construct characterized by persistent antisocial behavior, impaired empathy and remorse, superficial charm, and bold, disinhibited, and egotistical traits. It is not an official diagnosis in the DSM-5, where the closest category is antisocial personality disorder (ASPD), but it is widely used in forensic and research settings, particularly as measured by the Psychopathy Checklist.

History of the concept

The modern concept descends from 19th-century notions of "moral insanity." Hervey Cleckley's The Mask of Sanity (1941) gave the classic clinical portrait: glib, intelligent, and outwardly sane individuals lacking conscience, guilt, and the capacity for genuine emotion or loyalty. Robert D. Hare operationalized Cleckley's description in the Psychopathy Checklist (1980) and its revision, the PCL–R (1991/2003), a 20-item rating scale scored from interviews and records. Hare's popular book Without Conscience (1993) fixed the term in public discourse.

Assessment and structure

PCL–R items cluster into two broad factors: (1) interpersonal/affective traits — glibness, grandiosity, pathological lying, manipulation, lack of remorse, shallow affect, callousness — and (2) social deviance — impulsivity, irresponsibility, parasitic lifestyle, poor behavioral controls, and criminal versatility. Many researchers adopt a three- or four-facet structure, and the "triarchic model" describes psychopathy in terms of boldness, meanness, and disinhibition. Cut scores differ by jurisdiction (30 in North America, 25 in much of Europe).

Causes and treatment

Twin and neuroimaging studies indicate substantial heritability and implicate reduced amygdala responsiveness to fear and distress in others, and ventromedial prefrontal dysfunction. Childhood factors such as callous-unemotional traits, abuse, and inconsistent discipline interact with biology. Psychopathy is widely regarded as difficult to treat; some studies suggest conventional therapy can worsen outcomes in high-scoring offenders by sharpening manipulation skills, though structured, risk-focused interventions with juveniles show more promise.

Criminal justice and popular culture

PCL–R scores predict recidivism and institutional misconduct, and are used in risk assessments, parole decisions, and civil commitment proceedings — a role critics say gives the label undue and stigmatizing weight. The construct also looms large in popular culture, where "psychopath" is loosely applied to serial killers and corporate villains alike; researchers such as Kevin Dutton have argued that moderated psychopathic traits can appear in high-performing professions, a claim treated cautiously by specialists.
