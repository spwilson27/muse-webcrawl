Press Gang

A press gang was a group of men — usually led by an officer and acting under legal authority — tasked with forcing (or "pressing") other men into service in a navy. Press gangs were most famously employed by the British Royal Navy during the age of sail, when wartime demand for sailors far exceeded the supply of volunteers. The practice is known as impressment, and the men so taken were liable to be carried off with little or no warning from streets, taverns, or inbound merchant ships.

Operation

Press gangs were organized by the Impress Service, which maintained rendezvous stations in ports across Britain and the empire. Parties of sailors and marines, armed with clubs and cutlasses, patrolled waterfront districts seeking men with seafaring experience — legally, only "eligible" mariners between roughly 18 and 55 could be pressed, though in practice landsmen were sometimes swept up. A pressed man received the king's shilling as advance pay; refusing it made no difference once he was in custody. Gangs also boarded homeward-bound merchant vessels and removed much of their crews. Ashore, "hot presses" followed declarations of war, and magistrates in some towns cooperated by emptying jails into the navy.

Resistance and unpopularity

Impressment was bitterly resented, and press gangs faced riots, legal challenges through habeas corpus, and occasionally armed resistance; some communities hid sailors or attacked the gangs. In North America, impressment of sailors from American ships — including naturalized and native-born Americans — was a major grievance of the young United States and contributed to tensions leading to the War of 1812. Famous incidents include the 1747 Knowles Riot in Boston.

Decline

Impressment was last used during the Napoleonic Wars. After 1815 the Royal Navy's manpower needs shrank, long-service volunteers became the norm, and the practice fell into abeyance, though it remained technically legal. The press gang survives in popular culture — in novels such as Treasure Island and in film — as a symbol of arbitrary state power and the hardships of naval life.
