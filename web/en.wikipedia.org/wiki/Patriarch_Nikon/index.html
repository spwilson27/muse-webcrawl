Patriarch Nikon

Nikon (Russian: Никон, born Nikita Minin, 7 May 1605 – 17 August 1681) was the seventh Patriarch of Moscow and all Rus' (1652–1666). He is best known for his liturgical reforms, begun in 1653, which aimed to bring the practices of the Russian Church into conformity with contemporary Greek practice — and which provoked the Raskol, the great schism that produced the Old Believers.

Early life and rise

Nikita Minin was born to a peasant family in the Nizhny Novgorod region. After the death of his children he persuaded his wife to take monastic vows and himself became a monk, rising rapidly through the church hierarchy with the favour of Tsar Alexis Mikhailovich, who made him archimandrite of the Novospassky Monastery in Moscow and then Metropolitan of Novgorod. On the death of Patriarch Joseph in 1652, Nikon was elected patriarch.

Reforms and schism

Nikon's reforms corrected service books against Greek originals and changed ritual details: the sign of the cross was to be made with three fingers instead of two, the spelling of Jesus's name was altered, processions were to move counter-clockwise, and the number of alleluias was changed. To Nikon's opponents, led by the Archpriest Avvakum, these were intolerable corruptions of the faith; those who refused the reforms were anathematised by councils in 1666–1667, splitting Russian Orthodoxy permanently.

Conflict with the Tsar and downfall

Nikon also asserted the authority of the church against the state, modelling the patriarchate on his reading of the Byzantine ideal and building the New Jerusalem Monastery near Moscow as a replica of the Holy Sepulchre. His ambitions estranged Tsar Alexis; Nikon withdrew from Moscow in 1658, and a council in 1666 deposed him, though it confirmed his reforms. He died in exile at Yaroslavl in 1681 while returning under a reprieve.
