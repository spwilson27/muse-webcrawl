Syrinx

The syrinx is a musical instrument, commonly known in English as the panpipes or pan flute, consisting of a series of stopped tubes of gradually increasing length bound together. The player blows across the open tops of the tubes to produce notes of different pitches. The instrument takes its name from Greek mythology, and variants of it appear in musical traditions around the world, from the Andes to Romania and China.

Mythology

In Greek myth, Syrinx was a chaste nymph of Arcadia who fled the god Pan. Cornered at the river Ladon, she was transformed — at her own plea — into water reeds. Pan, sighing through the reeds, heard the sound they produced and bound them together to make the first panpipes, naming the instrument after her.

Acoustics

Each tube of a syrinx is closed at one end, so the fundamental pitch is determined by the length of the air column. Blowing across the top excites the resonance of the tube. Longer tubes give lower notes. Traditional construction uses bamboo, giant cane, wood, or clay.

World traditions

  • The nai of Romania, a diatonic pan flute made famous by Gheorghe Zamfir.
  • The zampoña and rondador of Andean music.
  • The paixiao of ancient China.
  • The siku of Aymara and Quechua peoples.

The syrinx also appears in classical music, most famously in the solo for pan flute imitated by other instruments; Claude Debussy wrote a celebrated solo flute piece titled Syrinx (1913).
