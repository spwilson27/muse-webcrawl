Music of Andalusia

Andalusia is a region in southern Spain with a rich and influential musical tradition. The music of Andalusia is best known internationally for flamenco, an art form combining singing (cante), guitar playing (toque), dance (baile), and rhythmic handclapping (palmas), which was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010.

Historical influences

Andalusia's music reflects centuries of cultural layering: the Arab-Berber heritage of Al-Andalus, Romani (Gitano) traditions, Jewish music, and Castilian folk forms all contributed. Scholars note melodic and rhythmic affinities between flamenco singing and the music of North Africa and the eastern Mediterranean.

Flamenco

Flamenco emerged in its recognizable form in the late 18th and 19th centuries in cities such as Seville, Cádiz, and Jerez de la Frontera. Its many song forms (palos) include soleá, bulerías, alegrías, tangos, siguiriyas, and fandangos. Legendary figures include singers Camarón de la Isla and La Niña de los Peines, guitarist Paco de Lucía, and dancer Carmen Amaya.

Other genres

Beyond flamenco, Andalusia has traditions of copla (Spanish song), sevillanas (a festive folk dance music heard at fairs such as Seville's Feria de Abril), verdiales from Málaga, and the ritual music of Holy Week processions. The region also produced noted classical composers such as Manuel de Falla, born in Cádiz.

Andalusian classical music

The Andalusi classical music tradition of North Africa (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya) traces its origins to the courts of Muslim Spain and is preserved in repertoires such as the Moroccan al-Āla and Algerian san'a, structured in suites called nawba.
