Ciprofloxacin is a broad-spectrum fluoroquinolone antibiotic used to treat a variety of bacterial infections, including urinary tract infections, respiratory tract infections, gastrointestinal infections, skin and bone infections, and anthrax exposure. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Medical uses

Ciprofloxacin is active against many Gram-negative bacteria, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa and the Enterobacterales, and some Gram-positive organisms. Uses include complicated urinary tract infections, pyelonephritis, infectious diarrhoea (such as cholera and travellers' diarrhoea), typhoid fever, intra-abdominal infections (with metronidazole), prostatitis, and inhalational anthrax post-exposure prophylaxis. Topical forms treat bacterial conjunctivitis and otitis externa.

Mechanism of action

Like other fluoroquinolones, ciprofloxacin inhibits the bacterial enzymes DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) and topoisomerase IV, which are required for DNA replication, transcription and repair. The drug is bactericidal, and resistance arises through target-site mutations and efflux mechanisms.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include nausea, diarrhoea, vomiting, rash and abnormal liver function tests. Regulators have issued boxed warnings for serious, potentially irreversible reactions: tendinitis and tendon rupture, peripheral neuropathy, and central nervous system effects; the drug can also exacerbate myasthenia gravis and is associated with aortic aneurysm risk and Clostridioides difficile colitis. Because of these risks, agencies such as the FDA and EMA advise reserving fluoroquinolones for cases without alternative treatments for uncomplicated infections.

History

Ciprofloxacin was developed by Bayer and approved in the 1980s, becoming one of the most widely prescribed antibiotics in the world. It drew wide public attention in 2001, when it was stockpiled and dispensed in response to the anthrax letter attacks in the United States.
