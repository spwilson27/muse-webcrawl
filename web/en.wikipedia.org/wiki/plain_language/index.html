Plain language

Plain language aims for writing that readers can find, understand, and use on first reading.

Plain language is writing designed to ensure the reader understands as quickly, easily, and completely as possible. Plain language strives to be easy to read, understand, and use. It avoids verbose, convoluted language and jargon, and instead uses clear structure, short sentences, everyday words, and layouts that help readers find what they need.

The International Plain Language Federation defines a communication as plain language "if its wording, structure, and design are so clear that the intended readers can easily find what they need, understand what they find, and use that information."

History and law

Advocacy for plain language in government and law dates to the mid-20th century and gained momentum through consumer movements of the 1970s. In the United States, the Plain Writing Act of 2010 requires federal agencies to use clear communication in public documents. The United Kingdom's Plain English Campaign, founded in 1979, awards "Crystal Mark" seals to clear documents, and similar plain-language laws and initiatives exist in the European Union, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, and elsewhere.

Techniques

Plain-language techniques include addressing the reader directly ("you"), using the active voice, choosing common words over formal synonyms, keeping sentences and paragraphs short, using headings and lists to organize information, and testing documents with real readers. In legal writing, plain language seeks to replace "legalese" without sacrificing precision.
