Air France Flight 4590 was a scheduled international charter flight from Paris to New York City operated by the supersonic airliner Concorde. On 25 July 2000, the aircraft, registered F-BTSC, caught fire during takeoff from Charles de Gaulle Airport and crashed into a hotel in the nearby town of Gonesse, killing all 100 passengers and nine crew members as well as four people on the ground. It remains the only fatal accident involving Concorde and was a decisive factor in the retirement of the type three years later.

Accident sequence

During the takeoff roll, the Concorde ran over a titanium wear strip that had fallen from a Continental Airlines McDonnell Douglas DC-10 which had departed minutes earlier. The strip cut a tire on the left main landing gear; a large chunk of the bursting tire struck the underside of the left wing, sending a pressure shock through the fuel in tank No. 5 that ruptured the tank. Leaking fuel ignited — probably from an electrical arc in the gear bay or contact with hot engine parts — producing a massive flame trailing from the left wing. With two left engines losing thrust and the gear unable to retract, the aircraft could not gain speed or altitude; it banked left, stalled, and crashed into the Hôtelissimo Les Relais Bleus hotel in Gonesse.

Investigation

The French Bureau of Enquiry and Analysis (BEA) concluded that the accident was caused by the tire failure over the metal strip, the resulting tank rupture, and the fire; contributing factors included the aircraft being slightly overweight and an unbalanced center of gravity, and the crew shutting down engine No. 2 during the takeoff. Concorde operations were suspended while modifications were made — including stronger tires, fuel-tank liners of Kevlar, and wiring protection — and both Air France and British Airways returned the aircraft to service in November 2001. Legal proceedings followed: in 2010 a French court convicted Continental Airlines and one of its mechanics of involuntary manslaughter, though the conviction of the mechanic was overturned on appeal in 2012.

Aftermath

The crash, the post-September 11 downturn in air travel, and rising maintenance costs led Air France and British Airways to retire Concorde in 2003. The accident is one of the most thoroughly documented air disasters, having been photographed and filmed by multiple bystanders, and a memorial to the victims stands in Gonesse.
