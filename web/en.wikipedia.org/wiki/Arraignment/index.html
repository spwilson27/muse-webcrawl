An arraignment is a formal reading of a criminal charging document—an indictment or an information—in the presence of the defendant, to inform the defendant of the charges against them. The defendant is then asked to enter a plea. In response to arraignment, the accused is expected to plead guilty, not guilty, or, where permitted, nolo contendere (no contest) or stand mute.

Procedure

At arraignment the court confirms the defendant's identity, advises them of their rights—including the right to counsel—and appoints a public defender if the defendant cannot afford an attorney. The court may address bail or release conditions and set dates for pretrial motions and trial. Many defendants appear by video link for arraignment, and in minor cases the proceeding may be combined with an initial appearance.

Pleas

A not-guilty plea preserves all defenses and leads toward trial, including later voir dire jury selection. A guilty plea waives trial and moves the case to sentencing. Some defendants enter an Alford plea, pleading guilty while asserting innocence. If a defendant refuses to plead, courts enter a not-guilty plea on the defendant's behalf. Plea bargaining often resolves cases at or shortly after the arraignment stage, since the overwhelming majority of criminal cases end in pleas rather than trials.

By jurisdiction

In the United States federal system, arraignment is governed by Rule 10 of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, requiring the hearing to be held in open court with the charges read or summarized and a copy given to the defendant. England and Wales conduct a comparable "plea before venue" and plea-and-trial-preparation process in the Crown Court.
