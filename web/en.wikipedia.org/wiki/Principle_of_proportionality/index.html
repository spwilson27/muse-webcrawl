Principle of proportionality

The principle of proportionality is a legal and political concept used in constitutional law, administrative law, international humanitarian law and moral philosophy. In general terms it requires that measures taken by authorities be suitable and necessary to achieve a legitimate aim, and that the burden imposed not be excessive in relation to the benefit gained.

Constitutional and administrative law

In German public law, from which the modern doctrine spread, proportionality review typically asks four questions: does the measure pursue a legitimate purpose; is it suitable to achieve it; is it necessary (no equally effective, less restrictive alternative); and is it proportionate in the strict sense (balancing the public benefit against the infringement of rights)? The test is central to the jurisprudence of the German Federal Constitutional Court, the Court of Justice of the European Union, and the European Court of Human Rights, and has been adopted in Canada, South Africa, Israel, India and many other jurisdictions.

International humanitarian law

In the law of armed conflict, proportionality prohibits attacks which may be expected to cause incidental loss of civilian life, injury to civilians, or damage to civilian objects which would be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated. The rule is codified in Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions.

Criminal law and policing

Proportionality also governs sentencing — the punishment should fit the crime — and the use of force by police, which must be no greater than required by the situation.
