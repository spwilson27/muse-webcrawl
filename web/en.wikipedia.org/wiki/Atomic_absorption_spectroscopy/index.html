Atomic absorption spectroscopy

Atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS)
TechniqueSpectroanalytical chemistry
MeasuresConcentration of chemical elements in a sample
Key componentsHollow-cathode lamp, atomiser (flame or graphite furnace), monochromator, detector

Atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) is a spectroanalytical technique for the quantitative determination of chemical elements in a sample. It works by measuring the absorption of light, at wavelengths characteristic of a particular element, by free ground-state atoms produced from the sample in a flame or electrothermal atomiser. Because each element absorbs at its own narrow set of resonance lines, the method is highly specific and can measure metal concentrations down to parts per million (flame AAS) or parts per billion (graphite furnace AAS).

Principle

A liquid sample is aspirated into a flame (or deposited in a graphite tube that is heated electrically), where it is dried, vaporised and atomised. Light from a hollow-cathode lamp — whose cathode is made of the element being measured — passes through the atom cloud. The atoms absorb light at their resonance wavelength in proportion to their concentration, following the Beer–Lambert law, and a monochromator and detector quantify the absorption against standards of known concentration.

History

The underlying physics goes back to the Fraunhofer lines in the solar spectrum and the work of Kirchhoff and Bunsen on spectral analysis in the nineteenth century, but AAS became a practical analytical method only in the 1950s, when Alan Walsh and colleagues at CSIRO in Melbourne developed the hollow-cathode lamp and demonstrated the technique's power. Commercial instruments followed quickly, transforming trace-metal analysis.

Applications and limitations

AAS is widely used in environmental monitoring (metals in water and soil), clinical analysis (lead in blood, calcium in serum), food safety, mining and metallurgy, and pharmaceuticals. Its main limitation is that it measures essentially one element at a time; multi-element work often uses inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) or ICP-MS instead. Interferences from matrix components and ionisation are managed with background correction, releasing agents and matrix modifiers.
