Federal Art Project

Parent agencyWorks Progress Administration (WPA)
Operated1935–1943
DirectorHolger Cahill
OutputMillions of artworks, posters, murals, and photographs

The Federal Art Project (FAP) was the visual arts arm of the Works Progress Administration's Federal Project Number One, the New Deal programme that put unemployed artists to work during the Great Depression. Directed by Holger Cahill, it was the largest of the Depression-era art programmes.

Programmes

At its peak the FAP employed more than 5,000 artists. Its workshops produced an enormous body of public art: easel paintings and sculpture for public buildings, murals in schools and hospitals, more than two million posters printed from hundreds of thousands of designs, and the Index of American Design, a pictorial archive of American decorative arts. The project also ran more than 100 community art centres that brought exhibitions and classes to towns across the country, many serving Black and rural communities that museums had ignored.

Artists and legacy

The FAP supported young artists who later defined Abstract Expressionism, including Jackson Pollock, Willem de Kooning, Arshile Gorky, and Mark Rothko, as well as regionalist and social-realist painters. Though criticized by congressional conservatives and wound down by 1943, the project is credited with democratizing American art, creating a national audience for it, and keeping a generation of artists working through the Depression.
