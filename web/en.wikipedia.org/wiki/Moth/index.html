Moths are a group of insects that includes all members of the order Lepidoptera that are not butterflies. Most moth species are nocturnal, though some are crepuscular or diurnal. There are some 160,000 described species of moth — many times the number of butterflies — with thousands more still undescribed.

Moths are distinguished from butterflies by several features: their antennae are typically feathery or taper to a point rather than being clubbed; most rest with their wings spread flat or folded tent-like over the body; and their pupae are usually enclosed in a silken cocoon, whereas butterflies form an exposed chrysalis.

Biology

Like all Lepidoptera, moths undergo complete metamorphosis: egg, larva (caterpillar), pupa and adult. Many caterpillars are important herbivores, and a few — such as the clothes moths and the Indianmeal moth — are pests of stored goods and textiles. Others are economically vital: the domesticated silkworm (Bombyx mori) is the source of commercial silk, and hawk-moths, noctuids and many other moths are major pollinators of night-flowering plants.

Attraction to light

Moths are famously attracted to artificial lights, a behaviour whose mechanism remains debated. Leading explanations involve celestial navigation: moths may steer by keeping a constant angle to a distant light source such as the Moon, a strategy that fails catastrophically around nearby lamps, spiralling the insect inward. Recent work suggests artificial light also disrupts the moth's sense of the horizon, causing it to stall and circle.

Notable moths

Among the largest are the atlas moth (Attacus atlas) of Asia, with a wingspan exceeding 24 cm, and the white witch moth of South America. The death's-head hawkmoth is known for the skull-like marking on its thorax, and the Madagascan sunset moth is prized for its iridescent colours.
