London dispersion force

London dispersion forces (LDF, also known as dispersion forces, London forces or instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces) are a type of intermolecular force acting between atoms and molecules that are normally electrically symmetric. They are part of the van der Waals forces. The LDF is named after the German physicist Fritz London, who explained the effect quantum-mechanically in 1930.

Mechanism

Electrons in an atom or molecule are in constant motion, so at any instant the electron distribution may be uneven, producing a fleeting instantaneous dipole. This dipole induces a corresponding dipole in a neighbouring atom, and the two attract. Although each instantaneous configuration is short-lived, the effect averages to a persistent weak attraction. For two identical atoms the leading term of the interaction energy is approximately

E ≈ − (3/4) · (I α²) / r⁶

where I is the ionisation energy, α the polarisability and r the separation. The force therefore grows with the polarisability of the particles: larger, more diffuse electron clouds give stronger dispersion forces.

Importance

Dispersion forces are the weakest intermolecular forces, yet they dominate the interactions of nonpolar species. They are responsible for the condensation of noble gases and nonpolar molecules such as hydrocarbons at low temperatures, and they explain trends such as the increasing boiling points of the halogens and alkanes with molecular size. They also contribute to the adhesion of gecko feet, the stacking of aromatic rings and the binding of many drugs to their targets.
