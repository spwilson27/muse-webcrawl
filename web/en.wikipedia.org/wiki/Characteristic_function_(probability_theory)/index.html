Characteristic function (probability theory)

In probability theory and statistics, the characteristic function of any real-valued random variable completely defines its probability distribution. It is defined as the expected value of eitX, where i is the imaginary unit and t is a real parameter: φX(t) = E[eitX]. The characteristic function is the Fourier transform of the probability density (or measure) and always exists, unlike the moment-generating function.

Properties

The characteristic function φ(0) = 1, is uniformly continuous and bounded by 1, and is Hermitian: φ(−t) is the complex conjugate of φ(t). Independent random variables multiply: the characteristic function of a sum of independent variables is the product of their characteristic functions, which makes convolutions of distributions easy to compute. Moments, when they exist, are recovered from derivatives of φ at zero: E[Xn] = i−n φ(n)(0).

Inversion and continuity

Lévy's inversion formula recovers the distribution from its characteristic function, so the correspondence between distributions and characteristic functions is one-to-one. Bochner's theorem characterises which functions are characteristic functions: exactly the continuous positive-definite functions with φ(0) = 1. Lévy's continuity theorem states that pointwise convergence of characteristic functions (with continuity at zero) is equivalent to convergence in distribution, making characteristic functions the standard tool for proving central limit theorems and laws of large numbers.

Examples

For a normal distribution N(μ, σ²), φ(t) = exp(iμt − σ²t²/2). For a degenerate distribution at a, φ(t) = eita. The Cauchy distribution has φ(t) = exp(−|t|), illustrating that characteristic functions exist even when no moments do.
