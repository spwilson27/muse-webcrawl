In J. R. R. Tolkien's legendarium, the Elves are one of the races inhabiting Arda, the world in which Middle-earth is set. Known in their own tongue as the Quendi ("those who speak"), they are the Firstborn of the Children of Ilúvatar, awakening before Men. Elves are immortal — they do not die of old age or disease, though they can be slain or die of grief — and they remain bound to the world until its end. They appear in The Hobbit (1937), The Lord of the Rings (1954–55) and The Silmarillion (1977).

Characteristics

Tolkien's Elves are taller and fairer than Men, with keen eyesight and hearing, great skill in crafts, languages and music, and a deep affinity with the natural world. Unlike the diminutive fairies of later folklore, they are human in scale and largely human in appearance, distinguished by their beauty, longevity and pointy ears — Tolkien noted in a letter that their ears were "leaf-shaped".

History

The Elves awoke at Cuiviénen beside a lake in the far east of Middle-earth. The Valar summoned them to Valinor in the Great Journey; those who completed the journey and saw the light of the Two Trees were the Eldar of the West, while those who refused or turned aside were the Avari and the Elves of Middle-earth. In Valinor, Fëanor created the Silmarils, whose theft by Morgoth drove the exile of the Noldor and the wars of Beleriand in the First Age. In the Second Age, Celebrimbor forged the Rings of Power, deceived by Sauron. Through the Third Age the Elves waned, their realms — Rivendell, Lothlórien, the Woodland Realm — diminishing as most sailed West from the Grey Havens.

Languages

Tolkien constructed full languages for his Elves, chiefly Quenya (the classical, Latin-like tongue of the High Elves) and Sindarin (the everyday language of the Grey Elves of Beleriand). The Elvish scripts — the Tengwar of Fëanor and the Cirth runes — are used throughout the legendarium.

Influence

Tolkien's depiction reshaped the modern fantasy genre: the tall, noble, long-lived elf of subsequent fantasy literature and role-playing games, such as Dungeons & Dragons, derives overwhelmingly from his version rather than from the older folkloric elf.
