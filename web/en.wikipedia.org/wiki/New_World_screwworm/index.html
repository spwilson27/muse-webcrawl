The New World screwworm (Cochliomyia hominivorax) is a parasitic blow fly native to the tropical and subtropical Americas. Its larvae ("screwworms") feed on the living flesh of warm-blooded animals, including livestock, wildlife, pets, and occasionally humans, making it one of the most destructive livestock pests in the Western Hemisphere.

Biology

Adult flies are metallic blue-green with orange eyes and three dark stripes along the back. Females lay masses of 200–400 eggs at the edges of open wounds — even minor ones such as tick bites, castration or dehorning wounds, or the navel of a newborn calf. The eggs hatch within about a day, and the larvae burrow into living tissue, feeding head-down in a characteristic spiral or screw-like posture that gives the pest its name. After five to seven days the mature larvae drop to the ground, pupate in the soil, and emerge as adults, completing a cycle of roughly three weeks in warm weather.

Unlike most blow fly maggots, which feed on dead tissue, screwworm larvae consume living flesh and continually enlarge the wound, attracting further egg-laying. Untreated infestations (myiasis) can kill livestock and wildlife within one to two weeks through tissue destruction, secondary infection, and toxemia.

Eradication

The screwworm was famously eliminated from the United States, Mexico, and Central America using the sterile insect technique, developed by entomologists Edward Knipling and Raymond Bushland. Mass-reared male flies sterilized by radiation were released in enormous numbers; because females mate only once, mating with sterile males produced no offspring, collapsing wild populations. The pest was declared eradicated from the United States in 1966 and pushed progressively southward. A permanent sterile-fly barrier is maintained in Panama's Darién region to prevent re-invasion from South America, where the parasite remains endemic. Notable later incursions — including a 2016 outbreak in the Florida Keys that threatened the endangered Key deer, and detections in Central America and Mexico in the 2020s — have been countered with renewed sterile releases and animal-movement controls.
