The Goldfields Water Supply Scheme is a pipeline and dam project that delivers potable water from Mundaring Weir, near Perth, to the communities of Western Australia's Eastern Goldfields, particularly Coolgardie and Kalgoorlie, over a distance of about 560 kilometres (350 mi). It is considered one of the great engineering feats of its era and one of the largest public works of the newly federated Australia.

Background

The gold rushes of the 1890s brought tens of thousands of people to the arid goldfields, where fresh water was scarce, expensive, and often contaminated; typhoid from polluted water killed many. The government of Premier John Forrest championed a scheme to pump water inland, and the colony's Engineer-in-Chief, C. Y. O'Connor, designed the project: a dam on the Helena River at Mundaring and a steel pipeline lifted over the Darling Range by a series of eight steam pumping stations.

Construction and aftermath

Construction began in 1898. The scheme was attacked in parliament and the press as extravagant and impractical, and O'Connor, under intense public criticism, died by suicide in March 1902, less than a year before the water reached Kalgoorlie in January 1903. The pipeline used locking-bar steel pipes patented by Mephan Ferguson and was the longest freshwater pipeline in the world at the time.

Legacy

The scheme still supplies the goldfields, augmented by later electric pumping stations and additional reservoirs. Much of the original route is interpreted along the Golden Pipeline Heritage Trail, and the project is listed as a National Engineering Landmark and on Australia's National Heritage List.
