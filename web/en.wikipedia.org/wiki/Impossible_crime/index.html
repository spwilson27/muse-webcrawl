An impossible crime is a crime in detective fiction that apparently cannot have been committed, or cannot have been committed by anyone: a murder in a room locked and sealed from the inside, a corpse found in untouched snow with no footprints, a victim stabbed in a watched and empty space. The most famous subtype is the locked-room mystery.

History

Edgar Allan Poe's "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" (1841) established the locked-room problem, and Gaston Leroux's The Mystery of the Yellow Room (1907) became a landmark of the form. John Dickson Carr, regarded as the genre's master, built most of his novels around impossible crimes; his detective Dr Gideon Fell delivers a celebrated "locked-room lecture" in The Three Coffins (1935), cataloguing the possible solutions. Clayton Rawson's magician-detective stories and, later, the Japanese shin honkaku school of Soji Shimada revived the form.

Types

Beyond locked rooms, impossible crimes include murders committed in places under constant observation, killings by apparently supernatural means, victims found in unmarked snow or sand, impossible disappearances of people or objects, and crimes where no weapon can be found. The solution, by genre convention, must be rational and in principle deducible from clues fairly presented to the reader — the appeal lies in reconciling the impossible with the mundane.
