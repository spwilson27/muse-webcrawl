The Geꞌez script (ግእዝ) is an abugida (alphasyllabary) used to write several languages of Ethiopia and Eritrea. It originated as a consonantal script (abjad) derived from the Ancient South Arabian script, and was transformed — uniquely among Semitic scripts — into a fully vocalized system in which each consonant letter is systematically modified to indicate one of seven vowels. The characters are called fidäl (ፊደል), a word also used for the script as a whole and for its traditional ordering.

History

The earliest inscriptions from the Horn of Africa, from around the 5th century BCE in the pre-Aksumite kingdom of Dʿmt, were written in the South Arabian musnad, sometimes in boustrophedon. A distinct local ductus developed, and by the early centuries CE the script was written left-to-right — possibly under Greek influence. The decisive innovation came in the 4th century CE, traditionally under King Ezana of Aksum: vowels began to be marked by systematic modifications of the consonant signs (added strokes, shortened legs, rings), converting the abjad into an abugida. Some scholars see a possible influence of Indian scripts in the concept, though the forms themselves are indigenous developments.

Geꞌez inscriptions of Ezana, including the famous trilingual victory stelae, are among the principal sources for the history of the Kingdom of Aksum. The script went on to be used for the extensive Geꞌez manuscript literature and, from the 19th and 20th centuries, was adapted with additional characters for modern languages including Amharic and Tigrinya.

Structure

The core inventory has 26 consonantal base signs. Each sign appears in seven "orders" corresponding to the vowels ä, u, i, a, e, ə, and o; the first order is the basic form and traditionally also serves for the consonant plus the vowel ä. The sixth order (ə) doubles as the vowelless (syllable-final or consonant-cluster) form. This gives a core grid of 182 syllabograms.

Labialized variants of the velars and a few other consonants form additional rows, and languages such as Amharic added signs for sounds absent from classical Geꞌez (for example ሸ sha, ቨ va, and ኸ xa). Traditional punctuation includes the word divider ፥ (two dots, now largely replaced by spaces), the sentence-final ።, and marks for colon, comma, and paragraph. Ethiopic numerals — a vigesimal-style system without a zero — survive mainly in liturgical and calendrical use alongside Arabic numerals.

Use today

The script is the everyday writing system of Ethiopia and Eritrea, used for Amharic (the working language of the Ethiopian federal government), Tigrinya, Tigre, and several other languages, serving well over 100 million people. It is encoded in the Unicode block "Ethiopic" (U+1200–U+137F) with supplement and extended blocks, and is fully supported on modern operating systems and mobile devices.
