Fermi level

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Fermi level of a solid-state body is the thermodynamic work required to add one electron to the body; equivalently, it is the electrochemical potential of electrons in the body. It is a central concept in the band theory of solids, named after Enrico Fermi. The probability that an electron state at the Fermi level is occupied, given by the Fermi–Dirac distribution, is exactly one half (at finite temperature).

Definition and interpretation

At absolute zero, the Fermi level coincides with the Fermi energy: the energy of the highest occupied state in a system of fermions. At higher temperatures the Fermi level (chemical potential) shifts somewhat. In a metal it lies within a band of allowed states; in a semiconductor or insulator it lies within the band gap, its position governed by doping — near the conduction band for n-type material and near the valence band for p-type.

Equilibrium and voltage

When two materials are brought into electrical contact, electrons flow until their Fermi levels align; this equalisation underlies contact potentials and the operation of p–n junctions. Because voltmeters measure differences in the electrochemical potential of electrons, the difference in Fermi levels between two terminals, divided by the electron charge, equals the measured voltage. Under non-equilibrium conditions (e.g. a device under bias or illumination), separate quasi-Fermi levels describe electrons and holes.
