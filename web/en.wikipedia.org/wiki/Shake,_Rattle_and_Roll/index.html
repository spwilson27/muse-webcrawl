Shake, Rattle and Roll

"Shake, Rattle and Roll" is a twelve-bar blues song written in 1954 by Jesse Stone, who used the pen name Charles Calhoun. It was first recorded by American blues shouter Big Joe Turner, whose version for Atlantic Records became a number-one rhythm and blues hit. A bowdlerized cover by Bill Haley & His Comets later that year reached the top ten of the pop charts and helped introduce rock and roll to a mass white audience. The song ranks among the most influential recordings of the genre's early years, and Turner's version was inducted into the Grammy Hall of Fame and the Blues Hall of Fame.

Big Joe Turner version

Turner recorded the song in New York on February 15, 1954, with producer Ahmet Ertegun singing backup and providing the handclaps and exhortations audible in the mix. The lyric, drawn from African-American slang and earlier blues idioms about dice games and romance, was raunchy by the standards of pop radio: Turner sang about a woman in a "dress the sun comes shinin' through" and urged her to "shake, rattle and roll." The record topped the Billboard R&B chart and crossed over modestly to pop, peaking in the low twenties.

Bill Haley version

Bill Haley's cover, recorded for Decca in June 1954, cleaned up the more suggestive lines — a standard industry practice of the day — and pepped the tempo. It reached number seven on the Billboard pop chart, becoming Haley's first top-ten hit before "Rock Around the Clock." The controversy over lyric changes has since been much debated: Haley's version was cleaner but also rhythmically energetic, and its success broadened the market for rock and roll.

Other versions and legacy

"Shake, Rattle and Roll" has been recorded by Elvis Presley (who combined the Turner and Haley arrangements), Jerry Lee Lewis, Buddy Holly, the Beatles (in a medley), and many others. Rolling Stone magazine placed Turner's recording on its list of the 500 Greatest Songs of All Time. The phrase itself entered the general culture as a byword for exuberant dancing and rock and roll itself.
