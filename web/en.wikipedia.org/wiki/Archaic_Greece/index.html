Archaic Greece

Archaic Greece was the period in Greek history lasting from circa 800 BC to the second Persian invasion of Greece in 480 BC, following the Greek Dark Ages and succeeded by the Classical period. In this period, the Greeks settled across the Mediterranean and Black Seas — a process called Greek colonisation — and established city-states (poleis) that remained the dominant political unit. The era saw the emergence of the alphabet (adapted from Phoenician), the poems of Homer and Hesiod, coinage, hoplite warfare, Panhellenic sanctuaries and games, and the first experiments in philosophy and democracy.

Political development

Early archaic communities were ruled by aristocracies; social and economic stress — land hunger, debt, and the costs of hoplite equipment — produced lawgivers and tyrants. Draco and Solon reformed Athens (594 BC); Cleisthenes' reforms (508/7 BC) laid the foundations of Athenian democracy. Sparta developed its distinctive militarized constitution under the "Lycurgan" system. Colonies from Syracuse and Tarentum in the west to Byzantium and the Black Sea coast spread Greek language and institutions abroad while importing grain, metals, and slaves.

Culture

Archaic art is marked by the kouros and kore statues with their characteristic "archaic smile," black-figure and then red-figure vase painting (invented c. 530 BC), and monumental stone temples in the Doric and Ionic orders. Lyric poetry flourished (Sappho, Alcaeus, Pindar), and in Ionia the first philosophers — Thales, Anaximander, Heraclitus, Pythagoras — began rational inquiry into nature. The period ended with the Greco-Persian Wars: Marathon (490 BC), Thermopylae and Salamis (480 BC).
