Carding

Carding is a mechanical process that disentangles, cleans and intermixes fibres to produce a continuous web or sliver suitable for subsequent processing in spinning. This is achieved by passing the fibres between differentially moving surfaces covered with card clothing — densely set pins, or in older implementations leather embedded with wire hooks. Carding is one of the key preparatory steps in the manufacture of textiles from staple fibres such as cotton and wool.

Process

In a revolving flat carding machine, raw fibre is fed by a lap roller to a spiked licker-in (taker-in) roller, which opens and cleans the fibre before transferring it to a large rotating cylinder covered in card clothing. A series of stationary or revolving flats work in close proximity to the cylinder, combing out short fibres, neps and remaining impurities. The cleaned fibre is then stripped from the cylinder by a smaller doffer roller and condensed into a continuous web, which is drawn through a trumpet to form a rope-like sliver.

History

Hand carding with pairs of wooden paddles studded with pins was practised for centuries before mechanisation. The word derives from the Latin carduus, meaning thistle or teasel, since dried vegetable teasels were first used to comb raw wool. In 1748 Lewis Paul received a patent for a roller carding machine, and later innovators including Richard Arkwright incorporated carding engines into the early factory system of the Industrial Revolution.
