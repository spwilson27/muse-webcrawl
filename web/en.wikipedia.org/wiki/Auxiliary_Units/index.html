Auxiliary Units

The Auxiliary Units were a secret resistance network established in the United Kingdom during the Second World War. Created in the summer of 1940 under the direction of Colonel Colin Gubbins, they were intended to act as a stay-behind force in the event of a successful German invasion of Britain, conducting sabotage and guerrilla operations behind enemy lines.

Organisation

The units were organised in small patrols of four to eight men, recruited locally from farm workers, gamekeepers, poachers and others with intimate knowledge of the countryside, often vetted through Home Guard channels. Each patrol was provided with a concealed underground Operational Base, stocked with explosives, weapons and supplies for a mission expected to last only a few weeks. A separate signals network, the Special Duties Section, staffed partly by civilian radio operators, was to pass intelligence on enemy movements.

Secrecy and legacy

The existence of the Auxiliary Units was classified for decades after the war; members had signed the Official Secrets Act and most never spoke of their role. Around 3,500 men are believed to have served in the operational patrols. Since declassification, surviving bases have been surveyed by researchers and the Coleshill Auxiliary Research Team, and the units are now commemorated as Britain's planned resistance organisation, sometimes nicknamed "the British Resistance".
