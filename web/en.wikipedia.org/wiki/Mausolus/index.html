Mausolus (Greek: Μαύσωλος) was a ruler of Caria in southwestern Anatolia, nominally a satrap of the Achaemenid Persian Empire but effectively an independent king, who reigned from 377 to 353 BCE. His name lives on in the word mausoleum, from the magnificent tomb built for him at Halicarnassus, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.

Reign

Mausolus succeeded his father Hecatomnus and inherited a wealthy, semi-autonomous principality. He moved his capital from Mylasa to Halicarnassus, refounding it as a fortified port city on Greek lines, and extended Carian influence over Lycia and parts of Ionia and the Aegean islands, participating in the "Revolt of the Satraps" against Artaxerxes II while carefully avoiding final defeat. Though Persian in allegiance, his court was deeply Hellenised, patronising Greek architects, sculptors and rhetors such as Isocrates' circle.

The Mausoleum

Mausolus planned his tomb in his lifetime at the centre of his new capital; after his death in 353 BCE his widow and sister Artemisia II — famed for her grief, drinking his ashes in wine according to Roman writers — completed it. Greek architects Satyros and Pythius designed the monument, and four master sculptors each decorated a side. The colossal statues of Mausolus and Artemisia, recovered in the 1850s, show the couple in a blend of Greek technique and Persian dress, and are now in the British Museum.
