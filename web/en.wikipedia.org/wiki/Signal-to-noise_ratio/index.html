Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR or S/N) is a measure used in science and engineering that compares the level of a desired signal to the level of background noise. SNR is defined as the ratio of signal power to noise power, often expressed in decibels. A ratio higher than 1:1 (greater than 0 dB) indicates more signal than noise.

SNR is an important parameter in any system that processes signals, from audio recording and radio communications to medical imaging and analytical instruments: it bounds the smallest signal that can be reliably detected and, through the Shannon–Hartley theorem, the maximum data rate of a communications channel.

Definition

The signal-to-noise ratio is the ratio of the powers of the signal and the noise:

SNR = Psignal / Pnoise

Expressed in decibels, using the power ratio:

SNRdB = 10 log10(Psignal / Pnoise)

Because many signals have a very wide dynamic range, SNR is usually reported on this logarithmic scale. When the quantities are amplitudes (such as voltages) rather than powers, the corresponding expression uses 20 log10 of the amplitude ratio, since power is proportional to the square of amplitude.

Measurement and improvement

For a digitised signal, SNR can be estimated from the ratio of the signal's root mean square value to the standard deviation of noise-only segments. The number of bits of an analogue-to-digital converter sets an ideal quantisation SNR of approximately 6.02 dB per bit.

Techniques for improving SNR include increasing signal power, reducing noise through shielding and filtering, lowering the temperature of components (to reduce thermal noise), oversampling and averaging repeated measurements (averaging improves SNR in proportion to the square root of the number of samples), and matched filtering, which maximises SNR for a known signal shape in white noise.

Applications

In audio engineering, SNR describes how much of an output is the intended sound versus hiss and interference; consumer hi-fi equipment commonly achieves 90 dB or more. In wireless communications, SNR determines achievable throughput and is closely related to the energy-per-bit to noise-power-spectral-density ratio (Eb/N0). In imaging, SNR is defined as the mean of a signal region divided by the standard deviation within it, and governs detectability in modalities such as MRI and astronomy, where long exposures and detector cooling are used to raise it. In analytical chemistry, the limit of detection is often taken as the concentration giving an SNR of about 3, and the limit of quantification an SNR of about 10.

Related measures

Related figures of merit include signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR), which adds interference to the denominator; signal-to-noise and distortion ratio (SINAD), which includes harmonic distortion; and carrier-to-noise ratio, measured before modulation detection in radio receivers. In image processing, peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) compares a signal's maximum possible value with the mean squared error of a reconstructed image and is widely used to assess compression quality.
