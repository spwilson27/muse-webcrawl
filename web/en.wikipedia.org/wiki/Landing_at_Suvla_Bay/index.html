The Landing at Suvla Bay was an amphibious landing made at Suvla on the Aegean coast of the Gallipoli peninsula in August 1915, during the First World War. Carried out by the British IX Corps under Lieutenant-General Sir Frederick Stopford, it was the largest single component of the August Offensive, intended to support the breakout from Anzac Cove by seizing the Anafarta hills behind the Ottoman lines. The landing achieved near-total surprise but is remembered as a classic of lost opportunity.

The landing

On the night of 6–7 August 1915, some 20,000 troops of the 10th (Irish) and 11th (Northern) Divisions came ashore — many in the new self-propelled armoured lighters nicknamed "Beetles" — against barely 1,500 Ottoman troops screening the bay. Initial objectives, the low hills of Lala Baba and the ridge lines of Tekke Tepe and the Anafarta heights, lay almost undefended. Confusion on the beaches, grounding lighters, a desperate shortage of water, and contradictory orders consumed the first day; Stopford, commanding from the sloop Jonquil offshore, congratulated his men on the landing but did not press inland.

Failure and stalemate

By the time IX Corps advanced in strength on 9 August, Ottoman reinforcements rushed from Bulair by Liman von Sanders had occupied the heights, and Mustafa Kemal took command of the sector just as the Chunuk Bair fight reached its climax. A final set-piece attack on 21 August — at Scimitar Hill and Hill 60, the largest single battle of the campaign, involving the dismounted yeomanry in a charge across open ground — failed with heavy loss. Stopford was relieved on 15 August, replaced temporarily by Major-General Henry de Beauvoir de Lisle. Suvla settled into trench stalemate and was evacuated on the night of 19–20 December 1915 along with Anzac, the guns and stores slipping away almost unmolested.

Assessment

Historians have debated how much blame belongs to Stopford — elderly, ailing, and chosen by seniority — how much to the plan's vagueness, imposed by Sir Ian Hamilton's headquarters, and how much to the rawness of Kitchener's New Army divisions. The Dardanelles Commission later censured the command arrangements. Suvla remains a byword for the squandering of a fleeting advantage.
