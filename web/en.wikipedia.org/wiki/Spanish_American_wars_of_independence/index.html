Spanish American wars of independence

The Spanish American wars of independence (1808–1833) were the series of revolutionary wars by which the Spanish colonies in the Americas achieved independence, destroying the Spanish Empire in the New World (except for Cuba and Puerto Rico, retained until 1898). Triggered by Napoleon's invasion of Spain in 1808 and the collapse of legitimate monarchical authority, the wars began as movements by colonial juntas claiming to govern in the name of the captive Ferdinand VII, and developed into struggles between royalists and patriots led by figures such as Simón Bolívar, José de San Martín, Miguel Hidalgo and Agustín de Iturbide.

Causes

Long-term causes included creole resentment of peninsular privilege and trade restrictions, Enlightenment ideas, and the examples of American and French revolutions and Haitian independence. The immediate crisis came when Napoleon deposed the Spanish Bourbons in 1808; colonial cities formed juntas, some of which radicalised into declarations of independence from 1810 onward.

Course of the wars

In Mexico, Hidalgo's 1810 uprising and Morelos's insurgency were suppressed, but independence was achieved in 1821 by Iturbide's Plan of Iguala. In South America, Bolívar liberated Venezuela, Colombia and Ecuador in the north — winning at Boyacá (1819) and Carabobo (1821) — while San Martín crossed the Andes to free Chile (1818) and proclaimed Peruvian independence (1821). Sucre's victory at Ayacucho (1824) ended Spanish power in South America; Upper Peru became Bolivia in 1825. Central America separated largely peacefully in 1821.

Consequences

The wars produced a patchwork of new republics rather than the continental unity Bolívar envisioned, devastated economies and populations, and entrenched militarism and caudillo politics. Britain and the United States recognised the new states in the 1820s, and Spain made its last attempts at reconquest in 1829 (Mexico) and formally relinquished claims over the following decades.
