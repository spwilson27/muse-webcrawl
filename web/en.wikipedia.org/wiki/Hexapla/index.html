The Hexapla (Greek: Ἑξαπλᾶ, "sixfold") is an edition of the Old Testament compiled by the Christian scholar Origen of Alexandria in the 3rd century AD. It arranged the biblical text in six parallel columns: the Hebrew text; a transliteration of the Hebrew into Greek letters; and four Greek versions — those of Aquila, Symmachus, the Septuagint (in Origen's revised text), and Theodotion. It is the most ambitious work of textual criticism produced in the ancient world, and a landmark in the history of the biblical text.

Purpose and method

Origen prepared the Hexapla at Alexandria and completed it at Caesarea Maritima to give Christians a reliable basis for debate with Jewish scholars and to correct the Septuagint toward the Hebrew. In the Septuagint column he marked passages present in Greek but not Hebrew with an obelus (÷), and passages supplied from the other versions with an asterisk (※) — critical signs borrowed from Homeric scholarship that became standard tools of textual criticism.

Transmission

The complete Hexapla was never copied in full — it filled perhaps fifty volumes — and was kept in the library at Caesarea, where it was consulted by Eusebius and Jerome. The original perished, most plausibly with the library in the Arab conquest of the 7th century. Its Septuagint text was copied separately, in a recension through which Origen's revisions deeply influenced later Septuagint manuscripts, and fragments of other columns survive in palimpsests and marginal notes. Frederick Field's 19th-century edition, Origenis Hexaplorum quae supersunt (1875), gathered the surviving remains, and modern projects continue to re-edit the fragments.

For some books Origen added further versions, producing the so-called Octapla; a shorter four-column edition of the Psalms is known as the Tetrapla.
