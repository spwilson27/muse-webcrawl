The Foucault pendulum is a simple device named after French physicist Léon Foucault, conceived as an experiment to demonstrate the rotation of the Earth. A long, heavy pendulum swings freely in a vertical plane; as the Earth rotates beneath it, the plane of oscillation appears to rotate, at a rate depending on latitude.

The 1851 demonstrations

Foucault first observed the effect in January 1851 with a pendulum in his cellar, then demonstrated it at the Paris Observatory and, famously, under the dome of the Panthéon in Paris, where a 28 kg bob hung from a 67-metre wire. The public demonstration, attended by Napoleon III, made Foucault famous; replicas were soon installed in museums around the world.

Physics

The rate of precession is proportional to the sine of the latitude: at the North Pole the plane rotates once per sidereal day, clockwise as seen from above (anticlockwise at the South Pole), and at the equator there is no rotation. In modern terms the effect is explained by the Coriolis force acting in the rotating frame of the Earth. Foucault pendulums remain popular science museum exhibits, often knocking over markers arranged around the swing's perimeter as the plane advances.
