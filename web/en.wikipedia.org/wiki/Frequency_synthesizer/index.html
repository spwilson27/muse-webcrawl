Frequency synthesizer

A frequency synthesizer is an electronic circuit that generates a range of frequencies from a single fixed reference frequency, typically provided by a stable crystal oscillator. Frequency synthesizers are used in radio receivers and transmitters, televisions, mobile telephones, GPS receivers, signal generators and many other devices that require stable, accurately tunable frequencies.

Types

Phase-locked loop (PLL) synthesizers

The most common design uses a phase-locked loop. A voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) produces the output; its signal is divided by a programmable counter and compared in a phase detector with the reference. The resulting error signal, filtered by a loop filter, drives the VCO so that the divided output locks to the reference. Changing the division ratio changes the output frequency in steps equal to the reference frequency (or a divided version of it). Fractional-N synthesizers alternate between division ratios to achieve finer steps than the reference spacing.

Direct digital synthesis

Direct digital synthesis (DDS) generates a waveform digitally: a phase accumulator advances through a lookup table of sine values at a rate set by a tuning word, and a digital-to-analogue converter produces the output. DDS offers extremely fine frequency resolution and fast switching, though output frequencies are limited relative to the clock rate and spurious performance is a concern.

Direct analog synthesis

Older and specialised designs mix, multiply and filter signals from several crystal sources to produce the output directly. Such synthesizers switch quickly and cleanly but are bulky and expensive.

Performance measures

Key specifications include frequency range and resolution, switching (settling) time, phase noise, spurious output levels and reference sidebands. Designers trade these against cost, size and power consumption; modern radio integrated circuits integrate complete synthesizers alongside transceiver circuitry.
