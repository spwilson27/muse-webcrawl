Accounting scandals

Accounting scandals
Notable casesEnron (2001), WorldCom (2002), Parmalat (2003), Satyam (2009), Wirecard (2020)
Typical methodsRevenue inflation, hidden debt, off-balance-sheet entities, fictitious assets

Accounting scandals are business scandals arising from the deliberate manipulation of financial statements by executives, auditors, or both, usually to inflate profits, hide debt, or sustain a company's share price. They frequently destroy the companies involved and trigger regulatory reform.

Major cases

The collapse of Enron in 2001 — then the largest bankruptcy in US history — exposed the use of off-balance-sheet special purpose entities to hide debt; its auditor, Arthur Andersen, was destroyed by an obstruction conviction. WorldCom surpassed it in 2002 after capitalising ordinary expenses to fabricate profits. In Europe, Italy's Parmalat collapsed in 2003 with a €14 billion hole, including a fictitious bank account. India's Satyam founder confessed in 2009 to inventing over $1 billion in cash. Germany's Wirecard failed in 2020 when €1.9 billion purportedly held by trustees could not be found. Separately, Bernard Madoff's operation (exposed 2008) was a Ponzi scheme rather than a public-company accounting fraud, but it is often grouped with the era's great financial scandals.

Responses

Major scandals prompted reforms such as the US Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002, which created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board and tightened internal-control and auditor-independence rules, and parallel governance reforms in Europe and Asia. Enforcement actions proceed through regulators and through civil litigation governed in the US by the rules described under civil procedure.
