Serial (radio and television)

Radio serials were a staple of 1930s broadcasting.

In broadcasting, a serial is a program presented in sequential episodes in which a continuing story unfolds over many installments, each episode building on the last and often ending in suspense. Serials are distinguished from series, whose episodes are largely self-contained stories featuring the same cast. The form embraces daytime soap operas, adventure serials for children, radio dramas, film serials shown in cinemas, and, in modern television, strongly serialized dramas whose season- or series-long arcs reward continuous viewing.

Radio serials

American network radio developed the serial in the late 1920s and 1930s. Daytime serials aimed at homemakers and sponsored by soap and detergent manufacturers gave the soap opera its name; programs such as The Guiding Light (from 1937) ran for decades and migrated to television. Evening and children's adventure serials — The Lone Ranger, The Green Hornet, Jack Armstrong, the All-American Boy — used daily or weekly cliffhangers to hold audiences, while film studios produced chapter plays such as Flash Gordon (1936) for Saturday matinees, adapted from and feeding back into radio and comics.

Television serials

Early television continued radio's daytime soaps and added prime-time experiments. The modern serialized drama emerged from the 1980s with shows such as Hill Street Blues, St. Elsewhere, and Dallas — whose "Who shot J.R.?" cliffhanger in 1980 became a global media event. Serial storytelling expanded with Twin Peaks, The X-Files' mythology episodes, and the intricate arcs of Babylon 5 and Buffy the Vampire Slayer. In the 2000s, programs such as The Sopranos, Lost, and 24 — the latter built entirely around real-time serialization — made continuing arcs the prestige norm, a trend consolidated by streaming services releasing entire seasons at once and viewers "binge-watching" them. Telenovelas, produced across Latin America and exported worldwide, are closed-ended serials with a predetermined conclusion, unlike open-ended soaps.

Narrative features

Serials depend on continuity: recurring characters whose lives develop, long-term mysteries, and episode-ending hooks. Writers manage continuity with series "bibles," and recaps ("Previously on…") orient intermittent viewers. The form raises characteristic production risks: unresolved arcs if a program is cancelled, cast changes disrupting storylines, and the tension between accessibility for new viewers and depth for loyal ones. Scholars distinguish degrees of serialization, from fully episodic series through "flexi-narratives" mixing standalone and continuing plots to fully serialized works.
