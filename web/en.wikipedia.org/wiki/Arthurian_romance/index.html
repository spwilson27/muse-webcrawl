Arthurian romance is a genre of medieval literature centered on the legendary King Arthur, his court at Camelot, the Knights of the Round Table, and the quest for the Holy Grail. Beginning in the twelfth century, the romances transformed Arthur from a figure of Welsh tradition and pseudo-historical chronicle into the center of a vast story-world that came to be known, after the French, as the matière de Bretagne (the "Matter of Britain").

Origins

Arthur appears in early Welsh sources such as the Historia Brittonum and the Annales Cambriae, and in Welsh poetry and prose tales later collected in the Mabinogion. The decisive expansion came with Geoffrey of Monmouth's Latin Historia Regum Britanniae (c. 1138), which gave Arthur a full royal biography. Wace's Anglo-Norman Roman de Brut (1155) adapted Geoffrey and introduced the Round Table, and Layamon's English Brut carried the legend further.

Chrétien de Troyes and the verse romances

The French poet Chrétien de Troyes, writing in the 1170s and 1180s, is generally credited with inventing the Arthurian romance as such. His five romances—Erec and Enide, Cligès, Yvain, the Knight of the Lion, Lancelot, the Knight of the Cart, and Perceval, the Story of the Grail—shifted focus from Arthur himself to individual knights, combining adventure (aventure) with the psychology of courtly love. Chrétien introduced Lancelot's adulterous love for Queen Guinevere and the first literary treatment of the Grail.

Prose cycles and the Grail quest

In the thirteenth century, French prose cycles—above all the Lancelot-Grail (Vulgate) Cycle and the later Post-Vulgate—organized the material into enormous interlaced narratives running from biblical times to Arthur's death. In these works the Grail quest becomes a spiritual trial that only the pure knight Galahad, a figure new to the prose tradition, can complete. German poets produced major versions of their own, including Wolfram von Eschenbach's Parzival and Gottfried von Strassburg's Tristan.

Later medieval and modern tradition

In England the alliterative and stanzaic traditions produced works such as Sir Gawain and the Green Knight, whose hero confronts a mysterious supernatural challenger. Sir Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur (completed 1469–70, printed by Caxton in 1485) synthesized the French cycles into a single English prose narrative and became the chief source for later retellings. Victorian poets such as Tennyson (in Idylls of the King) and Pre-Raphaelite artists revived the material, and twentieth- and twenty-first-century novels, films, and games have continued to reshape the romances for new audiences.

Themes

Recurring concerns of the romances include chivalric honor and its failures, the tension between earthly love and spiritual duty, the testing of knights through marvels and adventures, and the rise and fall of the Arthurian kingdom—framed as a golden age doomed by internal sin and betrayal.
