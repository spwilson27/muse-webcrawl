Goldschmidt classification

The Goldschmidt classification, developed by Victor Goldschmidt (1888–1947), is a geochemical classification of the chemical elements based on their preferred host phases and affinities in the Earth. It divides elements into four main groups:

The four groups

Lithophile ("rock-loving") elements concentrate in silicate phases of the crust and mantle and readily combine with oxygen; they include silicon, aluminium, magnesium, calcium, sodium, and potassium, as well as the rare earth elements, titanium, and uranium.

Siderophile ("iron-loving") elements dissolve in molten iron and are concentrated in the Earth's core; they include iron, nickel, cobalt, and the platinum-group metals, together with gold and molybdenum.

Chalcophile ("ore-loving") elements combine preferentially with sulfur and occur in sulfide minerals; they include copper, zinc, lead, silver, cadmium, and mercury.

Atmophile ("gas-loving") elements are volatile and concentrated in the atmosphere and hydrosphere; they include hydrogen, nitrogen, and the noble gases.

Significance

The classification reflects how elements were partitioned when the Earth differentiated into core, mantle, and crust, and it guides understanding of ore formation, planetary composition, and the distribution of elements in meteorites. Some elements show mixed affinities and switch groups depending on conditions such as oxygen fugacity.
