Boyar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Boyars in 17th-century ceremonial dress.

A boyar was a member of the highest rank of the feudal aristocracy in many Slavic states, including Kievan Rus', Bulgaria, Russia, Wallachia, and Moldavia. Boyars occupied the highest offices of state after the ruling princes and wielded great influence, often rivalling the power of the monarch. In Russia the boyar class dominated the court from the 10th century until the reforms of Peter the Great in the early 18th century, who abolished the rank and the Boyar Duma in favour of a service nobility ranked by the Table of Ranks.

Etymology

The word entered English from Russian boyarin (боярин). Its ultimate origin is disputed: it may derive from a Turkic title related to bai ("rich, lord") or from the Slavic root seen in boi ("battle"), suggesting a warrior-aristocrat. Related forms include Bulgarian bolyarin and Romanian boier.

In Russia

In Muscovy, boyars were hereditary aristocrats who sat in the Boyar Duma, the council advising the grand prince and later the tsar. Their precedence at court was governed by the elaborate system of mestnichestvo, which ranked families by genealogy and past service and was a frequent source of disputes. Boyars held vast estates (votchiny) worked by peasants and serfs, commanded armies, governed provinces, and conducted diplomacy.

The boyars' power waxed and waned. Ivan IV ("the Terrible") used the oprichnina in the 1560s to break the great boyar families, while during the minority of a tsar—such as Ivan's own childhood—boyar factions ruled the state. The 17th century saw boyar government again during the minority of Peter the Great, contested by the regent Sophia and the Naryshkin family (Peter's mother Natalya was a Naryshkina). In 1682 mestnichestvo was abolished, and in 1711 Peter replaced the Boyar Duma with the Governing Senate; the boyar title itself lapsed in the early 18th century as the old aristocracy was absorbed into the new service nobility (dvoryanstvo).

In the Balkans and Romania

In the First and Second Bulgarian Empires, bolyars formed the high nobility and council of the tsar. In the Romanian principalities of Wallachia and Moldavia, boiers were divided into great and lesser ranks; the great boyars monopolised high office and could elect or depose princes. Boyar privileges in Romania were curtailed by the reforms of Prince Alexandru Ioan Cuza in the 1860s, and the class disappeared with land reform and the advent of the modern state.
