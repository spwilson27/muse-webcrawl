Right to repair

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Right to repair is a legal concept and social movement holding that owners of products — especially electronics, vehicles, and agricultural machinery — should be able to repair them freely, and that manufacturers should provide access to spare parts, tools, manuals, schematics, and diagnostic software on fair terms. Advocates argue that manufacturer restrictions, parts pairing, and anti-circumvention rules create monopolies on repair, raise prices, and increase electronic waste.

Background

Early automobiles were sold with repair manuals and interchangeable parts, and third-party repair was normal for most consumer goods through the mid-20th century. From the 1990s onward, embedded software, glued assemblies, proprietary fasteners, and copyright locks shifted control to manufacturers. The US Copyright Office's triennial exemptions under DMCA §1201 (2015, 2018, 2021) progressively legalised circumvention for repair of vehicles, phones, and medical devices.

Legislation

The first US law was Massachusetts' Motor Vehicle Owners' Right to Repair Act (2012), followed by a national memorandum of understanding in the automotive sector. Broader consumer-electronics laws were enacted in New York (Digital Fair Repair Act, 2022), Minnesota and California (2023), and Colorado (including agricultural equipment, 2023). In the European Union, the Ecodesign regulations require spare-parts availability for appliances, the Right to Repair Directive was adopted in 2024, and the common charger rules mandate USB-C. Some manufacturers, including Apple and Samsung, launched self-service repair programmes partly in response.

Issues

  • Parts pairing — software serialisation that disables features when components are replaced without manufacturer authorisation.
  • Agricultural equipment — disputes involving tractor manufacturers and farmers' ability to repair machinery.
  • Environmental — repair extends product lifetimes and reduces e-waste.
