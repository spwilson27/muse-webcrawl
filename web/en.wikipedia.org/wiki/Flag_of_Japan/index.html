Flag of Japan

Use: National flag and civil ensign
Proportion: 2:3
Adopted: 27 February 1870 (civil ensign); 13 August 1999 (national flag)
Design: A white rectangle with a crimson-red disc in the centre

The national flag of Japan is a rectangular white banner bearing a crimson-red circle at its centre. This flag is officially called the Nisshōki (日章旗, "flag of the sun"), but is more commonly known in Japan as the Hinomaru (日の丸, "Ball of the Sun"). It embodies the country's sobriquet: the Land of the Rising Sun.

The Nisshōki flag is designated as the national flag in the Act on National Flag and Anthem, which was promulgated and became effective on 13 August 1999. Although no earlier legislation had specified a national flag, the sun-disc flag had already become the de facto flag of Japan. Two proclamations issued in 1870 by the Daijō-kan, the early Meiji government, each had a design for the flag. The sun disc flag was adopted as a flag for merchant ships on 27 February 1870 and as the naval ensign on 27 October 1870.

History

The exact origin of the Hinomaru is unknown, but the rising sun had symbolic meaning since the early 7th century; Japan is east of China, the direction from which the sun rises, and in 607 an official correspondence that began with "from the emperor of the rising sun" was sent to the Chinese Emperor Yang of Sui. The sun disc appeared on folding fans and banners of samurai during the Sengoku period, including examples associated with Toyotomi Hideyoshi's invasions of Korea.

Use of the flag was restricted in the early years of the Allied occupation after the Second World War, but restrictions were later relaxed. The flag remains a subject of debate in Japan, particularly regarding its association with the wartime period and its use in public schools.

Design

The 1999 act specifies that the flag's proportions are 2:3, and that the disc is centred with a diameter of three-fifths of the hoist. The white is simply white and the red is defined as "deep red" (shinku); historically, Prime Minister's Office Ordinance No. 57 of 1870 had given slightly different proportions of 7:10.
