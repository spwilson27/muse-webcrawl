Distinguished Service Medal (United Kingdom)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Distinguished Service Medal (DSM) was a British military decoration awarded to personnel of the Royal Navy and other services, up to and including the rank of chief petty officer, for bravery and resourcefulness on active service at sea. It was the naval equivalent of the Distinguished Conduct Medal for the Army and was instituted in October 1914, shortly after the outbreak of the First World War.

History

Before 1914 there was no medal, other than the Victoria Cross, available to reward acts of gallantry by naval ratings in war. The DSM was created by King George V on 14 October 1914 to fill this gap. Recipients were entitled to the post-nominal letters DSM, and bars could be awarded for subsequent acts. During its life the medal was also awarded to members of the other armed services serving afloat and to personnel of Commonwealth and allied navies.

Design

The medal is silver and circular. The obverse bears the effigy of the reigning sovereign; the reverse shows the crowned Royal Cypher within a laurel wreath, above the inscription "For Distinguished Service". The ribbon is of three equal vertical stripes of dark blue, white, and dark blue. The recipient's name and details are impressed on the rim.

Awards and discontinuation

About 11,000 DSMs were awarded during the First World War and some 15,000 during the Second World War. Following the 1993 review of gallantry awards, which abolished distinctions based on rank, the DSM was discontinued and its role taken over by the Conspicuous Gallantry Cross.
