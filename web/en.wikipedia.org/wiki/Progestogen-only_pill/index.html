Progestogen-only pill

The progestogen-only pill (POP), also known as the minipill, is an oral contraceptive that contains only a synthetic progestogen, without the estrogen component of combined oral contraceptives. It is used by women who cannot or prefer not to take estrogen, including those who are breastfeeding and those with cardiovascular risk factors.

Mechanism

Traditional progestogen-only pills (containing norethisterone or levonorgestrel) work primarily by thickening cervical mucus so that sperm cannot reach the egg, and by thinning the endometrium; ovulation is suppressed inconsistently. Newer pills containing desogestrel reliably inhibit ovulation as their principal mechanism.

Use

Older POPs must be taken within a strict three-hour window each day, whereas desogestrel pills allow a twelve-hour window. There is no pill-free interval; packs are taken continuously. Effectiveness with typical use is similar to combined pills, and fertility returns quickly after stopping.

Side effects

Irregular bleeding is the most common side effect. Because they lack estrogen, POPs do not increase the risk of venous thromboembolism to the same extent and are considered safe during breastfeeding.

History

Progestogen-only contraception was developed in the late 1960s as an alternative to estrogen-containing pills; the first POPs were marketed in the early 1970s.
