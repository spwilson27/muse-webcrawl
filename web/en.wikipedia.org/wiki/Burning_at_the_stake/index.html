Burning at the stake

Burning at the stake, or death by burning, is a method of execution in which the condemned is tied to a wooden stake and surrounded by a pyre of wood or faggots, which is then set alight. It has a long history as a penalty for crimes such as heresy, witchcraft, treason, and certain sexual offences, and was practiced in Europe and its colonies from antiquity through the early modern period.

History

Execution by fire appears in ancient legal codes and in the Roman Empire, where it was inflicted for offences including arson and, later, on some Christians during persecutions. In medieval and early modern Europe, burning became the standard penalty for heresy under canon and secular law; the condemned were formally "relaxed" to the secular arm for execution. During the Reformation both Protestant and Catholic authorities burned religious opponents: examples include Jan Hus (1415), Joan of Arc (1431), Giordano Bruno (1600), and the Marian martyrs of England, among them Hugh Latimer, Nicholas Ridley, and Thomas Cranmer (1555–1556).

Burning was also the standard sentence for convicted witches in much of continental Europe during the witch trials of the 16th and 17th centuries, though in England and its American colonies convicted witches were usually hanged instead. In England, women convicted of treason — including petty treason, the murder of a husband — were burned until the practice was abolished by the Treason Act 1790. Some victims were strangled or otherwise killed before the fire was lit, a customary mercy.

Modern practice

Judicial burning was abolished across Europe during the 18th and 19th centuries. Isolated instances of burning as murder or lynching have occurred in modern times, and the method has been used in extrajudicial killings by criminal and militant groups, drawing widespread condemnation.
