Legitimate expectation

In law, a legitimate expectation is a claim arising when a public authority has, by a clear promise or an established practice, led a person to expect a particular procedure or outcome, such that it would be unfair for the authority to depart from the expectation without justification. The doctrine is a ground of judicial review in English administrative law and in the law of many other common law jurisdictions, and an analogous principle — protection of legitimate expectations — exists in European Union law and international investment law.

Procedural legitimate expectation

The classical doctrine protects procedure: where an authority has promised a hearing or consultation, or has regularly followed a procedure in the past, fairness requires it to honour that expectation. The term was used by Lord Denning in Schmidt v Secretary of State for Home Affairs (1969), and the doctrine was developed in cases such as Attorney General of Hong Kong v Ng Yuen Shiu (1983) and R v North and East Devon Health Authority, ex parte Coughlan (2001).

Substantive legitimate expectation

More controversially, courts may protect the substance of an expectation — the benefit itself — where a promise was made to an individual or a small group, was clear and unambiguous, and resiling from it would be so unfair as to amount to an abuse of power. Coughlan is the leading English authority: a health authority's promise of a "home for life" to a severely disabled woman was enforced. Substantive protection remains exceptional, because it constrains a public authority's freedom to change policy.

Requirements

The representation relied on must be clear, unambiguous and devoid of relevant qualification; the claimant usually must have placed all their cards on the table and acted in good faith; and the expectation must be legitimate in the sense of being consistent with the authority's statutory duties. Detrimental reliance is not strictly necessary in English law but strengthens the claim.
