Protein primary structure
DefinitionLinear sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain
ConventionsWritten N-terminus → C-terminus; one- or three-letter codes

Protein primary structure is the sequence of amino acids in a peptide or protein. By convention, the primary structure of a protein is reported starting from the amino-terminal (N) end to the carboxyl-terminal (C) end, reflecting the direction of synthesis by the ribosome. The term was coined by Linderstrøm-Lang; the sequence is held together by peptide bonds between adjacent amino-acid residues.

Notation

Sequences are written with three-letter codes (e.g. Gly–Ala–Ser) or the compact one-letter codes (GAS). Modified residues can be indicated with special symbols or annotations, and disulfide bridges between cysteine residues are part of the covalent description of the mature protein.

Relation to higher structure

The primary structure is the first of four levels of protein organization, followed by secondary structure (helices and sheets), tertiary structure (overall fold) and quaternary structure (assembly of subunits). Anfinsen's dogma holds that, for many proteins, the primary structure contains all the information needed to specify the native three-dimensional fold under suitable conditions, because the sequence determines the energetics of folding.

Determination

Frederick Sanger determined the first complete protein sequence, that of insulin, in the early 1950s — work recognized by the 1958 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Classical sequencing used Edman degradation, which removes and identifies one N-terminal residue per cycle. Today most sequences are deduced from the DNA sequence of the encoding gene, or obtained by tandem mass spectrometry of peptides; databases such as UniProt store hundreds of millions of sequences.

Biological importance

Even a single amino-acid change in the primary structure can have profound effects: the substitution of valine for glutamate at position 6 of haemoglobin β chain causes sickle-cell disease. Sequences also evolve, so comparing primary structures reveals evolutionary relationships, conserved functional motifs and families of related proteins.

Post-translational modification

After synthesis the primary structure may be modified: signal peptides are cleaved, termini may be acetylated, and side chains can be phosphorylated, glycosylated, ubiquitinated and otherwise altered, expanding the functional repertoire beyond the 20 standard amino acids.
