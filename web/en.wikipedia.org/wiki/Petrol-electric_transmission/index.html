Petrol-electric transmission

Petrol-electric transmission, also known as gasoline-electric transmission, is a transmission system for vehicles powered by petrol (gasoline) internal combustion engines. The engine drives an electrical generator, and the electricity produced powers electric traction motors that drive the wheels or propeller. There is no mechanical connection between the engine and the driven wheels.

Principle

The system decouples the engine from the road load: the engine can run at an efficient, roughly constant speed while the electric motors provide variable torque, including high torque from rest. This removes the need for a clutch and multi-ratio gearbox and gives smooth, easily controlled drive. The same principle is used in diesel-electric locomotives and ships, the petrol variant being applied mainly to smaller and earlier vehicles.

Applications

Petrol-electric transmission was used in early twentieth-century road vehicles, including the Lohner–Porsche automobiles designed by Ferdinand Porsche — some with hub motors — and in petrol-electric buses in London and elsewhere. It was applied to military vehicles, most notably the French Char Saint-Chamond tank of the First World War and the Porsche-designed Tiger (P) tank prototype of the Second World War, whose petrol-electric drive proved heavy and unreliable. The concept survives in modern series hybrid vehicles, in which an engine acts primarily as a generator for electric drive.
