Max Reinhardt (born Maximilian Goldmann; 9 September 1873 – 31 October 1943) was an Austrian-born theatre and film director, intendant, and theatrical producer. Renowned for spectacle, innovative staging, and mastery of large ensembles and revolving stages, he was one of the most influential directors of the early 20th century.

Career

Reinhardt began as an actor in Salzburg and Berlin, joining Otto Brahm's Deutsches Theater in 1894. In 1905 he became director of the Deutsches Theater itself, later acquiring or founding several Berlin venues, including the Kammerspiele and the Großes Schauspielhaus. His productions of Shakespeare, Molière, Ibsen, Strindberg, and Wedekind, and lavish pantomime spectacles such as The Miracle (1911), made him famous across Europe and America. His 1911 production of Oedipus Rex and his mass spectacles pioneered arena staging.

Salzburg and exile

In 1920 Reinhardt co-founded the Salzburg Festival with Hugo von Hofmannsthal and Richard Strauss, staging Hofmannsthal's Jedermann (Everyman) on the cathedral square. As a Jew, he lost his German theatres after the Nazi seizure of power in 1933 and his Austrian castle, Schloss Leopoldskron, after the Anschluss in 1938. He emigrated to the United States, where he had earlier directed the film A Midsummer Night's Dream (1935) with William Dieterle, and founded an acting school and workshop in Hollywood. He died in New York in 1943.
