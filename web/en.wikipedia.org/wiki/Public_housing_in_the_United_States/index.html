Public housing in the United States is a form of federally subsidised rental housing owned and operated by local public housing agencies (PHAs) under programmes of the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD). Created by the Housing Act of 1937 during the New Deal, the programme built housing for low-income families, clearing slums in the process; at its peak it comprised about 1.4 million units, and today roughly one million units house some 1.8 million people, with the largest stock operated by the New York City Housing Authority (NYCHA), by far the nation's biggest landlord. Tenants generally pay rent set at 30 percent of adjusted household income, with the federal government funding the gap and capital repairs—chronically at levels below need, leaving a capital backlog estimated in the tens of billions of dollars.

History

Origins and expansion (1933–1949)

Federal involvement began with the Public Works Administration's housing division (1933), which built projects such as First Houses in New York (1935) and Techwood Homes in Atlanta. The Wagner–Steagall Housing Act of 1937 created the U.S. Housing Authority and the enduring model: federal loans and subsidies, local housing authorities owning and managing the developments, and an "equivalent elimination" rule tying new units to slum clearance. The Housing Act of 1949—promising "a decent home and a suitable living environment for every American family"—authorised 810,000 further units and launched urban renewal, though production lagged and renewal often destroyed more low-cost housing than it created.

High-rise era and crisis (1950s–1970s)

Post-war construction, pressed by expensive land and segregationist local politics, produced the notorious high-rise family projects: Chicago's Robert Taylor Homes and Cabrini–Green, St. Louis's Pruitt–Igoe (opened 1954, demolished 1972–76 in images that became symbols of modernist planning's failure), and Boston's Columbia Point. Operating subsidies were not provided until the 1960s; as working families left and the very poorest were concentrated by policy and by the Brooke Amendments' rent cap (1969, rent limited to 25, later 30, percent of income), revenues collapsed, maintenance failed, and many projects became bywords for crime and concentrated poverty. Desegregation litigation (notably Hills v. Gautreaux, 1976) exposed how deliberately authorities had sited projects to confine Black families. At the same time, much public housing—smaller-scale, elderly, or in smaller cities—functioned unremarkably well, and residents and scholars have stressed the stability the programme provided amid its failures.

Retrenchment and reinvention (1973–present)

President Nixon's 1973 moratorium halted new construction, and the Housing and Community Development Act of 1974 created Section 8, shifting policy toward vouchers and subsidies in private housing; from the 1980s, appropriations for new public housing nearly ceased. The HOPE VI programme (1993–2010) demolished some 150,000 distressed units and replaced a portion with mixed-income developments, reducing the stock while deconcentrating poverty—critics note net losses of affordable units and displacement of original residents. One-strike eviction rules (1996) and welfare reform tightened tenancy regimes. Since 2012 the Rental Assistance Demonstration (RAD) has let agencies convert public housing to long-term Section 8 contracts to raise private capital for repairs, converting hundreds of thousands of units; New York's NYCHA, with a capital-needs estimate exceeding $45 billion, has pursued conversions and public-private partnerships on a vast scale amid persistent controversy.

Administration and residents

Some 3,300 PHAs own the housing; HUD funds operating and capital budgets and inspects properties (historically under the REAC system). Eligibility is generally limited to families earning below 80 percent of area median income, with most new admissions targeted below 30 percent; waiting lists in large cities run to years or decades and are often closed. The resident population is disproportionately elderly, disabled, female-headed households and people of colour—about half of residents are elderly or disabled, and children constitute roughly a third. Resident councils and the "Section 3" employment rules give tenants formal roles, and a network of legal-aid litigation polices evictions and conditions.

Debate

Scholarship and politics dispute whether the programme's failures stem from its concept or from its sabotage: racially discriminatory siting, refusal to fund operations, and concentration of the poorest households are documented in works such as Arnold Hirsch's Making the Second Ghetto and Nicholas Dagen Bloom's Public Housing That Worked, which contrasts New York's relatively successful, heavily subsidised stock with the national story. Defenders of demolition-and-redevelopment cite improved neighbourhood outcomes; critics cite irreversible losses of deeply affordable homes and the unmet right of displaced residents to return. The shortage of any form of subsidised housing—only about one in four eligible households receives federal rental assistance—frames the modern debate less as public housing's failure than as its scarcity.
