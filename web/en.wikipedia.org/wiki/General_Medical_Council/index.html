General Medical Council

The General Medical Council (GMC) is a public body that maintains the official register of medical practitioners within the United Kingdom. Its chief responsibility is to "protect, promote and maintain the health and safety of the public" by controlling entry to the register, and suspending or removing members when necessary. It also sets standards for medical schools in the UK. The GMC is supported by annual fees paid by its registered doctors.

History

The Medical Act 1858 established the General Council of Medical Education and Registration of the United Kingdom, creating for the first time a single register distinguishing qualified practitioners. The body was renamed the General Medical Council in 1951. Major reforms followed inquiries such as the Shipman Inquiry (into the murders committed by GP Harold Shipman), leading to revalidation of doctors from 2012 and the transfer of adjudication on fitness-to-practise cases to the Medical Practitioners Tribunal Service in 2012.

Functions

The GMC's four main functions are: controlling entry to and maintaining the medical register; fostering good medical practice, chiefly through its guidance Good Medical Practice; promoting high standards of medical education and training; and dealing firmly and fairly with doctors whose fitness to practise is in doubt. Since 2024 the GMC has also begun regulating physician associates and anaesthesia associates in the UK.

Criticism

The GMC has faced criticism over perceived leniency or harshness in high-profile cases, over the fees it charges, and following the case of Dr Hadiza Bawa-Garba, whose erasure (later reversed) prompted widespread concern among doctors about systemic failures being individualized in regulatory proceedings.
