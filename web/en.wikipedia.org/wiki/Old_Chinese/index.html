Old Chinese, also called Archaic Chinese in older works, is the oldest attested stage of the Chinese language, and the ancestor of all modern varieties of Chinese. It was spoken from the period of the earliest Chinese inscriptions — the oracle bone inscriptions of the late Shang dynasty (c. 1250 BC) — until the Han dynasty.

Reconstruction

Because the Chinese script is not phonetic, the sounds of Old Chinese must be reconstructed from indirect evidence: rhymes in the Classic of Poetry (Shijing), the phonetic components of characters, transcriptions of foreign words, and comparison with Middle Chinese (known from rhyme dictionaries such as the Qieyun) and with conservative modern varieties such as the Min languages. The most widely used modern reconstruction is that of Baxter and Sagart (2014).

Features

Reconstructed Old Chinese lacked the tones of Middle and Modern Chinese, which are thought to have developed from final consonants; it had consonant clusters and probably a system of derivational prefixes and suffixes that later disappeared. Written Old Chinese of the classical period is the basis of Classical (Literary) Chinese, which remained the written standard in China into the early 20th century.
