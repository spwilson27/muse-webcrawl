Propylaea (Acropolis of Athens)

The Propylaea (Greek: Προπύλαια, "gateways") is the monumental entrance to the Acropolis of Athens. Built of white Pentelic marble under the direction of the architect Mnesikles between 437 and 432 BC, during the age of Pericles, it formed part of the great rebuilding programme that also produced the Parthenon. The name is plural — a succession of gates — and the building is considered a masterpiece of classical Greek architecture for its ingenious adaptation to a steep, irregular site.

The Propylaea from the west

Architecture

The building consists of a central hall with five doorways in a cross-wall, flanked by two wings. The central passage, aligned with the Panathenaic Way, was wide enough for processions and sacrificial animals; the doors decrease in size symmetrically to either side. Outside, a Doric hexastyle portico faces the approaching visitor, while behind the gate wall an Ionic colonnade lines the central ramp — one of the earliest great buildings to combine the Doric and Ionic orders. The north wing contained a dining hall (the Pinakotheke, or picture gallery, described by Pausanias as holding paintings) and the south wing was truncated to respect the sanctuary of Athena Nike, whose small temple stands on the bastion beside it. Because the Peloponnesian War began in 431 BC, the Propylaea was never quite finished: blocks were left with lifting bosses untrimmed and parts of the design unexecuted.

Later history

In the Byzantine and Frankish periods the building was converted into a residence — first for the bishop of Athens and then for the Frankish dukes, who built a tall tower (demolished in 1875) over the south wing. In 1656, under Ottoman rule, a lightning strike ignited the gunpowder stored in the building, causing an explosion that destroyed much of its roof and central section. Systematic restoration has proceeded since the nineteenth century; a major modern campaign, completed in the 2000s, re-erected columns and restored the central hall's coffered ceiling.

Legacy

The Propylaea's fame in the modern world is reflected in neoclassical imitations, most famously the Propylaea in Munich and the Brandenburg Gate in Berlin, whose design drew on the Athenian gateway.
