Deed in lieu of foreclosure

A deed in lieu of foreclosure is a deed instrument in which a mortgagor (the borrower) conveys all interest in a real property to the mortgagee (the lender) to satisfy a loan that is in default and avoid foreclosure proceedings. It is one of several loss mitigation options available to distressed homeowners, alongside loan modification, short sale, and forbearance.

How it works

Under a deed in lieu arrangement, the borrower voluntarily transfers title to the lender, typically in exchange for the release of the mortgage obligation and cancellation of the debt. Both parties benefit: the borrower avoids the public record and often the harsher credit consequences of a completed foreclosure, and the lender avoids the time and expense of foreclosure proceedings, which can take months or years in judicial-foreclosure states. Lenders may also offer "cash for keys" relocation assistance as an inducement.

Requirements and drawbacks

Lenders usually require that the property have clear title and that the borrower demonstrate financial hardship and an inability to sell the property, often after first attempting a market sale. A major drawback for the lender is that junior liens and other encumbrances are not extinguished by a deed in lieu — unlike a foreclosure, which generally wipes them out — so lenders often decline if there are second mortgages or liens. For the borrower, possible disadvantages include tax consequences if the cancelled debt is treated as income, and the loss of any equity in the property. Federal mortgage programs (such as FHA, VA, and the former Home Affordable Foreclosure Alternatives program) have formalized deed-in-lieu options for qualifying borrowers, and in some cases the borrower may still be liable for a deficiency if the property's value is less than the debt, unless the agreement expressly waives it.
