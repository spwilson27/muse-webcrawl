Hanbali

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Hanbali school (Arabic: ٱلْمَذْهَب ٱلْحَنْبَلِيّ, romanized: al-madhhab al-ḥanbalī) is one of the four major schools (madhhabs) of Sunni Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh). It is named after the Iraqi scholar Ahmad ibn Hanbal (780–855) and was founded by his students and later followers. Of the four schools, it is generally regarded as the most conservative and literalist in its approach to the sources of law.

History

Ahmad ibn Hanbal, a renowned hadith scholar in Abbasid Baghdad, resisted the rationalist theology promoted by the caliphal court during the Mihna (inquisition) and was imprisoned for his refusal to accept the doctrine of the createdness of the Qur'an. His legal method rested on strict adherence to the Qur'an and the hadith, with very limited use of personal reasoning (ra'y) and analogical deduction (qiyas).

The school remained a minority tradition for centuries, centred on Baghdad and later Damascus, where the jurist Ibn Taymiyyah (1263–1328) and his student Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya became its most famous exponents. In the eighteenth century the school was taken up by the reform movement of Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab in Arabia.

Distribution and influence

Today Hanbali jurisprudence is the official school of Saudi Arabia and Qatar and has significant followings elsewhere in the Arabian Peninsula and among Salafi movements worldwide. Despite being numerically the smallest of the four schools, its texts and scholars exercise considerable influence on contemporary Sunni thought.

Principles

Hanbali legal theory ranks sources as: the Qur'an, the Sunnah, consensus of the Companions, individual Companion opinions, weaker hadith, and only then analogy. The school is known for strictness in matters of creed and ritual, though its scholars have differed among themselves on many points.
