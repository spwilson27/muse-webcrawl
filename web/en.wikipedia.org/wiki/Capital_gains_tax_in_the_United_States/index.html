Capital gains tax in the United States

In the United States, capital gains are profits from the sale of a capital asset, such as shares of stock, a business, land, or artwork. The gains are taxed at different rates depending on the holding period and the taxpayer's income. Gains on assets held for one year or less are short-term and taxed at ordinary income rates; gains on assets held longer are long-term and taxed at preferential rates of 0%, 15%, or 20%.

History

The federal government has taxed capital gains since the Revenue Act of 1913, with a preferential rate introduced in 1922. Rates and definitions changed repeatedly through the twentieth century: the Tax Reform Act of 1986 briefly equalised the top rates for gains and ordinary income, while the Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997 and the Jobs and Growth Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2003 reduced long-term rates to 20% and then 15%, with the top rate returning to 20% in 2013 alongside the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax.

Rules and planning

Capital losses can offset capital gains, with up to $3,000 per year deductible against other income. Special rules apply to collectibles (28% maximum rate), real estate depreciation recapture, like-kind exchanges of property, and the exclusion of up to $250,000 ($500,000 for couples) of gain on a principal residence. Basis is generally "stepped up" at death, erasing gains accrued during the deceased's lifetime — a provision central to debates about the tax.

State taxes

Most states with income taxes also tax capital gains, usually as ordinary income; Washington State introduced a dedicated 7% capital gains tax in 2022, upheld by the state supreme court in 2023.
