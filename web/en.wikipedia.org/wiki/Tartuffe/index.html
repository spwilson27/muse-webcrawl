Tartuffe

Tartuffe, or The Impostor (French: Tartuffe, ou l'Imposteur) is a theatrical comedy by Molière, first performed in 1664. It satirises religious hypocrisy through the figure of Tartuffe, a pious fraud who worms his way into the household of the gullible Orgon. The word "tartuffe" has entered French and English usage as a term for a hypocrite who feigns virtue.

Plot

Orgon, a prosperous Parisian, has fallen under the spell of Tartuffe, a supposed holy man he has taken into his home. Orgon's family see Tartuffe as a scheming hypocrite, but Orgon and his mother Madame Pernelle refuse to listen; he even offers Tartuffe his daughter Mariane's hand, though she loves Valère. Orgon's wife Elmire stages a trap in which Orgon, hidden under a table, hears Tartuffe attempt to seduce her. Disillusioned too late — Orgon has signed over his property and a compromising casket — the family is saved only by the last-minute intervention of the King, whose officer arrests the impostor.

Controversy

The play was immediately attacked by religious groups who saw it as an assault on piety itself, and it was banned from public performance for five years despite the favour of Louis XIV, who had seen the first three-act version at Versailles in 1664. A revised five-act version reached the public stage triumphantly in 1669, and Tartuffe remains one of the most performed French classical plays.
