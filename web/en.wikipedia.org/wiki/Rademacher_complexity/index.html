Rademacher complexity

Image placeholder: a function class correlating with random (Rademacher) labels.

In computational learning theory (machine learning and theory of computation), Rademacher complexity measures the richness of a class of real-valued functions with respect to a probability distribution. The concept is named after Hans Rademacher. Roughly speaking, it measures how well the function class can correlate with random noise — that is, with randomly assigned ±1 labels: a class that can fit random labels well is more complex and more prone to overfitting.

Definitions

Given a sample S = (x1, …, xm) and a class of real-valued functions defined on the sample space, the empirical Rademacher complexity of the class on S is the expected maximum correlation of its functions with a random sign vector whose components are independent uniform {−1, +1} (Rademacher) variables. The Rademacher complexity of the class with respect to the underlying distribution is the expectation of this quantity over samples of size m.

Generalisation bounds

Rademacher complexity is used to bound the generalisation error of learning algorithms: with high probability, the gap between a function's true risk and its empirical risk is controlled by the Rademacher complexity of the class plus a term decaying with sample size. Such bounds are data-dependent and often tighter in practice than distribution-free measures such as VC dimension, with which Rademacher complexity is related (for binary-valued classes, the two agree up to logarithmic factors).

Related notions

Related concepts include Gaussian complexity, covering numbers, and the growth function. Key structural results include the contraction lemma of Ledoux and Talagrand, which bounds the complexity of Lipschitz compositions of a function class, and symmetrisation arguments linking Rademacher averages to uniform convergence.
