Cassander (Greek: Κάσσανδρος, c. 355 BC – 297 BC) was king of Macedon from 305 BC until his death in 297 BC, and de facto ruler of southern Greece from 317 BC. He was the eldest son of Antipater, the regent of Macedon, and one of the Diadochi, the generals who fought over the division of the empire of Alexander the Great after 323 BC.

Rise to power

Cassander was passed over for the regency on his father's death in 319 BC in favour of Polyperchon, and allied himself with Ptolemy and Antigonus against the regent. Returning to Macedon, he outmanoeuvred Polyperchon and the queen mother Olympias, whom he besieged at Pydna and had executed in 316 BC. He then consolidated his position by marrying Thessalonike, half-sister of Alexander the Great, and by refounding Thebes, which Alexander had destroyed. He founded the cities of Thessalonica, named for his wife, and Cassandreia.

Elimination of Alexander's heirs

Cassander is notorious for ordering the murders of Alexander's widow Roxana and their son Alexander IV around 310 BC, ending the Argead royal line, and later of Alexander's illegitimate son Heracles. With the other Diadochi he fought Antigonus, culminating in the Battle of Ipsus (301 BC), which he did not attend in person but to which he contributed troops under his brother Pleistarchus.

Death and succession

Cassander died of dropsy in 297 BC. His sons Philip IV, Antipater I, and Alexander V successively contested the throne, and within a few years Macedon fell into turmoil, ending the Antipatrid dynasty he had founded.
