The Dead Sea Scrolls, also called the Qumran Cave Scrolls, are ancient Jewish manuscripts discovered between 1946 and 1956 in eleven caves near Khirbat Qumran in the Judaean Desert, close to the north-western shore of the Dead Sea. Dating from the last three centuries BC and the first century AD, they are among the most important archaeological finds of the twentieth century for the study of Judaism and the origins of Christianity.

Discovery

The first scrolls were found in 1946/47 by Bedouin shepherds — according to the famous account, a boy searching for a lost goat threw a stone into a cave and heard pottery break. Fragments were sold through antiquities dealers; seven major scrolls from Cave 1 included the Great Isaiah Scroll, a virtually complete copy of the Book of Isaiah a thousand years older than previously known Hebrew manuscripts. Cave 4, found in 1952, yielded the majority of the manuscripts; further caves, and disputed later "post-2002" fragments, have kept the corpus in scholarly debate.

Contents

Roughly a quarter of the scrolls are copies of books of the Hebrew Bible, including every book except Esther. Others include Second Temple literature (1 Enoch, Jubilees), commentaries (pesharim), calendars, legal texts and works specific to a sectarian community — such as the Community Rule, the War Scroll and the Damascus Document — usually identified with the Essenes, with the settlement at Qumran as their centre. The Copper Scroll lists buried treasure; the Temple Scroll presents an idealised temple law.

Significance

The scrolls transformed knowledge of the textual history of the Bible, of Jewish belief in the Second Temple period — messianism, apocalypticism, purity law — and of the environment from which early Christianity emerged. After decades of restricted access, the full corpus was published in the Discoveries in the Judaean Desert series and digitised by the Israel Museum and the Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library.
