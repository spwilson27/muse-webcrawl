Tempering (spices)

Tempering is a cooking technique used in the cuisines of the Indian subcontinent in which whole or ground spices are briefly fried in hot oil or ghee to release their essential oils and flavours, and the flavoured fat is then poured over or stirred into a dish. It is known by many regional names, including tadka (Hindi), chaunk, bagar, phoron (as in panch phoron), thalippu (Tamil), and oggaraṇe (Kannada).

Method

Fat is heated in a small pan or ladle until very hot, and spices are added in an order determined by how quickly they cook: hardy whole spices such as mustard seeds (which are allowed to pop), cumin seeds, and dried red chillies first; then aromatics such as curry leaves, asafoetida, garlic, or ginger; ground spices, if used, go in last to avoid scorching. The sizzling mixture is immediately tipped into the dish — classically a pot of cooked dal (lentils), a raita, or a vegetable curry — and the pot is often covered briefly to trap the aroma.

Role in dishes

Tempering may be performed at the beginning of cooking to build a flavour base, or at the end as a finishing garnish; some dishes use both. Because many spice flavour compounds are fat-soluble, blooming them in oil distributes them through the dish far more effectively than adding raw spice. Common tempering combinations include mustard seed, cumin, and curry leaf for South Indian dishes, and cumin, dried chilli, and garlic for North Indian dals.
