Risk-neutral measure

In mathematical finance, a risk-neutral measure (also called an equilibrium measure or equivalent martingale measure) is a probability measure such that each share price is exactly the discounted expectation of the share price under this measure. Risk-neutral measures are central to the pricing of derivative securities: when a market is complete, there is a unique risk-neutral measure, and the arbitrage-free price of any derivative is its expected payoff discounted at the risk-free rate under that measure.

The concept arises because, in a risk-neutral world where all investors are indifferent to risk, expected returns on all assets equal the risk-free rate. Although real investors are risk-averse, the fundamental theorem of asset pricing shows that absence of arbitrage is equivalent to the existence of at least one risk-neutral measure, which justifies computing prices as if the world were risk-neutral.

Motivation

Consider a stock that will be worth either 120 or 80 in one period, currently priced at 100, with a risk-free rate of 0 for simplicity. Under the real-world measure the two outcomes might be equally likely, giving an expected value of 100—but generally investors demand a risk premium, so real-world probabilities do not directly give prices. The risk-neutral probability q solves 100 = q·120 + (1−q)·80, giving q = 0.5 here by construction; more generally q is determined by the current price, not by anyone's beliefs. Any derivative on the stock, such as a call option struck at 100 (paying 20 or 0), is then priced as q·20 + (1−q)·0 = 10.

Formal definition

A measure Q is a risk-neutral measure if it is equivalent to the real-world measure P (assigning zero probability to exactly the same events) and if the discounted price process of every traded asset is a martingale under Q. In continuous models such as Black–Scholes, the risk-neutral measure is obtained from the real-world measure by the Girsanov theorem, shifting the drift of asset prices from their real-world expected return to the risk-free rate while leaving volatility unchanged.

Applications

Risk-neutral valuation underlies the Black–Scholes option pricing formula, binomial tree models, and Monte Carlo simulation of derivative prices. In practice, risk-neutral probabilities are backed out from observed market prices (for example through implied volatility), allowing hedging and relative-value trading without estimating real-world expected returns.
