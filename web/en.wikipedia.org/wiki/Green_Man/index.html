Green Man

A medieval Green Man carving on a church

The Green Man is a motif in architecture and art, usually a sculpture or other representation of a face surrounded by, or made from, leaves. Branches or vines may sprout from the mouth, nostrils and other parts of the face, and these shoots may bear flowers or fruit. Commonly used as a decorative architectural ornament, Green Men are frequently found in carvings on both secular and ecclesiastical buildings.

History

Foliate heads appear in Roman art and in several ancient cultures, but the motif flourished particularly in the Romanesque and Gothic architecture of medieval Europe, where Green Men peer from roof bosses, capitals, corbels and misericords in cathedrals and parish churches across Britain, France and Germany. Examples also occur in later periods, enjoying revivals in the Renaissance, in Victorian church restoration and in the Arts and Crafts movement. The term "Green Man" itself was popularised by Lady Raglan in a 1939 article in the journal Folklore.

Interpretations

The meaning of the motif is debated. Lady Raglan connected the church carvings with fertility cults and figures of folklore such as Jack in the Green and the May Day traditions of Britain. Later writers have variously interpreted the Green Man as a symbol of rebirth and the cycle of growth each spring, a survival of pre-Christian nature religion, or simply a decorative convention of medieval masons. He has also been linked — speculatively — with figures such as the Mesopotamian Humbaba, the Egyptian Osiris, and Robin Hood.

Modern usage

The Green Man remains a popular name and sign for public houses in England. In contemporary culture the image has been adopted by environmental movements and by modern Pagan traditions as an emblem of humanity's relationship with the natural world, and foliate heads continue to be produced by sculptors and gardeners.
