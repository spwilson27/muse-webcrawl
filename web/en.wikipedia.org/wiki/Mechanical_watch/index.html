Mechanical watch

A mechanical watch is a watch that uses a clockwork mechanism to measure the passage of time, as opposed to quartz watches, which use electronics, or digital displays. It is driven by a mainspring that must be wound by hand (manual) or is kept wound by the movements of the wearer's arm (automatic/self-winding).

Mechanism

The core components of a mechanical watch movement (calibre) are:

  • Mainspring and barrel – stores energy as it is wound.
  • Gear train (wheel train) – transmits power from the barrel to the escapement while gearing up the rotation speeds that drive the hands.
  • Escapement – most commonly the Swiss lever escapement; releases the gear train in controlled increments and gives impulses to the balance.
  • Balance wheel and hairspring (spiral) – the timekeeping oscillator, beating typically 18,000–36,000 times per hour (2.5–5 Hz).
  • Dial train (motion work) – converts the rotation of the center wheel into hour and minute hand movement.

Most modern movements use synthetic ruby jewel bearings (typically 17 or more) to reduce friction, shock-protection devices such as Incabloc for the balance staff, and alloy hairsprings (e.g., Nivarox) resistant to magnetism and temperature change.

History

Spring-driven clocks appeared in 15th-century Europe; portable watches emerged around 1500 (the "Nuremberg eggs"). The balance spring (c. 1675, associated with Robert Hooke and Christiaan Huygens) greatly improved accuracy. John Harrison's marine chronometers solved the longitude problem in the 18th century. Watch production industrialized in Switzerland and the United States in the 19th century, and the wristwatch became dominant after World War I. The introduction of cheap quartz watches in the 1970s ("Quartz Crisis") devastated the Swiss industry, but mechanical watches revived from the 1980s onward as luxury goods and objects of craftsmanship.

Complications

Extra functions beyond hours, minutes, and seconds are called complications: calendars and perpetual calendars, moonphase displays, chronographs, minute repeaters and other chiming mechanisms, tourbillons, power-reserve indicators, and GMT/second time-zone displays.

Accuracy

Typical mechanical watches keep time within about −20/+40 seconds per day (better movements within a few seconds); certified chronometers (COSC) must average −4/+6 seconds per day across positions and temperatures.
