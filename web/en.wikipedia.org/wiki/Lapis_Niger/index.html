Lapis Niger

The Lapis Niger (Latin, "Black Stone") is an ancient shrine in the Roman Forum in Rome. Together with the associated Vulcanal — an open-air altar to Vulcan — it constitutes the only surviving remains of the old Comitium, the archaic assembly place of the Roman people, and was already regarded as a relic of great antiquity by the late Republic.

Description and inscriptions

The site, in front of the Curia Julia, is marked at ground level by a pavement of black marble slabs, from which it takes its name. Excavations by Giacomo Boni in 1899–1905 uncovered beneath it an archaic complex: a truncated tufa cone, the base of a column or altar, and a square pillar (cippus) bearing one of the oldest known Latin inscriptions, conventionally dated to the 6th or early 5th century BC. Written boustrophedon in archaic letterforms, the fragmentary text appears to be a sacred law attached to the shrine, threatening whoever violates the place and invoking the recei — "for the king" — which has made the inscription central to debates about kingship in early Rome. The shrine has been variously identified with the tomb of Romulus, of Faustulus, or of the Sabine king's envoy Hostus Hostilius.

History

The pavement was laid in the late Republic or early Empire, when the Comitium was reorganised, sealing and protecting the older structures beneath. Because of its fragility the excavated area is normally kept covered and is visible only in part from the Forum.
