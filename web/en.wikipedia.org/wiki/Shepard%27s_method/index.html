Shepard's method

Shepard's method, also known as inverse distance weighting (IDW), is a form of multivariate interpolation for scattered data points. It was introduced by Donald Shepard in a 1968 paper describing a two-dimensional interpolation function for irregularly spaced data.

Given a set of sample points with known values, the interpolated value at an unknown point is computed as a weighted average of the known values, where each weight decreases with distance from the interpolation point.

Formulation

For sample points (xi, fi), the interpolant is f(x) = Σ wi(x) fi / Σ wi(x), where the weights are wi(x) = 1 / ||x − xi||p. The power parameter p controls how rapidly influence falls off with distance; p = 2 is a common choice. If the interpolation point coincides with a sample point, the function returns that sample value.

Properties and variants

Shepard's method is simple and robust but produces surfaces that are flat (zero gradient) at the data points themselves, sometimes described as "bull's-eye" artefacts around sample locations. Modified versions, such as those of Franke and Nielson, incorporate local least-squares approximations and directional derivatives to improve smoothness. The method is widely used in geographic information systems for interpolating terrain, rainfall and other spatially distributed quantities.
