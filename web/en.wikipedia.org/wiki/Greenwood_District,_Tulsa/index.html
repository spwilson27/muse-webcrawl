Greenwood District, Tulsa

The Greenwood District in Tulsa, Oklahoma, was one of the most prominent concentrations of African-American businesses in the United States during the early twentieth century. Known as Black Wall Street, the district was founded by black settlers and entrepreneurs, among them O. W. Gurley, who purchased land in 1905 and sold lots exclusively to other African Americans, and J. B. Stradford, who built the luxurious Stradford Hotel.

Prosperity

Segregation laws confined black commerce largely to the Greenwood area, and dollars circulated within the community many times over before leaving it. By 1921 the district contained hundreds of black-owned businesses, including grocery stores, banks, law and medical offices, theatres, restaurants, and churches, serving a population of roughly 10,000. Booker T. Washington is said to have given the district its "Negro Wall Street" nickname, later shortened to Black Wall Street.

1921 massacre

From May 31 to June 1, 1921, the district was destroyed in the Tulsa race massacre, one of the worst episodes of racial violence in American history. Following an incident involving a young black shoeshiner, Dick Rowland, and a white elevator operator, Sarah Page, white mobs looted and burned some 35 square blocks of Greenwood. Official reports at the time recorded 36 deaths; modern scholarship estimates the toll may have reached 300. Thousands of black residents were left homeless, and many were interned. No one was ever prosecuted for the destruction.

Rebuilding and legacy

Despite efforts by the city to block reconstruction through a punitive fire ordinance—struck down in court—residents rebuilt Greenwood within a few years, and the district again flourished in the 1940s. It declined later in the twentieth century under urban renewal and the construction of an interstate highway through its heart. The massacre was largely absent from Oklahoma history curricula for decades, but entered wider national awareness around its centennial in 2021. The Greenwood Cultural Center, John Hope Franklin Reconciliation Park, and the Greenwood Rising history centre commemorate the district and its history.
