Paiute

Paiute (also spelled Piute) refers to two related but distinct groups of Indigenous peoples of the Great Basin in the western United States: the Northern Paiute of California, Nevada, and Oregon, and the Southern Paiute of Arizona, southeastern California, Nevada, and Utah. Both speak languages of the Numic branch of the Uto-Aztecan family, but they are culturally and politically distinct peoples with separate tribal governments.

Northern Paiute

The Northern Paiute (who call themselves Numa or Numa, "the people") traditionally occupied western Nevada, southeastern Oregon, and eastern California. Their economy centered on fishing in lakes such as Pyramid Lake and Walker Lake — home to the cui-ui and Lahontan cutthroat trout — together with hunting and gathering of pine nuts, seeds, and roots. Their language, Northern Paiute, is closely related to Mono.

The Northern Paiute prophet Wovoka (Jack Wilson) originated the Ghost Dance movement of 1889–1890, which spread widely among Native peoples of the West. The writer Sarah Winnemucca, daughter of Chief Winnemucca, became one of the first Native American authors to publish in English.

Southern Paiute

The Southern Paiute (self-designation Nuwuvi, "the people") traditionally inhabited southern Utah, southern Nevada, northwestern Arizona, and southeastern California along the Colorado River region. Unlike their northern relatives, many Southern Paiute groups practiced floodplain agriculture, growing corn, beans, squash, and sunflowers along waterways such as the Muddy and Virgin rivers, supplementing farming with hunting and gathering.

Federally recognized Southern Paiute tribes include the Paiute Indian Tribe of Utah, the Kaibab Band of Paiute Indians, the Las Vegas Tribe of Paiute Indians, the Moapa Band of Paiute Indians, and the San Juan Southern Paiute Tribe.

History of contact

Both groups experienced severe disruption from the mid-19th century onward: the California gold rush, mining booms at the Comstock Lode and elsewhere, and the expansion of ranches onto their lands destroyed food sources and triggered conflict. The Pyramid Lake War of 1860 was a major clash between Northern Paiutes and settler volunteers. Southern Paiutes suffered population losses from slave raiding and disease even before sustained American settlement, and their irrigation lands were later taken by Mormon settlers in Utah and Nevada.

Contemporary communities

Today, Northern and Southern Paiute people live in numerous federally recognized tribes and colonies across the Great Basin, including the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe, the Walker River Paiute Tribe, the Burns Paiute Tribe, and the Paiute-Shoshone Tribe of the Fallon Reservation. Language revitalization efforts are under way in many communities.
