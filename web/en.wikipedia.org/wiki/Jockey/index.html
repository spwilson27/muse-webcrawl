A jockey is someone who rides horses in horse racing or steeplechase racing, primarily as a profession. Jockeys are typically self-employed, nominated by horse trainers to ride their horses in races for a fee (which is paid regardless of the prize money the horse earns) and a percentage of the horse's winnings.

Physical characteristics

There is no height limit for jockeys, but most are short because of weight limits: flat-race jockeys usually weigh between about 108 and 118 pounds (49–54 kg), while jump jockeys can be somewhat heavier. Maintaining such low weights historically led to dangerous practices such as "wasting" (severe dieting, sweating and self-induced vomiting); modern racing authorities have raised minimum weights and provide nutritional support in response to health concerns.

Career

Jockeys typically begin as apprentice jockeys ("claiming" jockeys) attached to a trainer's stable, receiving a weight allowance that decreases as they ride winners. Successful riders are retained by leading owners and stables. The profession is hazardous: falls at speed over fences or in crowded flat fields cause frequent injury, and jockeys have among the highest injury rates of any athletes. Famous jockeys include Lester Piggott, Bill Shoemaker, Frankie Dettori and A. P. McCoy, the dominant jump jockey of his era.

Colours and equipment

Jockeys race in the registered colours ("silks") of the horse's owner, wearing a helmet (skull cap) with a silk cover, breeches and lightweight boots, and ride in very small, light saddles with short stirrups, perched above the horse in the "monkey crouch" that reduces aerodynamic drag and weight on the horse's back.
