Hallelujah

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hallelujah (also rendered Alleluia) is a transliteration of the Hebrew word הַלְלוּיָהּ (haləlūyāh), meaning "praise Yah" — a compound of halelu, "praise!" (plural imperative) and Yah, a shortened form of the divine name. It is used in Jewish and Christian worship as an expression of praise and thanksgiving to God.

Biblical usage

The word appears 24 times in the Hebrew Bible, all in the Book of Psalms, especially in the concluding psalms (146–150) which begin and end with it. In the Septuagint it was transliterated as allēlouia, and in the New Testament it appears in the Book of Revelation, where it accompanies praise in heaven. English translations variously render it "Praise the LORD" or retain "Hallelujah".

Liturgical usage

In Judaism, hallelujah is part of the Hallel psalms recited on festivals. In Christian liturgy, the Alleluia is sung before the Gospel reading in the Mass and in the Liturgy of the Hours; in the Roman Rite it is traditionally omitted during Lent and replaced by another acclamation. In Eastern Orthodox practice the Alleluia is sung in both festive and penitential seasons, including funerals.

In music

The word has been set to music countless times, most famously in the "Hallelujah" chorus from Handel's oratorio Messiah (1741), in which the choir repeats the word to the text of Revelation. It also appears in gospel music, hymns and contemporary worship songs, and forms the title of Leonard Cohen's widely covered 1984 song "Hallelujah", though that work uses the word in a broader, more secular meditation on love and loss.
