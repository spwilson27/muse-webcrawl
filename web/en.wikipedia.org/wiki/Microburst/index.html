Microburst

A microburst is a localised column of sinking air (a downdraft) within a thunderstorm, normally less than or equal to 4 kilometres (2.5 miles) in diameter. Microbursts can cause extensive damage at the surface, and in some instances can be life-threatening; their sudden, severe wind shear makes them a particular hazard to aircraft during takeoff and landing.

Characteristics

When the downdraft reaches the ground it spreads out in all directions, producing damaging straight-line winds that can exceed 45 m/s (100 mph) — comparable to a tornado, though the debris pattern radiates outward rather than rotating. Microbursts are classified as wet (accompanied by heavy rain) or dry (little or no rain reaching the ground, typical of the American high plains, where virga is a visual clue). They typically last only 5 to 15 minutes.

History and aviation safety

The phenomenon was identified and named by meteorologist T. Theodore Fujita, who investigated airline crashes in the 1970s. Microburst-induced wind shear was implicated in several accidents, including Delta Air Lines Flight 191 at Dallas/Fort Worth in 1985, which killed 137 people. Those disasters spurred the installation of terminal Doppler weather radar and low-level wind shear alert systems at airports, and pilot training in microburst escape manoeuvres; since the mid-1990s such accidents have become rare in the United States.
