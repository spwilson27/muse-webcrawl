George Canning

Born: 11 April 1770, London, England

Died: 8 August 1827, Chiswick, Middlesex

Office: Prime Minister of the United Kingdom (1827)

George Canning (11 April 1770 – 8 August 1827) was a British Tory statesman. He served as Foreign Secretary in two long spells and, in 1827, briefly as Prime Minister, dying in office after only 119 days — one of the shortest tenures in British history.

Early life

Canning was born in London to an Anglo-Irish family; after his father's death his mother worked as an actress, a background his political enemies never let him forget. Educated at Eton and Christ Church, Oxford, he was taken up by William Pitt the Younger and entered Parliament in 1793.

Foreign Secretary

As Foreign Secretary (1807–1809) during the Napoleonic Wars, Canning ordered the seizure of the Danish fleet at Copenhagen and supported the Peninsular War; his rivalry with the War Secretary, Lord Castlereagh, culminated in a duel in 1809 in which Canning was wounded, and both men resigned. Returning as Foreign Secretary in 1822 after Castlereagh's suicide, Canning championed the independence of Spain's American colonies, recognised the new Latin American republics, and cooperated tacitly with the United States' Monroe Doctrine, famously declaring: "I called the New World into existence to redress the balance of the Old." He also supported Greek independence from the Ottoman Empire.

Premiership and death

On Lord Liverpool's resignation in April 1827, Canning became Prime Minister, leading a coalition with Whigs that split the Tory party. His health collapsed under the strain and he died that August at Chiswick House — the same room in which Charles James Fox had died 21 years earlier.
