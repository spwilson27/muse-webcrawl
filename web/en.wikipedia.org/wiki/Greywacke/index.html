Greywacke

Greywacke (from the German Grauwacke) is a variety of hard, dark sandstone characterised by its poorly sorted, angular grains and abundant fine-grained matrix. A typical greywacke consists of quartz, feldspar and small rock fragments (lithic clasts) set in a matrix of clay and silt that makes up more than about 15 per cent of the rock. Its greenish-grey to dark colour comes from minerals such as chlorite in the matrix.

Formation

Greywackes are generally interpreted as the deposits of turbidity currents — underwater avalanches of sediment that sweep down continental slopes into deep ocean basins — which explains their graded bedding and mixture of coarse grains in mud. Thick greywacke sequences form the backbone of many mountain ranges; they are the dominant rock of New Zealand's Southern Alps and much of its axial ranges, and they underlie much of Scotland's Southern Uplands. Because of their hardness and durability, greywackes are used locally as building stone and crushed rock for aggregate.
