Breathability

Breathability is the ability of a fabric or material to allow moisture vapour to pass through it. More precisely, it is measured as the rate at which water vapour passes through the material under specified conditions of temperature and humidity, and is closely related to the concepts of moisture vapour transmission rate (MVTR) and air permeability. Breathable materials are central to outdoor clothing, sportswear, building construction, and wound care, because they let perspiration or trapped moisture escape while, in waterproof-breathable designs, keeping liquid water out.

Measurement

Breathability is commonly reported as MVTR in grams of water vapour per square metre per 24 hours (g/m²/24h), measured by standard methods such as the upright or inverted cup tests (ASTM E96) or the sweating guarded-hotplate test (ISO 11092), which yields the water-vapour resistance value Ret — lower Ret meaning greater breathability. Results from different test methods are not directly comparable, which complicates marketing claims: fabrics advertised at 20,000 g/m²/24h may perform very differently in the field.

Mechanisms

Membrane fabrics achieve breathability in two main ways. Microporous membranes such as expanded PTFE (e.g. Gore-Tex) contain pores large enough for individual water vapour molecules but far too small for liquid droplets. Hydrophilic (non-porous) membranes transport moisture by molecular diffusion through the polymer itself. In buildings, "breathable" wall and roof assemblies use vapour-permeable membranes and natural materials such as lime plaster and wood fibre to manage moisture without trapping it inside the structure.

Applications

  • Waterproof-breathable jackets, footwear, and gloves for outdoor activities
  • Medical dressings that balance moisture at a wound bed
  • Housewrap and roofing membranes in construction
  • Bedding, diapers, and hygiene products
