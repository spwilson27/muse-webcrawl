From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Action bias
FieldPsychology, behavioural economics
DefinitionTendency to favour action over inaction, even when inaction may be better
Related conceptsOmission bias, status quo bias, regret aversion

Action bias is the psychological tendency to favour taking action over inaction when faced with a decision, even in situations where doing nothing — or waiting — would produce an equal or better outcome. It is a form of cognitive bias studied in behavioural economics and decision psychology.

Penalty-kick goalkeepers dive left or right on nearly every kick, though staying centred would save more shots — a classic demonstration of action bias.

Evidence

A well-known study by Michael Bar-Eli and colleagues (2007) analysed hundreds of soccer penalty kicks and found that goalkeepers almost always jump to one side, although remaining in the centre would have stopped a larger share of kicks. The keepers reported that conceding a goal while standing still would feel — and look — worse than conceding while diving, illustrating how anticipated regret and social norms ("do something") drive the bias.

Causes and domains

Action bias is attributed to regret asymmetry (failures after inaction are regretted more), norms and expectations of decisiveness, overconfidence, and the illusion of control. It has been discussed in medicine (overtreatment and unnecessary intervention), investing (excessive trading), management, policing and environmental policy, where visible intervention may be rewarded even when watchful waiting is optimal.
