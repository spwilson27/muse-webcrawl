The House of Habsburg (German: Haus Habsburg), also known as the House of Austria, is one of the most prominent and important dynasties in European history. Originating as counts in what is now Switzerland, the Habsburgs rose to rule Austria from 1282, provided Holy Roman Emperors almost continuously from 1440 to 1806, and at their height under Charles V ruled an empire spanning Spain, the Netherlands, much of Italy, and Spain's American and Asian colonies — "the empire on which the sun never sets".

Origins and rise

The dynasty takes its name from Habsburg Castle in the Swiss canton of Aargau, built around 1020–1030 by Radbot, Count of Klettgau. The family steadily expanded its possessions in Alsace, Swabia and Austria. In 1273 Rudolf of Habsburg was elected King of the Romans, and in 1282 he enfeoffed his sons with the duchies of Austria and Styria, founding the family's Austrian power base.

Expansion by marriage

The Habsburgs famously expanded more by marriage than by war — a strategy summed up in the saying attributed to Matthias Corvinus: Bella gerant alii, tu felix Austria nube ("Let others wage wars; you, fortunate Austria, marry"). Maximilian I's marriage to Mary of Burgundy (1477) brought the Burgundian Netherlands; his son Philip's marriage to Joanna of Castile brought Spain and its empire. In 1526 the double marriage arrangements of Vienna brought Bohemia and Hungary to Ferdinand I after the death of Louis II at Mohács.

Division into Spanish and Austrian lines

On his abdication in 1556, Charles V divided his inheritance: his son Philip II received Spain, the Netherlands, Italy and the overseas empire (the Spanish Habsburgs), while his brother Ferdinand I retained Austria and the imperial title (the Austrian Habsburgs). The Spanish line died out with the inbred and childless Charles II in 1700, triggering the War of the Spanish Succession. The Austrian male line ended in 1740 with Charles VI, whose daughter Maria Theresa preserved the hereditary lands through the Pragmatic Sanction and the War of the Austrian Succession; her marriage to Francis of Lorraine founded the House of Habsburg-Lorraine, which ruled Austria until 1918.

Later history

Habsburg-Lorraine rulers included Joseph II, the enlightened reformer; Francis II, who dissolved the Holy Roman Empire in 1806 and took the title Emperor of Austria; Franz Joseph I, whose 68-year reign (1848–1916) saw the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867; and Charles I, the last emperor, who renounced participation in government in November 1918 after the First World War. Habsburgs also ruled as emperors of Mexico (Maximilian I, 1864–67) and briefly as kings of various states. The family's hereditary jaw, the "Habsburg jaw" (mandibular prognathism), became a famous example of the effects of dynastic inbreeding.
