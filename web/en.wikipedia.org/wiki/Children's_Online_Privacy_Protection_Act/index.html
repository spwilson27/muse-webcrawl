Children's Online Privacy Protection Act

The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act of 1998 (COPPA) is a United States federal law enacted on October 21, 1998, and effective from April 21, 2000, that imposes requirements on operators of websites and online services directed to children under 13 years of age, and on operators of other websites or services that have actual knowledge that they collect personal information from children under 13. It is enforced by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC).

Requirements

Under COPPA and the FTC's implementing Rule, covered operators must:

  • Provide clear and complete notice of their information-collection practices in a privacy policy.
  • Obtain verifiable parental consent before collecting, using or disclosing personal information from a child, subject to limited exceptions.
  • Give parents the choice of consenting to collection for internal use while withholding consent to disclosure to third parties.
  • Provide parents access to their child's personal information and the ability to have it deleted.
  • Maintain reasonable procedures to protect the confidentiality, security and integrity of the information collected.
  • Retain personal information only as long as reasonably necessary and delete it using reasonable measures.

"Personal information" includes names, addresses, email addresses, telephone numbers, photographs, voice recordings, geolocation and persistent identifiers used for behavioural advertising.

The COPPA Rule and amendments

The FTC's revised COPPA Rule, effective July 1, 2013, expanded the definition of personal information to cover photos, videos, audio files and persistent identifiers, and tightened rules for third-party advertising networks and plug-ins operating on child-directed services. The law also provides for FTC-approved "safe harbor" programmes administered by industry groups.

Enforcement

The FTC has brought numerous enforcement actions, often jointly with state attorneys general. Notable cases include a $170 million settlement with Google and YouTube in 2019 over alleged collection of children's data without parental consent — then the largest COPPA settlement — a $275 million civil penalty against Epic Games in 2022, and earlier actions against platforms and app developers such as TikTok (formerly Musical.ly), which paid $5.7 million in 2019. Violations can incur civil penalties per violation, adjusted for inflation.

Criticism and proposed reform

Researchers have criticised COPPA for encouraging websites to simply exclude or age-gate children (leading many children to lie about their age), for burdens on small operators, and for gaps as children's media use has shifted to mobile apps and connected toys. Proposed legislation ("COPPA 2.0") would extend protections to teenagers up to age 16 and ban targeted advertising to children and teens.
