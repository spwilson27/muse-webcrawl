James Franck (26 August 1882 – 21 May 1964) was a German-American physicist who won the 1925 Nobel Prize in Physics, shared with Gustav Hertz, for the Franck–Hertz experiment, which provided direct experimental confirmation of the quantised energy states of atoms predicted by the Bohr model. He later became known for the Franck Report of 1945, in which Manhattan Project scientists urged that the atomic bomb not be used against Japanese cities without warning.

Early life and career in Germany

Franck was born in Hamburg and studied physics at Heidelberg and Berlin, receiving his doctorate in 1906. At the University of Berlin he met Gustav Hertz, and in 1914 the two performed their famous experiment showing that electrons colliding with mercury atoms lose energy only in discrete amounts — key evidence for Bohr's atomic theory. Franck served in the German army in the First World War and was awarded the Iron Cross.

In 1920 he became professor and director of the Second Institute for Experimental Physics at the University of Göttingen, which under his leadership became a world centre of quantum physics, attracting Max Born and a generation of young theorists. With Edward Condon he formulated the Franck–Condon principle in molecular spectroscopy.

Emigration and the Franck Report

Although exempted as a war veteran from the Nazi racial laws, Franck resigned his Göttingen post in 1933 in protest at the dismissal of Jewish colleagues. He worked briefly in Copenhagen with Niels Bohr, then moved to the United States, where he became a professor at the University of Chicago. In June 1945, as chairman of a committee of Manhattan Project scientists, he authored the Franck Report, recommending a demonstration of the atomic bomb on an uninhabited area before its use in war. The recommendation was not adopted.

Later work

After the war Franck turned to the study of photosynthesis, applying physical methods to biological problems. He died suddenly in 1964 while visiting Copenhagen.
