Pāhoehoe


Type: basaltic lava flow morphology. Contrast: ʻaʻā (clinkery lava).

Pāhoehoe (Hawaiian pronunciation roughly "pah-HOY-hoy") is basaltic lava with a smooth, billowy, undulating, or ropy surface. Along with ʻaʻā, it is one of the two principal surface morphologies of basaltic lava flows, and the Hawaiian terms have been adopted into volcanology worldwide.

Formation

Pāhoehoe forms from low-viscosity, fluid lava that advances slowly enough for a continuous, flexible skin to form on its surface. As the molten interior continues to move, the skin wrinkles and folds into the characteristic ropy texture. Pāhoehoe flows commonly advance through networks of lava tubes, which insulate the lava and allow flows to travel many kilometres from the vent, as on Kīlauea in Hawaiʻi.

Transition to ʻaʻā

If a pāhoehoe flow accelerates, steepens, or loses heat and gas, its surface may tear apart into the rough, clinkery rubble of ʻaʻā. The transition is one-way under normal conditions: ʻaʻā does not revert to pāhoehoe downstream.

Surface features

Characteristic features include ropy folds, smooth "slabby" surfaces, tumuli (pressure domes), lava-rise pits, and inflated sheet flows. Solidified pāhoehoe is comparatively smooth to walk on, unlike the sharp, jagged surface of ʻaʻā fields.
