Soren Kierkegaard

Søren Aabye Kierkegaard (5 May 1813 – 11 November 1855) was a Danish philosopher, theologian, poet, social critic, and religious author, widely considered to be the first existentialist philosopher. He wrote critical texts on organized religion, Christendom, morality, ethics, psychology, and the philosophy of religion, displaying a fondness for metaphor, irony, and parables.

Life

Kierkegaard was born in Copenhagen to a wealthy, devoutly Pietist family. He studied theology at the University of Copenhagen, where he became critical of Hegelian philosophy. His broken engagement to Regine Olsen deeply influenced his life and writing. Much of his work was published under a series of pseudonyms, engaging in elaborate "indirect communication" with the reader.

Thought

Kierkegaard argued that truth is "subjectivity": faith is not a matter of objective proof but of passionate inward commitment, epitomized by Abraham's willingness to sacrifice Isaac in Fear and Trembling (1843). He described three stages of life — the aesthetic, the ethical, and the religious — and in The Concept of Anxiety (1844) and The Sickness Unto Death (1849) he analysed anxiety and despair as fundamental conditions of human freedom and selfhood. Major works include Either/Or (1843) and Concluding Unscientific Postscript (1846).

Influence

Little read outside Denmark in his lifetime, Kierkegaard was rediscovered in the early 20th century and profoundly influenced existentialism, modern theology (including Karl Barth), and thinkers such as Heidegger, Sartre, and Wittgenstein.
