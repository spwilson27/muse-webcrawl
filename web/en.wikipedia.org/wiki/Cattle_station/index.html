Cattle station

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Hero image placeholder: an aerial view of an outback cattle station]

In Australia, a cattle station is a large farm or ranch devoted to raising cattle, the equivalent of the American ranch. Because much of Australia's inland grazing land is arid, stations are enormous: the largest, Anna Creek Station in South Australia, covers roughly 23,000 square kilometres — an area larger than some countries — though it carries only a few thousand head of stock.

Characteristics

Most stations are held on pastoral leases from the Crown rather than freehold. The station homestead is the hub of a small, self-contained community, with stockmen (ringers), jackaroos and jillaroos learning station work, a cook, and often its own airstrip, fuel store, workshop, and school-of-the-air arrangements for children. Mustering is done by motorbike, helicopter, and horseback, gathering cattle from paddocks that may be hundreds of square kilometres in size, to yards for branding, drafting, and transport by road train to market.

History

Stations spread with European pastoral expansion into the outback from the mid-nineteenth century, frequently displacing Aboriginal peoples, whose labour was nonetheless central to the industry for generations — often unpaid, a history addressed in the equal-wage cases of the 1960s and in the Wave Hill walk-off of 1966. Companies such as the Australian Agricultural Company and the Kidman empire once controlled chains of stations across the continent.
