Proton-pump inhibitors (PPIs) are a class of medications whose main action is a profound and prolonged reduction of stomach acid production. They irreversibly block the gastric hydrogen potassium ATPase (H+/K+-ATPase, the "proton pump") of the stomach's parietal cells — the final step of acid secretion — and are the most potent inhibitors of acid secretion available.

Medical uses

PPIs are used to treat gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and its complications such as erosive esophagitis and Barrett's esophagus; peptic ulcer disease, including ulcers caused by Helicobacter pylori as part of combination eradication therapy; Zollinger–Ellison syndrome; and functional dyspepsia. They are also given to prevent stress ulcers in critically ill patients and to protect the stomach in people taking NSAIDs. Many are available over the counter for frequent heartburn.

Mechanism of action

PPIs are prodrugs that concentrate in the acidic canaliculi of activated parietal cells, where they are converted to a reactive sulfenamide that forms covalent disulfide bonds with cysteine residues of the proton pump. Because the bond is irreversible, acid secretion resumes only as new pumps are synthesized, giving an effect lasting 24–48 hours despite a short plasma half-life. They are most effective when taken before a meal, when pumps are active.

Adverse effects

PPIs are generally well tolerated; common effects include headache, nausea, diarrhoea and abdominal pain. Long-term use has been associated with hypomagnesemia, vitamin B12 and iron malabsorption, increased risk of enteric infections including Clostridioides difficile, community-acquired pneumonia, bone fractures, and acute interstitial nephritis. Because several PPIs inhibit the liver enzyme CYP2C19, they can interact with drugs such as clopidogrel. Rebound acid hypersecretion can occur after stopping prolonged therapy, and clinicians are encouraged to deprescribe PPIs when the original indication has resolved.

History

Omeprazole, developed by Astra (later AstraZeneca) and launched in 1988, was the first PPI and became one of the world's best-selling drugs under the name Losec/Prilosec. Its single-enantiomer successor esomeprazole (Nexium) followed in 2000.
