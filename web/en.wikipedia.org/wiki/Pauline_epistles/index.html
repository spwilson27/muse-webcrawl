Pauline epistles

The Pauline epistles are the thirteen books of the New Testament attributed to Paul the Apostle, in which Paul addresses early Christian communities and individuals across the Mediterranean world. Composed in the 50s and early 60s AD (on most scholarly accounts), they are the earliest surviving Christian writings and foundational sources for Christian theology — justification by faith, the nature of the Church, the resurrection, and Christian ethics.

The letters

In canonical order (ordered in the Bible roughly by length) they are:

  • Romans — Paul's most systematic exposition of the gospel
  • 1 Corinthians and 2 Corinthians — community problems and Paul's apostolic defence
  • Galatians — justification by faith against "Judaizing" opponents
  • Ephesians — the Church as the body of Christ
  • Philippians — a warm letter to a favoured congregation, including the Christ hymn
  • Colossians — Christology against a local heresy; associated with the letter to Philemon
  • 1 Thessalonians and 2 Thessalonians — among the earliest writings, on the Second Coming
  • 1 Timothy, 2 Timothy, Titus — the "Pastoral epistles", on church order
  • Philemon — a brief personal appeal about the slave Onesimus

The Epistle to the Hebrews, traditionally ascribed to Paul in the Eastern church and included among his letters in some manuscripts, is anonymous and stylistically distinct; most modern scholars deny Pauline authorship.

Authorship and scholarship

Critical scholarship distinguishes seven letters regarded as undoubtedly authentic — Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians and Philemon — from six whose authorship is disputed ("deutero-Pauline"): Ephesians, Colossians and 2 Thessalonians are widely debated, while the three Pastoral epistles are ascribed by most critical scholars to a later follower writing in Paul's name, a common practice in antiquity though its acceptability remains contested. Arguments turn on vocabulary and style statistics, theological development, and the picture of church organisation they presuppose. Traditional and conservative scholarship defends the authenticity of all thirteen.

Form and transmission

The letters follow and adapt Greco-Roman epistolary conventions: an opening greeting naming senders and recipients with grace and peace, thanksgiving, a body addressing doctrine and conduct, and closing greetings and benediction. Paul dictated to secretaries (amanuenses) such as Tertius (Romans 16:22), sometimes adding a greeting in his own hand as a mark of authenticity. His letters were collected early — already known collectively by the early 2nd century (2 Peter 3:16 refers to them) — and survive in papyri such as 𝔓46 (c. AD 200) and in the great uncial codices.
