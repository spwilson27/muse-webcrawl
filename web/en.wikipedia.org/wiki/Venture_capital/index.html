Venture capital

[Image: a startup founder pitching to investors around a conference table in a glass-walled office]

Venture capital (VC) is a form of Private equity financing provided to young, high-growth companies — usually technology startups — in exchange for equity, accepting a high failure rate in pursuit of outsized returns from a few winners. Venture capitalists raise funds from pension funds, endowments and wealthy investors, sit on boards, and exit through an IPO or acquisition after roughly five to ten years.

How it works and its history

Funding proceeds in stages — seed, Series A, B, C and beyond — at rising valuations, with rare 'unicorns' reaching $1 billion. The industry took modern shape in Silicon Valley after 1957 (Fairchild Semiconductor; firms such as Kleiner Perkins and Sequoia in the 1970s) and bankrolled Apple, Google, Amazon-era internet firms, and successive waves through social media to artificial intelligence. Returns follow a power law: most investments fail, a few return the fund. Critics note cycles of over-funding (the dot-com boom, the 2021 peak) and the model's concentration in few places and sectors; governments nonetheless promote VC through tax incentives and public funds as an engine of entrepreneurship and innovation.
