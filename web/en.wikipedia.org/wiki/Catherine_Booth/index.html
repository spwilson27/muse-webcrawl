Catherine Booth

Catherine Booth (née Mumford; 17 January 1829 – 4 October 1890) was a British preacher, social reformer, and, with her husband William Booth, co-founder of The Salvation Army. Often called the "Mother of the Salvation Army", she was its theological voice and a formidable preacher in her own right, at a time when women in the pulpit were highly controversial.

Early life and ministry

Raised in a devout Methodist family, Catherine Mumford read the Bible through many times as a child and developed strong convictions on temperance and social reform. She married the Methodist minister William Booth in 1855. Her pamphlet Female Ministry (1859), written in response to criticism of the woman preacher Phoebe Palmer, argued forcefully for women's right to preach, and from 1860 she preached herself, drawing large and admiring congregations.

The Salvation Army

In 1865 the Booths began evangelistic work in London's East End, founding the mission that in 1878 became The Salvation Army, organised on military lines with "soldiers", "officers", uniforms, and brass bands. Catherine shaped its theology, campaigned for the welfare of women and children, championed the match girls and other exploited workers, and insisted that women officers hold equal rank and authority with men — a strikingly egalitarian position for Victorian Britain. She was mother of eight children, nearly all of whom became leaders in the Army, including Bramwell, its second General, and Evangeline, later General herself.

Death and legacy

Catherine Booth died of cancer in 1890 after a long illness borne with characteristic public fortitude; tens of thousands filed past her coffin. Her writings — sermons, pamphlets, and the book Practical Religion — and her defence of women's ministry and of the poor remain central to Salvation Army identity.
