St. Petersburg paradox

The St. Petersburg paradox or St. Petersburg lottery is a paradox related to probability and decision theory in economics. It is based on a theoretical lottery game that leads to a random variable with infinite expected value but which nevertheless seems worth only a small amount to participants.

St. Petersburg paradox
Field: Decision theory, economics
Proposed: Nicolaus Bernoulli, 1713
Published: Daniel Bernoulli, 1738

The game

A casino offers a game: a fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads. If heads appears on the n-th toss, the player wins 2n ducats (in variants, 2n-1 dollars). The expected value sums to infinity, yet few would pay more than a modest fee to play — the paradox.

Resolutions

Daniel Bernoulli (1738) resolved it with diminishing marginal utility of money: expected utility, using a logarithmic utility function, is finite. Other resolutions invoke finite casino resources, risk aversion, probability weighting, and the idea that people ignore very unlikely large payoffs. Gabriel Cramer independently proposed a similar utility solution.

Variations

The "super" St. Petersburg paradox uses payoffs growing even faster so expected utility is also infinite under common utility functions.
