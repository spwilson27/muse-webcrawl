Girsanov theorem

In probability theory, the Girsanov theorem describes how the dynamics of stochastic processes change when the original measure is replaced by an equivalent (or absolutely continuous) probability measure. Named after the Russian mathematician Igor Vladimirovich Girsanov, who published it in 1960, the theorem gives an explicit formula — via the Radon–Nikodym derivative — for how a Brownian motion acquires a drift under the new measure, and conversely how drift can be removed.

Statement

Let Wt be a Brownian motion on a filtered probability space (Ω, ℱ, (ℱt), ℙ) and let θt be an adapted process satisfying a condition such as Novikov's condition,

𝔼[ exp( ½ ∫₀T θs² ds ) ] < ∞,

so that the stochastic exponential

Zt = exp( −∫₀t θs dWs − ½ ∫₀t θs² ds )

is a martingale. Defining a new measure ℚ by dℚ/dℙ = ZT, the theorem states that the process

W̃t = Wt + ∫₀t θs ds

is a Brownian motion under ℚ. Thus a drift of −θ under ℙ becomes zero drift under ℚ: change of measure and change of drift are equivalent operations.

Applications

The Girsanov theorem is a cornerstone of mathematical finance. In the Black–Scholes model and its generalisations, it justifies passing from the real-world measure to the risk-neutral measure under which discounted asset prices are martingales: the market price of risk plays the role of θ, and Girsanov's theorem shows the drift can be replaced by the risk-free rate without changing the volatility. It is also used in stochastic control, filtering theory, the theory of weak solutions of stochastic differential equations, and in statistics for computing likelihoods of diffusion processes.

Related results

The theorem is closely related to the Cameron–Martin theorem, which describes how the Wiener measure transforms under translations in Cameron–Martin space; Girsanov's result is the general stochastic-process extension. Sufficient conditions guaranteeing that the stochastic exponential is a true martingale include Novikov's condition and Kazamaki's condition.
