The Sino-British Joint Declaration, formally the Joint Declaration of the Government of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland and the Government of the People's Republic of China on the Question of Hong Kong, is an international treaty signed in Beijing on 19 December 1984 between the United Kingdom and China concerning the future of Hong Kong.

Background

Hong Kong Island and the Kowloon Peninsula had been ceded to Britain in perpetuity in the 19th century, but the New Territories — the bulk of the colony's land — were held on a 99-year lease expiring on 30 June 1997. With the lease approaching, negotiations opened after Deng Xiaoping received British prime minister Margaret Thatcher in September 1982. China insisted on resuming sovereignty over the whole territory, and two years of often difficult talks followed.

Terms

Under the declaration, the United Kingdom agreed to restore Hong Kong to China on 1 July 1997, and China declared its basic policies under the principle of "one country, two systems": Hong Kong would become a Special Administrative Region, retaining its capitalist system, way of life, legal system, and a high degree of autonomy for 50 years, until 2047, with foreign and defence affairs reserved to Beijing. The treaty was registered with the United Nations in 1985, and its stipulations were incorporated into the Basic Law of Hong Kong, adopted in 1990.

Later disputes

Since the 2019–20 Hong Kong protests and the imposition of the Hong Kong national security law in 2020, the British government has declared China to be in "ongoing non-compliance" with the declaration. Chinese officials have described the declaration as a historical document with no practical significance — a position the United Kingdom and other Western governments reject, maintaining it remains a binding treaty in force.
