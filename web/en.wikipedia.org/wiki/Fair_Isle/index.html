Fair Isle

Fair Isle, midway between Shetland and Orkney, famed for its bird observatory and knitting.

Fair Isle (from Old Norse Frjóey) is an island in Shetland, in northern Scotland, lying about halfway between the Shetland Mainland and Orkney. It is the most remote inhabited island in the United Kingdom. Administratively it forms part of the civil parish of Dunrossness in the Shetland Islands council area. The island is roughly 4.8 km (3 miles) long and 2.4 km (1.5 miles) wide, with a population of around 50 to 70 people engaged mainly in crofting, fishing, and tourism.

Bird observatory

Fair Isle is internationally famous among birdwatchers for its bird observatory, founded by George Waterston in 1948, and for the rare migrant birds that appear there in spring and autumn. The island's position on migration routes means scarce species from Siberia and North America are recorded regularly. The observatory building was destroyed by fire in 2019 and has since been rebuilt. Fair Isle is also an important breeding site for seabirds, including puffins, and is designated a Site of Special Scientific Interest and a Special Protection Area.

Knitting

The island gave its name to Fair Isle knitting, a traditional technique of multicoloured stranded knitting using geometric patterns, and to the patterned sweaters made famous in the 1920s when the Prince of Wales (later Edward VIII) wore them publicly. Genuine Fair Isle garments are still produced on the island.

History and ownership

Fair Isle has been inhabited since the Bronze Age and has Norse and Scots heritage. Admiralty and lighthouse history includes the two lighthouses built in 1892 by David Alan Stevenson. In 1954 the island was purchased by George Waterston and given to the National Trust for Scotland, which owns it today.

Transport

The island is served by the ferry Good Shepherd IV from Grutness on the Shetland Mainland, and by flights to Fair Isle Airport from Tingwall and Sumburgh.
