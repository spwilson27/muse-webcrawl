Fairey Swordfish

Fairey Swordfish
Manufacturer: Fairey Aviation
First flight: 17 April 1934
Introduction: 1936
Retired: 1945 (frontline)
Number built: 2,391
Role: torpedo bomber / anti-submarine aircraft

The Fairey Swordfish is a biplane torpedo bomber designed by the Fairey Aviation Company and flown by the Royal Navy's Fleet Air Arm during the Second World War. Affectionately known as the "Stringbag", it was already obsolete in appearance by 1939 — an open-cockpit fabric-covered biplane — yet it achieved a distinguished combat record, sinking a greater tonnage of Axis shipping than any other Allied aircraft type.

Design

The Swordfish was powered by a Bristol Pegasus radial engine and carried a crew of three (two in later anti-submarine versions): pilot, observer and telegraphist/air gunner. It could carry a 1,670 lb torpedo, bombs, mines, depth charges, and later rockets and radar. Its very low stalling speed made it ideally suited to operations from small escort carriers in rough Atlantic weather.

Operational history

Swordfish achieved fame in the attack on the Italian fleet at Taranto in November 1940 — the first all-aircraft naval attack in history — and in May 1941, when Swordfish from HMS Ark Royal crippled the German battleship Bismarck's steering, enabling her destruction. The type also suffered grievously when caught by modern fighters, as in the disastrous Channel Dash attack of February 1942, in which all six attacking Swordfish were shot down. Operating from escort carriers, Swordfish flew anti-submarine patrols until the end of the war in Europe, outlasting its intended replacement.
