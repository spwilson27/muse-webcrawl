Semantic memory

Semantic memory

Semantic memory is a system of memory that encodes, stores, and retrieves general knowledge about the world — facts, concepts, ideas, and the meanings of words and objects — without reference to the personal experiences in which that knowledge was acquired. It is one of the two principal subdivisions of declarative memory, the other being episodic memory.

History

The distinction between semantic and episodic memory was proposed by the cognitive psychologist Endel Tulving in 1972. Tulving argued that episodic memory stores personally experienced events situated in time and place, while semantic memory stores context-free knowledge. The division was controversial at first but became one of the most influential ideas in the psychology of memory.

Semantic versus episodic memory

A canonical illustration: a person may know that Paris is the capital of France (semantic memory) without remembering when or where they learned it, whereas remembering the trip on which they first saw the Eiffel Tower draws on episodic memory. Patients with amnesia affecting the hippocampus can sometimes retain previously learned semantic knowledge while being unable to form new episodic memories, suggesting the two systems are at least partly dissociable.

Models of semantic memory

Feature-comparison and prototype models

Early models proposed that concepts are represented as lists of semantic features, or as prototypes — abstracted central members of a category against which instances are compared.

Semantic network models

Network models represent concepts as nodes connected by labelled links expressing relationships such as "is a" or "has a". Retrieval is modelled as spreading activation through the network. Notable variants include Collins and Quillian's hierarchical network (1969) and Collins and Loftus's spreading-activation model (1975).

Connectionist and hub-and-spoke models

More recent accounts treat semantic knowledge as patterns of activity across distributed neural networks, with the anterior temporal lobes acting as a "semantic hub" that binds together modality-specific information.

Neural basis

Neuroimaging studies associate semantic processing with a widely distributed left-lateralized network including the inferior frontal gyrus, middle and inferior temporal gyri, angular gyrus, and the anterior temporal lobes. The hippocampus is critical for consolidating new semantic facts into long-term neocortical stores.

Disorders

Semantic dementia, a variant of frontotemporal lobar degeneration, produces a progressive erosion of conceptual knowledge — patients lose word meanings and the ability to recognize objects — while episodic memory and perceptual processing remain relatively intact. Semantic deficits also appear in Alzheimer's disease and after left temporal-lobe injury.
