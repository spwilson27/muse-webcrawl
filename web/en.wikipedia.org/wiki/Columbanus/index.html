Columbanus

Saint Columbanus (Irish: Columbán, meaning "white dove"; c. 543 – 21 November 615) was an Irish missionary notable for founding a number of monasteries on the European continent from around 590 onwards, most notably Luxeuil Abbey in France and Bobbio Abbey in Italy. He is considered one of the early fathers of Hiberno-Scottish monasticism and spread Irish penitential practices and the Irish monastic rule across Francia and Italy. The Catholic Church celebrates his feast day on 23 November.

Life

Columbanus was born in Leinster, Ireland, and educated at home before studying under Sinell of Cleenish and at the great monastery of Bangor under Comgall, where he was ordained and taught for many years. Around 591, in accordance with the Irish practice of peregrinatio (voluntary exile for Christ), he sailed for the continent with twelve companions, among them Saint Gall.

Work in Francia

Columbanus founded monasteries in the Vosges — Annegray, Luxeuil, and Fontaine — which quickly attracted many novices and became centres of learning. His monastic rule, the Regula Monachorum, was famously austere. He clashed with the Frankish episcopate over the Irish (Celtic) dating of Easter and with the court of King Theuderic II, whom he rebuked for his morals; in 610 he was expelled from the kingdom. Journeying through Neustria and Austrasia, he worked briefly around Lake Constance with Gall, then crossed the Alps into Lombard Italy in 612.

Bobbio and death

King Agilulf of the Lombards granted Columbanus land at Bobbio, where in 614 he founded his last monastery, which became one of the great libraries and intellectual centres of early medieval Europe. Columbanus died at Bobbio on 21 November 615 and is buried there.

Writings and legacy

Columbanus's writings include sermons, poems, letters, his monastic rule, and a penitential — among the earliest Irish Latin works to survive. His monasteries preserved classical and patristic texts, and the Irish tradition of private, repeatable confession disseminated through his foundations profoundly influenced Western Christian practice.
