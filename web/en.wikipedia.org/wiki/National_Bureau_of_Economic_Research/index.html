National Bureau of Economic Research

The National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) is a private, non-profit, non-partisan research organization based in Cambridge, Massachusetts, dedicated to conducting and disseminating economic research. Founded in 1920, it is best known to the public for its Business Cycle Dating Committee, which officially determines the start and end dates of recessions in the United States.

History

The NBER was founded in 1920 by Wesley Clair Mitchell, an economist who had worked in the federal government during the First World War, with support from figures across the political spectrum who wanted economic statistics and analysis free of partisan control. Early research directors included Mitchell himself, and the Bureau's early work on national income accounting by Simon Kuznets laid foundations for modern macroeconomic statistics. Kuznets's work at the NBER led to the standard measures of national income and product used by the U.S. government.

Under long-serving president Martin Feldstein (1977–2008, with a break for government service), the NBER expanded greatly, organising its research into programmes and greatly enlarging its network of affiliated scholars. James Poterba succeeded Feldstein in 2008.

Research

The Bureau supports research by more than 1,600 affiliated economists at North American universities, organised into around twenty programmes covering areas such as aging, asset pricing, corporate finance, economic fluctuations and growth, education, health economics, industrial organization, international finance, labour studies, monetary economics, political economy and public economics. Its best-known output is the NBER Working Paper series, one of the largest distributors of new economic research, issuing thousands of papers per year. The Bureau also hosts widely used data resources and the Summer Institute, a large annual research conference.

Business cycle dating

The NBER's Business Cycle Dating Committee, a group of leading macroeconomists, determines peaks and troughs in U.S. economic activity. Because the committee weighs multiple indicators — including employment, real personal income, industrial production and real sales — and announces its decisions only after trends are clear, official recession calls often lag turning points by many months. The committee dated, for example, the Great Recession as beginning in December 2007 and ending in June 2009, and the COVID-19 recession as lasting from February to April 2020, the shortest on record.
