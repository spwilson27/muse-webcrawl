Picatrix

Original titleGhāyat al-Ḥakīm ("The Goal of the Wise")
LanguageArabic (later Latin, Spanish)
Datec. mid-11th century
SubjectAstrology, astral magic, talismans

The Picatrix is the Latin name of a 400-page book of magic and astrology originally written in Arabic under the title Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm (غاية الحكيم), "The Goal of the Wise", most probably composed in al-Andalus in the mid-11th century. It is one of the most important medieval treatises on astral magic and talisman-making, synthesising Arabic, Persian, Greek and Harranian (Sabian) sources.

Contents

The work is divided into four books covering the nature of the heavens and their influence on the sublunary world; the figures and forms of the heavens, the motions of the planets and their effects; the properties of planets and zodiac signs, including images and talismans to be constructed under favourable configurations; and a large collection of prayers, invocations and conjurations addressed to the spirits of the planets. It includes famous material such as the mansions of the Moon and the "perfect nature" meditation.

Authorship and transmission

The Arabic text was traditionally attributed to the astronomer and mathematician Maslama ibn Ahmad al-Majrīṭī (d. c. 1008), though scholars generally regard the attribution as pseudonymous, with compilation dated to around 1047–1051. It was translated into Spanish at the court of Alfonso X of Castile around 1256, and from Spanish into Latin; the Latin version circulated widely in Renaissance Europe and influenced figures such as Marsilio Ficino and Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa. The name "Picatrix" is thought to derive from a supposed author named in the text, possibly a garbled transliteration of an Arabic name, and in the prologue "Picatrix" is presented as the work's author-persona.
