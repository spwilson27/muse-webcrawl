Epistemic injustice

Epistemic injustice is injustice related to knowledge — wrongs done to someone specifically in their capacity as a knower. The term was coined by the philosopher Miranda Fricker in her 2007 book Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing, which introduced the field's two central concepts.

Testimonial injustice

Testimonial injustice occurs when prejudice causes a hearer to give a deflated level of credibility to a speaker's word. Fricker's central example is the racial prejudice in Harper Lee's To Kill a Mockingbird, where the testimony of Tom Robinson is unjustly discounted. The credibility deficits track social identity — race, gender, class — and wrong the speaker both practically and in their standing as a knower.

Hermeneutical injustice

Hermeneutical injustice occurs when a gap in shared interpretive resources unfairly disadvantages someone in making sense of their experience. Fricker's example is the period before the term "sexual harassment" existed, when a woman subject to it lacked a concept to understand and communicate her experience — a gap that itself reflected her group's exclusion from shaping collective meanings.

Later developments

Subsequent philosophers have extended the framework: Kristie Dotson described testimonial quieting and testimonial smothering; José Medina and Gaile Pohlhaus Jr. developed accounts of epistemic resistance and "willful hermeneutical ignorance"; others have proposed concepts such as contributory injustice and epistemic exploitation. The framework has been applied to medicine, law, education and psychiatry, where patients' testimony may be systematically discounted.
