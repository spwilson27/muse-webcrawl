In theoretical astrophysics, a Strömgren sphere is the sphere of ionised hydrogen (H II) around a young star of the spectral classes O or B. Its counterpart in the real world is the H II region, a type of emission nebula. It was proposed by the Danish astrophysicist Bengt Strömgren in 1939.

The model assumes a hot, luminous star embedded in a uniform cloud of hydrogen gas. Ultraviolet photons from the star ionise hydrogen atoms out to a radius where the rate of ionisation is exactly balanced by the rate of recombination of protons and electrons. Outside this radius the gas remains neutral, producing a sharp ionisation front.

Derivation

Suppose the star emits Q ionising photons per second and the surrounding gas has number density n. In equilibrium, every ionisation is balanced by a recombination. The total number of recombinations inside a sphere of radius RS is (4/3)πRS³ αB n², where αB is the case-B recombination coefficient. Setting this equal to Q gives the Strömgren radius:

RS = ( 3Q / (4π n² αB) )1/3

Typical Strömgren radii range from a fraction of a parsec in dense molecular clouds to tens of parsecs for luminous stars in diffuse gas. The boundary is thin compared with the radius because hydrogen's ionisation cross-section is large, so the transition from ionised to neutral gas occurs over a very short distance.

Limitations and extensions

Real H II regions depart from the idealised model: the gas is clumpy rather than uniform, stellar winds and radiation pressure blow bubbles and champagne flows, dust absorbs ionising photons, and multiple stars may share a region. Modern photoionisation codes numerically solve for the structure of such nebulae, but the Strömgren sphere remains the standard first approximation and a benchmark for those codes.
