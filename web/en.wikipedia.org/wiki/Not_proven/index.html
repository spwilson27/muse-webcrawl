Not proven

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Not proven is a verdict available in Scottish criminal trials, alongside guilty and not guilty. It is an acquittal with the same legal effect as not guilty — the accused is freed and cannot be retried (subject to the general double-jeopardy exceptions) — but is popularly understood to convey “we believe you did it, but the Crown has not proved it”, a stigma that has made the verdict controversial since the eighteenth century (Sir Walter Scott called it “that bastard verdict”).

Not proven is returned in a disproportionate share of acquittals in sexual-offence cases, fuelling the modern abolition campaign (supported by victims' organisations and successive reviews): reform legislation before the Scottish Parliament in the mid-2020s proposed abolishing the verdict as part of a package (with jury majority changes) after earlier bills failed. Defenders — much of the Scottish legal profession — argue the third verdict honestly expresses the jury's state of mind and protects against wrongful conviction in a system permitting conviction by simple majority of fifteen jurors. Scotland is the only modern jurisdiction with the verdict in this form (historical cousins existed elsewhere), making its fate a watched experiment in jury design.
