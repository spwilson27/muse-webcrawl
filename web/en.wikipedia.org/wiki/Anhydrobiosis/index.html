Anhydrobiosis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anhydrobiosis (from Greek for "life without water") is a state of dormancy in which an organism loses almost all of its body water yet remains viable, resuming normal activity when water becomes available again. It is the most studied form of cryptobiosis and is found in organisms including tardigrades, rotifers, nematodes, brine shrimp, some insects, and many plants and microorganisms.

Mechanism

As drying proceeds, anhydrobiotic organisms protect their cells with sugars such as trehalose, which replaces water in membranes and proteins and forms a glassy matrix (vitrification) that immobilises cellular structures. Special proteins, including late embryogenesis abundant (LEA) proteins and tardigrade-specific intrinsically disordered proteins, shield enzymes and DNA from damage. Metabolism falls to undetectable levels, and the organism may persist for years or decades; tardigrades in this state have survived exposure to the vacuum and radiation of open space.

Examples

Bdelloid rotifers are entirely female, reproduce parthenogenetically, and survive desiccation at any life stage, apparently using the repair demands of drying to purge fungal parasites and fix accumulated DNA damage. Brine shrimp (Artemia) embryos are sold as cysts that can remain dry for years before hatching on rehydration. Among plants, "resurrection plants" such as Selaginella lepidophylla curl up when dry and unfold and photosynthesise within hours of watering. The sleeping chironomid midge Polypedilum vanderplanki is the largest animal known to achieve anhydrobiosis.

Applications

Understanding anhydrobiosis has inspired research into dry preservation of vaccines, blood products, and food, as well as astrobiology studies of life's limits. Expressing protective proteins in sensitive cells is an active area of biotechnology research.
