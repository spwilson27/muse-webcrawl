Employee Retirement Income Security Act

The Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA) is a U.S. federal law that sets minimum standards for most voluntarily established retirement and health plans in private industry to provide protection for individuals in these plans. Enacted on 2 September 1974, it is codified principally at 29 U.S.C. ch. 18.

Provisions

ERISA requires plans to provide participants with plan information, sets minimum standards for participation, vesting, benefit accrual, and funding, requires accountability of plan fiduciaries, and gives participants the right to sue for benefits and breaches of fiduciary duty. It established the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC) to insure defined-benefit pension plans. ERISA is administered by the Employee Benefits Security Administration of the Department of Labor, the Internal Revenue Service, and the PBGC.

History

The law followed public concern over pension failures, most famously the 1963 shutdown of the Studebaker automobile plant, which left thousands of workers with reduced or lost pensions. Long-running congressional studies culminated in ERISA's passage under President Gerald Ford. Later amendments include COBRA (continuation of health coverage), HIPAA (health insurance portability), and the Pension Protection Act of 2006.

Preemption and criticism

ERISA broadly preempts state laws that "relate to" employee benefit plans, a clause that has generated extensive litigation, particularly over state regulation of employer health plans and remedies for denied benefits. ERISA does not cover plans established by governments or churches, nor does it require employers to offer plans.
