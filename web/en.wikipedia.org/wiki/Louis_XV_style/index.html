Louis XV style

Louis XV style

Period: c. 1722–1774

Country: France

Characteristics: Rococo curves, asymmetry, shell and floral ornament

Preceded by: Régence style

Followed by: Louis XVI style

The Louis XV style or Louis Quinze (French pronunciation: [lwi kɛ̃z]) is a style of architecture and decorative arts which appeared during the reign of Louis XV of France. From 1715 to about 1722, the style — during the Régence of Philippe d'Orléans — was a transition from the more formal classicism of Louis XIV. From about 1730 until about 1760, it reached its height in the light, curving ornament of the Rococo, before reaction against its excesses produced the straighter lines of the Louis XVI style.

Architecture

Louis XV interiors preferred comfort and intimacy to the grandeur of Versailles' state rooms: smaller salons, rounded corners, boiserie panelling painted in light colours with gilded carving of shells, flowers, palm fronds and scrolls. Exteriors, such as Ange-Jacques Gabriel's Petit Trianon (begun 1762) and the Place de la Concorde in Paris, remained more classical.

Furniture

Furniture in the Louis XV style is famous for its curved ("cabriole") legs, serpentine fronts, marquetry inlay, ormolu mounts and the avoidance of straight lines and right angles. Signature pieces include the fauteuil armchair, the commode, the bureau à cylindre and the bergère. Great ébénistes of the period included Charles Cressent, Jean-François Oeben and Bernard II van Risamburgh (BVRB).

Legacy

The style became synonymous with 18th-century French elegance and was widely copied across Europe; it enjoyed a major revival in the 19th century, when Paris makers produced large quantities of "Louis XV style" furniture for wealthy clients.
