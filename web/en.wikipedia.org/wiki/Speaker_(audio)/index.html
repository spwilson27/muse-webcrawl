Loudspeaker

Invented1861 (Reis); dynamic driver patented 1925 (Rice & Kellogg)
TypeElectroacoustic transducer
FunctionConverts electrical audio signal into sound

A loudspeaker (commonly called a speaker) is an electroacoustic transducer that converts an electrical audio signal into corresponding sound. The most widely used type is the dynamic loudspeaker, in which a voice coil suspended in a magnetic field moves a diaphragm (cone) to produce sound waves in air.

History

The first form of loudspeaker appeared in Johann Philipp Reis's telephone of 1861. The moving-coil dynamic driver, the basis of nearly all modern speakers, was patented in 1925 by Chester W. Rice and Edward W. Kellogg of General Electric. Edwin Pridham and Peter Jensen developed the first practical loudspeaker systems earlier, in the 1910s, founding Magnavox.

Design

A dynamic driver consists of a magnet, voice coil, suspension (spider and surround), and a cone or dome diaphragm. Because one driver cannot reproduce the full audible range efficiently, loudspeaker systems combine woofers (low frequencies), mid-range drivers, and tweeters (high frequencies) with a crossover network. Enclosures — sealed, bass-reflex, horn-loaded, and others — strongly affect bass response. Other technologies include electrostatic, planar magnetic, ribbon, and piezoelectric speakers.

Specifications

Loudspeakers are characterised by frequency response, sensitivity (dB per watt at 1 metre), nominal impedance (often 4 or 8 ohms), power handling, and distortion. Hi-fi performance is assessed by measurements of response flatness, directivity, and nonlinear distortion, as well as by listening tests.
