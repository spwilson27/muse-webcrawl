Liver failure

The liver, whose failure affects nearly every organ system

Liver failure is the inability of the liver to perform its normal synthetic, metabolic, and detoxifying functions. It may be acute, developing over days to weeks in a person with no prior liver disease, or chronic, the end stage of long-standing liver disease (cirrhosis). A third form, acute-on-chronic liver failure, describes rapid deterioration in a patient with existing chronic liver disease.

Signs and symptoms

Characteristic features arise from loss of the liver's functions: jaundice (failure of bilirubin excretion); coagulopathy and easy bleeding (failure to synthesise clotting factors); hepatic encephalopathy ranging from confusion to coma (accumulation of ammonia and other toxins); portal hypertension with ascites and variceal bleeding; susceptibility to infection; kidney dysfunction (hepatorenal syndrome); and hypoglycaemia. In acute liver failure, cerebral oedema and raised intracranial pressure are leading causes of death, alongside sepsis and multi-organ failure.

Causes

Causes of acute liver failure differ by region. In the United Kingdom and United States, paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose is the most common cause; elsewhere viral hepatitis (especially hepatitis A, B, and E) predominates. Other causes include idiosyncratic drug reactions, autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson's disease, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, ischaemic hepatitis, and mushroom (Amanita) poisoning. Chronic liver failure results from cirrhosis due to chronic alcohol use, viral hepatitis, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, autoimmune and genetic conditions.

Classification

Acute liver failure is defined by coagulopathy (INR ≥ 1.5) and hepatic encephalopathy in a patient without pre-existing cirrhosis, with illness of less than 26 weeks. It is subdivided by the interval from jaundice to encephalopathy into hyperacute (within 7 days, often paracetamol-related, with better spontaneous survival), acute (8–28 days), and subacute (29 days to 12 weeks, with poorer prognosis without transplant).

Treatment

Management takes place in specialist intensive care units. Treatment includes finding and treating the cause (N-acetylcysteine for paracetamol poisoning and increasingly for other causes), supporting failing organs, preventing and treating infection and cerebral oedema, and correcting coagulopathy when clinically needed. King's College Criteria and similar prognostic scores guide selection for emergency liver transplantation, which offers the best chance of survival in poor-prognosis cases; survival has improved substantially with modern intensive care, and some patients recover spontaneously with supportive treatment alone.
