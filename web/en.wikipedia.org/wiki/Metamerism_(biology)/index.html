Metamerism (biology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

For other uses, see Metamerism.

In biology, metamerism is the phenomenon of having a linear series of body segments fundamentally similar in structure, though not all such structures are entirely alike in any single form of life because some of them perform special functions. In animals, metameric segments are referred to as somites or metameres. In plants, they are referred to as metamers or, more concretely, phytomers.

In animals

True segmentation is most conspicuous in annelid worms, where each segment contains repeated sets of organs — nephridia, ganglia, blood vessels, and circular and longitudinal muscles — separated by septa, and in arthropods, where segments are often fused and specialised into tagmata such as the head, thorax, and abdomen. Chordates show a more cryptic metamerism: the somites of the embryo, the vertebrae, ribs, spinal nerves, and segmental musculature of fishes reflect an underlying segmental plan. Advantages proposed for segmentation include more effective locomotion (especially burrowing by peristalsis in annelids), redundancy and robustness, and the evolutionary flexibility to specialise individual segments.

In plants

Botanists describe the shoot as a series of repeated units — each phytomer consisting of a leaf, node, internode, and axillary bud — whose repetition builds the plant body. This modular construction underlies plant growth, branching, and propagation.

Homonomous and heteronomous metamerism

Where segments remain nearly identical (as in much of an earthworm) metamerism is called homonomous; where segments are differentiated for different functions (as in insects) it is heteronomous. The evolutionary origin of segmentation is debated: it likely evolved independently in annelids, arthropods, and chordates, and developmental genes such as engrailed and the Notch signalling pathway are involved in segment formation across groups.
