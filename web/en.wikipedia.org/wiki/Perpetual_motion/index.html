Perpetual motion is the motion of bodies that continues indefinitely without any external source of energy. A perpetual motion machine is a hypothetical device that does useful work indefinitely — either without any energy input at all, or in defiance of the inevitable dissipation of energy into heat. Such machines are impossible under the laws of thermodynamics, but designs for them have been proposed for more than eight centuries.

Classification

Proposed machines are usually classified by the law they would violate. A machine of the first kind produces work without any energy input, violating the law of conservation of energy (the first law). A machine of the second kind spontaneously converts thermal energy into mechanical work with no other effect, violating the second law. A machine of the third kind merely seeks to eliminate friction and other losses so that motion, once started, continues forever — impossible in practice for macroscopic systems because dissipation can never be reduced to zero.

History

The earliest known design is the overbalanced wheel of the Indian astronomer Bhāskara II (c. 1150), with curved spokes partly filled with mercury. Villard de Honnecourt sketched a similar wheel in the 13th century, and countless inventors followed with wheels, capillary-action devices, magnetic motors and buoyancy engines. Leonardo da Vinci examined and dismissed such schemes. By the 19th century, the formulation of thermodynamics explained their universal failure, and patent offices — notably those of the UK, France and the United States — came to require working models or refused applications for perpetual motion devices outright.

Why designs fail

Careful analysis of each proposal invariably finds a hidden energy loss, an error in the force balance, or an unaccounted energy input. Overbalanced wheels do not in fact overbalance through a full turn; magnetic motors stall when the field energy gained on approach equals that spent on retreat; buoyancy schemes expend as much work inserting objects into the fluid as buoyancy returns. The pursuit nonetheless spurred genuine advances in mechanics and, through attempts to prove or disprove particular machines, in the theory of energy itself.
