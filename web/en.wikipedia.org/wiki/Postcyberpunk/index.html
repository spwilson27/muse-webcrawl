Postcyberpunk

Postcyberpunk fiction imagines high technology woven into ordinary, functioning societies

Postcyberpunk is a genre of science fiction that emerged in the 1990s as a successor to and reaction against cyberpunk. Where classic cyberpunk depicted alienated loners in decaying, corporation-dominated dystopias, postcyberpunk tends to feature protagonists who are integrated into their societies, with families, jobs, and communities, living in futures where advanced technology is pervasive but society itself still functions. The term was popularised by the critic Lawrence Person in his 1998 essay "Notes Toward a Postcyberpunk Manifesto".

Characteristics

Postcyberpunk shares cyberpunk's fascination with information technology, biotechnology, nanotechnology, and virtual reality, but examines their effects on everyday life rather than on a marginal underclass. Its characters typically accept and use the technology around them instead of rebelling against it or being destroyed by it. Settings often extrapolate the social consequences of technologies such as ubiquitous computing, genetic engineering, and life extension, and may be cautiously optimistic or at least ambivalent rather than bleakly dystopian.

Person contrasted the genres by noting that cyberpunk's protagonists were often alienated outsiders, whereas postcyberpunk characters are frequently professionals, parents, or members of institutions who work within the system. The stakes of postcyberpunk stories are often social and personal rather than apocalyptic.

Examples

Writers frequently associated with postcyberpunk include Neal Stephenson, whose novels The Diamond Age (1995) and Snow Crash (1992) helped define the mode; Bruce Sterling's Holy Fire (1996); Greg Egan's work on uploading and posthuman identity; Ken MacLeod's Fall Revolution series; and Charles Stross's Accelerando. Earlier works, such as Vernor Vinge's Marooned in Realtime, anticipated many of its themes. In Japanese media, works such as Ghost in the Shell: Stand Alone Complex are sometimes described as postcyberpunk for their focus on professionals operating inside state institutions.

Relationship to other genres

Postcyberpunk overlaps with the broader category of mundane science fiction and with later "-punk" subgenres, though it is generally treated less as a marketing label than as a descriptive term for the maturation of cyberpunk themes after the personal computer and the internet became everyday realities. Critics have debated whether it constitutes a genuinely distinct genre or simply cyberpunk updated for a world in which its once-startling technologies have become ordinary.
