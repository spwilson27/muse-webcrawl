The Siege of Drogheda took place in September 1649, early in the Cromwellian conquest of Ireland. Oliver Cromwell's New Model Army stormed the port town of Drogheda, defended by a mixed Royalist and Irish Confederate garrison of about 3,000 men under Sir Arthur Aston, and put most of the garrison — and an unknown number of civilians — to the sword.

The siege

Cromwell landed in Ireland in August 1649 to suppress the Royalist-Confederate alliance. At Drogheda he summoned the town to surrender; Aston refused. Parliamentarian artillery breached the walls near St Mary's church, and on 11 September the storming parties broke in after two assaults. Cromwell ordered that no quarter be given to those in arms: Aston was killed (legend says beaten to death with his own wooden leg), defenders who took refuge in Millmount Fort and St Peter's church tower were killed or burned out, and executions continued into the following days. Perhaps 2,700 soldiers and an unknown number of townspeople died.

Interpretation

Cromwell justified the massacre as a "righteous judgment of God" that would prevent further bloodshed by terrifying other garrisons into surrender — a strategy that worked at several towns but failed at others. Under the conventions of 17th-century warfare, a garrison that refused a summons and was taken by storm had forfeited quarter, though the killing of civilians and prisoners after resistance ended was controversial even at the time. Drogheda remains one of the most notorious episodes of the Wars of the Three Kingdoms in Irish historical memory.
