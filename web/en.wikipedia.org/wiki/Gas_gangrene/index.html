Gas gangrene, also known as clostridial myonecrosis, is a rapidly progressive, life-threatening bacterial infection of muscle tissue, most often caused by the gas-producing bacterium Clostridium perfringens. It typically develops after traumatic injuries or surgery when clostridial spores contaminate deep wounds with poor blood supply.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually begin within 6–48 hours of injury. Early features include sudden, severe pain out of proportion to the wound, swelling, and a feeling of heaviness in the affected part. The skin may become pale, then bronze, purple or reddish-black, with blood-filled blisters and a foul-smelling discharge. Gas produced by the bacteria accumulates in the tissues and can be felt as crepitus (a crackling sensation) and seen on X-rays. Systemic features — fever, rapid heart rate, low blood pressure, confusion and kidney failure — reflect the effects of potent clostridial exotoxins, especially alpha toxin, which destroys cell membranes and blood cells.

Causes and risk factors

C. perfringens causes about 80–90% of cases; other species include C. novyi, C. septicum and C. histolyticum. Spores are widespread in soil and the human gut. Risk factors include deep, crushed or contaminated wounds, compound fractures, bowel or gallbladder surgery, diabetes, peripheral vascular disease and immunosuppression. C. septicum can cause spontaneous gas gangrene in patients with colon cancer or neutropenia.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is primarily clinical and should not wait for tests. Imaging may show gas dissecting along muscle planes; Gram stain of wound fluid typically shows large Gram-positive rods with a striking absence of white blood cells, and blood cultures may grow the organism. Surgical exploration reveals grey, non-contractile, necrotic muscle.

Treatment

Gas gangrene is a surgical emergency. Treatment consists of immediate, aggressive surgical debridement of all necrotic tissue — amputation is sometimes unavoidable — together with high-dose intravenous antibiotics (typically penicillin plus clindamycin, the latter to suppress toxin production) and intensive supportive care. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy is used as an adjunct in some centres. Even with prompt treatment, mortality is commonly in the range of 20–40%, and approaches 100% for untreated truncal or spontaneous cases.

History

Gas gangrene was a dreaded complication of battlefield wounds in the First World War, where heavily manured soil and delayed wound care produced high rates of infection; improvements in early wound excision, blood transfusion and antibiotics greatly reduced its incidence in later conflicts and civilian practice.
