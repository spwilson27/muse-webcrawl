Great Mississippi Flood of 1927

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A flooded street in Arkansas during the Great Mississippi Flood of 1927.

The Great Mississippi Flood of 1927 was the most destructive river flood in the history of the United States, with 27,000 square miles (70,000 km²) inundated to depths of up to 30 feet (9.1 m) in the Lower Mississippi Valley. The uninflated cost of the damage was estimated at about $400 million. Heavy rains across the central United States in the summer of 1926 and into 1927 swelled the Mississippi River and its tributaries beyond the capacity of the levee system.

Course of the flood

Levees began failing in the spring of 1927. The most famous break occurred at Mounds Landing, Mississippi, on April 21, 1927, releasing a torrent that flooded the Mississippi Delta. To save New Orleans, officials deliberately dynamited the levee at Caernarvon, Louisiana, flooding St. Bernard and Plaquemines parishes. Much of the Delta remained under water for months, and parts of Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Tennessee, Illinois, Missouri, and Kentucky were inundated.

Human impact

Official counts recorded about 246 deaths, though later estimates run higher, and some 700,000 people were displaced. African Americans, who made up the majority of the Delta's population, were disproportionately affected: many were confined to Red Cross relief camps on the levees and in some places were forced at gunpoint to work on levee repairs. The disaster and its racially unequal relief effort accelerated the Great Migration of Black southerners to northern cities.

Response and legacy

Secretary of Commerce Herbert Hoover directed the federal relief effort, raising his national profile ahead of his successful 1928 presidential campaign. The flood led directly to the Flood Control Act of 1928, which committed the federal government to a comprehensive system of levees, floodways, and spillways — the "Project Flood" design — and made flood control a federal rather than purely local responsibility. The event remains a touchstone in American cultural memory, commemorated in blues songs such as "When the Levee Breaks."
