Eteocypriot was a pre-Indo-European language spoken on the island of Cyprus in the Iron Age. The name, modelled on Eteocretan, means "true Cypriot". It is known from a small corpus of inscriptions written in the Cypriot syllabary, which was deciphered in the nineteenth century because it was also used to write an archaic dialect of Greek. Although the script can be read, the language itself remains only partially understood and has resisted classification.

Corpus

Most Eteocypriot inscriptions come from Amathus on the south coast of Cyprus, a city that preserved its non-Greek language and cults far longer than the island's Greek-speaking kingdoms. The most important text is a bilingual Eteocypriot–Greek epitaph from Amathus (c. 600 BC), which allows a few words and grammatical features to be identified: the Greek portion dedicates the monument to the Nymphs on behalf of Ariston, son of Aristonax. About a dozen further short inscriptions, mostly funerary and dedicatory, are known.

Known features

From the bilingual and recurring formulae, scholars have identified probable words for "in", "and", personal names, and an ending -o-ti associated with verbs, but no convincing translation of a full sentence exists. Proposed connections with Eteocretan, with the languages of Anatolia, or with Lemnian have not won general acceptance, and Eteocypriot is usually treated as an unclassified isolate, a survival of the languages spoken on Cyprus before Greek settlement in the Late Bronze Age.

Decline

As the Greek-speaking kingdoms and later the Hellenistic world absorbed the island, Eteocypriot contracted to Amathus and disappeared; the latest inscriptions date to roughly the third century BC. The city itself was later said by Greek authors to have been founded by autochthonous inhabitants, matching its linguistic conservatism.
