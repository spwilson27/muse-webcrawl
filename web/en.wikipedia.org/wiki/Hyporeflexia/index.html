Hyporeflexia

Hyporeflexia refers to below-normal or absent reflexes. It is a sign found on neurological examination, generally indicating a problem in the reflex arc — in the sensory or motor nerves, the spinal interneurons, the lower motor neurons, or the muscle itself. It is the opposite of hyperreflexia, the exaggerated reflexes associated with upper motor neuron lesions.

Causes

Causes of hyporeflexia include peripheral neuropathies (for example diabetic neuropathy), Guillain–Barré syndrome, in which symmetrically reduced or absent reflexes accompany ascending weakness, nerve root compression by a prolapsed intervertebral disc, motor neuron disease, hypothyroidism (classically with a delayed relaxation phase), electrolyte disturbances such as hypokalemia and hypermagnesemia, and spinal shock following acute spinal cord injury. Absent reflexes throughout — areflexia — may also accompany tabes dorsalis and certain hereditary neuropathies such as Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease.

Assessment

Deep tendon reflexes are graded on a scale from 0 (absent) to 4+ (hyperactive with clonus); 1+ denotes a diminished response. Reflexes tested routinely include the biceps, triceps, brachioradialis, patellar (knee), and Achilles (ankle) jerks, and reinforcement manoeuvres such as the Jendrassik maneuver may be used to elicit a response before a reflex is declared absent. The pattern of reflex loss, combined with the distribution of weakness and sensory findings, helps localise the lesion.
