Powder coating

Powder coating is a dry finishing process in which a finely ground pigment-and-resin powder is applied to a surface — most commonly metal — and then cured under heat to form a hard, durable skin. Unlike conventional liquid paint, it contains no solvent: the powder is usually sprayed on using an electrostatic gun that gives the particles an electric charge so they adhere to the grounded workpiece, which is then baked in a curing oven (typically around 200 °C) so the powder melts, flows and chemically cross-links.

Powder coating
Process typeDry surface finishing
ApplicationElectrostatic spray deposition; fluidised bed
CuringHeat, typically 180–200 °C for about 10–20 minutes
Common resinsEpoxy, polyester, polyurethane, acrylic, hybrids
UsesAppliances, automotive parts, aluminium extrusions, bicycles, outdoor furniture

History

The process was developed in the 1940s and 1950s — early fluidised-bed methods were pioneered in Germany by Erwin Gemmer — and spread through industry from the 1960s with the introduction of electrostatic spray guns. It grew rapidly because it emits virtually no volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and overspray can be collected and reused, giving transfer efficiencies much higher than liquid paint.

Properties

Powder coatings produce thicker films in a single coat than liquid paint without runs or sags, and are generally tougher and more resistant to chipping, scratching, corrosion and fading. Thermoset powders (epoxy, polyester and hybrids) cross-link irreversibly during curing; thermoplastic powders (such as nylon and PVC) simply melt and solidify. Limitations include difficulty achieving very thin films, colour-change cleaning in production, and the need for substrates that withstand curing temperatures — though low-bake and UV-cure powders extend the process to wood and MDF.
