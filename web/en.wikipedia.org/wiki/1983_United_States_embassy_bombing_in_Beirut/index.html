The 1983 United States embassy bombing in Beirut was a suicide car bombing on April 18, 1983, against the United States Embassy in West Beirut, Lebanon. A van packed with explosives detonated at the front of the building, collapsing much of its central facade and killing 63 people, including 17 Americans. Among the dead were Central Intelligence Agency officers, including Near East analyst Robert C. Ames.

Context and responsibility

The attack occurred during the Lebanese Civil War and after the multinational force, including United States Marines, had deployed to Beirut following the 1982 Israeli invasion. A group calling itself the Islamic Jihad Organization claimed responsibility. United States investigations and later court findings attributed the operation to Hezbollah acting with Iranian and Syrian support, conclusions Iran has denied.

Aftermath

The bombing was a precursor to the October 1983 barracks bombings against American and French peacekeepers in Beirut. It led to major changes in United States diplomatic security, including setback standards, hardened embassy construction and the Inman Report reforms. Families of victims later pursued litigation against Iran in United States courts over state sponsorship of terrorism.
