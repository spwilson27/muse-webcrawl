Shugendō (Japanese: 修験道, "the way of ascetic training") is a highly syncretic ascetic tradition that originated in Heian Japan, blending elements of local Shinto mountain worship, Esoteric Buddhism (especially the Shingon and Tendai schools), Taoism, and Onmyōdō. Its practitioners, known as yamabushi or shugenja, undertake rigorous physical and spiritual training in sacred mountains in order to attain supernatural powers, enlightenment, and closeness to the mountain deities (kami) and buddhas.

Yamabushi in their distinctive dress, including the small black cap (tokin) and conch-shell trumpet (horagai).

History

Shugendō is traditionally traced to the semi-legendary 7th-century ascetic En no Gyōja (En no Ozunu), said to have founded the practice on Mount Katsuragi and Mount Ōmine. During the Heian period (794–1185), mountain asceticism flourished as aristocrats and monks retreated to sacred peaks. By the medieval period, Shugendō had organised into two main branches: the Honzan-ha, affiliated with the Tendai temple of Shōgo-in in Kyoto, and the Tōzan-ha, affiliated with the Shingon temple of Daigo-ji.

The tradition was suppressed during the Meiji period, when the government separated Shinto and Buddhism (shinbutsu bunri) and banned Shugendō in 1872 as a "superstitious" mixture of religions. After the Second World War, religious freedom allowed its revival, and today yamabushi groups again conduct mountain austerities, and the sacred sites and pilgrimage routes of the Kii Mountain Range, including Ōmine, were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2004.

Practices

Core practices include mountain pilgrimage (mineiri), waterfall austerities (takigyō), fire rituals (goma), fasting, chanting of sutras and mantras, and dangerous feats such as traversing steep ridges. The Dewa Sanzan mountains in Yamagata Prefecture — Haguro, Gassan, and Yudono — remain major centres, famous for the autumn peak (aki no mine) retreat and for the sokushinbutsu tradition of self-mummification practised by some ascetics in earlier centuries.
