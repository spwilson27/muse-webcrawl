Mani (Middle Persian: Mānī; c. 216 – c. 274 CE) was an Iranian prophet and the founder of Manichaeism, a religion that was once widespread across Eurasia, from the Roman Empire in the west to China in the east. Mani presented himself as the final prophet in a line that included Zoroaster, the Buddha, and Jesus, and his teachings combined elements of Zoroastrianism, Christianity, and Buddhism.

Life

Mani was born in the village of Mardinu, near Seleucia-Ctesiphon in Babylonia, then part of the Parthian Empire. His father, Pātik (Pattug), was from Ecbatana, and his mother was of Parthian royal stock. At the age of four, Mani joined with his father a religious community known in later sources as the Elchasaites, a Jewish-Christian baptist sect living in southern Mesopotamia.

According to his own account, Mani received revelations from a heavenly "Twin" (Syriac: taumā), first at the age of twelve and again at twenty-four, when the Twin called him to leave the sect and proclaim his own message publicly. Mani travelled widely: he journeyed to India, where he encountered Buddhism, and then toured Iran, preaching at the courts of the early Sasanian kings. He was received favourably by Shapur I, to whom he dedicated his only work written in Middle Persian, the Shabuhragan.

Imprisonment and death

Under Bahram I, and under pressure from the Zoroastrian high priest Kartir, Mani was arrested. After a period of imprisonment he died in Gundeshapur around 274 CE. Manichaean tradition remembered his death as a martyrdom analogous to the crucifixion of Jesus, and the chains in which he died were venerated by his followers.

Teachings

Mani taught a cosmic dualism between two eternal principles: the Kingdom of Light, ruled by the Father of Greatness, and the Kingdom of Darkness, ruled by the King of Darkness. A catastrophic invasion of the realm of light by darkness led to the creation of the material world as a mechanism for rescuing particles of light trapped within it. Human beings, containing imprisoned light in their souls, could advance the cosmic liberation through knowledge (gnosis), asceticism, and ritual.

Writings

Mani was unusual among ancient prophets in that he wrote down his own teachings in canonical books, most of them in Syriac, an eastern Aramaic dialect. His works include the Living Gospel, the Treasure of Life, the Pragmateia, the Book of Mysteries, the Book of Giants, letters, and psalms. He was also a painter, and his picture-book, the Arzhang, used images to teach his doctrine to the illiterate.

Legacy

Manichaeism spread rapidly along the Silk Road. It was persecuted in the Roman Empire and in Sasanian Iran, but it survived for over a millennium in Central Asia and China, for a time becoming the state religion of the Uyghur Khaganate. Saint Augustine of Hippo was a hearer of the Manichaeans for nine years before his conversion to Christianity, and his later writings against them preserve much information about the faith.
