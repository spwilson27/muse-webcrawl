An aqueduct in water-supply engineering is any artificial channel, pipeline or tunnel built to convey water from a remote source — a reservoir, river or spring — to a city, town or irrigation district. Water-supply aqueducts typically flow by gravity along a carefully graded route, supplemented by pumping where terrain requires.

History

The qanat systems of ancient Persia, the aqueducts of Rome and the great canal-and-tunnel works of the 19th and 20th centuries all served water supply. New York City's Catskill and Delaware aqueducts, and Los Angeles's aqueduct from the Owens Valley (1913), are prominent modern examples.

Design

Designers balance gradient, friction losses and water quality: covered or piped sections protect drinking water from contamination, while open canals suit irrigation. Inverted siphons carry water across depressions under pressure.
