Persian architecture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (article title: Iranian architecture)


Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, Isfahan

Iranian architecture or Persian architecture is the architecture of Iran and of parts of the wider Persian cultural sphere, with a history extending from the Elamite and Achaemenid periods to the present. It is characterised by structural ingenuity, rich ornament — especially tilework and stucco — and building types adapted to an arid climate, including the iwan, the dome on squinches, windcatchers (badgir), qanats and courtyard gardens (the Persian garden).

Pre-Islamic architecture

Achaemenid architecture (c. 550–330 BC) is represented by the palaces of Pasargadae, Persepolis and Susa, with their columned halls (apadana), rock-cut royal tombs and monumental stairways. Parthian builders developed the iwan — a vaulted hall open on one side — and Sasanian architecture added huge brick domes and vaults, as at Firuzabad, the Taq Kasra at Ctesiphon with its record-breaking single-span iwan, and Sarvestan. Sasanian techniques of transitioning from square chamber to dome using squinches were foundational for later Islamic architecture.

Islamic periods

After the Arab conquest, Persian builders created distinctive mosque forms: the four-iwan plan around a courtyard, minarets, and elaborate muqarnas (stalactite vaulting). The Seljuk period produced masterpieces such as the Jameh Mosque of Isfahan. Mongol Ilkhanid and Timurid architecture raised dome-building to new heights — the turquoise double-shelled dome of Soltaniyeh and the mosques and mausoleums of Samarkand. Under the Safavids (1501–1736), Isfahan was rebuilt as a showcase capital with the Shah Mosque, Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque and the palaces of Naqsh-e Jahan Square, famed for their seven-colour tile mosaic.

Vernacular and later architecture

Desert cities such as Yazd developed windcatcher towers, ice houses and qanat-fed cisterns for passive cooling. Qajar architecture introduced European influences and mirror-work palaces such as Golestan Palace in Tehran. In the 20th and 21st centuries, Iranian architects have combined modern construction with traditional forms; major monuments include the Azadi Tower in Tehran.
