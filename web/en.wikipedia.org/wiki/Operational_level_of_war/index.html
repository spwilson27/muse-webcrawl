Operational level of war

The operational level of war (also called the operational level) is the level of command and planning in warfare that sits between the strategic level and the tactical level. At the operational level, commanders design, organize, integrate and conduct campaigns and major operations to achieve strategic objectives within theatres or other operational areas.

Description

The strategic level concerns the overall political aims of a war and the allocation of national resources. The tactical level concerns individual battles and engagements. The operational level links the two: it translates strategic guidance into a sequence of tactical actions — a campaign — whose cumulative effect achieves the strategic objective. Its intellectual discipline is known as operational art.

History

The level was first conceptualized in Soviet military theory in the 1920s, and was formally incorporated into United States doctrine in the 1982 and 1986 editions of the Army's FM 100-5 Operations manual, influenced by the AirLand Battle concept. NATO doctrine likewise recognizes the operational level, typically embodied by a Joint Force Command.

Criticism

Some theorists argue that the operational level is an artificial construct, or that it has encouraged commanders to think in terms of large formations at the expense of strategy. Others contend it remains essential for coordinating modern joint operations over vast distances.
