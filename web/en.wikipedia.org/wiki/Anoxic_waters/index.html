Anoxic waters

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anoxic waters are areas of seawater, freshwater or groundwater that are depleted of dissolved oxygen. The condition is generally a consequence of strong stratification that prevents oxygenated surface water from mixing downward, combined with the consumption of oxygen by decomposing organic matter. Waters with low but nonzero oxygen are termed hypoxic; where sulfide accumulates, they are termed euxinic.

Natural examples

The largest permanently anoxic basin on Earth is the Black Sea, whose waters below roughly 150–200 metres contain no oxygen and abundant hydrogen sulfide. Similar natural anoxia occurs in the deep Baltic Sea deeps, the Cariaco Basin off Venezuela, silled fjords such as Framvaren in Norway, and in meromictic lakes whose deep layers never mix.

Human influence

Nutrient pollution (eutrophication) from fertiliser runoff and sewage has greatly expanded seasonally anoxic "dead zones" in coastal seas, including the northern Gulf of Mexico, Chesapeake Bay and the Baltic. Warming waters hold less oxygen and stratify more strongly, and global ocean oxygen content has measurably declined since the mid-twentieth century. In anoxic sediments, denitrification and sulfate reduction replace aerobic respiration, altering nutrient cycles.
