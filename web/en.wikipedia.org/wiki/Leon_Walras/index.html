Marie-Esprit-Léon Walras (16 December 1834 – 5 January 1910) was a French mathematical economist and Georgist. He formulated the marginal theory of value (independently of William Stanley Jevons and Carl Menger) and pioneered the development of general equilibrium theory. Joseph Schumpeter later called him "the greatest of all economists" for his system of general equilibrium, and his work laid the foundations of modern mathematical economics.

Life

Walras was born in Évreux, the son of Auguste Walras, a school administrator and amateur economist from whom he inherited ideas about value and land reform. After failing twice to enter the École Polytechnique for want of mathematical preparation, he studied mining engineering, then drifted through journalism, novel-writing, railway clerking and banking before turning seriously to economics. In 1870 he was appointed to the newly created chair of political economy at the University of Lausanne in Switzerland — the beginnings of the Lausanne School — where he remained until his retirement in 1892, when he was succeeded by Vilfredo Pareto. He died at Clarens, near Montreux, in 1910.

Work

Walras's masterwork, Éléments d'économie politique pure (Elements of Pure Economics, published in two parts in 1874 and 1877), presented the first comprehensive mathematical model of an entire economy: systems of simultaneous equations for exchange, production, capital formation and money, in which prices adjust so that supply equals demand in every market at once. To explain how such equilibrium might be reached, he proposed tâtonnement, a hypothetical "groping" process of price revision analogous to an auction. From his equations follows Walras's law: the sum of the values of excess demands across all markets is zero, so if all markets but one are in equilibrium, the last must be as well.

Walras built value theory on rareté — marginal utility — arriving at the theory almost simultaneously with Jevons and Menger in the "marginal revolution" of the 1870s. Alongside his pure theory he wrote applied and social economics, advocating land nationalisation (following Henry George) as the just means of financing the state. Long read mainly by specialists, his reputation grew enormously in the 20th century through Hicks's Value and Capital and the Arrow–Debreu formalisation of general equilibrium.
