IBM Personal Computer

The IBM Personal Computer (model 5150, commonly known as the IBM PC) is the first microcomputer released in the IBM PC model line and the basis for the IBM PC compatible de facto standard. Released on August 12, 1981, it was created by a team of engineers and designers at International Business Machines (IBM), directed by William C. Lowe and Philip Don Estridge in Boca Raton, Florida.

The machine was based on open architecture and a handful of third-party peripherals. Over time, expansion cards and software technology increased to support it. The PC had a substantial influence on the personal computer market; the specifications of the original IBM PC became a de facto standard for the industry.

History

Prior to the 1980s, IBM had largely been known as a provider of mainframe computers and minicomputers. The company sought to enter the personal computer market that was being led by companies such as Apple, Commodore, and Tandy. In July 1980, IBM representatives met for the first time with Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates to talk about writing an operating system for the new computer.

The team decided to use off-the-shelf components and an open architecture, allowing third-party suppliers to produce expansion cards and software. The operating system, PC DOS, was supplied by Microsoft; a version called MS-DOS was sold by Microsoft for use on compatible machines.

Specifications

The original IBM PC used an Intel 8088 microprocessor running at 4.77 MHz, with 16 KB of RAM expandable to 256 KB on the motherboard. Storage was provided by optional 5.25-inch floppy disk drives, and the machine could be connected to a color or monochrome display. Prices started at $1,565 for a basic configuration.

Legacy

The success of the IBM PC led to a large market of IBM PC compatibles produced by other manufacturers. The architecture's descendants — modern "PCs" running Microsoft Windows or Linux on x86-64 processors — remain the dominant form of personal computer.
