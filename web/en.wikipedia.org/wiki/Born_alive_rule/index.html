Born alive rule

The born alive rule is a common law legal principle, dating from medieval England, under which a fetus can only be the victim of a homicide (such as murder or manslaughter) if it is first born alive — that is, completely expelled from the mother and showing signs of independent life, such as breathing or a heartbeat — and subsequently dies from injuries inflicted before or during birth.

Development

The rule arose from the practical difficulty of proving that a fetus had been alive in the womb and that an unlawful act, rather than natural causes, had caused its death. Historically, killing a fetus in utero was treated as a lesser offence or as an offence against the mother. Many jurisdictions have modified or abolished the rule by statute, enacting feticide laws that criminalise the killing of an unborn child at some or all stages of gestation, while the rule continues to matter in jurisdictions that retain common law homicide definitions.
