College of Cardinals

Purpose: Elect the Pope; advise the Pope
Head: Dean of the College of Cardinals
Church: Roman Catholic Church

The College of Cardinals, formerly the Sacred College, is the body of all cardinals of the Catholic Church. Cardinals are created by the Pope, normally from among bishops, and serve as his principal advisers. The College's most distinctive function is to elect a new pope in a conclave when the Holy See becomes vacant.

History

The College developed in the 11th–12th centuries as the cardinals — clergy of Rome in three orders: cardinal bishops, cardinal priests and cardinal deacons — took over papal elections, a right formalised in 1059 and restricted to cardinals in 1179. Rules for the conclave evolved, notably with Ubi periculum (1274).

Composition

Since Paul VI and John Paul II, the number of cardinal electors — those under 80 years old entitled to vote in a conclave — has been set at a maximum of 120, though popes have at times exceeded it. Cardinals wear scarlet, symbolising willingness to shed blood for the faith, and are addressed as "Eminence". The College is headed by a Dean elected by the cardinal bishops and approved by the Pope.

Functions

Besides electing the pope, cardinals meet in consistories, head dicasteries of the Roman Curia, and govern the Church during a vacancy through limited provisions of apostolic constitutions such as Universi Dominici gregis.
