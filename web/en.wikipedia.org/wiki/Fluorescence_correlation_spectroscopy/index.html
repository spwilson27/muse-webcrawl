Fluorescence correlation spectroscopy

Fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS) is a statistical analysis method that uses the fluctuations of fluorescence intensity to yield information about the processes causing those fluctuations. It is a powerful tool for measuring diffusion, concentration, and molecular interactions at the single-molecule level in very small volumes.

Confocal FCS measurement volume

Principle

A focused laser excites fluorescent molecules inside a tiny observation volume, typically set up with a confocal microscope so that the volume is on the order of a femtolitre. As molecules diffuse in and out of the volume, the detected fluorescence fluctuates. The autocorrelation function of the intensity trace decays with a characteristic time related to how long molecules dwell in the volume; fitting this curve yields the diffusion coefficient and the average number of molecules, hence their concentration. Additional components in the fit reveal binding reactions, triplet-state blinking, or flow.

History

FCS was introduced in the early 1970s by Douglas Magde, Elliot Elson, and Watt W. Webb at Cornell University. It saw a renaissance in the 1990s with confocal detection, sensitive avalanche photodiodes, and brighter, more stable dyes, making it practical for measurements in living cells.

Applications

FCS is used to study protein–protein interactions, aggregation (relevant to amyloid diseases), membrane dynamics, and molecular concentrations in cells. Related techniques include fluorescence cross-correlation spectroscopy (FCCS), which uses two colours to detect co-diffusion of two labelled species.
