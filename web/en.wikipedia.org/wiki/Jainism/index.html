Jainism is an ancient Indian religion teaching a path to liberation through non-violence toward all living beings. Its followers revere 24 enlightened teachers (Tirthankaras), the last being Mahavira, a contemporary of the Buddha. Jains hold that the universe is eternal and uncreated, that every soul is potentially divine but weighed down by karma — conceived as a subtle material — and that liberation comes by shedding karma through asceticism and the 'three jewels': right faith, right knowledge, right conduct.

Practice

The supreme vow is ahimsa: Jains are strict vegetarians, avoid harming even insects (monks may wear mouth-cloths and sweep their path), and have excelled in commerce and scholarship partly through avoiding violent trades. The doctrine of anekantavada ('many-sidedness') holds that truth is too complex for any single viewpoint — the parable of the blind men and an elephant is Jain in origin. The faith split into two main sects, Digambara ('sky-clad', whose monks own nothing, not even robes) and Svetambara ('white-clad'), and its temples at Mount Abu and Ranakpur are masterpieces of marble carving.
