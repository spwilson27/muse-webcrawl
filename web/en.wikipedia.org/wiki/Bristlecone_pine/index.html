The bristlecone pines are three closely related species of pine — the Great Basin bristlecone pine (Pinus longaeva), the Rocky Mountain bristlecone pine (Pinus aristata) and the foxtail pine (Pinus balfouriana) — that grow in isolated groves on high, arid mountain slopes in the western United States. They are among the longest-lived organisms on Earth: individual Great Basin bristlecones have been dated at nearly 5,000 years old.

Description

Bristlecone pines are small to medium trees with short needles borne in bundles of five; the cones carry the slender bristles that give the group its name. In the harshest sites the trees grow extremely slowly, with dense, resinous, decay-resistant wood; often only a narrow strip of bark remains alive, feeding a few branches on an otherwise dead, sculpturally twisted trunk.

Longevity

The tree known as Methuselah, a Great Basin bristlecone in the White Mountains of California, was dated by core samples to about 4,789 years of age, and another unnamed tree in the same region has been reported at over 5,000 years. Because their ring sequences extend back millennia, bristlecone pines provided the calibration backbone for radiocarbon dating through the work of A. E. Douglass and the Laboratory of Tree-Ring Research, and climatologists use their rings to reconstruct past climate.

Conservation

The groves are protected in places such as Inyo National Forest (Ancient Bristlecone Pine Forest), Great Basin National Park and Cedar Breaks. Threats include the introduced fungal disease white pine blister rust, mountain pine beetles, and climate change.
