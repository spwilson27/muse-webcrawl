Quickstep

The quickstep is a light-hearted dance of the standard ballroom dances. The movement is fast and powerful, flowing and sprinkled with syncope. It is danced to music at about 50–52 bars per minute (around 200 beats per minute) in 4/4 time, and is one of the five International Style Standard dances, alongside the slow waltz, tango, slow foxtrot, and Viennese waltz.

History

The quickstep evolved in the 1920s in England from a combination of the foxtrot, Charleston, shag, Peabody, and one-step. When American jazz and ragtime bands toured England after the First World War, British dancers adapted the fast foxtrot (also called the "quick-time foxtrot") and blended it with the Charleston. In 1924 the dance was standardised, and by 1927 it had taken the separate name quickstep. Famous early exponents included the dancers who won championships in London in the 1920s, and the dance gained a broader audience through music-hall and later television performances.

Technique and figures

Quickstep is characterised by a light, gliding movement up on the toes, hops, runs, kicks, flicks, and rapid direction changes, while the couple maintains the closed ballroom hold and body contact. Basic figures include the quarter turn to right, natural turn, progressive chassé, forward lock step, running finish, tipple chassé, V6, and cross swivel. At advanced levels, dancers add pepper-pot runs, six quick run, and syncopated jumps.

Because of its speed, the dance demands good stamina and floorcraft: couples must travel fast around the floor while avoiding collisions. Music is typically upbeat big-band or swing numbers, such as "It Don't Mean a Thing (If It Ain't Got That Swing)".

In popular culture

The quickstep features in televised ballroom competitions such as Strictly Come Dancing and Dancing with the Stars, where it is notable for lifts being prohibited (unlike showdance) and for contestants' struggles with its speed.
