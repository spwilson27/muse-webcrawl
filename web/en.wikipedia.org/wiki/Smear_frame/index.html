Smear frame

A smear frame is a form of motion blur used in hand-drawn animation. It is a single drawing, occupying one or a few frames of film, in which a moving object or character is distorted and elongated along the path of a very fast action so that, when projected at full speed, the distortion reads to the viewer as a blur of motion.

Technique

Traditional animation is shot at 24 frames per second, and large movements between successive drawings can appear strobing or disjointed. To convey speed, animators draw a smear: the subject is stretched, duplicated, or trailed with multiple limbs or after-images within one drawing. Because the frame is on screen for only a fraction of a second, audiences perceive fluid, rapid motion rather than the bizarre shapes visible when the film is paused. Smear frames were widely used in the theatrical cartoons of the 1930s–1950s, notably in the work of animators at Warner Bros. and MGM such as Chuck Jones and Tex Avery.

Variants

Related techniques include multiple images (drawing the subject in several positions in one frame), long smears that stretch a character across the screen, and dry-brush effects in painted media. In modern 2D and 3D animation, smear frames continue to be used — sometimes created by deforming a computer-generated model for a single frame — to preserve the snappy feel of classical animation. Video games likewise employ smears in sprite and model animation.

Reception

Once noticed mainly by animation professionals, smear frames became a popular subject of online interest as frame-by-frame viewing grew common; collections of paused smear frames from classic cartoons circulate widely, celebrated as examples of the draftsmanship and exaggeration of the golden age of animation.
