Burden of proof (philosophy)

Burden of proof
Latin: Onus probandi
Field: Logic, epistemology, argumentation theory

In philosophy and argumentation, the burden of proof (Latin: onus probandi) is the obligation resting on a party who advances a claim to provide sufficient warrant for it. The burden is generally held to lie on the person making a positive or extraordinary claim rather than on those who doubt it; a claim asserted without evidence may be dismissed without evidence, a maxim popularized as "Hitchens's razor."

Shifting the burden

The fallacy of shifting the burden of proof occurs when a claimant demands that others disprove the claim instead of supporting it themselves — for example, an appeal to ignorance (argumentum ad ignorantiam), in which a proposition is treated as true because it has not been proven false, or vice versa.

Related ideas

Bertrand Russell's teapot — an undetectable teapot orbiting the Sun — illustrates that the inability to disprove an unfalsifiable claim does not oblige belief in it. Carl Sagan's maxim that "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" expresses a related standard. The concept parallels the legal burden of proof, where the prosecution in criminal cases must establish guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, though the philosophical burden is informal and context-dependent rather than codified.
