Proteomics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Proteomics is the large-scale study of proteins — their identities, quantities, modifications and interactions — within a cell, tissue or organism (its proteome). Because proteins carry out most cellular functions and are the targets of most drugs, proteomics complements Genomics: a genome predicts the proteins an organism can make, while the proteome shows which are actually present, where, and in what state.

Methods

The central tool is mass spectrometry, which identifies proteins from their fragments after digestion into peptides. Older two-dimensional gel electrophoresis separates proteins by charge and size, and protein microarrays and affinity methods measure many proteins at once. Post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation — central to signalling — are a major focus.

Applications

Proteomics is used to find disease biomarkers in blood, to understand how cells respond to drugs, and to characterise the protein machinery of pathogens. It remains technically harder than genomics because proteins cannot be amplified like DNA and span a huge range of abundances.
