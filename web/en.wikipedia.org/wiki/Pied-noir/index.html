Pied-noir

The pieds-noirs (French: [pje nwaʁ]; singular pied-noir; literally "black feet") were the European settlers — predominantly of French, Spanish, Italian and Maltese descent — who lived in French Algeria from the start of French colonisation in 1830 until Algerian independence in 1962. The term is also used for their descendants who resettled in France after independence.

At independence the pieds-noirs numbered roughly one million, about ten percent of Algeria's population. The vast majority left Algeria in 1962 during and after the Algerian War, in an exodus often summarised by the slogan la valise ou le cercueil — "the suitcase or the coffin".

Etymology

The origin of pied-noir is disputed. Proposed derivations include the black boots worn by French soldiers, the blackened feet of settlers who worked in marshy land or trod grapes, and the dark footwear of coal stokers on steamships. The term was little used before the 1950s, when it became common during the Algerian War.

History

French colonisation began with the invasion of Algiers in 1830. European settlement was encouraged by the French state, which confiscated land and offered it to colonists. Settlers arrived not only from metropolitan France but also from Spain (especially Alicante and the Balearic Islands), Italy, Malta and elsewhere in the Mediterranean. Under the Crémieux Decree of 1870, Algeria's Jewish population received French citizenship, and the decree of 1889 granted automatic citizenship to children of European foreigners born in Algeria, forging a single French-Algerian European population.

The pieds-noirs were socially diverse — wealthy landowners coexisted with urban workers who were often poorer than metropolitan French — but shared a strong attachment to French Algeria. During the Algerian War (1954–1962) most supported Algérie française; some joined the militant OAS. After the Évian Accords of March 1962 and the independence referendum, nearly the entire population departed within months, most arriving in France as rapatriés (repatriates).

Repatriation and legacy

The sudden arrival of about a million repatriates in 1962 reshaped southern France in particular, stimulating growth in cities such as Marseille, Toulon, Perpignan and Montpellier. Pied-noir associations have campaigned for recognition and compensation, and the memory of French Algeria remains politically charged in France. Notable pieds-noirs include the writer Albert Camus, born in Mondovi, Algeria.
