The Rankine cycle is an idealized thermodynamic cycle describing the process by which certain heat engines, such as steam turbines or reciprocating steam engines, allow mechanical work to be extracted from a fluid as it moves between a heat source and heat sink. The cycle is named after William John Macquorn Rankine, a Scottish polymath professor at Glasgow University.

Heat energy is supplied externally to a closed loop, which usually uses water (in liquid and vapour phase) as the working fluid. Steam power stations following this cycle supply the great majority of the world's electrical energy from fossil, nuclear, geothermal and solar thermal sources.

The four processes

  1. 1 → 2 — Isentropic compression (pump). Liquid water is pumped from low to high pressure. This requires little work because the liquid is nearly incompressible.
  2. 2 → 3 — Constant-pressure heat addition (boiler). The high-pressure liquid enters a boiler where it is heated by an external source, evaporating and (in real plants) being superheated to a dry, high-temperature vapour.
  3. 3 → 4 — Isentropic expansion (turbine). The dry vapour expands through a turbine, generating power. Temperature and pressure fall and the vapour usually becomes a saturated mixture.
  4. 4 → 1 — Constant-pressure heat rejection (condenser). The wet vapour enters a condenser, rejects its latent heat to cooling water or air, and condenses back into liquid water, completing the cycle.

Efficiency and improvements

The thermal efficiency of a real Rankine plant is typically 33–48%, far below the Carnot limit for the same temperatures, mainly because most heat is added at a temperature below the maximum. Efficiency is improved by raising the boiler pressure and superheat temperature, by reheating the steam between turbine stages, and by regeneration, in which steam bled from the turbine pre-heats the feedwater. "Supercritical" and "ultra-supercritical" plants operate above the critical point of water (22.1 MPa, 374 °C) to raise efficiency further.

Variations

Organic Rankine cycle

The organic Rankine cycle (ORC) replaces water with an organic fluid of higher molecular mass and lower boiling point, allowing economical power generation from low-temperature heat sources such as geothermal brines, biomass, industrial waste heat and solar ponds.

Combined cycle

In a combined-cycle gas turbine plant, the hot exhaust of a gas turbine (Brayton cycle) raises steam for a Rankine bottoming cycle, pushing overall efficiencies toward and beyond 60%.
