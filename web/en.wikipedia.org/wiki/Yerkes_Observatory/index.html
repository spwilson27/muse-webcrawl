Yerkes Observatory

Yerkes Observatory is an astronomical observatory located in Williams Bay, Wisconsin, United States. Founded in 1897 by the astronomer George Ellery Hale with funding from the Chicago financier Charles T. Yerkes, it was operated by the University of Chicago for more than a century. The observatory houses the 40-inch (102 cm) Alvan Clark refractor, the largest refracting telescope ever successfully used for astronomical research, and is often called "the birthplace of modern astrophysics".

The Yerkes Observatory

History

George Ellery Hale persuaded Charles Tyson Yerkes, a Chicago streetcar magnate, to fund a great observatory in the early 1890s. The original grant paid for the telescope but not the building, and Hale leveraged further donations to complete the institution. The observatory was dedicated on 21 October 1897. Its 40-inch lens, figured by Alvan Clark & Sons, remains the largest refractor ever built; larger refractors were proposed but never surpassed it in successful operation.

Yerkes became a centre for stellar spectroscopy and the classification of stars. The Morgan–Keenan–Kellman (MKK) spectral classification system was developed there by William Wilson Morgan, Philip Keenan and Edith Kellman. Directors included Hale, Edwin B. Frost, Otto Struve and Gerard Kuiper. Albert Einstein visited the observatory in 1921. The institution also produced The Astrophysical Journal, founded by Hale.

Instruments

Besides the great refractor, the observatory holds a 41-inch (104 cm) reflecting telescope and a 24-inch (61 cm) reflector, along with a historically important collection of some 170,000 photographic plates. The massive refractor's mounting and 27-metre dome were engineering landmarks of their time.

Closure and rebirth

The University of Chicago ceased operations at Yerkes on 1 October 2018 and transferred the property to the Yerkes Future Foundation in 2020. The foundation has restored the buildings and grounds and reopened the observatory for tours, education and events, and the great refractor was returned to operation for public viewing.
