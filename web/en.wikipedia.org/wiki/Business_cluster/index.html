Business cluster

A business cluster is a geographic concentration of interconnected businesses, suppliers, service providers, and associated institutions in a particular field, linked by commonalities and complementarities. The concept was popularised by Michael Porter in his 1990 book The Competitive Advantage of Nations and his subsequent work on the competitive advantage of locations.

Characteristics

Clusters include concentrations of interconnected companies and institutions: final product manufacturers, suppliers of specialised inputs and machinery, financial institutions, firms in related industries, and specialised infrastructure such as universities and trade associations. Porter argued that clusters raise productivity through better access to inputs and employees, improved information flows, complementarities between firms, and the pressure of local rivalry combined with sophisticated local demand.

Examples

Well-known examples include Silicon Valley for information technology, Hollywood for film production, the City of London and Wall Street for finance, the Italian industrial districts for textiles and footwear, and the automotive cluster around Detroit and in southern Germany.

Cluster policy

Governments at national and regional level have adopted cluster-based economic development policies, attempting to strengthen existing concentrations through investment in skills, infrastructure and research. Critics note that clusters are difficult to create from scratch and that policy often simply labels existing agglomerations.
