George Gordon Meade (December 31, 1815 – November 6, 1872) was a United States Army officer and civil engineer best known for defeating Confederate general Robert E. Lee at the Battle of Gettysburg (1863) as commander of the Union Army of the Potomac during the American Civil War. Although criticised at the time for not pursuing Lee's beaten army aggressively, and overshadowed later by Ulysses S. Grant, Meade retained command of the Army of the Potomac through the end of the war.

Early career

Meade was born in Cádiz, Spain, where his father, a wealthy Philadelphia merchant, served as a naval agent. He graduated from West Point in 1835 and served in the artillery and then in the Corps of Topographical Engineers, seeing action in the Mexican–American War. In the 1850s he supervised lighthouse construction on the Atlantic and Gulf coasts — several of his lights still stand — and directed the survey of the Great Lakes.

Civil War

Appointed brigadier general of volunteers in August 1861, Meade commanded a brigade and then a division of Pennsylvania Reserves, fighting through the Peninsula Campaign (where he was wounded at Glendale), Second Bull Run, Antietam, and Fredericksburg — where his division made the only successful Union breakthrough of the day. He commanded V Corps at Chancellorsville. On June 28, 1863, three days before Gettysburg, President Lincoln appointed him to command the Army of the Potomac. At Gettysburg (July 1–3) Meade fought a skilful defensive battle, defeating Lee's invasion of the North and repulsing Pickett's Charge, in what is widely considered the turning point of the war.

Through the autumn 1863 campaigns and, from March 1864, in effective partnership with general-in-chief Grant, Meade led the Army of the Potomac through the Overland Campaign, the Siege of Petersburg, and the Appomattox Campaign that ended the war. A furious temper — soldiers called him "a damned old goggle-eyed snapping turtle" — and quarrels with the press and the Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War clouded his reputation.

Later life

After the war Meade commanded military districts in the South during Reconstruction and served as commissioner of Fairmount Park in Philadelphia. He died there of pneumonia in 1872. A major equestrian statue of Meade stands in Washington, D.C., and Fort George G. Meade in Maryland bears his name.
