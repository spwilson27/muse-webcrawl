Order in Council

An Order in Council is a type of legislation in many countries, especially the Commonwealth realms and other states with Westminster-style government. It is a formal order made by the sovereign (or the governor-general) "in council" — that is, on the advice of the Privy Council or its equivalent executive council — and is used both for executive acts under the royal prerogative and for delegated legislation made under authority granted by an Act of Parliament.

United Kingdom

In the United Kingdom, Orders in Council are made by the King at meetings of the Privy Council, where a quorum of counsellors and the Lord President attend; by convention the monarch acts on ministerial advice. Statutory Orders in Council exercise powers granted by Parliament and form a species of secondary legislation. Prerogative Orders rest on the royal prerogative and cover matters such as the civil service, overseas territories, and letters patent — for example, the 1988 Order in Council relating to GCHQ staff unions, whose legality the courts reviewed in the famous CCSU (GCHQ) case, and orders governing British Overseas Territories, whose lawfulness was tested in the Chagos Archipelago (Bancoult) litigation.

Other realms

In Canada, orders in council are made by the Governor General on the advice of the Queen's (King's) Privy Council for Canada and are the standard instrument for senior appointments, regulations and treaty ratification. Australia achieves the same effect through the Governor-General acting with the advice of the Federal Executive Council, while New Zealand uses Orders in Council extensively under statutes. Orders of Council — made by the Privy Council itself without the sovereign — are a closely related but distinct instrument in British practice.
