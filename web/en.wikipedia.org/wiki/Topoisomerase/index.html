Topoisomerases are enzymes that participate in the overwinding or underwinding of DNA. The winding problem of DNA arises due to the intertwined nature of its double-helical structure. During DNA replication and transcription, DNA becomes overwound ahead of a replication fork; if this torsional stress is not relieved, it will halt the progress of the DNA or RNA polymerases. Topoisomerases relieve this stress by introducing temporary breaks in DNA.

Function

Topoisomerases catalyse the relaxation of supercoiled DNA, the decatenation (unlinking) of intertwined DNA circles after replication, and chromosome condensation and segregation. They act by cutting the phosphate backbone of DNA, passing another segment through the break, and resealing it, thereby changing the linking number of the DNA.

Types

Type I topoisomerases

Type I enzymes cut one strand of the DNA duplex. Type IA enzymes (e.g. bacterial topoisomerase I, encoded by topA) attach to a 5' phosphate and relax negative supercoils, while type IB enzymes (e.g. human topoisomerase I) attach to a 3' phosphate, rotate the cut strand around the intact strand, and can relax both positive and negative supercoils without requiring ATP.

Type II topoisomerases

Type II enzymes cut both strands of one DNA duplex and pass a second duplex through the gap in an ATP-dependent reaction, changing the linking number in steps of two. They include bacterial DNA gyrase — the only topoisomerase that can introduce negative supercoils — and topoisomerase IV, which specialises in decatenation, as well as eukaryotic topoisomerase II.

Clinical significance

Topoisomerases are important drug targets. Antibacterial fluoroquinolones (such as ciprofloxacin) inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV. Anticancer drugs that target human topoisomerases include etoposide and doxorubicin (topoisomerase II) and irinotecan and topotecan (topoisomerase I). These agents stabilise the enzyme–DNA cleavage complex, converting the enzyme into a DNA-damaging poison.

Discovery

The first topoisomerase, E. coli omega protein, was discovered by James C. Wang in 1971. DNA gyrase was described by Gellert and colleagues in 1976.
