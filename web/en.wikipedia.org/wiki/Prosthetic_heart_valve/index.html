Prosthetic heart valve

A prosthetic heart valve is an artificial device implanted in the heart of a patient with valvular heart disease, replacing a damaged native valve that has become too narrowed (stenosis) or leaky (regurgitation) to function. The two main categories are mechanical valves, made of synthetic materials, and bioprosthetic (tissue) valves, made from animal or human tissue.

Types

Mechanical valves have evolved through ball-and-cage, tilting-disc, and bileaflet designs; modern bileaflet valves are made of pyrolytic carbon and are highly durable but require lifelong anticoagulation with warfarin to prevent thrombosis. Bioprosthetic valves—porcine valves or valves fashioned from bovine pericardium—generally avoid long-term anticoagulation but degenerate over time, typically lasting 10 to 20 years.

Insertion

Conventionally, valves are implanted during open-heart surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass. Transcatheter techniques, especially transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), deliver a crimped tissue valve via catheter and have expanded treatment to patients at high surgical risk. Complications of prosthetic valves include thromboembolism, endocarditis, structural deterioration, and patient–prosthesis mismatch.
