Arbeia

Arbeia was a large Roman fort in South Shields, Tyne and Wear, England, now ruined and partly reconstructed as a museum. Situated at the mouth of the River Tyne, it guarded the seaward approach to Hadrian's Wall and served as a maritime supply base for the garrisons along the Wall.

History

A fort was first established on the site around AD 160, and the stone fort visible today dates largely from about AD 200, when it was rebuilt with numerous granaries to store grain for campaigns into Scotland. The name Arbeia is thought to derive from a Latinised form meaning "fort of the Arab troops", reflecting a garrison drawn at one time from boatmen of the Tigris in Mesopotamia. The fort was occupied until the end of Roman rule in Britain in the early 5th century.

Museum

The site has been excavated since the 19th century, producing finds including tombstones of Regina, a British woman married to a Palmyrene soldier, and of Victor, a Moorish freedman. The West Gate, a barrack block and part of the commanding officer's house have been reconstructed on their original foundations. Arbeia Roman Fort and Museum is managed by Tyne & Wear Archives & Museums and forms part of the Frontiers of the Roman Empire World Heritage Site.
