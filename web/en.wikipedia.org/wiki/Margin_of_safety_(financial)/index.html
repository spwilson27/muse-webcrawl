Margin of safety (financial)

Illustration: Intrinsic value, market price, and margin of safety (placeholder image)

Margin of safety is an investment principle in which an investor purchases securities only when their market price is significantly below their intrinsic value. In other words, when the market price is considerably lower than the investor's estimate of intrinsic value, the difference constitutes the margin of safety. The concept was made famous by Benjamin Graham and David Dodd in Security Analysis (1934) and is considered the cornerstone of value investing.

Concept

Graham defined the margin of safety as the difference between a security's intrinsic value and its price, expressed so that the investor is protected against errors in judgment, miscalculation, and bad luck. Because estimates of intrinsic value are inherently imprecise, buying at a substantial discount provides a cushion: even if the investor's estimate proves somewhat optimistic, the purchase can still work out satisfactorily. Graham wrote that the investor who permits himself a margin of safety is protected "against loss in the event of a future decline in net earnings".

Application

Warren Buffett, Graham's student, has described margin of safety as the most important concept in investing; his partner Charlie Munger emphasised buying "a wonderful company at a fair price" while still demanding a discount that compensates for uncertainty. In accounting and break-even analysis, a related term — the margin of safety — measures the difference between actual sales and break-even sales, showing how much sales can fall before a business becomes unprofitable.

Quantitatively, a value investor might require a price of two-thirds or less of estimated intrinsic value before buying. Graham insisted the margin of safety does not guarantee a profitable outcome; it merely improves the odds and limits downside risk when combined with diversification and rational analysis.
