The Salvation Army

Founded: 2 July 1865, London, England
Founders: William Booth and Catherine Booth
Type: Christian church and charitable organisation
Headquarters: London, United Kingdom
Area served: 130+ countries

The Salvation Army (TSA) is a Protestant Christian church and international charitable organisation headquartered in London, England. It is aligned with the Wesleyan-Holiness tradition and is known for its quasi-military structure, its brass bands, and its extensive social services, including homeless shelters, disaster relief, addiction rehabilitation, and charity shops.

History

The organisation was founded in 1865 by Methodist preacher William Booth and his wife Catherine Booth in London's East End, initially as the East London Christian Mission. In 1878 it was renamed The Salvation Army and reorganised on military lines: Booth became its first "General", ministers became "officers", members became "soldiers", and local churches became "corps". Its motto is "Blood and Fire", referring to the blood of Jesus Christ and the fire of the Holy Spirit.

Beliefs and practice

The Salvation Army's doctrine follows mainstream Methodist teaching, emphasising salvation by grace, holiness, and practical service to the needy—summarised in its mission to preach the gospel and to meet human needs "without discrimination". Unusually among churches, it does not observe baptism or the Eucharist as outward sacraments. Officers are ordained ministers, and women have held equal rank since the beginning, Catherine Booth having argued for female ministry.

Charitable work

The Army operates in more than 130 countries and is one of the largest providers of social services in many of them. Its red Christmas kettles, staffed by bell-ringers, are a well-known sight in North America, and its red shield is a familiar emblem on disaster relief operations. It also runs the International Social Justice Commission and campaigns against human trafficking.
