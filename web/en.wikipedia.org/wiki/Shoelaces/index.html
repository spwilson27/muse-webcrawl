Shoelaces

Shoelaces, also called shoestrings (US English) or bootlaces (when longer, for boots), are a system commonly used to secure shoes, boots, and other footwear. They typically consist of a pair of strings or cords, one for each shoe, finished off at both ends with stiff sections known as aglets. Each shoelace typically passes through a series of holes, eyelets, loops, or hooks on either side of the shoe. Loosening the lacing allows the shoe to open wide enough for the foot to be inserted or removed; pulling the lacing tight and tying off the ends secures the foot within the shoe.

History

It is not known precisely when shoelaces were first used; the 5,300-year-old ice mummy Ötzi wore shoes tied with leather laces, and the medieval English word for a lace, "tip", survives in the aglet. A frequently cited patent for the modern shoelace with aglets was granted in England in 1790 to Harvey Kennedy. Archaic footwear such as moccasins and sandals used thongs long before woven laces became standard in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Construction

Traditional laces were made of leather, cotton, hemp, or jute; modern laces are usually woven from synthetic fibers such as nylon or polyester, which are stronger and more abrasion-resistant. Laces may be round or flat in cross-section and come in many lengths, matched to the number of eyelet pairs on the shoe. The aglet — the sheath at the end — is commonly made of plastic or metal and prevents fraying while easing passage through eyelets.

Lacing and tying

Many lacing patterns exist, including criss-cross, straight (bar) lacing, and lattice lacing, chosen for function or display. The standard closure is a reef-knot-based bow; the common "granny knot" variant tends to work loose. Analyses by mathematicians such as John Horton Conway and Burkard Polster have enumerated the millions of mathematically possible ways to lace a shoe with multiple eyelet pairs. A 2017 study by mechanical engineers at UC Berkeley explained why knots come undone: the combination of the swinging motion of walking and the whipping of the free ends gradually loosens the knot.
