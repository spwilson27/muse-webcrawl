Balletto


Title page of Gastoldi's Balletti (1591).

A balletto (plural balletti) is a short, light, dance-like vocal composition that flourished in Italy and England in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. Balletti were typically written for several voices in a simple homophonic style, in two repeated sections, with syllabic "fa-la-la" refrains that invited dancing as well as singing.

History

The form was popularised by Giovanni Giacomo Gastoldi, whose Balletti a cinque voci (1591) — including the well-known "L'innamorato" — became a model across Europe. The collection was reprinted many times and imitated by composers such as Orazio Vecchi and Adriano Banchieri.

In England the balletto was naturalised as the "ballet" by Thomas Morley, whose First Booke of Balletts to Five Voyces (1595) includes pieces such as "Now Is the Month of Maying" and "Sing We and Chant It", modelled directly on Gastoldi. Morley's balletts, along with the related madrigals and canzonets of Thomas Weelkes and John Wilbye, became staples of the English madrigal school.

Characteristics

  • Light, rhythmical, predominantly homophonic textures suited to dancing.
  • Two sections, each repeated, often with "fa-la" refrains.
  • Cheerful, pastoral, or amorous texts rather than the serious poetry of the madrigal proper.
  • Usually for four or five voices, sometimes with instrumental participation.

The word survives in the modern term ballet, which shares the same root in the Italian ballo (dance) and its diminutive balletto ("little dance"), though the staged dance genre developed along a separate path from the vocal form.
