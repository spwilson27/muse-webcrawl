Obsessive-compulsive disorder

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a mental disorder characterized by intrusive, unwanted thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive behaviors or mental acts (compulsions) performed to relieve the distress those thoughts cause. Common themes include contamination and washing, doubts and checking, symmetry and ordering, and taboo thoughts about harm, sex, or religion.

Signs and symptoms

Obsessions are recurrent, persistent thoughts, urges, or images experienced as intrusive and inappropriate, causing marked anxiety. The sufferer responds with compulsions — repetitive behaviors (such as hand washing, ordering, checking) or mental acts (praying, counting, repeating words) aimed at preventing or reducing distress or some dreaded event, even though the actions are not realistically connected with what they are meant to prevent, or are clearly excessive.

Unlike everyday checking, OCD symptoms are time-consuming — often more than an hour a day — and cause significant impairment. People with OCD generally recognize that their fears are excessive ("good insight"), though a minority have poor or absent insight. The disorder should be distinguished from obsessive-compulsive personality disorder, a separate condition involving perfectionism and rigidity without true obsessions and compulsions.

Causes

The causes are incompletely understood. Twin and family studies show a substantial genetic contribution. Neuroimaging implicates the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuits, and serotonergic dysfunction is suggested by the response to selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors. In a small subset of children, symptoms begin abruptly after streptococcal infection (the controversial PANDAS hypothesis).

Treatment

First-line treatments are cognitive behavioral therapy — specifically exposure and response prevention, in which patients confront feared situations while refraining from compulsions — and SSRI antidepressants, often at higher doses and for longer trials than in depression. Combination treatment is used for more severe cases; treatment-resistant OCD may be addressed with antipsychotic augmentation, intensive programs, or, rarely, neurosurgical interventions such as deep brain stimulation.

History

Descriptions of obsessional illness appear in religious literature of past centuries, when sufferers were often assumed to be possessed. The condition was delineated medically in the nineteenth century by Esquirol and others, and Freud analyzed it in his case of the "Rat Man" (1909). Behavioral treatments developed in the 1960s–70s transformed prognosis.
