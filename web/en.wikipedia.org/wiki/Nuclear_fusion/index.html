Nuclear fusion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of deuterium–tritium fusion: the two nuclei merging into helium-4 and a neutron, releasing energy]
Deuterium–tritium fusion, the easiest reaction to ignite.

Nuclear fusion is a reaction in which two or more atomic nuclei combine to form a larger nucleus, releasing or absorbing energy depending on whether the product lies below or above iron-56 in binding energy. Fusion powers the stars: in the Sun, the proton–proton chain fuses hydrogen into helium at a core temperature of 15 million degrees, possible at such (relatively) low temperatures only because of quantum tunnelling; more massive stars use the CNO cycle identified by Hans Bethe.

On Earth, fusion energy research pursues the deuterium–tritium reaction (easiest to ignite, ~100 million degrees) in magnetic confinement (tokamaks such as ITER, stellarators) and inertial confinement (the National Ignition Facility, which achieved target gain above 1 in December 2022 — more fusion energy out than laser energy in). Net electric power remains undemonstrated; the attraction is abundant fuel (deuterium from seawater, tritium bred from lithium), no carbon emissions, no runaway risk and little long-lived waste. Private ventures (2020s) have accelerated timelines with high-temperature superconducting magnets enabling compact tokamaks.

Why it is hard

Nuclei repel each other electrically; only extreme temperature or density makes tunnelling frequent enough — and containing a 100-million-degree plasma without touching it, while extracting more energy than the heating systems consume (engineering gain), has been a seventy-year engineering ascent. Lawson's criterion (1957) states the required product of density, temperature and confinement time.
