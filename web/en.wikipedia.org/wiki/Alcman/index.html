Alcman (Ancient Greek: Ἀλκμάν) was an ancient Greek lyric poet from Sparta, active in the 7th century BC. He is regarded as the earliest Alexandrian canon representative of choral lyric poetry and the inventor, or at least the earliest known practitioner, of the partheneion, a song performed by a chorus of young women.

Life

Almost nothing is known of Alcman's life. Ancient sources disagree on whether he was born in Sparta or in Sardis in Lydia and later brought to Sparta as a slave before being freed. He is conventionally dated to the reigns of the Spartan kings of the mid-to-late 7th century BC.

Works

Alcman's poetry was collected in six books in antiquity, but only fragments survive, quoted by later authors and recovered on papyrus. The most substantial fragment is the so-called Louvre Papyrus, discovered in Egypt in 1855, which preserves about one hundred lines of a partheneion. His verse, written in a literary Doric dialect, treats themes of ritual, nature, love, and the praise of Spartan cults and deities such as Artemis and Helen.

Legacy

Alcman stood at the head of the tradition of Spartan choral lyric and influenced later poets including Stesichorus. His vivid nature imagery and descriptions of the night sky are among the most admired passages in early Greek poetry.
