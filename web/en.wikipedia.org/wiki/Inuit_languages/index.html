The Inuit languages are a closely related group of Indigenous American languages traditionally spoken across the North American Arctic — from northwest Alaska, across Arctic Canada to Labrador, and in Greenland. They form the Inuit branch of the Eskimo group of the Eskaleut language family, whose other branch is Yupik. The Inuit varieties constitute a dialect continuum stretching some 6,000 km, in which neighbouring varieties are mutually intelligible but the extremes (Alaskan Iñupiaq and Greenlandic) are not; the continuum spread eastward with the Thule migration from Alaska around 1000 CE, remarkably recently.

Varieties and status

Principal standardised varieties include Iñupiaq in northern Alaska; Inuvialuktun and Inuinnaqtun in the western Canadian Arctic; Inuktitut in Nunavut and Nunavik (northern Quebec); Inuttitut in Nunatsiavut (Labrador); and Greenlandic (Kalaallisut), the official language of Greenland and the most widely spoken variety, with East Greenlandic (Tunumiisut) and the divergent Inuktun (Polar Inuit) dialect of the far north. Kalaallisut is vigorous; most Canadian and Alaskan varieties are endangered, with Inuktitut retaining tens of thousands of speakers and official status in Nunavut, while Inuinnaqtun and Inuttitut have only hundreds to a few thousand speakers each. Greenlandic, Inuktitut and Iñupiaq together account for the great majority of speakers.

Linguistic features

Inuit languages are polysynthetic and agglutinative: a single word built from a root plus many derivational and inflectional suffixes can express what English needs a whole sentence for — the famous examples of hundreds of "words for snow" are exaggerated, but the languages do have rich sets of snow- and ice-related roots and derivations. Phonology is relatively simple: three vowels (i, u, a, with length contrasts) and around 13–20 consonants depending on variety. Syntax is ergative–absolutive, and verbs inflect for both subject and object. Canadian Inuktitut is written in a distinctive syllabic script adapted from Cree syllabics by missionaries in the 19th century; other varieties use Latin alphabets, and Greenlandic spelling was standardised in a 1973 reform.

Revitalisation

Colonial schooling policies in Canada (including residential schools) and Denmark suppressed the languages for generations. Current revitalisation includes Inuit-language protection legislation (Nunavut's Inuit Language Protection Act, 2008; Greenland's language act making Kalaallisut its sole official language in 2009), immersion schooling, media such as APTN broadcasts and Greenlandic television, terminology development for modern concepts, and master-apprentice and digital-learning programmes.
