Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (English: The Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy), often referred to simply as the Principia, is a book by Isaac Newton presenting his laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation. Published in Latin in three books on 5 July 1687, it is considered one of the most important works in the history of science.

Contents

The work opens with definitions and the axioms, or laws of motion. Book I treats motion in the absence of resisting media, proving that gravitational attraction toward a point mass yields conic-section orbits and deriving Kepler's laws from the inverse-square law. Book II treats motion in resisting media and refutes Descartes' vortex theory. Book III, the "System of the World," applies the theory to the motions of planets, moons, comets and tides.

Background and publication

The Principia grew out of Newton's 1684 tract De motu corporum in gyrum, prompted by a question from Edmond Halley, who edited and financed the publication through the Royal Society. A second edition (1713), edited by Roger Cotes, added the famous General Scholium; the third (1726) was edited by Henry Pemberton.

Legacy

The Principia founded classical mechanics and dominated physics until Einstein's theories of relativity in the early 20th century. Not to be confused with the Principia Mathematica of Alfred North Whitehead and Bertrand Russell, a 20th-century work on the foundations of mathematics.
