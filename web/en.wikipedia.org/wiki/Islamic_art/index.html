Islamic art

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Intricate arabesque tilework and calligraphy covering the walls of a mosque in Isfahan]
Intricate arabesque tilework and calligraphy covering the walls of a mosque in Isfahan

Islamic art is the art produced in the lands where Islam was the dominant religion, from the seventh century to the present, encompassing architecture, calligraphy, painting, ceramics, metalwork, textiles and the famous geometric and arabesque ornament. Though unified by faith and by the Arabic script's prestige, it is not a single style: Persian, Ottoman, Mughal, Arab, Andalusi and other traditions developed distinctly under a shared visual language.

Themes and techniques

Religious art in mosques avoids figural images of God and (in most contexts) the Prophet, channelling creativity into Arabic calligraphy — the supreme art form — geometric patterns of mathematical sophistication (girih tiles, muqarnas vaulting) and vegetal arabesque. Secular contexts, by contrast, are rich in figures: Persian miniature painting, Mughal portraiture and courtly objects teem with people and animals, correcting the common Western assumption that Islamic art is wholly aniconic.

Masterpieces and media

Landmarks include the Dome of the Rock (691), the Great Mosque of Cordoba, the Alhambra's palaces, Sinan's Ottoman mosques, Isfahan's Shah Mosque and the Taj Mahal's inlay (a Mughal, Islamic monument). Iznik pottery, Persian carpets, enameled glass mosque lamps and metalwork ewers set world standards of craft; manuscript illumination reached summits in copies of the Shahnameh and the Qur'an.

Influence

Islamic ornament entered European art through Spain and trade (the very word 'arabesque'), influenced the Arts and Crafts movement and modern abstraction, and its mathematical patterns continue to inspire contemporary artists and designers in the Muslim world and beyond.
