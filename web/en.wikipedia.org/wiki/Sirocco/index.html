The sirocco (Italian: scirocco) is a Mediterranean wind that blows from the Sahara and can reach hurricane speeds in North Africa and southern Europe, especially during drought conditions. It arises from a warm, dry, tropical air mass pulled northward by low-pressure cells moving eastward across the Mediterranean Sea, with winds originating in the Arabian or Sahara deserts.

Characteristics

The sirocco blows from the south or southeast. Leaving North Africa it is hot and extremely dry, often laden with fine sand and dust; after crossing the Mediterranean it picks up moisture and arrives in southern Europe as a warm, humid, oppressive wind. It can raise temperatures abruptly, reduce visibility, and coat surfaces with reddish dust — rain falling through it is sometimes called "blood rain."

Effects

Sirocco conditions are associated with headaches and irritability in popular lore, can damage crops, aggravate respiratory conditions, and stir heavy seas. The wind typically lasts from half a day to several days, most commonly in spring and autumn.

Names along the Mediterranean

The same or closely related winds bear many local names: jugo in Croatia, ghibli in Libya, khamsin in Egypt and the Levant, leveche in southeastern Spain, and marin in southern France.
