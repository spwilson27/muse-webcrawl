Tax reform

Tax reform is the process of changing the way taxes are collected or managed by government.

Tax reform is the process of changing the way taxes are collected or managed by the government and is usually undertaken to improve tax administration or to provide economic or social benefits. Reforms may lower or raise tax levels, broaden or narrow the tax base, simplify compliance, shift the mix between direct and indirect taxes, or change how the burden is distributed.

Objectives

Commonly stated goals of reform include raising revenue adequacy, improving economic efficiency (often by broadening the base and lowering marginal rates), increasing fairness or progressivity, simplifying the system, and improving administration and compliance. Reforms involve trade-offs among these objectives, and their effects depend on design and context.

Notable reforms

  • United States, 1986: The Tax Reform Act of 1986 lowered top marginal rates, broadened the income tax base by repealing preferences, and was designed to be revenue-neutral.
  • Flat taxes in Eastern Europe: Estonia (1994) and other post-communist states adopted single-rate income taxes with broad bases.
  • Goods and services taxes: The introduction of VAT/GST systems, as in Canada (1991), Australia (2000) and India (2017), replaced cascades of narrower sales taxes.
  • United States, 2017: The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act cut the corporate rate from 35 to 21 percent and restructured international taxation.
  • International minimum tax: The OECD-brokered 15 percent global minimum corporate tax agreed in 2021 and implemented from 2024.

Political economy

Because reforms create concentrated losers and diffuse winners, they are politically difficult and frequently depend on crisis conditions, strong executives, or bipartisan commissions. Transitional relief and grandfathering are commonly used to ease passage, at the cost of added complexity.
