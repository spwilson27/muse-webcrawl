The Albatros D.III was a biplane fighter aircraft used by the Imperial German Army Air Service (Luftstreitkräfte) and the Austro-Hungarian Air Service during the First World War. It was modelled on the successful Albatros D.I and D.II designs, but incorporated a sesquiplane wing arrangement inspired by the French Nieuport fighters.

Design and development

Designed by Robert Thelen, the D.III retained the sleek plywood semi-monocoque fuselage of its predecessors but featured narrower-chord lower wings connected to the upper wings by V-shaped interplane struts. It was powered by a Mercedes D.IIIa six-cylinder water-cooled engine of about 170–180 hp and armed with two synchronised 7.92 mm LMG 08/15 machine guns.

Operational history

The D.III entered service in December 1916 and quickly proved superior to most Allied fighters of the period. In the hands of pilots such as Manfred von Richthofen, it helped Germany achieve air superiority during "Bloody April" 1917. However, the sesquiplane layout inherited a structural weakness: the lower wing was prone to twisting and failure in steep dives, a problem also seen in the Nieuport designs it copied. Despite remedial strengthening, the flaw was never entirely cured.

The type was built under licence in Austria-Hungary by Oeffag, whose later series with stronger wings and more powerful Austro-Daimler engines were considered among the finest fighters of the war. By 1918 the D.III was being superseded by the Albatros D.V and the Fokker D.VII, though it remained in service until the end of the war and afterwards with several air forces, including those of Poland and Turkey.
