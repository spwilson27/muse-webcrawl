Khinkali (Georgian: ხინკალი) is a Georgian dumpling made of twisted knobs of dough stuffed with spiced meat and broth. It originated in the mountainous regions of eastern Georgia — Mtiuleti, Pshavi, and Khevsureti — and has become one of the most popular dishes throughout Georgia and the Caucasus.

Preparation

The traditional filling is raw minced meat — a mixture of beef and pork, or lamb in the mountains — seasoned with onions, red chili pepper, salt, cumin, and sometimes caraway or wild herbs. In modern urban versions herbs such as coriander and parsley are added, and meatless varieties are made with cheese, mushroom, or potato. The filling is wrapped in a circle of unleavened dough, which is gathered into pleats and twisted shut to form a tough stem handle (the kuchi). The dumplings are boiled in salted water until they float, then served hot, usually sprinkled with coarse black pepper.

How to eat

Khinkali are eaten by hand: the diner holds the dumpling upside down by its stem, sprinkles it with pepper, and takes a first small bite while drinking the broth from inside so that none of the juice spills onto the plate. The tough dough stem is usually left uneaten, and diners traditionally tally their consumption by the pile of stems remaining. In mountain custom, surrendering one's stems or failing to finish was a matter of honor at feasts. Khinkali are classically accompanied by beer or Georgian wine and are eaten in large quantities — a full portion for a hearty eater may number in the dozens.

Origins debate

Some accounts link the dumpling's arrival to Mongol influence across Eurasia in the 13th century, comparing khinkali with Central Asian manti and Chinese soup dumplings, while Georgian tradition holds it to be an indigenous highland invention suited to herdsmen needing portable, nourishing food.
