Galton–Watson process

The Galton–Watson process (also called the Bienaymé–Galton–Watson process) is a branching stochastic process arising from Francis Galton's statistical investigation of the extinction of family names. It models a population in which each individual in each generation independently produces a random number of offspring according to a fixed probability distribution, and is the simplest example of a branching process.

Definition

Start with one ancestor, Z0 = 1. Each individual in generation n produces a number of children drawn independently from an offspring distribution {pk} on the non-negative integers. Then Zn+1 is the sum of the offspring of the Zn individuals of generation n. The process is a Markov chain on the non-negative integers with absorbing state 0.

Extinction

Let μ be the mean number of offspring. The classic result, proved rigorously by Watson and later authors after Galton posed the problem in 1873, is that if μ ≤ 1 (and the process is not the trivial one-child case), extinction occurs with probability 1. If μ > 1, the extinction probability is the unique root in [0,1) of the equation s = G(s), where G is the probability generating function of the offspring distribution. The three regimes are called subcritical, critical and supercritical.

History and applications

Galton raised the question of the probability that a surname dies out in The Educational Times in 1873; the Reverend Henry William Watson supplied a solution, published jointly in 1874, though the phenomenon had been treated earlier by Irénée-Jules Bienaymé (1845) and the critical case was not fully understood until the work of Steffensen and Kolmogorov in the 1930s. Galton–Watson processes are now used in demography, nuclear chain reactions, epidemiology (early spread of infection), genetics, and the analysis of algorithms and random trees.
