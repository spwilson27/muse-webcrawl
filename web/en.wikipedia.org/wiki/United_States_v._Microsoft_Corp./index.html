United States v. Microsoft Corp.

Antitrust case
Filed: 18 May 1998
Court: U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia
Settled: 2001–2002

United States v. Microsoft Corporation, 253 F.3d 34 (D.C. Cir. 2001), was a landmark American antitrust law case in which the United States government accused Microsoft of illegally maintaining its monopoly in the personal computer market, primarily through restrictions on PC manufacturers and its bundling of the Internet Explorer web browser with Windows.

Background

The Department of Justice, 20 states, and the District of Columbia sued Microsoft in May 1998 under Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Antitrust Act. The government's case focused on Microsoft's agreements with computer makers and Internet access providers, and on its campaign against Netscape Navigator and Sun's Java platform.

Trial and judgment

After a bench trial, Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson issued findings of fact in November 1999 concluding that Microsoft held monopoly power in the market for Intel-compatible PC operating systems. In April 2000 he ruled that Microsoft had violated the Sherman Act, and in June 2000 ordered the company broken into two separate units.

Appeal and settlement

In June 2001 the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit reversed the breakup order and remanded the case, while affirming much of the monopoly-maintenance liability. The Department of Justice then settled in November 2001, imposing conduct remedies — including uniform licensing terms and disclosure of application programming interfaces — that were overseen under a consent decree expiring in 2011.
