Philology


Philology is the study of language in written historical sources, combining literary criticism, history and linguistics.

Philology is the study of language in oral and written historical sources; it is a combination of literary criticism, history, and linguistics. In its classical sense it concerns the establishment, interpretation and editing of texts — determining their authenticity, their original form, and their meaning — and was long the core discipline of the humanities.

Classical philology

Philology originated in the ancient world: scholars of Hellenistic Alexandria, such as Zenodotus and Aristarchus, edited the poems of Homer in the great Library, developing techniques of textual criticism. Roman and later Byzantine and Renaissance scholars preserved and emended Greek and Latin literature. Early modern humanists such as Erasmus applied these methods to classical and biblical texts.

Comparative philology

In the late 18th and 19th centuries, the study of Sanskrit after Sir William Jones's 1786 observation of its affinity with Greek and Latin gave rise to comparative (historical) linguistics. Scholars such as Franz Bopp, Rasmus Rask and Jacob Grimm reconstructed the Indo-European language family and formulated sound laws (notably Grimm's law), making philology a model of a rigorous historical science.

Branches

Modern philology includes textual criticism (stemmatics, critical editions), palaeography and epigraphy, decipherment — as with Egyptian hieroglyphs, Linear B and cuneiform — and the study of individual language traditions (Romance, Germanic, Slavic, Semitic philology, and so on). In the 20th century much of its theoretical work was absorbed into linguistics and literary studies, but philological methods remain fundamental to editing and interpreting historical texts.
