Dasein

Dasein (German pronunciation: [ˈdaːzaɪn]) is a German word meaning "being there" or "presence" (from da "there" and sein "being"). It is the central concept of Martin Heidegger's 1927 work Being and Time, where it denotes the kind of being that human beings have: an existence that is always already situated in a world, understands itself and its surroundings, and for whom its own being is an issue. Because Heidegger regards it as inseparable from its meaning in context, the term is usually left untranslated in English-language scholarship.

Heidegger's usage

Heidegger distinguishes Dasein from objects that are merely "present-at-hand" (vorhanden) or tools "ready-to-hand" (zuhanden). Dasein always finds itself thrown into a world it did not choose — a language, culture, body and historical situation — yet must take over its existence through its own projects. Its basic constitution is "being-in-the-world" (In-der-Welt-sein), a unitary phenomenon prior to any subject–object split, and its being is structured as "care" (Sorge).

Authenticity and death

Everyday Dasein tends to lose itself in "the They" (das Man) — anonymous public norms. By confronting its own finitude in "being-toward-death" (Sein-zum-Tode) and experiencing anxiety (Angst), Dasein can achieve authenticity (Eigentlichkeit), owning its possibilities rather than merely conforming. Heidegger analyses these structures through temporality: Dasein's being is fundamentally time.

Earlier and later usage

The word was used before Heidegger by philosophers including Hegel and Jaspers in the general sense of "existence". Heidegger's redefinition made Dasein a technical term of existential phenomenology, influencing Sartre, Merleau-Ponty, Gadamer and existential psychotherapy (notably Ludwig Binswanger's and Medard Boss's "Daseinsanalysis"). Critics, including Theodor Adorno in The Jargon of Authenticity, attacked its obscurity.
