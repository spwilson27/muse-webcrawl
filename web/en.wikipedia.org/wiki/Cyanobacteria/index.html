Cyanobacteria are a phylum of bacteria that obtain their energy through photosynthesis, and are the only prokaryotes known to produce oxygen in the process. Long called "blue-green algae" for their pigmentation, they are true bacteria, not algae. Cyanobacteria are among the oldest forms of life on Earth: fossil evidence and molecular clocks place their origin more than 2.4 billion years ago, and their photosynthetic activity caused the Great Oxidation Event, transforming Earth's atmosphere and making aerobic life possible.

Origin of chloroplasts

By the endosymbiotic theory, the chloroplasts of plants and algae descend from a cyanobacterium taken up by an early eukaryotic cell; the plastids of all photosynthetic eukaryotes ultimately trace to this single primary endosymbiosis.

Morphology and ecology

Cyanobacteria range from unicells a few micrometres across (such as Prochlorococcus, the most abundant photosynthetic organism in the oceans) to long filaments and colonies, some forming specialised cells: heterocysts, which fix atmospheric nitrogen, and akinetes, thick-walled resting spores. They live in oceans, fresh water, damp soil, hot springs, deserts and Antarctic rocks, often forming stromatolites — layered structures among the oldest evidence of life. Nitrogen-fixing genera such as Trichodesmium fertilise wide areas of the open ocean.

Harmful blooms and toxins

Under nutrient-rich conditions some freshwater cyanobacteria form harmful algal blooms and produce cyanotoxins (such as microcystins and anatoxins) dangerous to livestock, pets and people; warming climates and fertiliser runoff are increasing bloom frequency.
