Physical examination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Auscultation during a physical examination.

In a physical examination, also called a medical examination or clinical examination, a medical practitioner examines a patient for possible medical signs of disease. Together with the medical history, it forms the foundation of diagnosis. A routine examination in an asymptomatic person is sometimes called a physical or check-up.

Components

The examination traditionally follows four techniques: inspection (looking), palpation (feeling), percussion (tapping to assess underlying structures by sound), and auscultation (listening, usually with a stethoscope). Vital signs — temperature, blood pressure, pulse, and respiratory rate — are measured, and the examination proceeds by system: head, eyes, ears, nose and throat; neck; cardiovascular; respiratory; abdominal; musculoskeletal; neurological; and skin.

Scope and evidence

For patients presenting with symptoms, the examination is directed at the problem. For general health checks in healthy adults, evidence for the benefit of the full ritual examination is limited, and modern practice emphasises targeted components with proven value, such as blood pressure measurement, rather than exhaustive routine manoeuvres. The examination also has a role beyond diagnosis: it establishes rapport and is a meaningful ritual of care between doctor and patient. Classical instruments of the examination include the stethoscope (invented by René Laennec in 1816), the ophthalmoscope, the otoscope, the sphygmomanometer, and the reflex hammer.
