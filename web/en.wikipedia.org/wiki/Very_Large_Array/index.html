The Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (VLA) is a centimeter-wavelength radio astronomy observatory in central New Mexico, United States. It is located on the Plains of San Agustin, between the towns of Magdalena and Datil, some 80 km (50 mi) west of Socorro. The observatory consists of 27 independent antennas, each 25 metres (82 ft) in diameter, arranged along a Y-shaped configuration of three railway arms. It is operated by the National Radio Astronomy Observatory (NRAO).

The VLA is one of the world's premier astronomical radio observatories. Its antennas can be moved on rails into four standard configurations, allowing astronomers to trade angular resolution against surface-brightness sensitivity. Between 2010 and 2012 the array underwent a major upgrade, the Expanded VLA project, which replaced its original electronics and increased its sensitivity by a factor of up to ten; on completion it was renamed in honour of Karl Guthe Jansky, the founder of radio astronomy.

Characteristics

Each of the 27 antennas weighs about 230 tons and is mounted on a double set of railroad tracks so that the array can be reconfigured. In its most compact "D" configuration the antennas span about 1 km, while in the extended "A" configuration the longest baselines reach some 36 km, giving an effective angular resolution comparable to a single dish of that diameter. The array operates at frequencies between roughly 1 and 50 GHz.

Signals from the antennas are combined in a special-purpose supercomputer correlator known as WIDAR. The data are used to image cosmic radio sources including radio galaxies, quasars, pulsars, supernova remnants and the gas in star-forming regions.

History

Conceived in the 1960s as a successor to earlier interferometers, the VLA was approved by the United States Congress in 1972, construction began in 1973, and the array was formally dedicated in October 1980. It quickly became the most productive ground-based telescope in astronomy.

The site is open to the public and includes a visitor centre and a self-guided walking tour. The array has featured prominently in popular culture, notably in the 1997 film Contact, adapted from Carl Sagan's novel, in which its dishes are used to receive an extraterrestrial signal.
