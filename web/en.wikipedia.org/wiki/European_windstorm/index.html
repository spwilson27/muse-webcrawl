European windstorm

A powerful extratropical cyclone making landfall on northwestern Europe.

A European windstorm is a severe extratropical cyclone that affects Europe, bringing damaging winds—often exceeding hurricane force in gusts—together with heavy rain and coastal flooding. Such storms form over the North Atlantic, typically between October and March, and intensify as they approach the continent; they rank among Europe's costliest natural hazards for insurers.

Meteorology

Windstorms develop along the polar front in the Atlantic storm track, sometimes undergoing explosive cyclogenesis ("weather bombs") as they interact with the jet stream. The strongest winds occur south of the low centre, and the most violent gusts are associated with features such as the sting jet—a descending airstream identified in the Great Storm of 1987—and narrow cold-frontal rainbands. A related, smaller-scale hazard is the derecho.

Notable storms

Historic events include the North Sea flood of 1953, the Great Storm of 1987, the Burns' Day storm (1990), Lothar and Martin (1999), Kyrill (2007), Xynthia (2010) and, more recently, storms in the annual naming lists introduced by European meteorological services from 2015. Individual storms have caused tens of billions of euros in damage and scores of deaths.

Naming and forecasting

Since the 2015–16 season the UK Met Office and Met Éireann (later joined by the Netherlands' KNMI) name storms expected to have significant impact, while other national groupings in northern, western and southwestern Europe operate parallel lists coordinated under EUMETNET. The Free University of Berlin's long-running naming scheme remains widely cited in research on earlier events.
