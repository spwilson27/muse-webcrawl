Charité

The Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin is Europe's largest university hospital, affiliated with Humboldt University and Freie Universität Berlin. Founded in 1710 outside the city walls of Berlin as a plague hospital, it is one of the oldest teaching hospitals in Germany and has been associated with many of the country's most famous physicians and scientists, including Rudolf Virchow, Robert Koch, Emil von Behring and Paul Ehrlich.

History

In 1709–10, fearing the plague then spreading through Prussia, King Frederick I ordered the construction of a pest house outside Berlin's Spandau gate. The plague never reached the city, and the building instead served as a charity hospital for the poor — hence the name Charité ("charity"), bestowed by Frederick William I in 1727, who also made it a teaching institution for military surgeons. In the nineteenth century the Charité became a leading centre of scientific medicine: Virchow founded cellular pathology there, and Koch developed his bacteriological methods in its laboratories.

Modern institution

After German reunification, the Charité merged with the medical faculties and hospitals of both East and West Berlin, and in 2003 with the medical school of the Freie Universität, creating a single joint institution of the two universities. It now operates four campuses — Campus Charité Mitte, Campus Virchow-Klinikum, Campus Benjamin Franklin and Campus Berlin Buch — treats around 150,000 inpatients and well over a million outpatients annually, and educates several thousand medical and dental students. The Charité is consistently ranked among the best hospitals in the world in international surveys.
