Offa (died 29 July 796 AD) was King of Mercia, a kingdom of Anglo-Saxon England, from 757 until his death. The son of Thingfrith and a descendant of Eowa, Offa came to the throne after a period of civil war following the assassination of Æthelbald. In the early years of Offa's reign, it is likely that he consolidated his control of Midland peoples such as the Hwicce and the Magonsæte. Taking advantage of instability in the kingdom of Kent to establish himself as overlord, Offa also controlled Sussex by 771, though his authority in both territories was disputed at times.

By the 780s, Offa had extended Mercian supremacy over most of southern England, and he negotiated an alliance with Beorhtric of Wessex sealed by Beorhtric's marriage to Offa's daughter Eadburh in 789. He also became the overlord of East Anglia and had its king Æthelberht II beheaded in 794, perhaps for rebelling against him.

Overlordship and Charlemagne

Offa was a Christian king who came into conflict with the Church, particularly with Jænberht, the Archbishop of Canterbury. Offa persuaded the pope to divide the archdiocese of Canterbury in two, creating a new archdiocese of Lichfield. This reduction in the power of Canterbury may have been motivated by Offa's desire to have an archbishop consecrate his son Ecgfrith as king, since it may have been Jænberht who refused to perform the ceremony in 787.

Offa corresponded with Charlemagne, king of the Franks, who treated him as an equal — addressing him as "brother" — though relations soured over a proposed marriage alliance and a trade dispute, during which Charlemagne briefly closed Frankish ports to English merchants. Surviving letters show negotiations over the protection of English pilgrims and merchants on the continent.

Coinage and works

Many historians regard Offa as the most powerful Anglo-Saxon king before Alfred the Great. His coinage reform produced silver pennies of exceptional artistic quality, many bearing his name and that of his queen, Cynethryth — the only Anglo-Saxon queen known to have had coins issued in her name. His dyke, Offa's Dyke, a great earthwork running along the border with Wales, is traditionally attributed to him and remains one of the most striking monuments of early medieval Britain.

Offa died in July 796 and was succeeded by his son Ecgfrith, who reigned for only 141 days before dying of unknown causes.
