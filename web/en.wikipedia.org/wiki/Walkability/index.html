Walkability

Walkability is a measure of how friendly a built environment is to walking. Factors defining walkability include the presence and quality of footpaths, sidewalks or other pedestrian rights-of-way, the density and mixture of land uses, street connectivity, building accessibility, traffic and crime safety, and the presence of shade and street furniture.

Continuous sidewalks, street trees and mixed uses support walking.

Measurement

The best-known metric is Walk Score, which rates addresses from 0 to 100 based on proximity to amenities. Urbanist Jeff Speck's "general theory of walkability" holds that a walk must be useful, safe, comfortable and interesting. Jane Jacobs earlier argued that "eyes on the street" from mixed-use, dense neighbourhoods make streets safer and livelier.

Benefits

Higher walkability is associated with better physical health, lower transport emissions and costs, stronger local retail, and higher property values. Walkable design is a central aim of New Urbanism and of concepts such as the 15-minute city.
