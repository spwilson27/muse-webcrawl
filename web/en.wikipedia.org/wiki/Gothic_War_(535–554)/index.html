Gothic War (535–554)

Gothic War
Date535 – 554
LocationItaly and Dalmatia
ResultByzantine victory; Italy annexed to the empire
BelligerentsByzantine Empire — Ostrogothic Kingdom (with Frankish and Alamannic intervention)
CommandersBelisarius, Narses, Mundus — Vitiges, Totila, Teia

The Gothic War between the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire and the Ostrogothic Kingdom of Italy took place from 535 to 554. It was the centerpiece of the emperor Justinian I's renovatio imperii, the attempt to recover the western provinces lost in the 5th century, following the swift reconquest of Vandal North Africa in 533–534. The war destroyed the Ostrogothic kingdom and restored imperial rule in Italy, but at a cost so devastating — compounded by plague and famine — that Italy's cities, population, and economy did not recover for centuries.

Background

The Ostrogothic kingdom founded by Theodoric the Great had governed Italy in nominal subordination to Constantinople since 493. After Theodoric's death in 526, the regency of his daughter Amalasuntha for her son Athalaric favored accommodation with the empire. Athalaric died in 534; Amalasuntha was deposed and murdered in 535 by her cousin and co-ruler Theodahad. Justinian seized on the murder, and on Gothic mistreatment of Roman senators, as a casus belli. Our principal narrative source is the historian Procopius, who accompanied the commanding general Belisarius as his secretary.

First phase: Belisarius, 535–540

In 535 Belisarius landed in Sicily with some 7,500 men and took the island with little resistance, while a second force under Mundus occupied Dalmatia. Crossing to the mainland in 536, Belisarius stormed Naples — his troops entering through an aqueduct — and entered Rome unopposed on 9 December 536 after the Gothic garrison withdrew. The new Gothic king, Vitiges, besieged Rome from March 537 to March 538; Belisarius, heavily outnumbered, held the walls, and the siege was lifted as imperial reinforcements arrived and Gothic forces were threatened in their rear. Imperial forces then seized Picenum and in 539 took Milan — soon retaken by the Goths with Frankish help and its male population massacred. In 540 Belisarius besieged Vitiges in Ravenna and, feigning acceptance of the Goths' offer to make him emperor of the West, entered the city and captured the king. Justinian, suspicious of his general, recalled Belisarius to face Persia, leaving Italy incompletely subdued.

Second phase: Totila's revival, 541–551

After brief reigns of Ildibad and Eraric, the Goths in 541 elected Totila (Baduila), a brilliant commander who rebuilt Gothic resistance. Exploiting imperial disorganization — eleven regional commanders with divided authority, unpaid and mutinous troops, and the catastrophic Plague of Justinian from 541 — Totila defeated imperial armies at Faventia and Mugello (542), retook Naples (543), and captured Rome itself in December 546 after a year-long siege, the city's population reduced by famine to a few hundred. Rome changed hands repeatedly: Belisarius, recalled to Italy in 544 with inadequate forces, retook it in 547 only for Totila to seize it again in 550. Totila also built a navy, raiding Sicily, Sardinia, Corsica, and Corfu, and courted the Italian peasantry by redistributing senatorial estates — a social revolution that alarmed the Roman aristocracy.

Third phase: Narses and conquest, 552–554

In 551 Justinian entrusted the war to the eunuch chamberlain Narses, who marched into Italy in 552 with a large, well-funded army of perhaps 25,000–30,000 men, recruited heavily among Lombards, Heruls, and other federates. At the Battle of Taginae (Busta Gallorum) in Umbria, June/July 552, Narses's dismounted archers shattered the Gothic cavalry charge; Totila was mortally wounded and died soon after. His successor Teia made a last stand at the Battle of Mons Lactarius near Mount Vesuvius (October 552), where he was killed and Gothic resistance collapsed. A Frankish and Alamannic invasion was crushed at the Volturnus in 554, and in August 554 Justinian issued the Pragmatic Sanction, reorganizing Italy as an imperial province.

Consequences

Nineteen years of war, siege, famine, and plague ruined Italy. Rome's aqueducts were cut during the sieges and never fully restored in antiquity; the city's population fell from several hundred thousand to perhaps tens of thousands. The senatorial aristocracy of Italy was largely extinguished as a landowning class. The imperial hold on the peninsula proved brief in the north: in 568 the Lombards — many of them veterans of Narses's army — invaded and conquered most of the interior, inaugurating Italy's long medieval division.
