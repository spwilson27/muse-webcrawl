SS-Totenkopfverbände

The SS-Totenkopfverbände (SS-TV; literally "Death's Head Units" or "Skull Units") was the SS organisation responsible for administering the Nazi concentration camps and extermination camps for Nazi Germany. Formed from the earlier concentration camp guard units in 1934–1936, it was headed by Theodor Eicke, the inspector of concentration camps. The SS-TV ran the camp system in which millions of people were imprisoned, enslaved and murdered during the Holocaust and the Nazi regime's wider programme of terror.

Formation and role

After the SS took control of the early concentration camps, SS-Obergruppenführer Theodor Eicke, commandant of Dachau, became Inspector of Concentration Camps in 1934 and reorganised the camps under his "Dachau model", with guards organised as Totenkopf units—the Totenkopfstürme—named for the skull insignia they wore. In 1936 the units were formally designated the SS-Totenkopfverbände. Their duties included guarding camp perimeters, supervising prisoner labour details, and staffing camp administrations.

War years

At the outbreak of war in 1939 the SS-TV numbered roughly 22,000–24,000 men, guarding camps including Dachau, Sachsenhausen, Buchenwald, Mauthausen, Flossenbürg and Ravensbrück. Eicke formed the SS Division Totenkopf from SS-TV personnel for front-line service in the Waffen-SS, while camp administration passed in 1942 to the SS Economic and Administrative Main Office (WVHA), under Amtsgruppe D headed by Richard Glücks. As the camp system expanded enormously during the war—including the extermination camps at Auschwitz-Birkenau, Majdanek and elsewhere—SS-TV personnel were augmented by older reservists, ethnic German auxiliaries, female overseers (Aufseherinnen) for women's camps, and, late in the war, conscripts from other services.

Postwar

The SS, including the Totenkopfverbände, was declared a criminal organisation at the Nuremberg trials. Many camp personnel were prosecuted in subsequent trials, such as the Dachau and Mauthausen trials conducted by the United States, and in German courts; others evaded justice. Theodor Eicke had been killed in 1943 while commanding the Totenkopf Division on the Eastern Front.
