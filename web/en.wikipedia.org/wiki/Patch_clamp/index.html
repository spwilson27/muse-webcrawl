Patch clamp

The patch clamp technique is a laboratory technique in electrophysiology used to study ionic currents in individual isolated living cells, tissue sections, or patches of cell membrane. It is especially useful in the study of excitable cells such as neurons, cardiomyocytes, muscle fibres, and pancreatic beta cells.

A patch pipette pressed against a cell membrane.

Technique

The technique uses a glass micropipette with an open tip about one micrometre in diameter, filled with an electrolyte solution and a recording electrode. The pipette is pressed against the cell membrane and suction applied, forming a high-resistance "gigaseal" (resistance above 109 ohms) that electrically isolates the patch of membrane under the tip. This permits recording of the picoampere currents flowing through single ion channels.

Configurations

  • Cell-attached — the pipette remains sealed to the intact cell, recording single-channel activity within the patch.
  • Whole-cell — stronger suction ruptures the patch, giving electrical access to the entire cell interior; currents across the whole membrane can be recorded or the membrane potential controlled.
  • Inside-out — the pipette is withdrawn, leaving the patch attached with its cytosolic surface facing the bath.
  • Outside-out — after whole-cell formation the pipette is retracted and the membrane reseals with its extracellular surface facing outward.
  • Perforated patch — pore-forming antibiotics grant electrical access while preserving intracellular contents.

History

The technique was developed by Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann at the Max Planck Institute in Göttingen in the late 1970s and early 1980s, building on earlier work with less precise seals. Neher and Sakmann received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1991 "for their discoveries concerning the function of single ion channels in cells". Patch clamping revolutionised the study of ion channels and is now a standard method in neuroscience, physiology, and drug screening; automated planar patch clamp systems can record from hundreds of cells in parallel.
