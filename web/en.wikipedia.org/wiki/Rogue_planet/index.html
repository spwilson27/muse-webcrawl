Rogue planet

DefinitionA planetary-mass object that orbits the galactic centre directly, not bound to any star

A rogue planet, also known as a free-floating planet or interstellar planet, is a planetary-mass object that was ejected from the planetary system in which it formed, or that never became gravitationally bound to any star or brown dwarf, and thus orbits the galactic centre directly. Such objects are dark and cold, retaining only internal heat, which makes them extremely difficult to detect.

Origin

Two formation channels are proposed: rogue planets may form like other planets, in a protoplanetary disc around a young star, and then be ejected by gravitational scattering with sibling planets or a passing star; or they may collapse directly from a small cloud of gas and dust, like a star that failed — the lowest-mass products of this process overlapping with sub-brown dwarfs. Simulations suggest ejection is common, and some estimates hold that rogue planets may outnumber stars in the Milky Way, though microlensing surveys constrain the abundance of Jupiter-mass free-floaters.

Detection

Because they emit almost no light, rogue planets are found mainly by gravitational microlensing, in which a passing object briefly brightens a background star (surveys such as OGLE and MOA have reported numerous candidates, including Earth-mass ones), and by direct infrared imaging of the nearest, warmest young examples, such as PSO J318.5-22, discovered in 2013. NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is expected to greatly expand the census through microlensing.

Properties and habitability

A rogue planet keeps the heat of its formation and of radioactive decay in its interior; a sufficiently thick hydrogen atmosphere or insulating ice layer over a subsurface ocean could in principle keep water liquid without any starlight, leading to speculation about life in such oceans. A large moon could add tidal heating. Most rogue planets, however, have surface temperatures approaching those of interstellar space.
