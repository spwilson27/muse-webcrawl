The Kingdom of Egypt (Arabic: al-Mamlaka al-Miṣriyya) was the legal form of the Egyptian state under the Muhammad Ali dynasty from the country's nominal independence from the United Kingdom in 1922 until the abolition of the monarchy following the Egyptian revolution of 1952. It was preceded by the Sultanate of Egypt, a British protectorate, and succeeded by the Republic of Egypt in 1953.

History

Britain's unilateral declaration of Egyptian independence in February 1922 allowed Sultan Fuad I to proclaim himself King, and a constitution was promulgated in 1923. Although formally independent, Egypt remained heavily influenced by Britain, which retained control over the Suez Canal Zone, defence and the Sudan. The Anglo-Egyptian Treaty of 1936 confirmed British military presence around the canal.

King Farouk, who succeeded in 1936, presided over a period marked by the Second World War, the growth of nationalist movements such as the Muslim Brotherhood, and Egypt's defeat in the 1948 Arab–Israeli War. Dissatisfaction with the monarchy and the continuing British presence culminated in the Cairo fire of January 1952 and the July 1952 coup by the Free Officers Movement. Farouk abdicated in favour of his infant son Fuad II, and the monarchy was formally abolished on 18 June 1953.

Politics and society

Under the 1923 constitution Egypt was a parliamentary monarchy dominated for long periods by the Wafd Party, founded by Saad Zaghloul. The era saw a cultural flowering in Cairo, growth of the Egyptian film and press industries, and expanding education, alongside persistent rural poverty and political instability.
