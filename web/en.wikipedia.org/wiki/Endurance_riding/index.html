Endurance riding is an equestrian sport in which horse and rider compete over long-distance trails, typically 50 to 100 miles (80–160 km) in a single day. The horse's welfare is central: at mandatory veterinary checkpoints along the course, horses must meet pulse, hydration and soundness criteria before continuing, and a horse judged unfit is eliminated regardless of its position.

History

The modern sport grew out of cavalry tests and the Pony Express tradition. In 1955 Wendell Robie and friends rode the Western States Trail from Lake Tahoe to Auburn, California, reviving a route of the old pony express era; the ride became the Tevis Cup, the world's most famous 100-mile endurance event. The American Endurance Ride Conference (AERC) was founded in 1972, and the FEI recognised endurance as a discipline in the 1980s.

Competition

Rides are divided into stages separated by vet gates with compulsory holds. Riders aim to finish within a maximum time while keeping the horse sound — the sport's motto is "to finish is to win". Arabian horses dominate because of their stamina, efficient gaits and heat tolerance. Alongside placings, a coveted "Best Condition" award honours the horse finishing in the best physical shape among the top ten.
