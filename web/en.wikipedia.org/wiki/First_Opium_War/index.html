First Opium War

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The iron steamship Nemesis destroying Chinese war junks, 1841]

The First Opium War (1839–1842), also known as the First Anglo-Chinese War, was fought between the United Kingdom and Qing China over trade — above all the British East India Company's opium trade, which reversed the silver flow into China and created millions of addicts, and over China's restrictions on foreign commerce at Canton. Commissioner Lin Zexu's destruction of 20,000 chests of opium at Humen (1839) triggered war; British steam-powered naval forces defeated Qing armies along the coast and up the Yangtze, and the Treaty of Nanjing (1842) — the first of the 'unequal treaties' — ceded Hong Kong Island, opened five treaty ports and ended the Canton system.

Causes and course

Britain's appetite for tea and China's insistence on silver payment drained British bullion until Indian-grown opium, sold by private traders to Chinese smugglers, reversed the flow. The Daoguang Emperor's crackdown under Lin Zexu — blockading the Canton factories and destroying the confiscated opium — gave London its casus belli. From 1840 a British expeditionary force, centred on steam gunboats like the iron Nemesis, took Zhoushan, blockaded the coast, stormed the Bogue forts, Guangzhou and the Yangtze cities, and by August 1842 threatened Nanjing itself.

Consequences

The Treaty of Nanjing (29 August 1842) opened Shanghai, Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou and Ningbo to British trade and residence, abolished the cohong monopoly, fixed tariffs, exacted a 21-million-dollar indemnity and ceded Hong Kong (held until 1997). France and the United States obtained parallel treaties (1844). In Chinese historiography the war opens the 'century of humiliation' and the modern era; it exposed the Qing's military-technological backwardness while leaving the opium trade itself — legalised after the Second Opium War (1856–1860) — to expand further.
