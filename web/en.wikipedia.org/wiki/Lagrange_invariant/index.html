Lagrange invariant

In optics, the Lagrange invariant is a measure of the light propagating through an optical system. It is defined for two rays—the marginal ray and the chief ray—travelling through the system, and states that the product n u y is constant throughout the system, where n is the refractive index, y is the ray height, and u is the ray angle to the optical axis.

Definition

More generally, for the marginal ray (height ȳ, angle ū) and the chief ray (height y, angle u), the quantity H = n(ūy − uȳ) is invariant at every surface and in every space of a centred optical system. When angles are taken in the paraxial approximation, the invariant is equivalently expressed as the product of refractive index, image height, and marginal ray angle: H = n u y.

Significance

The Lagrange invariant expresses a fundamental conservation law of geometrical optics, closely related to the Smith–Helmholtz invariant and, in modern terms, to the conservation of étendue: an optical system cannot increase the throughput of a light beam, so gains in image size must be traded against convergence angle and vice versa. It therefore sets the basic limits on the design of imaging systems, including the relationship between field of view, aperture, and image size in cameras, telescopes, and microscopes.
