Elizabethan architecture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Elizabethan architecture refers to buildings of a certain style constructed during the reign of Elizabeth I of England (1558–1603). It is the early phase of Renaissance architecture in England, succeeding the predominantly Gothic Tudor style and combining it with Flemish and Italian classical detail.

Characteristics

Elizabethan country houses — the so-called "prodigy houses" built by courtiers hoping to host the queen on progress — favour symmetry, tall many-paned windows with stone mullions and transoms, long galleries, strapwork ornament, and elaborate plaster ceilings and carved chimneypieces. Plans often formed an E or H shape; glass became a conspicuous display of wealth, as in the saying that Hardwick Hall was "more glass than wall".

Notable examples

  • Longleat House, Wiltshire
  • Burghley House, Lincolnshire
  • Hardwick Hall, Derbyshire (1590–97, Robert Smythson)
  • Wollaton Hall, Nottingham
  • Montacute House, Somerset

The style gave way in the early seventeenth century to the more rigorously classical Jacobean and then Palladian manners associated with Inigo Jones.
