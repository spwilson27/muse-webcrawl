The Great Seal of the United States is the principal national emblem used to authenticate documents issued by the federal government. Its obverse features a bald eagle holding an olive branch and thirteen arrows, with a shield on its breast and the motto E pluribus unum ("out of many, one") in its beak. The reverse depicts an unfinished pyramid of thirteen levels surmounted by the Eye of Providence, with the mottos Annuit cœptis ("He has favoured our undertakings") and Novus ordo seclorum ("a new order of the ages") and the date MDCCLXXVI (1776).

History

The Continental Congress appointed the first committee on a seal on 4 July 1776, the day independence was declared; two further committees followed over six years. The final design, adopted on 20 June 1782, was synthesised by Charles Thomson, Secretary of Congress, drawing chiefly on proposals by William Barton and earlier committee work by Pierre Du Simitière, Francis Hopkinson, and others. The first brass die was cut that year and first used in September 1782. The present die, the seventh, dates from 1985 and reproduces the 1903 design.

Symbolism

The thirteen stars, stripes, arrows, and pyramid levels refer to the original thirteen states. The eagle's olive branch and arrows signify the nation's power of peace and war; the shield's blue chief over red and white pales represents Congress uniting the states. On the reverse, the pyramid signifies strength and endurance, and its unfinished state the nation's continued growth; the Eye of Providence denotes divine favour.

Use

By law the Secretary of State is custodian of the seal, which is affixed to presidential proclamations, ratifications of treaties, commissions of officers, and other instruments — roughly two to three thousand documents a year. The obverse is the model for the presidential seal and appears on the one-dollar bill (since 1935, together with the reverse), passports, and military insignia.
