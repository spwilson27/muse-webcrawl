Canadian cultural protectionism

Canadian cultural protectionism refers to the body of Canadian government policies intended to promote and protect Canadian culture against the overwhelming influence of the United States, whose films, television programmes, music and magazines dominate the Canadian market. These policies rest on the argument that, without intervention, Canada's much smaller cultural industries cannot compete with American imports and that a distinct national identity requires Canadian stories told by Canadians.

Measures

The best-known instrument is the system of Canadian content ("CanCon") requirements administered by the Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission (CRTC), which obliges broadcasters to devote a proportion of their schedules and music programming to Canadian material, certified through systems such as the MAPL criteria for music. Other measures include:

  • Public funding and institutions such as the CBC/Radio-Canada, Telefilm Canada and the Canada Council for the Arts;
  • Tax measures and restrictions aimed at foreign "split-run" magazines, a long-running dispute with the United States;
  • Foreign ownership limits in broadcasting, publishing and film distribution;
  • The "cultural exemption" Canada sought in trade agreements, preserving the right to subsidise and regulate culture, retained in modified form in successive agreements including CUSMA/USMCA.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that protectionist rules shelter mediocrity, limit consumer choice and amount to trade barriers, while supporters counter that culture is not an ordinary commodity and that most countries support their cultural sectors in some way. The rise of streaming services weakened the broadcasting-based system and prompted the Online Streaming Act (2023), which extended Canadian-content obligations to foreign platforms such as Netflix and Spotify.
