Subsistence agriculture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Subsistence agriculture occurs when farmers grow food crops to meet the needs of themselves and their families on smallholdings. Subsistence agriculturalists target farm output for survival and for mostly local requirements, with little or no surplus trade. The typical subsistence farm has a range of crops and animals needed by the family to feed and clothe themselves during the year.

Types

  • Shifting cultivation (slash-and-burn): land is cleared by fire and cropped for a few years until fertility declines, then abandoned to regenerate during a long fallow.
  • Intensive subsistence farming: small plots are worked intensively with family labour, common in densely populated parts of Asia where rice or other cereals are grown.
  • Primitive subsistence farming: simple tools, few inputs, and reliance on natural fertility.
  • Pastoral nomadism: herders move livestock seasonally in search of pasture, subsisting on animal products.

Distribution and significance

Subsistence farming remains the main occupation of a large share of the population in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and Asia. Hundreds of millions of smallholder households depend on it, cultivating plots often under two hectares. Because output is consumed directly, subsistence farmers are especially vulnerable to drought, floods, and crop failure, and the system is often (though not always) associated with poverty and low productivity.

Transition

Development policy frequently seeks to raise subsistence productivity or integrate farmers into markets through improved seed, fertiliser, irrigation, credit, extension services, and secure land tenure. Critics note that subsistence systems can also be resilient, diverse, and ecologically adapted, and that commercialisation brings its own risks.
