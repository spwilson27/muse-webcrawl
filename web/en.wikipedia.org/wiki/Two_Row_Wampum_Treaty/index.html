Two Row Wampum Treaty

Native nameGuswenta / Kaswentha
Date1613 (traditionally)
PartiesHaudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy) and Dutch settlers
TypeAgreement of peace, friendship and non-interference

The Two Row Wampum Treaty, known in the Mohawk language as Guswenta or Kaswentha, is an agreement traditionally dated to 1613 between representatives of the Haudenosaunee (the Five Nations of the Iroquois Confederacy) and Dutch settlers in what is now New York. It is recorded in a wampum belt bearing two parallel rows of purple beads on a field of white beads.

The two purple rows symbolise the two peoples' vessels travelling the same river in parallel, neither steering the other.

Meaning

According to Haudenosaunee oral tradition, the belt's two rows represent two vessels travelling down the river of life side by side: one, a birch-bark canoe, carries the Indigenous peoples with their laws, customs, and way of life; the other, a sailing ship, carries the Europeans with theirs. The three white rows are commonly interpreted as peace, friendship, and respect (or "forever"). The governing principle is non-interference: neither people will try to steer the other's vessel, and neither will attempt to impose its laws or religion on the other, while both share the river in peace.

Historical record

No surviving Dutch document from 1613 records the agreement, and historians debate its precise documentary basis. A document known as the Tawagonshi document, said to record a 1613 agreement, surfaced in the twentieth century but is regarded by many scholars as a later production, while Haudenosaunee tradition maintains that the agreement was made and subsequently renewed in later councils, including with the British and the United States. The principles of the Two Row are closely related to those of the Covenant Chain alliance system.

Legacy

The Two Row Wampum remains a living reference point in Haudenosaunee diplomacy and in Indigenous–settler relations in Canada and the United States. In 2013, the 400th anniversary was marked by the Two Row Wampum Renewal Campaign, in which Indigenous and non-Indigenous paddlers travelled the Hudson River from Onondaga to New York City and brought the treaty's message to the United Nations, drawing attention both to the treaty relationship and to environmental protection of the river.
