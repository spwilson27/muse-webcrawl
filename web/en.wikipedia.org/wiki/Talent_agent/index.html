A talent agent, or booking agent, is a person who finds jobs for actors, musicians, writers, directors, athletes, models and other professionals in the entertainment and sports industries. Agents represent their clients' interests to employers—studios, producers, publishers, teams and promoters—negotiating contracts and seeking opportunities, and are usually paid a commission, classically 10 per cent of the client's earnings from work the agent obtains.

Role and regulation

Unlike a manager, who advises on career direction and may work with very few clients, an agent's business is employment: submitting clients for roles, pitching projects, and arranging auditions and meetings. In many jurisdictions agents must be licensed—in California under the Talent Agencies Act, enforced by the Labor Commissioner—and franchised by unions such as SAG-AFTRA, which cap commissions. The legal boundary between agents (who may procure work) and managers (who historically may not) has been a recurring source of litigation.

Agencies

The industry's largest firms—known as the "majors" and including Endeavor (WME), Creative Artists Agency (CAA), United Talent Agency (UTA) and, historically, the William Morris Agency (founded 1898)—package talent across film, television, music and sport, sometimes assembling an entire film's cast, director and writer in a "package" for a studio. Powerful agents such as Lew Wasserman of MCA reshaped Hollywood economics in the mid-20th century, and the modern agency business has consolidated into public companies spanning representation, sports and live events.

History

Theatrical and vaudeville booking agents organised acts for circuits in the 19th century; the rise of film, radio, television and professional sport expanded the trade to literary, broadcasting and sports representation. Agents became central gatekeepers of the entertainment economy, and the agent–client relationship a standard feature of popular culture.
