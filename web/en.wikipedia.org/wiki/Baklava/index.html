Baklava is a layered pastry dessert made of filo pastry, filled with chopped nuts and sweetened with syrup or honey. It is a characteristic dessert of the cuisines of the former Ottoman Empire and is enjoyed across the Middle East, the Balkans, Greece, Turkey, and Central Asia.

History

The origins of baklava are debated; layered bread and nut desserts existed in ancient and medieval cuisines of the region, and the dessert was refined in the imperial kitchens of the Ottoman sultans in Istanbul. Variations developed in many countries, with disputes over its origin forming a light-hearted part of regional culinary rivalry.

Preparation

Sheets of filo are brushed with butter or oil and layered in a pan with finely chopped nuts — commonly pistachio (as in the celebrated Gaziantep baklava of Turkey), walnut, or hazelnut. The pastry is cut into pieces before baking, then soaked while still hot with a cold syrup of sugar, water, and lemon juice, or with honey. It is typically served cut into diamonds, squares, or "bird's nest" rolls.
