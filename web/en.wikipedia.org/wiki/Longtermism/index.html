Longtermism is the ethical view that positively influencing the long-term future of humanity is a key moral priority of our time. Its core reasoning is simple to state: the future could be vast — humanity's potential lifespan spans millions or billions of years, and the number of people who could exist in it dwarfs everyone alive today — so actions that affect the long-run trajectory, even slightly, and especially actions that reduce the risk of premature extinction or permanent catastrophe, may matter more than almost anything else we do. The term gained wide currency in the late 2010s and early 2020s through the work of Oxford philosophers William MacAskill and Toby Ord and the Swedish philosopher Nick Bostrom.

Arguments

MacAskill distinguishes weak longtermism (the long-run future is among the most important considerations) from strong longtermism (it is the overriding consideration), and argues for the strong form in What We Owe the Future (2022) through three claims: future people count morally, the future could be enormous, and — the contested step — our actions can predictably affect it, chiefly by preventing existential catastrophe (existential risk, a term from Bostrom) during what Ord calls humanity's precarious adolescence, The Precipice (2020). Ord estimates, as personal judgements, roughly a one-in-six chance of existential catastrophe in the next century, with the largest risks from engineered pandemics and unaligned artificial intelligence; longtermists also study "trajectory changes" short of extinction — for instance, avoiding permanent lock-in of bad values or institutions — and the idea of a "hinge of history" at which the present century is unusually consequential. The view grows out of total utilitarian and consequentialist population ethics, and its institutional home is largely within and around the effective altruism movement, with research at Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute (2005–2024) and Global Priorities Institute.

Criticism

Critics challenge each step: that astronomical expected values from tiny probabilities of vast futures license "Pascal's mugging" reasoning and fanaticism; that we cannot predict the long-term effects of our actions (the "cluelessness" problem); that longtermism diverts attention and money from present suffering; that its vision of the future reflects the values of a narrow, wealthy, Western community (the philosopher Émile Torres, a former adherent, attacks it as an ideology entangled with transhumanism and eugenics-adjacent thought); and that some population-ethics conclusions are repugnant or illiberal. Defenders respond that the core claim is modest compared to its critics' caricatures, that near-term and long-term goods rarely trade off so starkly, and that preventing extinction is common ground across ethical theories.
