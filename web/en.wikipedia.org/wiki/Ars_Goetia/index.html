The Ars Goetia is the first section of the 17th-century grimoire The Lesser Key of Solomon (the Lemegeton), attributed pseudepigraphically to King Solomon. It contains descriptions of the seventy-two demons that Solomon is said to have evoked and confined in a bronze vessel, giving each demon's rank, seal, appearance, powers, and the number of legions of spirits under its command.

Contents and sources

The word goetia derives from the Greek goēteia, sorcery. The catalogue draws heavily on Johann Weyer's Pseudomonarchia Daemonum (1577), which lists 69 demons, and on Reginald Scot's The Discoverie of Witchcraft (1584). Alongside the demon list, the text includes directions for constructing the magic circle, triangle of art, and the bronze vessel used in conjuration.

The seventy-two demons

The spirits are ranked as kings, dukes, princes, marquises, earls (counts), knights and presidents. The first is Bael (Ba'al), a king who appears with the heads of a man, a toad and a cat, and who grants invisibility; others include Paimon, Beleth, Asmodeus, Astaroth, Bune, Andras, and Decarabia. Each entry specifies the seal that must be worn as a lamen during the rite.

Editions and influence

Modern editions derive from manuscript copies in the British Library; S. L. MacGregor Mathers translated the text, and Aleister Crowley published an edition, The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King, in 1904. The Goetia's demonology has had a lasting influence on ceremonial magic, occult fiction, games, and popular culture.
