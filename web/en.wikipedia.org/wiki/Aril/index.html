Aril

Aril
DefinitionFleshy outgrowth covering or attached to a seed
OriginFuniculus (seed stalk)
ExamplesNutmeg (mace), yew, pomegranate, lychee, ackee
FunctionAttracts animals for seed dispersal

An aril is a specialized outgrowth from a seed that partly or completely covers it. It develops from the funiculus, the stalk that attaches the seed to the ovary wall, and is often brightly colored and fleshy, attracting animals that disperse the seed after eating the aril.

The fleshy red aril of the yew

Examples

The spice mace is the lacy red aril of the nutmeg seed. The edible flesh of the lychee and rambutan is an aril, as is the fleshy part of the ackee fruit. In the yew (Taxus), the seed is surrounded by a red, berry-like aril that is one of the few non-toxic parts of the plant (the seed itself is poisonous). The juicy sacs eaten in the pomegranate are sometimes described as arils (more precisely, the sarcotesta of the seed). The bright scarlet arils of the spindle tree and of many members of the family Celastraceae are conspicuous in autumn.

Related structures

Structures similar to arils include the caruncle of castor bean seeds, the elaiosomes on seeds dispersed by ants (such as violets and trilliums), and the strophiole. In some plants, such as certain acacias, an aril-like funicle supports the seed and aids ant dispersal. The term "arillate" describes seeds bearing an aril.

Dispersal

Arils are typically rich in sugars, lipids, or proteins and evolved as rewards for seed dispersers, particularly birds and mammals, which swallow the aril and void or drop the hard seed unharmed.
