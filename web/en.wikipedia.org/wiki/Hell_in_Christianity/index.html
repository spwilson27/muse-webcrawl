Hell in Christianity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Christian theology, hell is the place or state of punishment for the wicked after death and final judgement. The concept is traditionally understood as eternal separation from God, the fate of those who die unrepentant in serious sin. The subject has been one of the most debated doctrines in Christian history, with divergent views held across Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant traditions.

Biblical terminology

The English word hell translates several distinct words in the Bible: the Hebrew Sheol (the grave or abode of the dead) and the Greek Hades, its near-equivalent; Tartarus, used once in 2 Peter for the prison of fallen angels; and Gehenna, a valley near Jerusalem associated with fire, used by Jesus as an image of eschatological punishment. Jesus describes Gehenna with imagery of unquenchable fire, a worm that does not die, darkness, and "weeping and gnashing of teeth".

Historical development

Early church fathers debated the fate of the wicked. Some, such as Origen and Gregory of Nyssa, leaned toward apocatastasis — the eventual restoration of all. Augustine of Hippo argued forcefully for eternal conscious punishment for the damned and greatly influenced Western doctrine. The medieval church elaborated the doctrine, describing the pains of sense and the pain of loss; Dante's Inferno gave the idea its most famous literary form, with nine circles of torment fitted to sins.

Denominational views

Roman Catholicism

The Catholic Church teaches that hell is the state of definitive self-exclusion from communion with God, freely chosen by those who die in mortal sin without repentance. Its chief punishment is eternal separation from God; the Church affirms its eternity while leaving the fate of particular individuals to divine mercy.

Eastern Orthodoxy

Orthodox teaching often frames heaven and hell as the same experience of God's uncreated light and love, received as joy by the righteous and as torment by the unrepentant — hell as a condition rather than a separate place.

Protestantism

Most evangelical and confessional Protestant traditions teach eternal conscious torment. However, a significant minority hold annihilationism (conditional immortality), teaching that the wicked are ultimately destroyed rather than tormented forever; this view is official in Seventh-day Adventism. Christian universalists maintain that all will eventually be reconciled.

Modern debate

The doctrine of hell remains contested both theologically and philosophically, centred on the problem of hell: how eternal punishment can be reconciled with God's love and justice. Modern theologians across traditions have reinterpreted the traditional imagery in less literal terms, while others defend it as essential doctrine.
