Maggot therapy
Medicinal maggots applied to a wound.
Other namesMaggot debridement therapy (MDT), larval therapy, biosurgery
Species usedLucilia sericata (common green bottle fly)
UsesDebridement of chronic and necrotic wounds

Maggot therapy (also known as larval therapy or maggot debridement therapy, MDT) is a type of biotherapy involving the introduction of live, disinfected maggots (fly larvae) into the non-healing skin and soft-tissue wounds of a human or other animal for the purpose of cleaning out the necrotic (dead) tissue within a wound (debridement) and disinfection. The larvae most often used are those of the common green bottle fly, Lucilia sericata, reared sterile for medical use.

Mechanism of action

Medicinal maggots act in three main ways. First, they debride wounds by secreting proteolytic enzymes that liquefy necrotic tissue, which they then ingest, while sparing healthy tissue. Second, they disinfect: their secretions contain antimicrobial substances, and they raise wound pH and ingest bacteria, reducing bacterial load including, in laboratory settings, MRSA. Third, their secretions and the mechanical stimulus of their movement appear to promote healing by stimulating granulation tissue formation. Larvae are applied either loose under a dressing ("free-range") or contained in a mesh bag ("BioBag"), typically for 48–72 hours per cycle.

History

The beneficial effect of maggots in wounds was observed in battlefields for centuries — including by Napoleonic surgeon Dominique Jean Larrey and during the American Civil War. After the First World War, orthopaedic surgeon William S. Baer at Johns Hopkins systematically developed the technique following his wartime observation that soldiers whose wounds were infested with maggots fared better than expected. Maggot therapy was widely used in the 1930s but declined with the advent of antibiotics and improved surgical debridement. Interest revived in the 1990s amid antibiotic resistance and the burden of chronic wounds such as diabetic foot ulcers and pressure sores. In 2004, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration cleared medicinal maggots as a prescription medical device.

Uses and evidence

Maggot therapy is used mainly for chronic wounds — diabetic foot ulcers, venous leg ulcers, pressure ulcers, and other wounds with necrotic tissue — and occasionally for infected wounds unresponsive to conventional treatment. Clinical trials, including the UK VenUS II trial, found that larvae debride wounds more quickly than standard dressings but do not shorten overall healing time and may cause more pain in some patients. Its use remains a matter of clinician and patient preference, and some patients decline it because of the "yuck factor" despite its efficacy at debridement.
