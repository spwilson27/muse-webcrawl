Proboscidea is the order of mammals that contains the elephants and their extinct relatives. The name derives from the proboscis, or trunk, that characterises the group. Only three living species survive — the African bush elephant, African forest elephant and Asian elephant, in the family Elephantidae — but more than 170 fossil species and subspecies have been described, making the order far more diverse in the past than today.

Evolution

The earliest proboscideans, such as Eritherium and Phosphatherium from the Paleocene of Morocco, were small animals without a full trunk. Later forms including Moeritherium were pig- to tapir-sized, semi-aquatic browsers. During the Oligocene and Miocene the order diversified into the long-jawed gomphotheres, the down-curved-tusked deinotheres, and the stegodonts. Proboscideans belong to the Afrotheria and, with sirenians and hyraxes, form the clade Tethytheria/Paenungulata.

Characteristics

Proboscideans are characterised by the fusion of nose and upper lip into a trunk, ever-growing incisors forming tusks in many lineages, large column-like limbs, and high-crowned cheek teeth that move forward through the jaw in a conveyor-like succession, most fully developed in elephants.

Extinction

The mammoths and mastodons of the Ice Ages survived until the end of the Pleistocene and, in the case of Wrangel Island dwarf mammoths, until about 4,000 years ago. Their decline is attributed to climate change and human hunting.
