Memento mori

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Memento mori (Latin for "remember that you [have to] die") is the medieval Latin Christian theory and practice of reflection on mortality, especially as a means of considering the vanity of earthly life and the transient nature of earthly goods and pursuits. It is related to the ars moriendi ("The Art of Dying") and the danse macabre. Memento mori is also an artistic and symbolic trope whose purpose is to remind the viewer of their own mortality and of the brevity and fragility of human life.

Origins

The concept has roots in classical antiquity: philosophers such as Plato and the Stoics treated the practice of death as essential to freedom, and according to tradition a slave in a Roman triumph would whisper to the victorious general words recalling his mortality. Early Christians adopted the theme; the phrase is linked to the Latin of the Vulgate and to monastic rules enjoining monks to keep death daily before their eyes.

In art

From the late Middle Ages, memento mori imagery proliferated: skeletons and corpses on tombs (the transi tomb), the danse macabre murals, and later the vanitas still-life paintings of the 16th and 17th centuries — with skulls, hourglasses, extinguished candles, wilting flowers and soap bubbles symbolising life's brevity. Jewellery and mourning objects — rings, pendants and watches engraved with skulls and mottos — made the reminder portable.

Modern usage

The phrase and its imagery survive in funerary art, Día de los Muertos celebrations, tattooing and popular culture, and the practice of reflecting on death has been revived in contemporary movements around "death positivity" and mindfulness.
