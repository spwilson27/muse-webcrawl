Lotharingia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lotharingia (Latin: Lotharii regnum, "Lothair's kingdom") was a short-lived Carolingian kingdom in Western Europe created by the Treaty of Verdun in 843 as the Middle Frankish realm, and reduced in 855 to the northern portion assigned to Lothair II, from whom it takes its name. It stretched from the North Sea to the Alps, encompassing much of the modern Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Lorraine in France, and the German Rhineland, with important cities such as Aachen, Cologne, Trier and Metz.

History

On the death of Lothair I in 855, the Treaty of Prüm divided Middle Francia among his three sons; Lothair II received the northern part, Lotharingia. When Lothair II died childless in 869, his uncles Charles the Bald and Louis the German partitioned the kingdom by the Treaty of Meerssen (870). Lotharingia thereafter became a contested borderland between the West and East Frankish kingdoms.

Later status

In 911 the Lotharingian nobility recognised Charles the Simple of West Francia as king rather than the East Frankish Conrad I, but in 925 Henry the Fowler secured the territory for East Francia, organising it as a stem duchy of the German kingdom. It was subsequently split into the duchies of Upper and Lower Lorraine. The name survives in the French region of Lorraine (Lothringen in German), and Lotharingia's position between France and Germany shaped the political geography — and conflicts — of the region for a thousand years.
