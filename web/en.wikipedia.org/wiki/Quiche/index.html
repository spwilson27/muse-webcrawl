Quiche

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A golden quiche Lorraine in its fluted tart tin, one slice lifting out]

A quiche is a French savoury tart of eggs and cream baked in a pastry crust, open-faced, its filling suspended in a just-set custard. The archetype is quiche Lorraine, from the Lorraine region of eastern France: shortcrust pastry, eggs, cream (traditionally no milk, and in strictest accounts no cheese), and lardons of smoked bacon. Its name betrays a borderland past — quiche comes from the Lorraine dialect word for cake, itself from German Kuchen — and the dish was village fare for centuries before Paris, then London and New York, adopted it in the 20th century.

History and fame

Larded egg tarts were baked in Lorraine by the 16th century, and the recipe settled in 19th-century cookbooks. Quiche became internationally fashionable in the 1970s, a dinner-party emblem of sophisticated French home cooking in Britain and America — fashionable enough to provoke a backlash immortalised in Bruce Feirstein's 1982 satire Real Men Don't Eat Quiche. The mockery backfired into endearment; quiche survived as bakery staple, picnic food and brunch anchor.

Variations

Add Comté or Gruyère and Lorraine purists object that you have made quiche vosgienne instead. Onions give quiche alsacienne its sweetness; spinach makes it "Florentine"; salmon and broccoli, leeks, roasted peppers and goat's cheese fill modern café cases. The rules that hold: blind-bake the crust so the base crisps, keep the custard ratio rich (roughly equal egg and cream by volume), season boldly, and serve warm, never scalding, with a green salad. Miniature quiches and crustless versions are concessions to parties and the pastry-averse.
