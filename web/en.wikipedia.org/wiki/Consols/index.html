Consols

Consols were the archetypal safe asset of the British investor for more than two and a half centuries.

Consols (originally short for consolidated annuities, later consolidated stock) were British government perpetual bonds — undated securities paying a fixed coupon forever, redeemable at par only at the government's option. First issued in 1751 under Chancellor of the Exchequer Henry Pelham, who consolidated several existing annuities into a single 3% stock, they became the cornerstone of the British national debt and of private saving throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries.

History

The original consolidation merged annuities bearing higher rates into "Three Per Cent Consolidated Annuities", administered by the Bank of England. Consols financed Britain's wars from the Seven Years' War to the Napoleonic Wars, and their yield became the benchmark "risk-free rate" of classical economics. Chancellor George Goschen converted the 3% stock to 2.75% (1888) and then 2.5% (1903), the famous "Two-and-a-half per cents". During the First World War new consols were issued at 4% (1917), and in 1932 Neville Chamberlain's celebrated conversion replaced 5% War Loan with a 3.5% issue, saving the Treasury millions annually.

Final redemption

By the 21st century the remaining undated gilts were small relics of the debt. In 2014–2015 Chancellor George Osborne announced the redemption of the last of them — including the 3.5% War Loan and the residue of the original consols — ending, after 264 years, the history of Britain's perpetual national debt. Because a perpetuity's price is simply coupon divided by yield, consols were also long used in finance teaching as the cleanest illustration of duration and interest-rate risk.
