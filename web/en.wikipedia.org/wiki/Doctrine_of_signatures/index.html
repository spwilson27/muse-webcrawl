Doctrine of signatures

The doctrine of signatures, dating from the time of the ancient physicians Dioscorides and Galen, states that herbs resembling various parts of the body can be used by herbalists to treat ailments of those body parts. A theological justification, as stated by botanists such as William Coles, was that God would have wanted to show men what plants would be useful for by marking them conspicuously.

History

The concept was developed by the Swiss physician Paracelsus (1493–1541), who proposed that nature marked objects with a sign (signatura) indicating their purpose. It was named, developed and popularised by the German mystic Jakob Böhme in his book The Signature of All Things (Signatura Rerum, 1621), and appears in the writings of herbalists such as Nicholas Culpeper. The doctrine was a feature of much traditional herbal medicine and folk medicine in Europe and elsewhere.

Examples

Classic examples include:

  • Lungwort (Pulmonaria), whose spotted leaves were thought to resemble diseased lungs, was used for respiratory complaints.
  • Walnuts, whose wrinkled kernels resemble the brain (and which come in a skull-like shell), were held to benefit the head.
  • Liverworts and Hepatica, with liver-shaped leaves, were used for liver ailments.
  • Eyebright (Euphrasia), whose flowers bear markings resembling an eye, was used for eye conditions.
  • The blood-red sap of Sanguinaria (bloodroot) suggested a use in disorders of the blood.

Criticism

The doctrine of signatures is regarded today as a form of sympathetic magic and a pseudoscientific belief. Resemblance is not a reliable guide to pharmacological action: some signature-based remedies happen to have real effects for unrelated chemical reasons, while others are useless or dangerous — birthwort, for example, suggested by its shape for use in childbirth, contains carcinogenic aristolochic acids. The doctrine nonetheless shaped plant names in many languages and remains of interest to historians of medicine and ethnobotany.
