Cinnamaldehyde is an organic compound with the formula C6H5CH=CHCHO. Occurring naturally as the predominant trans (E) isomer, it gives cinnamon its flavour and odour. It is a pale yellow, viscous liquid that occurs in the bark of cinnamon trees and other species of the genus Cinnamomum, where it constitutes the major component of cinnamon bark oil. It belongs to the class of phenylpropanoids and is biosynthesised via the shikimate pathway.

Synthesis and production

Cinnamaldehyde was first isolated from cinnamon essential oil in 1834 and synthesised in 1856. Industrially it is most often prepared by the aldol condensation of benzaldehyde and acetaldehyde, or obtained by steam distillation of cinnamon bark oil. It is slightly soluble in water and miscible with many organic solvents, and it slowly oxidises in air to cinnamic acid.

Applications

Cinnamaldehyde is used extensively as a flavouring in foods, chewing gum, ice cream, and beverages, and as a fragrance in perfumes and cosmetics. It has antimicrobial and antifungal properties and is investigated as an environmentally benign corrosion inhibitor, a fungicide in agriculture, and an insecticide and animal repellent. It is also a chemical intermediate in synthesis. In cell biology research, cinnamaldehyde is studied as an agonist of the TRPA1 ion channel, which mediates its pungent sensation.

Safety

Cinnamaldehyde is a known skin sensitiser and a common cause of allergic contact dermatitis from cosmetics and oral-care products; its concentration in consumer products is therefore regulated in some jurisdictions. It can irritate skin and mucous membranes in concentrated form.
