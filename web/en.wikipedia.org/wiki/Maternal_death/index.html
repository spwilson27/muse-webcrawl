Maternal death

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Maternal death or maternal mortality is defined by the World Health Organization as the death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes. The maternal mortality ratio (MMR) expresses maternal deaths per 100,000 live births.

Causes

The major complications that account for most maternal deaths worldwide are:

  • Severe bleeding (haemorrhage), mostly postpartum haemorrhage
  • Infections, usually after childbirth (puerperal sepsis)
  • Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, particularly pre-eclampsia and eclampsia
  • Complications from delivery, including obstructed labour
  • Unsafe abortion

Indirect causes — such as malaria, anaemia, HIV/AIDS and cardiovascular conditions aggravated by pregnancy — account for a substantial share, and their proportion is growing as direct obstetric deaths decline.

Global burden and trends

The WHO estimates that roughly 287,000 women died from maternal causes in 2020, with the great majority of deaths in low- and lower-middle-income countries; sub-Saharan Africa alone accounted for about 70 per cent. Globally the MMR fell by about a third between 2000 and 2020, to around 223 deaths per 100,000 live births, but progress has stalled in recent years and large disparities persist: a woman's lifetime risk of maternal death is around 1 in 49 in sub-Saharan Africa compared with roughly 1 in 5,300 in high-income regions. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals set a target of reducing the global MMR to below 70 per 100,000 live births by 2030.

Prevention

Most maternal deaths are preventable. Key interventions include access to contraception, skilled care during childbirth, timely emergency obstetric care (including caesarean section, blood transfusion and antibiotics), antenatal and postnatal care, and safe abortion and post-abortion care. Delays — in deciding to seek care, in reaching a facility, and in receiving adequate treatment once there (the "three delays" model) — are a common underlying pattern. High-income countries investigate maternal deaths through confidential enquiries, such as the long-running UK enquiry now run by MBRRACE-UK.
