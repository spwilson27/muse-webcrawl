Emerging adulthood and early adulthood

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Emerging adulthood is a proposed phase of human development between adolescence and full adulthood, spanning roughly the ages of 18 to 29, introduced by psychologist Jeffrey Jensen Arnett in 2000. Arnett argued that in industrialised societies the traditional markers of adulthood — finishing education, stable work, leaving the parental home, marriage, and parenthood — are now typically postponed into the late twenties, creating a distinct developmental period. Early adulthood follows, roughly the twenties and thirties, in Erik Erikson's stage of intimacy versus isolation.

Features of emerging adulthood

Arnett identified five features that characterise the period more than any other stage of life:

  • Identity exploration — trying out possibilities in love, work, and worldviews.
  • Instability — frequent changes of residence, job, education, and relationships.
  • Self-focus — freedom from social obligations, with decisions centred on oneself.
  • Feeling in-between — not yet feeling fully adult, yet no longer an adolescent.
  • A sense of possibilities — optimism that one's life can still be transformed.

Early adulthood

In early adulthood, typically from the late twenties through the thirties or early forties, most people consolidate careers, form long-term partnerships, and often become parents. Erikson framed the central developmental task as achieving intimacy without losing one's identity. Developmental psychologists note a peak of physical performance in the twenties followed by gradual decline, alongside major growth in expertise and social roles. Robert Havighurst and Daniel Levinson described stage-like "seasons" of adult life, though later research stresses variability across cultures and circumstances.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that emerging adulthood is a product of affluent, educated populations in wealthy countries rather than a universal stage, that its age boundaries are fuzzy, and that many young people who work full-time or raise children early do not experience it. Arnett replies that the theory describes demographic and psychological changes specific to societies where adult roles are delayed. The concept nevertheless reshaped developmental psychology, which had previously treated the age of 18 as the boundary between adolescence and adulthood.
