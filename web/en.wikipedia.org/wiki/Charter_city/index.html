Charter city
DefinitionMunicipality governed under its own charter rather than general state law
Common inCalifornia, Texas, Ohio, and other U.S. states
ContrastGeneral law city (Dillon Rule states)
Related conceptHome rule; proposed charter cities (special jurisdiction developments)

A charter city is a city whose system of local government is defined by its own charter, adopted by its voters, rather than by general law enacted by the state. In the United States, charter cities enjoy varying degrees of "home rule": on municipal affairs, the city's charter and ordinances can supersede conflicting state statutes, while matters of statewide concern remain subject to state law.

Charter cities adopt their own municipal constitutions

United States

States authorize charter cities in different ways. In California, a city may adopt, amend, or repeal a charter by majority vote; more than 120 California cities, including Los Angeles, San Francisco, and San Jose, are charter cities, with authority over matters such as the method of electing officials, municipal elections, and compensation of employees. Courts decide whether an issue is a "municipal affair" or a "statewide concern" when laws conflict. Texas home-rule cities gain broad authority to act unless prohibited by the state constitution or statutes.

Charter cities as a development proposal

The term acquired a second meaning from the economist Paul Romer, who from 2009 promoted "charter cities" as a development strategy: a host country would set aside uninhabited land for a new city governed under a special charter of rules, potentially administered in partnership with other countries, to attract investment and residents. A related Honduran law creating ZEDEs (Zones for Employment and Economic Development) enabled the private city of Próspera on Roatán; the ZEDE law was repealed in 2022, leading to legal disputes. Critics of the concept raise concerns about sovereignty, labor rights, and accountability.
