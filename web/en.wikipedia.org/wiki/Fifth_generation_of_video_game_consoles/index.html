Fifth generation of video game consoles

The fifth generation of video game consoles, also known as the 32-bit era, the 64-bit era, or the 3D era, began in 1993 with the release of the Fujitsu FM Towns Marty and Panasonic 3DO, and is best remembered for three dominant systems: the Sega Saturn (1994), the Sony PlayStation (1994) and the Nintendo 64 (1996). The generation marked the industry's transition from 2D sprites to polygonal 3D graphics and, for most manufacturers, from cartridges to optical discs.

History

Sony, a newcomer to the console market, launched the PlayStation in Japan in December 1994 and worldwide in 1995. Its CD-ROM format, low licensing costs, aggressive pricing and strong third-party support made it the first console to sell over 100 million units. Sega's Saturn, strong in Japan, faltered in the West after a surprise early launch and a higher price. Nintendo, arriving later with the cartridge-based Nintendo 64, retained a loyal audience with landmark titles such as Super Mario 64 and The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, but cartridges' limited capacity cost it key third-party releases, most notably Final Fantasy VII, which became a signature PlayStation title.

Other systems

The generation also included the 3DO Interactive Multiplayer, Atari Jaguar, Amiga CD32, NEC PC-FX, Bandai Pippin and the handheld Virtual Boy (1995), Nintendo's short-lived stereoscopic system. Handheld gaming was otherwise dominated by the ageing Game Boy, refreshed late in the generation by the Game Boy Color (1998).

Legacy

Fifth-generation consoles established 3D graphics as the industry standard, standardized the dual-analog/D-pad controller layout with built-in rumble, and shifted distribution to optical media. The era ended as the Sega Dreamcast launched the sixth generation in 1998–1999.
