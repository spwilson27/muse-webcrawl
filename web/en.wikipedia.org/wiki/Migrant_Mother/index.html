Migrant Mother is a 1936 photograph by Dorothea Lange that has become the most famous image of the Great Depression in the United States. It depicts Florence Owens Thompson, a mother of seven children, at a pea-pickers' camp in Nipomo, California, gazing into the distance with a worried expression while two of her children lean on her shoulders, their faces turned away. Taken for the Resettlement Administration (later the Farm Security Administration, FSA), the photograph was published in newspapers across the country and prompted the government to rush food aid to the camp.

Background

Lange was employed by the federal Resettlement Administration/FSA photographic unit under Roy Stryker to document conditions among migrant farmworkers displaced by the Dust Bowl and the Depression. In February–March 1936 she was returning home along California's central coast after a month of fieldwork when she passed a sign for the pea-pickers' camp at Nipomo.

The sitting

At the camp, Lange encountered a family sheltering in a lean-to tent whose breadwinner had gone to find work and whose crops had frozen, leaving them without food. She took a sequence of exposures — commonly described as five or six frames — progressively moving closer to Thompson and her children. Lange wrote in her field notes of "a hungry and desperate mother" and later recalled that Thompson cooperated, understanding that the pictures might help her situation. The tightest frame, showing Thompson's hand raised pensively to her face, became the iconic image.

Publication and impact

Lange rushed the negatives to her editors; the photograph was published in the San Francisco News on 10 March 1936, and the federal government sent some 20,000 pounds of food to the camp — though the Thompson family had by then moved on. The image was reproduced so widely that it came to symbolise the plight of Depression-era migrants.

Identity of the subject

For decades the sitter was anonymous. In the late 1970s she was publicly identified as Florence Owens Thompson (1903–1983), a woman of Cherokee descent born in Indian Territory (Oklahoma). Thompson expressed ambivalence about the photograph's fame, feeling it had brought her little benefit. In 1983, an appeal publicised in the press raised donations to help pay her medical bills in her final illness.

Legacy

The photograph is held by the Library of Congress and is in the public domain. It has been exhibited worldwide, issued on a US postage stamp (1998), and is frequently cited as a landmark of documentary photography. The original negative shows Thompson's thumb gripping the tent pole in the lower right corner; it was retouched out of many later prints at Lange's request.
