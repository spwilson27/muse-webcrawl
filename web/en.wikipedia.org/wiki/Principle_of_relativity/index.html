Principle of relativity

In physics, the principle of relativity is the requirement that the equations describing the laws of physics have the same form in all admissible frames of reference.

Galilean relativity

For example, in the framework of special relativity, the Maxwell equations have the same form in all inertial frames of reference. In the framework of general relativity, the Maxwell equations or the Einstein field equations have the same form in arbitrary frames of reference.

The idea dates to Galileo Galilei, who argued in his Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems (1632) that the uniform motion of a ship cannot be detected from within its cabin: experiments below deck give the same results whether the ship is at rest or moving smoothly. In Newtonian mechanics, inertial frames are related by Galilean transformations, under which the laws of mechanics are invariant.

Special relativity

Albert Einstein made the principle a postulate of his 1905 theory of special relativity, combining it with the constancy of the speed of light. Together these require the Lorentz transformations rather than the Galilean ones, leading to time dilation, length contraction, and the relativity of simultaneity. Einstein later extended the principle in general relativity (1915), demanding that the laws of physics take the same form in all frames of reference, inertial or accelerating — the principle of general covariance.
