An alternative trading system (ATS) is a trading venue in the United States that matches the buy and sell orders of its subscribers but is not regulated as a national securities exchange. Instead, ATSs are regulated by the Securities and Exchange Commission under Regulation ATS, adopted in 1998, which requires them to register as broker-dealers and comply with rules on fair access, order display, and — since the 2018 amendments (Regulation ATS-N) — public disclosure of their operations for venues trading listed stocks. The European equivalent category is the multilateral trading facility (MTF).

Types

ATSs include electronic communication networks (ECNs), which display orders publicly and match them automatically; crossing networks, which execute orders at a price derived from another market (such as the midpoint) at scheduled or continuous crosses; and dark pools, which do not display orders at all, allowing institutions to trade large blocks with reduced market impact. Some ATSs are operated by large broker-dealers for their own clients (single-dealer platforms), while others are independent or consortium-owned.

Significance and criticism

By the 2010s, ATSs and other off-exchange venues accounted for a large share — often around 40 per cent — of U.S. equity trading volume. Supporters credit them with competition, lower costs, and liquidity for institutional investors. Critics argue that widespread dark trading can harm price discovery on the public exchanges, create conflicts of interest (especially where brokers route client orders to their own pools), and favour high-frequency traders; regulators have brought enforcement actions against several dark-pool operators for misleading subscribers about who traded in their pools.
