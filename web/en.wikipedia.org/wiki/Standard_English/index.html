Standard English

Standard English (SE) is the variety of the English language that is used as the national norm in an English-speaking country, especially as the language of public institutions such as education, government, and the media. Unlike a regional dialect, Standard English is defined primarily by its grammar, vocabulary, and orthography rather than by pronunciation, although certain accents — such as Received Pronunciation in Britain — are associated with it.

Characteristics

Standard English is a relatively small, codified subset of English: it encompasses the forms described in grammar books, dictionaries, and style guides, and taught in schools. Any regional accent can be combined with Standard English grammar, and speakers frequently shift between standard and local forms depending on context (code-switching).

National varieties

There is no single worldwide Standard English. Major national standards include Standard British English, Standard American English, Standard Canadian English, and Standard Australian English, which differ modestly in spelling (e.g., colour/color), vocabulary, and some grammatical preferences. Debates continue over the status of newer standards such as Indian English and whether a "World Standard English" is emerging in international communication.

History

The standardization of English accelerated with the introduction of printing by William Caxton in the 1470s, the administrative use of Chancery English in the 15th century, and the publication of dictionaries such as Samuel Johnson's in 1755 and Noah Webster's American spellings in the early 19th century.
