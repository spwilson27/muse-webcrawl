Backlight

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A backlight is a form of illumination used in liquid-crystal displays (LCDs). As LCDs do not produce light themselves — unlike, for example, cathode-ray tube (CRT), plasma (PDP) and OLED displays — they need illumination (ambient light or a special light source) to produce a visible image. Backlights illuminate the LCD from the side or back of the display panel. LCDs that use a frontlight are less common; frontlights were widely used in earlier handheld devices such as the original Game Boy and in reflective e-paper readers.

Types of light source

Cold-cathode fluorescent lamps

Early LCD televisions and monitors used cold-cathode fluorescent lamps (CCFLs) as their light source. CCFL backlights were long-lived and bright but contained mercury, required high-voltage inverters, and have been almost entirely replaced by LEDs.

LED backlights

Modern displays use white light-emitting diodes. In edge-lit designs, LEDs sit along the edge of the panel and a light guide distributes the light across the screen, allowing very thin displays. In direct-lit or full-array designs, an array of LEDs behind the panel can be dimmed in zones ("local dimming") to increase contrast. Mini-LED backlights use thousands of smaller LEDs for finer control, while quantum-dot films can be added to widen the colour gamut.

Dimming and colour

Because the liquid crystal layer only modulates the backlight, the achievable contrast ratio of an LCD depends on how completely the crystals block light and how uniformly and dimly the backlight can operate. Pulse-width modulation (PWM) is often used to control brightness, and colour temperature can be tuned by blending phosphors or RGB LEDs.

Other uses

Backlighting is also used for illuminated keyboards, instrument panels, advertising light boxes and backlit signage, and as a photographic and cinematographic lighting technique in which the main light source is placed behind the subject.
