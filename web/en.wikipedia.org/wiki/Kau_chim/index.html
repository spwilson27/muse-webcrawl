Kau chim

Kau chim
Chinese: 求籤
Practice: fortune telling with numbered bamboo sticks
Regions: China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Vietnam, overseas Chinese communities

Kau chim (Chinese: 求籤, "requesting a stick"), also written kau cim, is a Chinese fortune-telling practice in which a querent shakes a cylindrical container of numbered bamboo sticks until one falls out. The number of the fallen stick corresponds to a written oracle — typically one of one hundred verses — which is then interpreted, often with the aid of a fortune teller or a temple keeper, as an answer to the querent's question.

Practice

The ritual is performed at Taoist and Chinese folk temples, famously at the Wong Tai Sin Temple in Hong Kong. The worshipper kneels, states a question silently, and shakes the tube (chim tong) until a single stick drops. The stick is exchanged for a paper slip bearing the matching oracular poem, whose classical allusions are interpreted as favourable, neutral or unfavourable. A related step uses moon blocks (jiaobei) to confirm that the answer has been correctly received.

Oracles

The standard set of 100 Wong Tai Sin sticks draws its verses from stories of historical and legendary figures; each poem is classified into grades from upper-upper to lower-lower. Interpretations are traditionally sold around major temples, and the practice remains popular at Lunar New Year.
