George Herbert Mead (27 February 1863 – 26 April 1931) was an American philosopher, sociologist and psychologist, associated with the pragmatist tradition and the University of Chicago. He is regarded as a founder of social psychology and of the approach later named symbolic interactionism.

Life

Mead studied at Oberlin College and Harvard, where he worked with William James and Josiah Royce, and spent several years in Germany. In 1894 he joined John Dewey at the new University of Chicago, where he taught for the rest of his life. He published relatively little in book form during his lifetime; his reputation rests largely on posthumous volumes edited from students' lecture notes, above all Mind, Self, and Society (1934).

Thought

Mead argued that the self is not innate but arises through social interaction. Mind emerges through communication by significant symbols — gestures, above all language, that call out the same response in the speaker as in the hearer. The self develops in stages: the play stage, the game stage, and finally the capacity to take the standpoint of the generalised other, the organised attitude of one's community. Mead distinguished two phases of the self: the "me", the internalised expectations of others, and the "I", the spontaneous, unpredictable response of the individual to the "me".

His social behaviourism sought a naturalistic account of consciousness grounded in the social act, influencing Chicago-school sociology and, through Herbert Blumer (who coined "symbolic interactionism"), much of twentieth-century microsociology.
