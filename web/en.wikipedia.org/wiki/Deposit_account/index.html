Deposit account

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A deposit account is a bank account maintained by a financial institution in which a customer can deposit and withdraw money. Deposit accounts may pay interest to the depositor. In law and banking practice, money deposited becomes a liability of the bank — the depositor is effectively a creditor of the institution — and the bank may use the funds for lending, subject to reserve and liquidity rules.

Types

  • Transaction accounts — current accounts (UK) or checking accounts (US), allowing unlimited deposits and withdrawals, cheque writing and debit-card payments
  • Savings accounts — paying interest, sometimes with withdrawal restrictions
  • Time deposits — fixed-term deposits such as certificates of deposit (CDs), with penalties for early withdrawal
  • Money market accounts — hybrid accounts paying money-market interest with limited transaction features
  • Call deposit accounts — accessible on short notice for larger balances

Demand and time deposits

Economists classify deposits as demand deposits (withdrawable on demand, part of the narrow money supply) and time deposits (held for a fixed term, counted in broader monetary aggregates).

Deposit insurance

Many countries insure deposit accounts against bank failure: in the United States the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) covers up to $250,000 per depositor per insured bank; the European Union guarantees €100,000; the United Kingdom's Financial Services Compensation Scheme covers £85,000. Such schemes were introduced, beginning with the FDIC in 1933, to prevent bank runs.
