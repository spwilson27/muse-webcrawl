Multiple myeloma (MM), or simply myeloma, is a cancer of plasma cells — the white blood cells that normally produce antibodies. The malignant clone accumulates in the bone marrow, crowds out normal blood formation, and secretes a single abnormal antibody (an M-protein or paraprotein). It is the second most common blood cancer after non-Hodgkin lymphoma, with a median age at diagnosis around 69.

Signs and presentation

Classic features are summarised by the acronym CRAB: hyperCalcaemia, Renal (kidney) impairment, Anaemia and Bone lesions. Myeloma cells stimulate osteoclasts, producing "punched-out" lytic lesions and bone pain — often in the back — and fractures; excess calcium from bone breakdown causes thirst, confusion and kidney damage. The paraprotein and its light chains (Bence Jones protein in urine) injure the kidneys, and suppression of normal antibodies leads to recurrent infections.

Diagnosis and spectrum

Nearly all cases arise from the benign precursor MGUS (monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance), which progresses at about 1% per year; an intermediate stage, smouldering (asymptomatic) myeloma, is watched rather than treated until end-organ damage appears. Diagnosis combines serum and urine protein electrophoresis with immunofixation and free light-chain assay, bone marrow biopsy showing ≥10% clonal plasma cells, and whole-body low-dose CT, MRI or PET imaging for bone disease. Cytogenetic abnormalities (such as t(4;14), del(17p) and 1q gain) define risk groups.

Treatment and outlook

Myeloma remains generally incurable but increasingly controllable. Modern first-line therapy combines a proteasome inhibitor (bortezomib), an immunomodulatory drug (lenalidomide) and dexamethasone — increasingly with the anti-CD38 antibody daratumumab — followed in eligible patients by high-dose melphalan with autologous stem-cell transplant. Newer options include other antibodies, the XPO1 inhibitor selinexor, bispecific T-cell engagers and BCMA-directed CAR T-cell therapies. Median survival has roughly doubled since the 1990s to around 6–10 years, varying strongly with age and risk group.
