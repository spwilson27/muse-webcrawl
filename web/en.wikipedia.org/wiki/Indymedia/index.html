Indymedia

Indymedia, also known as the Independent Media Center (IMC), is an open publishing network of activist journalist collectives that report on political and social issues. Founded during the 1999 Seattle WTO protests, it grew into a global network of more than 150 local Independent Media Centers. Indymedia's slogan, "Don't hate the media, become the media", expressed its model of participatory, non-corporate citizen journalism, and it pioneered the open-publishing newswire later echoed by blogs and social media.

History

The first Independent Media Center was established by activists to cover the protests against the World Trade Organization ministerial conference in Seattle in November–December 1999. Its website received hundreds of thousands of visits and its open newswire allowed protesters to publish text, photos, audio and video directly, bypassing mainstream media framing. The model spread rapidly: IMCs were set up for subsequent anti-globalization mobilisations — Washington DC (A16, 2000), Prague, Quebec City, Genoa (2001) and Cancún — and standing local centres formed in cities across the Americas, Europe, Africa, Asia and Oceania.

Principles and technology

Local IMCs operated as non-hierarchical collectives with consensus decision-making, and the network was coordinated through global working groups rather than a central office. Technologically, Indymedia collectives were early adopters of free software: the original "Active" codebase (developed in Sydney) powered open-publishing newswires, and collectives contributed to projects in free radio and video streaming. Content was typically published under open licences.

Controversies and state action

In October 2004, hard drives of an Indymedia server hosted in London were seized by the US company Rackspace after a request originating with US authorities, temporarily taking about 20 national sites offline — an incident widely criticised by press-freedom groups. Police also raided the Genoa IMC during the 2001 G8 summit; Italian courts later convicted officers for the raid on the Diaz school housing activists and journalists. Open publishing drew criticism for unmoderated offensive content, which collectives addressed through editorial policies of varying strictness.

Decline

From the late 2000s, activity waned as commercial social media absorbed much of the network's function and as the anti-globalization movement receded. Many local sites closed or became archives, though a number of centres — and the global site — continued operating into the 2020s.
