The blue-headed macaw or Coulon's macaw (Primolius couloni) is a small macaw native to the southwestern Amazon Basin of South America. It is named for the Swiss naturalist Paul Louis Coulon (1804–1894). Unlike most macaws it inhabits forest edge, clearings and disturbed habitats rather than deep primary forest.

Description

The species measures about 41 cm (16 in) in length. The plumage is mainly green, with a blue head and flight feathers, a maroon patch at the base of the upper tail, and a distinctive bare facial patch of dark grey skin around the eye. The bill is pale with a black tip, and the eyes are yellow. It is quieter than most macaws, with a purring call. The sexes are similar.

Distribution and habitat

The blue-headed macaw occurs in eastern Peru, northwestern Bolivia and the state of Acre in far western Brazil. It favours humid lowland forest edges, riverine forest, bamboo stands and partially cleared areas, up to about 1,550 m elevation. It often occurs in pairs or small groups and feeds on fruits, seeds and nuts.

Conservation

The species is classified as Vulnerable by the IUCN. It was rare in aviculture until the early 2000s, when wild-caught birds from Peru entered the trade in numbers, raising concern about trapping pressure; it is listed on CITES Appendix I. Habitat loss is a further threat, though its tolerance of disturbed habitat provides some buffer. Population estimates have ranged from about 10,000 to 70,000 individuals, and the population is thought to be declining.

Aviculture

Blue-headed macaws breed readily in captivity relative to other macaws and have become more common in collections since the 1990s. They are known for comparatively calm temperaments and lower noise levels among macaws.
