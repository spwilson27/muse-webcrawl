Karuna

Karuṇā (Sanskrit and Pali: करुणा) is a central concept in Indian religions, usually translated as "compassion". It denotes the heartfelt wish to relieve the suffering of others, coupled with the resolve to act on that wish. Together with loving-kindness (mettā/maitrī), sympathetic joy (muditā) and equanimity (upekkhā), it forms the four brahmavihāras ("divine abidings") of Buddhist practice, and it is a foundational virtue in Hinduism and Jainism as well.

In Buddhism

In Theravada Buddhism, karuṇā is cultivated as a meditative absorption directed towards beings who are suffering, and it is a quality perfected by Buddhas and arahants. In Mahayana Buddhism, compassion assumes even greater prominence: the bodhisattva vows to work for the liberation of all beings, and karuṇā, balanced by wisdom (prajñā), is regarded as indispensable to Buddhahood. The bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara (Guanyin in East Asia, Chenrezig in Tibet) is the embodiment of compassion. The Dalai Lama has frequently summarised his teaching with the phrase "my religion is kindness", rooted in this ideal.

In Hinduism and Jainism

In Hindu traditions karuṇā is one of the virtues associated with a sattvic character, praised in the Bhagavad Gita among the divine qualities, and embodied in devotional accounts of divine grace. In Jainism, compassion towards all living beings follows naturally from the supreme vow of ahiṃsā (non-violence): because every soul is capable of suffering, the Jain practitioner extends karuṇā to humans, animals and even the smallest organisms.

Distinctions

Karuṇā is distinguished from pity (anukampā in some usages, or mere sentimentality): classical commentators stress that genuine compassion is active, courageous and free of sorrow — sentimental grief at others' suffering is called its "near enemy", while cruelty is its "far enemy". Modern engaged Buddhism and compassion-based psychotherapies have adapted the practice for secular contexts.
