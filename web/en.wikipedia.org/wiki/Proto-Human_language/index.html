The Proto-Human language, also known as Proto-World or Proto-Sapiens, is the hypothetical direct genetic predecessor of all the world's spoken languages. It would be ancestral to all modern language families under the monogenesis hypothesis: the idea that human language originated once, with all contemporary languages descending from a single ancestral tongue spoken by early Homo sapiens.

The monogenesis hypothesis

Proponents such as Joseph Greenberg and Merritt Ruhlen argued that similarities among so-called "global etymologies" — proposed cognates found across many unrelated language families, such as roots for "water", "finger/one", and "mother" — hint at a common ancestor. Ruhlen's The Origin of Language (1994) popularized a list of candidate Proto-World words reconstructed by mass comparison.

Criticism

Most historical linguists reject the reconstruction of Proto-Human vocabulary. The comparative method, which reliably reconstructs proto-languages, is generally effective only over time depths of roughly 6,000–10,000 years, whereas the dispersal of modern humans and their languages began far earlier, perhaps more than 100,000 years ago. Critics argue that the proposed global etymologies can arise by chance resemblance, onomatopoeia, nursery words (such as "mama"), and borrowing, and that at such time depths any genuine signal has been erased by cumulative linguistic change.

Relation to the origin of language

The existence of a single ancestral language does not by itself settle how language evolved. Even if all living languages descend from one system, that system may itself have evolved gradually from earlier proto-language stages with limited vocabulary and syntax. Genetic studies of population history and genes such as FOXP2 are sometimes invoked in the debate but cannot directly identify linguistic ancestry.
