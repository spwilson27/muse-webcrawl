Imputed righteousness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Imputed righteousness is a concept in Christian theology proposing that the "righteousness of Christ" is attributed to (or "reckoned" as belonging to) Christians, enabling them to be justified in the sight of God. The doctrine is a central tenet of the Protestant Reformation, particularly in Lutheran and Reformed theology, and is closely associated with the doctrine of justification by faith alone (sola fide).

Doctrine

According to the teaching, sinful human beings cannot merit righteousness before God by their own works. Instead, through faith in Christ, believers are credited with Christ's perfect obedience and righteousness, just as their sins were imputed to Christ in his crucifixion — sometimes described as a "double imputation." Martin Luther called this an "alien righteousness" (iustitia aliena), a righteousness that comes from outside the believer.

Distinctions

Imputed righteousness is distinguished from imparted righteousness, in which God is understood actually to infuse righteousness into the believer, making them inwardly righteous. The Roman Catholic tradition, affirmed at the Council of Trent, teaches justification as a process involving both forgiveness and inner renewal, and formally rejected the purely imputational account. Within Protestantism, imputed righteousness is also paired with sanctification, the lifelong process of growth in holiness that follows justification.

Biblical basis

Proponents cite passages such as Genesis 15:6 ("Abram believed the LORD, and he credited it to him as righteousness"), Romans 4, 2 Corinthians 5:21, and Philippians 3:9, where Paul speaks of "the righteousness that comes from God on the basis of faith."
