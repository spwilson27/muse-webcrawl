Marko Mrnjavčević (c. 1335 – 17 May 1395), known in South Slavic epic poetry as Marko Kraljević (Serbian Cyrillic: Марко Краљевић, "Marko the King's Son"), was a Serbian king who ruled from Prilep after 1371 as an Ottoman vassal. Historically a relatively minor regional ruler, he became by far the most popular hero of Serbian, Bulgarian, and Macedonian folk epic — a superhuman champion who defends the weak against Ottoman oppression.

Historical Marko

Marko was the son of Vukašin Mrnjavčević, who had risen to become co-ruler and king under Emperor Stefan Uroš V. When Vukašin and his brother Uglješa were killed by the Ottomans at the Battle of Maritsa in 1371, Marko inherited his father's title and lands around Prilep in western Macedonia. Unable to resist Ottoman power, he became a vassal of Sultan Bayezid I, owing tribute and military service, while much of the old Serbian imperial territory was divided among stronger lords such as Prince Lazar. Marko died fighting on the Ottoman side against the Wallachians at the Battle of Rovine in 1395; Christian sources reported that before the battle he said he prayed God would help the Christians, even if he should be the first slain.

Epic hero

In the epic tradition Marko is transformed: he lives for centuries, serves the Sultan reluctantly and tricks him at every turn, and roams the Balkans righting wrongs. Endowed with immense strength, a huge mace, and the sabre of his father, he rides the wonder-horse Šarac, who shares his wine and speaks and laughs with him; his blood-brothers include the heroes Miloš Obilić and Relja, and his companions include the vila (fairy) Ravijojla. Famous songs recount his duels with Musa Kesedžija, the Arab champion, his conflict with his father and uncle, his ploughing of the Sultan's roads in protest at taxes, and his recognition of his father's sabre. In Bulgarian folklore he is Krali Marko, and in North Macedonia he is celebrated as Krale Marko; traditions around Prilep point out "Marko's Towers" (Markovi Kuli) above the town and rocks he supposedly split or threw.

Legacy

The cycle of Marko songs, collected by Vuk Karadžić and others in the nineteenth century, is among the masterpieces of oral epic, and Marko became a national symbol for Serbs, Bulgarians, and Macedonians alike — each tradition claiming him — as the embodiment of resistance to foreign rule and of rough, generous justice.
