Pyu language

Pyu
Region: Central Burma (Myanmar)
Era: c. 2nd–13th century AD; extinct
Family: Sino-Tibetan, Tibeto-Burman
Script: Pyu (Brahmic)

The Pyu language is an extinct Tibeto-Burman language once spoken by the Pyu people of the Pyu city-states in what is now central and northern Myanmar (Burma). It was the lingua franca of the Pyu realm until roughly the late eighth century, after which Burmese from the north grew dominant; Pyu survived as a classical, epigraphic language into the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.

Corpus and decipherment

Pyu is attested in inscriptions on stone, urns, votive tablets, and coins, written in a Brahmic script adapted to the language. The key to its decipherment came from the quadrilingual Myazedi inscription (c. 1113), which records the same text in Pyu, Pali, Mon, and Burmese, allowing scholars such as Charles Otto Blagden to unlock the language in the early twentieth century.

Characteristics

Known Pyu is a largely monosyllabic, tonal, analytic language with subject–object–verb word order — features typical of its relatives — though comparison is hampered by the small surviving corpus and the extent to which written Pyu had been archaic by the time of the latest inscriptions. Its precise position within Tibeto-Burman remains debated.
