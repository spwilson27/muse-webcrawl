Historical school of economics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The historical school arose among German and Austrian economists of the 19th century.

The historical school of economics was an approach to academic economics and public administration that emerged in the 19th century in Germany, and held sway there until well into the 20th century. The professors involved compiled massive economic histories of particular industries, regions, and nations, and argued that economic laws could only be discovered through historical and empirical study rather than through abstract deductive theory.

The school was divided into an "older" generation, led by Wilhelm Roscher, Bruno Hildebrand, and Karl Knies, and a "younger" generation centred on Gustav von Schmoller at the University of Berlin. The younger school engaged in the famous Methodenstreit ("dispute over methods") with the Austrian School of Carl Menger in the 1880s, over whether economics should seek universal theoretical laws or historically contingent regularities.

Origins and doctrine

The historical school grew out of German historicism and the legal-historical work of Friedrich Carl von Savigny, which treated institutions as products of a people's particular history. Against the "classical" economics of Adam Smith and David Ricardo, Roscher's Grundriss zu Vorlesungen über die Staatswirthschaft nach geschichtlicher Methode (1843) proposed that political economy should study the laws of economic development of nations in their concrete historical setting.

Members of the school were often associated with the Verein für Socialpolitik (Association for Social Policy, founded 1872), and were derided by liberals as Kathedersozialisten ("socialists of the chair") for advocating state intervention, social insurance, and factory legislation — policies partly realised in Bismarck's social legislation of the 1880s.

The Methodenstreit

In 1883 Carl Menger published his Investigations into the Method of the Social Sciences, a defence of theoretical, deductive economics that attacked the historical school's hostility to theory. Schmoller answered dismissively, and the ensuing quarrel hardened into a national and methodological divide between German historicism and Austrian marginalism. The controversy is generally seen as partly sterile, but it shaped the institutional development of economics in both language areas for decades.

Legacy

Figures influenced by the school include Max Weber, Werner Sombart, and Joseph Schumpeter, who studied in its milieu. Its emphasis on institutions, empirical description, and scepticism toward universal laws anticipates aspects of institutional economics and economic history as a discipline. Related figures include Friedrich List, whose "national system" of political economy the school took up, and the English historical economists such as William Ashley and Arnold Toynbee.
