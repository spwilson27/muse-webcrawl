Pelt

A pelt is the skin of an animal with its fur or hair still attached, especially when prepared for use in the fur trade. The term is used both for the raw, untanned skin removed from a fur-bearing animal and, in the trade, for a dressed (tanned) fur skin ready for making into garments. Major pelt-producing animals include mink, fox, sable, beaver, muskrat, raccoon, rabbit, chinchilla and various species of seal and sheep (where the wool-bearing skin is also called a shearling).

Production

After skinning, the pelt is fleshed (fat and flesh scraped away), stretched on a board or frame to a standard shape, and dried. Raw pelts are traded at international auction houses in Copenhagen, Helsinki, Toronto and elsewhere, then dressed — tanned by specialist processes that keep the fur intact — dyed or left natural, and cut by furriers into garments. Since the late 20th century most high-value pelts, particularly mink and fox, have come from animals raised on fur farms, while others, such as Canadian beaver and muskrat, are still largely taken by trapping under regulated seasons.

Historical role

Pelts were a major commodity of early modern commerce: the North American fur trade, driven by demand for beaver pelts for felt hats, shaped the exploration and colonisation of Canada and the northern United States, and Siberian sable and other furs were a principal revenue of the Russian Empire. "Soft gold" paid for posts, forts and entire trading companies such as the Hudson's Bay Company.

Controversy

The use of pelts has been widely criticised on animal-welfare grounds, leading to fur-farming bans in a number of countries, prohibitions on certain traps, and campaigns by organisations such as PETA. Sales of furs fell sharply in the late 20th century, partially recovering in some markets in the 2000s before renewed decline and legislative restriction. See also fur trade and tanning.
