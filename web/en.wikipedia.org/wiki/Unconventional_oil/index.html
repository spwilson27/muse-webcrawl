Unconventional oil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Unconventional oil is petroleum produced or extracted using techniques other than conventional vertical wells flowing under natural reservoir pressure. Because unconventional resources are trapped in low-permeability rock or in viscous forms that will not flow on their own, they require special methods such as horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing, mining and thermal treatment, or in-situ heating. Major categories include tight oil (shale oil), oil sands (natural bitumen), extra-heavy oil, oil shale (kerogen), and coal- and gas-to-liquids products.

Types and methods

Tight oil is extracted from shale and tight sandstone formations by drilling horizontal wells and fracturing the rock; the United States shale boom after 2008, centred on the Bakken, Permian, and Eagle Ford plays, transformed global markets. Oil sands, notably Canada's Athabasca deposits in Alberta and Venezuela's Orinoco Belt extra-heavy oil, are mined at the surface or produced in place with steam (steam-assisted gravity drainage), then upgraded into synthetic crude. Oil shale contains kerogen that must be retorted at high temperature to yield oil; large deposits exist in the Green River Formation of the western United States but have seen little commercial production.

Reserves and issues

Unconventional resources are enormous — the Orinoco Belt and Athabasca sands each hold more oil in place than the world's conventional reserves — but they are costlier, more energy-intensive, and generally more carbon-intensive per barrel than conventional production. Water use, land disturbance from mining, induced seismicity from wastewater disposal, and methane emissions are persistent environmental concerns. Higher prices and improved technology in the 2000s and 2010s made unconventional oil a decisive factor in world supply, with the United States becoming the world's largest crude producer largely on the strength of tight oil.
