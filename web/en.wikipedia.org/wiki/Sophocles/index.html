Sophocles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A Roman bust of Sophocles: the aged tragedian in the classical portrait type, calm and bearded]

Sophocles (c. 497/6 – winter 406/5 BC) was one of the three great tragedians of classical Athens, alongside Aeschylus and Euripides. He wrote more than 120 plays, won at least 18 victories at the Dionysia, and transformed Greek tragedy by adding a third actor, enlarging the chorus and perfecting plays of character and irony.

Of his tragedies seven survive complete: Ajax, Antigone, Women of Trachis, Oedipus Rex, Electra, Philoctetes and Oedipus at Colonus (produced after his death). Aristotle took Oedipus Rex as the model tragedy; Freud took it as the key to the psyche; Antigone — the sister who buries her brother against the king's decree — has been restaged by every generation since as the drama of conscience against power.

Life and Athens

Sophocles was a citizen of substance: he served twice as a general (strategos), once with Pericles, held priesthoods, and was chosen at 83 to help govern Athens after the Sicilian disaster. Tradition says he danced naked with a lyre as a boy celebrating Salamis, introduced his own plays' actors to painted scenery, and died at about 90 — by one famous tale, choking on a grape or expiring while reading Antigone aloud.

Craft

Where Aeschylus works in trilogies on cosmic themes, Sophocles builds single, tightly jointed plots: Oedipus's investigation turning upon himself is the original detective story in reverse. His choruses comment with lyric beauty and moral unease; his heroines — Antigone, Electra, Deianira — are among the strongest figures of ancient drama.
