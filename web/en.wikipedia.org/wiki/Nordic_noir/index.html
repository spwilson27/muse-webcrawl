Nordic noir, also known as Scandinavian noir, is a genre of crime fiction and television drama from the Nordic countries, characterised by bleak settings, muted naturalistic style, morally complex protagonists and a critical depiction of the Scandinavian welfare state. From the 1990s onward it achieved extraordinary international success in translation and on television.

Origins

The genre's founders are generally taken to be the Swedish husband-and-wife team Maj Sjöwall and Per Wahlöö, whose ten Martin Beck novels (1965–1975) used the police procedural to dissect Swedish society. Henning Mankell's Kurt Wallander series, beginning with Faceless Killers (1991), brought the form to a mass audience, followed by Jo Nesbø's Harry Hole novels from Norway and Arnaldur Indriðason's Inspector Erlendur books from Iceland. Stieg Larsson's posthumously published Millennium trilogy (2005–2007), featuring Lisbeth Salander, became a global publishing phenomenon.

Television

The Danish series The Killing (Forbrydelsen, 2007), with Sofie Gråbøl's Sarah Lund, and the Danish-Swedish co-production The Bridge (Broen/Bron, 2011), built around the Øresund Bridge and the detective Saga Norén, defined "Scandi noir" for international television audiences and inspired remakes in Britain, the United States and elsewhere. Icelandic series such as Trapped extended the genre's reach.

Themes

Common features include long dark winters and empty landscapes used as more than backdrop; weary, laconic detectives in the lineage of Raymond Chandler's Philip Marlowe; plots that expose corruption, xenophobia, misogyny and the gap between Scandinavia's egalitarian self-image and its realities; and a slow, observational realism comparable in spirit to post-war Italian neorealism.
