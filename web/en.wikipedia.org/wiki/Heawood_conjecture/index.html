Heawood conjecture

In graph theory, the Heawood conjecture or Ringel–Youngs theorem gives a formula for the number of colours needed to colour any graph drawn on a surface of a given genus — equivalently, the maximum number of colours needed to colour any map on that surface so that no two adjacent regions share a colour.

Statement

Percy John Heawood conjectured the formula in 1890, the same paper in which he exposed the flaw in Kempe's attempted proof of the four colour theorem. For a surface of genus g > 0, the conjectured chromatic number is:

H(g) = ⌊ (7 + √(49 − 24g)) / 2 ⌋ — usually written with the genus-related Euler characteristic χ as ⌊ (7 + √(49 − 24χ)) / 2 ⌋.

For the torus (genus 1) the formula gives 7 colours; for genus 2 it gives 8; and so on.

History and proof

Heawood himself proved that the formula is an upper bound: no map on such a surface needs more colours. The hard direction — showing that this many colours are sometimes necessary, by constructing complete graphs embedded in each surface — resisted proof for nearly 80 years. It was finally completed by Gerhard Ringel and J. W. T. Youngs in 1968, in one of the monumental case analyses of 20th-century combinatorics, splitting the problem into twelve cases according to the genus modulo 12, with contributions from many collaborators including Jean Mayer and Michael Guy.

The exceptional case

The formula is exact for every orientable surface except, famously, it fails to apply at genus 0 — the sphere, where the four colour theorem (proved in 1976) gives 4 rather than the formula's value. The Klein bottle is a genuine exception: the formula predicts 7 colours, but the correct number is 6, as shown by the Franklin graph. Every other surface — orientable or not — follows the Heawood numbers.

Significance

The Ringel–Youngs theorem is often called the natural generalisation of the four colour theorem to other surfaces, and paradoxically was proved first and by purely human reasoning, while the planar case required computer assistance.
