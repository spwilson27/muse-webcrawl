Truth value

Truth value
Fields:Logic, philosophy, computer science
Classical values:True (T/1), False (F/0)

In logic and mathematics, a truth value, sometimes called a logical value, is a value indicating the relation of a proposition to truth. In classical two-valued (bivalent) logic, every proposition is assigned exactly one of two truth values: true or false. Truth values are the objects manipulated by truth tables in propositional calculus, and they correspond in computing to the Boolean values 1 and 0.

Non-classical values

Many non-classical logics employ additional truth values. Three-valued logics add a third value, such as "unknown" or "indeterminate" (used, for example, in SQL's NULL semantics and in the logics of Kleene and Łukasiewicz). Intuitionistic logic rejects the law of the excluded middle, so truth is tied to provability. Fuzzy logic assigns a continuum of truth values between 0 and 1 to represent degrees of truth, while probability theory distinguishes the probability that a proposition is true from its truth value itself.

Notation and computation

Truth values are commonly denoted T and F, ⊤ and ⊥, or 1 and 0. In semantics, the truth value of a sentence relative to a model or interpretation is its denotation under an assignment of values to its parts; Frege held that sentences denote their truth values, "the True" or "the False". In digital electronics, truth values map onto voltage levels, and logic gates compute the truth values of compound statements.
