Bond order

In molecular orbital theory, bond order is a measure of the number of chemical bonds between a pair of atoms, defined as one half of the difference between the number of electrons in bonding molecular orbitals and the number in antibonding molecular orbitals: bond order = (Nbonding − Nantibonding) / 2. It indicates the stability of a bond: the higher the bond order, the stronger and generally the shorter the bond.

FieldChemistry, molecular orbital theory

Examples

The hydrogen molecule H2 has two bonding electrons and none antibonding, giving a bond order of 1 — a single bond. Dioxygen, O2, has a bond order of 2 (a double bond), and molecular orbital theory additionally explains its paramagnetism through two unpaired electrons in antibonding π* orbitals. Dinitrogen, N2, has a bond order of 3, consistent with its triple bond and exceptional inertness. The helium molecule He2 would have bond order 0, explaining why it does not exist under ordinary conditions, whereas the ion He2+ has bond order 0.5 and is observed in gas discharges.

Fractional bond orders

In molecules with resonance structures, such as benzene, the bond order between equivalent atoms can be fractional — 1.5 for the carbon–carbon bonds of benzene, reflecting delocalised π electrons. In more advanced treatments, bond orders computed from electron densities (for example Wiberg and Mayer bond indices) need not be integers and are used in computational chemistry to analyse bonding in complex molecules.

Relation to bond properties

Bond order correlates with bond length and bond dissociation energy: within a family of related bonds, higher order means shorter and stronger bonds. The C–C single bond (order 1, ~154 pm), C=C double bond (order 2, ~134 pm), and C≡C triple bond (order 3, ~120 pm) illustrate the trend.
