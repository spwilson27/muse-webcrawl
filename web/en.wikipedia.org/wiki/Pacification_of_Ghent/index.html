The Pacification of Ghent, signed on 8 November 1576, was an alliance of the provinces of the Habsburg Netherlands — both the loyalist southern provinces and the rebellious northern provinces of Holland and Zeeland — against the mutinous Spanish troops of King Philip II of Spain. It was a landmark moment of unity in the early years of the Dutch Revolt.

Background

By 1576 the Dutch Revolt against Spanish rule had been under way for nearly a decade. Holland and Zeeland, under the leadership of William of Orange, held out against Spain, while the other provinces remained nominally loyal to the king. The death of the Spanish governor Luis de Requesens in March 1576 left the Netherlands without effective government, and unpaid Spanish soldiers increasingly mutinied and lived off the land.

The Spanish Fury

The immediate cause of the Pacification was outrage at the atrocities of the mutinous tercios. On 4 November 1576 Spanish mutineers sacked Antwerp — then one of the richest cities in Europe — in the so-called "Spanish Fury", killing an estimated 7,000 people over three days of looting and arson. The horror of the sack drove the loyalist provinces into common cause with the rebels.

Terms

Negotiated principally by the States General in Brussels with delegates of William of Orange, and concluded at Ghent, the Pacification provided that:

  • All Spanish troops were to leave the Netherlands;
  • The provinces would unite to expel them by force if necessary;
  • Philip II was still recognised as sovereign, and the Catholic religion was to be maintained in the south;
  • In Holland and Zeeland, the religious settlement imposed by William of Orange (in practice favouring Calvinism) would stand until a future States General decided otherwise;
  • Repressive edicts against heresy were suspended, exiles could return, and confiscated property would be restored.

Aftermath

The union proved fragile. Religious conflict between Calvinists and Catholics intensified, especially after radical Calvinists seized power in Ghent in 1577. In January 1579 the southern provinces formed the Union of Arras and reconciled with Spain, while the northern provinces answered with the Union of Utrecht, laying the foundations of the Dutch Republic. The new Spanish governor, Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma, systematically reconquered the south. Nevertheless, the Pacification remains a symbol of Netherlandish unity against foreign domination.
