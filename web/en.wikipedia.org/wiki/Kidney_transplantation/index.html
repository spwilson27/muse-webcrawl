Kidney transplantation or renal transplantation is the organ transplant of a kidney into a patient with end-stage kidney disease. It is the most commonly performed type of organ transplantation worldwide and generally offers better survival and quality of life than long-term dialysis. Donor kidneys may come from deceased donors or from living donors, who can live normally with one healthy kidney.

History

Early attempts at renal transplantation were made in the first decade of the 20th century, including Alexis Carrel's vascular suture work. The first successful kidney transplant was performed on 23 December 1954 at the Peter Bent Brigham Hospital in Boston by Joseph Murray and colleagues, transplanting a kidney between identical twins Ronald and Richard Herrick, avoiding rejection without immunosuppression. Murray shared the 1990 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The introduction of azathioprine in the 1960s and ciclosporin in the late 1970s transformed outcomes for unrelated recipients.

Procedure

The donor kidney is usually placed in the recipient's iliac fossa (lower abdomen) rather than the anatomical position; the native kidneys are normally left in place. The renal artery and vein are anastomosed to the iliac vessels, and the ureter is implanted into the bladder. Living-donor nephrectomy is commonly performed laparoscopically.

Compatibility and rejection

Matching considers ABO blood group and HLA tissue typing, with a crossmatch test to detect pre-formed antibodies. Recipients take lifelong immunosuppressive therapy (typically a calcineurin inhibitor such as tacrolimus, an antimetabolite, and corticosteroids) to prevent acute and chronic rejection. Kidney paired exchange programmes allow incompatible donor–recipient pairs to swap donors.

Outcomes

One-year graft survival exceeds 90–95% in most modern programmes; living-donor kidneys typically function 15–20 years or longer. Complications include infection, cardiovascular disease, post-transplant diabetes and increased cancer risk from immunosuppression. Demand greatly exceeds supply: over 100,000 candidates are typically waiting in the United States alone, and tens of thousands of transplants are performed worldwide each year.
