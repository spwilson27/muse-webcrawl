Laguna Copperplate Inscription

Laguna Copperplate Inscription
Created: 900 CE (21 April 900, Saka era 822)
Discovered: 1989, near Lumban, Laguna, Philippines
Present location: National Museum of the Philippines, Manila
Language: Old Malay with Sanskrit, Old Javanese and Old Tagalog elements
Script: Kawi script

The Laguna Copperplate Inscription (Filipino: Sulat na Inukit sa Bingwit na Tumbaga ng Laguna) is an official acquittance or debt-relief document inscribed on a copper plate in the Saka year 822, corresponding to 21 April 900 CE. It is the earliest known dated written document found in the Philippines and provides key evidence of a literate, Indianised society in the archipelago centuries before Spanish colonisation.

Discovery and decipherment

The plate was found in 1989 by a worker dredging sand near the mouth of the Lumbang River in Lumban, Laguna. It was eventually acquired by the National Museum of the Philippines. The Dutch anthropologist Antoon Postma deciphered and translated the inscription in the early 1990s, with assistance from Hector Santos.

Contents

Written mainly in Old Malay using the Kawi script, with Sanskrit technical terms and some Old Javanese and Old Tagalog words, the document records that a man named Namwaran and his children, Lady Angkatan and Bukah, were cleared of a debt in gold (measured in kati and suwarna) owed to the Commander-in-Chief of Tondo. It mentions places including Tondo, Pila, Pulilan and Mda (possibly Medan), and names Dewata — evidence of links extending across maritime Southeast Asia, possibly as far as Sumatra and Java.

Significance

The inscription demonstrates the reach of Indian cultural and legal influence — Hindu deities, Sanskrit vocabulary, the Saka calendar and Indian weights — into the pre-colonial Philippines, and the existence of organised polities engaged in regional trade by 900 CE.
