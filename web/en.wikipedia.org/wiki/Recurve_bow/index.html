Recurve bow

A recurve bow is a bow with limbs that curve away from the archer when the bow is strung. A recurve bow stores more energy and delivers energy more efficiently than the equivalent straight-limbed bow, giving a greater amount of energy and speed to the arrow. A recurve will permit a shorter bow than the simple bow for a given arrow energy, and this form was often preferred by archers in environments where long weapons could be cumbersome, such as in brush and forest terrain, or on horseback.

Recurved limbs also put greater strain on the materials used to make the bow, and they may make more noise with the shot. Extreme recurves make the bow unstable when being strung; an unstrung recurve bow can have a confusing shape and many Native American weapons identified as bows were recurves mistaken for other implements.

History

The recurve design was known across the ancient world. Central Asian mounted peoples — Scythians, Huns, Mongols, Magyars and Turks — developed short, powerful composite recurves of wood, horn and sinew, ideally suited to horseback archery and carried in the wars that reshaped Eurasia. Assyrian and Egyptian art also depicts recurved bows. In the twentieth century the design was standardised in fibreglass and later carbon laminates; the modern "Olympic recurve", with a machined metal riser, stabilisers, a clicker and a sight, is the only bow style permitted at the Olympic Games.

Mechanics

When the bow is drawn, the recurved tips act as levers that straighten progressively, keeping the draw force rising smoothly and storing energy in the limbs for more of the draw than a straight limb of the same length. On release the string follows the recurve profile, delaying the fall-off of string tension and accelerating the arrow over a longer power stroke. The result is a flatter trajectory and higher arrow speed for a given draw weight, at the cost of more demanding tillering and greater sensitivity to brace height and string alignment.

Modern forms

  • Olympic recurve — takedown riser (typically 25 in), limbs to 70 in overall, draw weights around 30–50 lb, used with sights and stabilisers in target archery.
  • Barebow recurve — the same bow shot without sights or clicker, popular in field and 3D archery.
  • Traditional recurve — one-piece or takedown wooden bows in the mid-century American style, widely used for hunting where legal.
  • Asiatic recurve — reproductions of historical composite bows, shot with a thumb draw.
