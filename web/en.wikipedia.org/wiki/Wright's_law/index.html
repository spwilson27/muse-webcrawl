Wright's law

Wright's law, also known as the learning curve or experience curve effect, states that for many technologies, unit costs decline by a roughly constant percentage each time cumulative production doubles. It is named after Theodore Paul Wright, an American aeronautical engineer who described the effect in a 1936 paper on aircraft manufacturing costs.

Formulation

Wright observed that the labour cost of building an aeroplane fell by about 20% with each doubling of cumulative output. Formally, if the first unit costs C1, the cost of the n-th unit is C1nlog2(1−r), where r is the learning rate (the fractional cost reduction per doubling). A progress ratio of 80% corresponds to a learning rate of 20%.

Applications

Experience curves have been documented for semiconductors, solar photovoltaic modules, lithium-ion batteries, aircraft, shipbuilding and many other manufactured goods. Technology forecasters use Wright's law to project future costs of clean-energy technologies, since cost declines correlate more robustly with cumulative production than with time alone — a contrast with Moore's law, which is framed in terms of calendar time.

Explanations

The effect arises from workers becoming more proficient, process improvements, redesign for manufacturability, economies of scale, and cumulative innovation across a supply chain. It is an empirical regularity rather than a physical law, and rates vary widely by technology.
