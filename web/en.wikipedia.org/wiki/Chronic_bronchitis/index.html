Chronic bronchitis is a type of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) characterised by the long-term inflammation of the bronchi of the lungs and a productive cough that lasts at least three months in each of two consecutive years. It is caused mainly by prolonged exposure to irritants — above all tobacco smoke — which damages the airways and their mucus-producing glands.

Signs and symptoms

The defining feature is a chronic productive cough with sputum production. Other symptoms include wheezing, breathlessness (dyspnoea), frequent respiratory infections and, in advanced disease, cyanosis and peripheral oedema. Patients with predominantly chronic bronchitis were historically called "blue bloaters", in contrast to the "pink puffers" of emphysema-predominant COPD.

Causes

The overwhelming majority of cases are attributable to cigarette smoking. Other causes include long-term exposure to air pollution, biomass smoke from cooking fires, and occupational dusts such as coal, silica and grain.

Pathophysiology

Chronic irritant exposure causes hypertrophy and hyperplasia of the submucosal mucus glands (an increased Reid index), goblet-cell hyperplasia, and chronic inflammatory infiltration of the airway walls. The result is excessive mucus secretion, impaired mucociliary clearance and recurrent infection, with progressive airflow limitation.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on the duration and character of the cough; spirometry demonstrates obstructive airflow limitation and assesses severity.

Treatment

Smoking cessation is the single most effective intervention. Bronchodilators (beta-2 agonists and antimuscarinics) and inhaled corticosteroids relieve symptoms; pulmonary rehabilitation and vaccination against influenza and pneumococcus reduce exacerbations. Antibiotics are used for acute bacterial exacerbations. There is no cure; management aims to slow progression and improve quality of life.
