Kaizen (Japanese: 改善, "improvement") is a concept referring to business activities that continuously improve all functions and involve all employees, from executives to assembly-line workers. In the context of lean methodology, kaizen is generally understood to mean small, incremental, ongoing improvements to processes, as distinct from radical redesign.

Origins

The practice developed in Japanese manufacturing after the Second World War, drawing partly on American training programmes (such as Training Within Industry) introduced during the occupation and on the quality teachings of W. Edwards Deming and Joseph Juran. It became a core element of the Toyota Production System, where workers are encouraged to stop production to fix problems and to propose improvements (teian).

Methods

Kaizen activity typically follows the Plan–Do–Check–Act (PDCA) cycle and is supported by tools such as standardised work, 5S workplace organisation, visual management and root-cause analysis (the "five whys"). In Western firms it is often implemented through short, focused "kaizen events" or "kaizen blitzes" lasting a few days, though Japanese practice emphasises continuous small changes as part of daily work.

Spread and criticism

Masaaki Imai's 1986 book Kaizen: The Key to Japan's Competitive Success popularised the term internationally, and the approach has spread into healthcare, software development and personal productivity. Critics note that poorly implemented kaizen can become a pretext for work intensification or cost-cutting without corresponding benefits to employees.
