Semi-trailer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A semi-trailer attached to a tractor unit, forming a semi-truck or articulated lorry.

A semi-trailer is a trailer without a front axle. A large proportion of its weight is supported either by a road tractor or by a detachable front assembly known as a dolly. When coupled to a tractor unit by means of a fifth-wheel coupling, the combination forms a semi-truck (in North America) or articulated lorry (in the United Kingdom). The trailer's kingpin fits into the tractor's fifth wheel, transferring part of the load onto the towing vehicle — hence "semi".

History

The semi-trailer emerged in the early twentieth century as motor trucks replaced horse-drawn wagons; the fifth-wheel coupling, adapted from horse-drawn carriage practice, let a relatively small tractor haul a long trailer whose front rested on the tractor itself. Designs by August Fruehauf and others commercialized the concept from the 1910s onward, and the semi-trailer became the backbone of long-distance road freight transport.

Types

Common types include dry van box trailers, refrigerated ("reefer") trailers, flatbeds, tankers for liquids and gases, lowboys for heavy machinery, car carriers, curtain-siders, and container chassis. Standard lengths in North America are 48 and 53 feet; in Europe, semi-trailers up to about 13.6 metres are typical within a 16.5 m overall combination length.

Coupling and support

When detached, a semi-trailer is supported at the front by retractable landing gear legs. Coupling involves reversing the tractor so the fifth wheel engages the kingpin; air lines (gladhands) and an electrical cable connect the trailer's brakes and lights to the tractor. Converter dollies allow a second semi-trailer to be towed behind the first in road-train and doubles configurations.
