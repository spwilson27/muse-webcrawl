Thebes (Greek: Θήβα, Thíva) is a city in Boeotia, central Greece, and one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world. In antiquity it was the largest city of Boeotia and, in the 4th century BC, briefly the hegemonic power of Greece under generals such as Pelopidas and Epaminondas. Thebes plays a central role in Greek mythology as the setting of the stories of Cadmus, Oedipus, and Dionysus.

Mythology

According to myth, Thebes was founded by the Phoenician prince Cadmus, who sowed the teeth of a slain dragon and harvested the Spartoi, the "sown men" who became the Theban nobility. The city's royal house was haunted by the curse of Laius: his son Oedipus unwittingly killed his father and married his mother, Jocasta. Thebes was also the birthplace of Dionysus and of the hero Heracles, and the setting of Sophocles' Oedipus Rex and Antigone and Aeschylus' Seven Against Thebes.

History

Thebes was a major Mycenaean centre; excavations on the Kadmeia acropolis have uncovered a palace with Linear B tablets. In the classical period Thebes generally rivalled Athens and aligned with Sparta or Persia as advantage dictated. After the Peloponnesian War, the Theban generals Pelopidas and Epaminondas built the elite Sacred Band and shattered Spartan power at the Battle of Leuctra (371 BC); Theban supremacy ended with Epaminondas' death at Mantinea (362 BC). In 335 BC Alexander the Great destroyed the city after a revolt, sparing only temples and the house of the poet Pindar. Thebes was refounded in 315 BC and continued as a regional town through Roman, Byzantine, and Frankish periods; today it is a market town known for its archaeological museum.
