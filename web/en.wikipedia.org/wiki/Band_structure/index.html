Electronic band structure


Valence band, conduction band, and band gap in a solid.

In solid-state physics, the electronic band structure (or simply band structure) of a solid describes the ranges of energy levels that electrons may have within it, as well as the ranges of energy that they may not have (called band gaps or forbidden bands). Band theory derives these bands from the allowed quantum mechanical wave functions of electrons in a periodic lattice of atoms or molecules, generalising the discrete energy levels of isolated atoms.

Origins

When many atoms are brought together to form a crystal, their atomic orbitals overlap and split into a dense set of closely spaced levels — effectively continuous bands. The Pauli exclusion principle requires each state to hold at most two electrons, so electrons fill the bands from the bottom up. The highest occupied energy at absolute zero is the Fermi level.

Conductors, semiconductors, insulators

  • Metals (conductors) — a band is partially filled, or bands overlap, so electrons have empty states immediately above them and can move freely; electrical conductivity is high.
  • Semiconductors — the filled valence band is separated from the empty conduction band by a small band gap (about 0.1–3 eV, e.g. 1.12 eV for silicon), which thermal energy or doping can bridge.
  • Insulators — a large gap (several eV, e.g. about 5.5 eV for diamond) prevents electrons reaching conduction states under normal fields.

Band diagrams

Band structure is usually plotted as energy E versus crystal momentum (wave vector k) along high-symmetry directions of the Brillouin zone, producing the "spaghetti diagrams" used to identify direct and indirect band gaps, effective masses, and band extrema. In a direct-gap material (e.g. gallium arsenide) the valence maximum and conduction minimum share the same k, allowing efficient light emission — the basis of LEDs and lasers — whereas in silicon the gap is indirect and optical transitions require phonon assistance.

Calculation and measurement

Band structures are computed by methods ranging from the nearly free electron model and tight binding to density functional theory (DFT) and many-body GW calculations. Experimentally they are measured by angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES), optical absorption, and tunnelling spectroscopy.
