Max Wertheimer (15 April 1880 – 12 October 1943) was a Bohemian-born German psychologist who was one of the three founders of Gestalt psychology, along with Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler. He is known for his 1912 paper on the phi phenomenon, which marked the formal beginning of the Gestalt school, and for his posthumously published book Productive Thinking (1945).

Life

Wertheimer was born in Prague, then part of Austria-Hungary, into a German-speaking Jewish family. He studied law and then psychology at Charles University in Prague, and took his doctorate at the University of Würzburg in 1904 under Oswald Külpe, working on the detection of deception using word-association methods.

In 1910, at the University of Frankfurt, he conducted the famous experiments on apparent motion using a tachistoscope: when two lights are flashed in succession at the right interval, observers see a single light moving — the phi phenomenon — an experience that cannot be reduced to the sum of the two static stimuli. Koffka and Köhler served as his subjects and became his collaborators. The resulting paper, "Experimental Studies on the Seeing of Motion" (1912), launched Gestalt psychology, whose central claim is that perceptual wholes have properties not derivable from their parts.

During the First World War Wertheimer worked on sound-localisation devices. He became a professor at Frankfurt in 1929. After the Nazi seizure of power in 1933, Wertheimer, who was Jewish, emigrated to the United States, where he taught at the New School for Social Research in New York until his death in 1943. His son Michael Wertheimer also became a psychologist.

Gestalt principles

In his 1923 paper "Laws of Organization in Perceptual Forms", Wertheimer set out principles by which the mind groups elements into wholes: proximity, similarity, continuity (good continuation), closure, and common fate, governed by the overarching principle of Prägnanz — the tendency toward the simplest, most regular organisation available. These "Gestalt laws" remain standard material in perception research and design.

Productive thinking

Productive Thinking (1945), assembled from his manuscripts, applied Gestalt ideas to problem-solving and education, analysing episodes such as the young Gauss summing an arithmetic series and Einstein's path to relativity, and contrasting genuine structural understanding with rote, "reproductive" learning.
