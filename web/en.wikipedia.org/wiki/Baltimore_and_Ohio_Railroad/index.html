Baltimore and Ohio Railroad

The Baltimore and Ohio Railroad (reporting mark BO) was the first common carrier railroad and the oldest railroad in the United States. Chartered in 1827 and beginning regular service in 1830, it was founded by merchants of Baltimore who feared the city would lose trade to New York following the opening of the Erie Canal.

Early history

Construction began on 4 July 1828, with Charles Carroll of Carrollton, the last surviving signer of the Declaration of Independence, laying the first stone. The first section, from Baltimore to Ellicott's Mills, opened on 24 May 1830; early trains were horse-drawn before steam locomotives such as Peter Cooper's Tom Thumb were introduced. The line reached the Ohio River at Wheeling in 1853, fulfilling its founding ambition.

Civil War and expansion

During the American Civil War the B&O was a vital Union supply line and a frequent target of Confederate raids. After the war the railroad expanded west to Chicago and St. Louis. In the early twentieth century it came under the control of the Pennsylvania Railroad for a period, then regained independence. Financial difficulties in the 1960s led the Chesapeake and Ohio Railway to acquire control, and the B&O became part of the Chessie System and ultimately of CSX Transportation in 1987, when its corporate existence ended.

Legacy

The B&O is remembered as one of the four railroad properties in the standard American version of Monopoly. Its Mount Clare Shops in Baltimore now house the B&O Railroad Museum, and landmarks such as the Thomas Viaduct survive as monuments of early American railroading.
