Mahalwari system

The Mahalwari system was a land revenue system introduced in British India in the early 19th century, applied principally to the North-Western Provinces, parts of central India, and the Punjab. Under it, revenue was assessed on the mahal — a village or estate — rather than on individual cultivators or great landlords.

Design

The system was devised by Holt Mackenzie, whose memorandum of 1819 laid its theoretical basis, and put into operation by Regulation VII of 1822 and the more workable Regulation IX of 1833 under Governor-General Lord William Bentinck, with officers such as Robert Mertins Bird and James Thomason carrying out detailed field-by-field surveys (khasra) and records of rights. The village community, represented by its headmen (lambardars), was jointly responsible for paying the assessed demand, which was initially fixed at an onerous share — up to 83 percent — of the estimated rental value, later moderated.

Comparison with other systems

Mahalwari was a middle course between the Permanent Settlement of Bengal (1793), which settled revenue permanently with zamindars, and the Ryotwari system of Madras and Bombay, which settled directly with individual peasant proprietors (ryots). Periodic revisions, typically every 20–30 years, distinguished Mahalwari from the Bengal settlement.

Effects

The heavy initial assessments, rigorous measurement, and sale of estates for arrears caused widespread hardship and transfers of village land, though later settlements were more moderate and the system generated detailed agrarian statistics unmatched elsewhere in India.
