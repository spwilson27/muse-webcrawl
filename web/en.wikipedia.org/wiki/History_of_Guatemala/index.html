History of Guatemala

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Tikal, a great city of the Classic Maya civilisation.

The history of Guatemala begins with the Maya civilisation, whose Classic-period cities such as Tikal and Calakmul flourished in the Petén lowlands. Spanish conquest under Pedro de Alvarado in the 1520s made the region the Captaincy General of Guatemala within New Spain. Independence in 1821 was followed by conservative and liberal regimes, a democratic spring in 1944–1954 ended by a US-backed coup, a 36-year civil war, and the peace accords of 1996.

Pre-Columbian and colonial eras

Maya civilisation in the region dates back millennia; the Classic period (c. 250–900 CE) produced monumental cities, hieroglyphic writing and advanced astronomy. After the Classic collapse, highland kingdoms such as the K'iche' and Kaqchikel dominated until the Spanish invasion of 1524. Colonial Guatemala supplied the empire with indigo, cacao and later coffee, on land and labour extracted from Indigenous communities.

Independence and the liberal era

Guatemala joined Central American independence in 1821 and the short-lived Federal Republic of Central America. Rafael Carrera's conservative regime dominated until 1865. The Liberal Reform of Justo Rufino Barrios from 1871 expropriated Indigenous and church lands for coffee estates and built railways and telegraphs, largely for foreign — especially German and later American — planters. The United Fruit Company became a dominant economic power in the early 20th century.

Democratic spring and coup

The October Revolution of 1944 brought Juan José Arévalo and then Jacobo Árbenz to power. Árbenz's Decree 900 land reform expropriated uncultivated United Fruit land; in 1954 the CIA's Operation PBSuccess installed Carlos Castillo Armas, beginning decades of military-dominated rule.

Civil war and peace

From 1960 to 1996 the state fought leftist guerrillas, unified in the URNG. Counterinsurgency campaigns under Ríos Montt in the early 1980s were later judged by the UN-sponsored Historical Clarification Commission to have included acts of genocide against Maya communities; about 200,000 people were killed, most by state forces. Peace accords signed in 1996 ended the war, though Guatemala continues to struggle with poverty, corruption and organised crime.
