Pauli exclusion principle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of electrons filling atomic orbitals, no two sharing the same set of quantum numbers]
Electrons occupy distinct quantum states in an atom.

The Pauli exclusion principle is a quantum mechanical principle stating that two or more identical fermions (particles with half-integer spin) cannot occupy the same quantum state within a system simultaneously. Proposed by Austrian physicist Wolfgang Pauli in 1925 to explain atomic spectra, it is one of the most important principles in physics.

For electrons in an atom, the principle says no two electrons can have identical values of all four quantum numbers (n, ℓ, m_ℓ, m_s) — hence at most two electrons (spin up and spin down) per orbital. This single rule dictates the shell structure of atoms, the organisation of the periodic table, the chemistry of the elements, and the stability of matter; in astrophysics it supplies the degeneracy pressure supporting white dwarfs and (for neutrons) neutron stars. Bosons are exempt and can crowd into one state, enabling lasers and Bose–Einstein condensates.

Origin and general statement

Pauli formulated the principle in early 1925, before the discovery of spin, from spectroscopic data (the anomalous Zeeman effect and the closure of electron shells). In modern form it follows from the requirement that the Wave function of identical fermions be antisymmetric under exchange, which forces it to vanish when two fermions share a state. The spin–statistics theorem (Wolfgang Pauli, 1940) proved the connection between half-integer spin and antisymmetry within relativistic quantum field theory.

Consequences

Exclusion shapes chemistry: electron shells, valence, and the sizes of atoms all follow from it. Freeman Dyson and Andrew Lenard (1967–68) and later Elliott Lieb and Walter Thirring proved rigorously that exclusion is what makes bulk matter stable and extensive rather than collapsing. In metals it defines the Fermi surface; in semiconductors it underlies band filling; in dense stars it resists gravity up to the Chandrasekhar limit.
