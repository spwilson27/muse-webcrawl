William Gilpin

Born4 June 1724, Carlisle, Cumberland, England
Died5 April 1804 (aged 79), Boldre, Hampshire, England
OccupationClergyman, schoolmaster, writer, artist
Known forPopularising the idea of the picturesque

William Gilpin (4 June 1724 – 5 April 1804) was an English clergyman, schoolmaster, artist and writer, best known as one of the originators of the idea of the picturesque in landscape aesthetics. His illustrated tour journals of the British countryside shaped how a generation of travellers, painters and poets saw rural scenery.

Life

Gilpin was educated at The Queen's College, Oxford, and became a curate and then a schoolmaster, running a boys' school at Cheam in Surrey for some thirty years. In 1777 he became vicar of Boldre in the New Forest, where he remained for the rest of his life, endowing local schools with the proceeds of his publications and art sales.

The picturesque tours

During summer holidays Gilpin toured scenic parts of Britain — the Wye Valley, the Lake District, the Scottish Highlands, the New Forest and the south coast — sketching and writing accounts that he later published with his own aquatint illustrations. Observations on the River Wye (1782) was the first and most influential of the series. Gilpin defined picturesque beauty as "that kind of beauty which is agreeable in a picture": rough, irregular, ruin-fringed scenery rather than smooth formal landscapes.

Influence

Gilpin's tours helped create British domestic tourism and influenced landscape painting, garden design and Romantic poetry. His ideas were later debated — and gently satirised — by contemporaries including Uvedale Price and Jane Austen.
