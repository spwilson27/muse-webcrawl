A Japanese idol (アイドル, aidoru) is a type of entertainer marketed for image, attractiveness and personality in Japanese popular culture. Idols are typically young performers — solo singers or members of idol groups — produced and trained by talent agencies, who appear across music, television variety shows, advertising, modelling and acting. The idol industry emphasises the performer's growth and closeness with fans as much as polished artistry.

History

The phenomenon began in the 1960s and boomed in the 1970s with televised talent contests such as Star Tanjō!, producing stars like Momoe Yamaguchi. The 1980s are often called the "golden age of idols", led by Seiko Matsuda and the group Onyanko Club, produced by Yasushi Akimoto. Male idol groups from Johnny & Associates, such as SMAP and later Arashi, dominated television for decades. From 2005, Akimoto's AKB48 pioneered the "idols you can meet" concept, with daily theatre performances, handshake events and fan-elected line-ups, and spawned numerous sister groups across Japan and Asia.

Business model and culture

Agencies scout teenagers, train them (often as kenkyūsei, trainees) and tightly manage their public image; rules restricting dating have been common in the industry and have drawn criticism and occasional litigation. Revenue comes from music sales bundled with event tickets, merchandise, photobooks and endorsements. Fan practices include organised chanting (ōendan), glow-stick choreography (wotagei) and election-style popularity contests. A parallel underground (chika) idol scene operates in small live houses.

Criticism

Commentators criticise aspects of the industry — long contracts, image control and pressures on minors — while scholars analyse the idol as a distinctive media phenomenon related to kawaii culture and parasocial relationships. The Japanese system has influenced idol industries in South Korea (K-pop), China and Southeast Asia.
