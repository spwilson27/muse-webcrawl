Giant component

In network theory, a giant component is a connected component of a given random graph that contains a significant proportion of the entire graph's vertices.

More precisely, in graphs drawn randomly from a probability distribution over graphs, a giant component is a connected component whose fraction of the overall number of vertices is not vanishingly small as the size of the graph tends to infinity. The emergence of a giant component is the hallmark of a phase transition in random graph theory: below a critical threshold, all components are small (typically logarithmic in size), while above it, one component absorbs a positive fraction of all vertices.

Erdős–Rényi model

In the Erdős–Rényi model G(n, p), a giant component emerges when the average degree exceeds 1, that is, when p passes 1/n. For average degree c > 1, the giant component contains a fraction S of vertices satisfying S = 1 − e−cS, and all other components have size O(log n). This transition was first described by Paul Erdős and Alfréd Rényi in 1960.

Applications

Giant components appear in the study of percolation theory, the spread of epidemics on networks (where the giant component corresponds to the set of individuals reachable in a large outbreak), and the robustness of infrastructure and communication networks. The dual concept, the susceptibility of a network to fragmentation under node removal, is measured by how quickly the giant component breaks apart.
