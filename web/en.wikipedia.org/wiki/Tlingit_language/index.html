Tlingit language

The Tlingit language (Lingít) is spoken by the Tlingit people of Southeast Alaska and Western Canada. It is a branch of the Na-Dené language family. Tlingit is severely endangered: of about 10,000 ethnic Tlingit, only a few hundred native speakers remain, most of them elderly, though revitalisation programmes are under way.

Phonology

Tlingit is famous for its complex consonant system: it has a full series of ejective consonants and fricatives at multiple places of articulation, and — uniquely among most languages of the world — lacks labial consonants almost entirely in some dialects. Its four tones (in northern dialects) contrast high, low, rising and falling.

Grammar and writing

The language is polysynthetic, with complex verb morphology in which prefixes encode subject, object and aspect. A popular orthography was developed in the 1960s by linguists working with Tlingit speakers; earlier documents exist in a Cyrillic-based script from the Russian colonial period. Storytellers such as Nora Marks Dauenhauer and Richard Dauenhauer helped document and publish Tlingit oral literature.
