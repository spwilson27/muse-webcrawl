Millstone

Millstones or mill stones are stones used in gristmills for grinding wheat or other grains. A millstone pair consists of a stationary bedstone and a rotating runner stone above it, separated by a small gap. Grain is fed through a central hole (the "eye") of the runner stone, is drawn outward between the grinding faces by the pattern of grooves (furrows) cut into them, and emerges as flour at the rim, where it is collected.

History

Grinding stones are among the oldest human technologies: saddle querns were used from the Neolithic period, and rotary querns spread through the ancient world. The Romans developed large powered mills, and watermills and windmills driving millstones were the backbone of flour production through the Middle Ages and into the nineteenth century, until roller mills with steel cylinders largely replaced stones in industrial milling. Millstones are still valued in some artisanal and whole-grain milling.

Materials and dressing

Good millstone material is hard, tough, and slightly porous so the grinding edge stays sharp. Famous sources included French buhrstone, quarried in the Paris Basin and exported worldwide, as well as millstone grit in England and various local stones. The furrows must be periodically re-cut — "dressing" the stone — by a miller with special picks, a skilled trade judged by the feel of the flour and the ring of the stone.

Figurative use

Because of their great weight, millstones appear in proverb and scripture as a burden ("a millstone around one's neck") and as an instrument of punishment.
