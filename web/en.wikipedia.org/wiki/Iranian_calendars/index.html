The Iranian calendars are a succession of calendars used in Persia and Greater Iran over more than two millennia. The calendar in official use today is the Solar Hijri calendar (Persian: گاه‌شماری هجری خورشیدی), a solar calendar beginning at the March equinox whose epoch is the Hijra of Muhammad in 622 CE. Adopted by Iran in 1925 and by Afghanistan in 1957, it is regarded as one of the most astronomically accurate calendars in use.

History

Early Iranian peoples used calendars tied to seasonal and religious observances. Under the Achaemenid Empire an administrative calendar derived partly from Egyptian and Babylonian models was used, while the Zoroastrian religious calendar of 365 days — twelve months of thirty days plus five extra days (the Gatha days) — became standard in the Sasanian era. The scholar Omar Khayyam led the commission that produced the Jalali calendar in 1079 under Sultan Jalal al-Din Malik-Shah; based on precise observation at Isfahan, it calculated the year at 365.24219858156 days, remarkably close to the true value.

The Solar Hijri calendar

The modern calendar has twelve months: the first six have 31 days, the next five 30 days, and the last, Esfand, has 29 days (30 in leap years). The year begins at Nowruz, determined by the exact moment of the vernal equinox as calculated for the longitude of Tehran. Month names derive from Zoroastrian divinities, including Farvardin, Ordibehesht, Khordad, Tir, Mordad, Shahrivar, Mehr, Aban, Azar, Dey, Bahman and Esfand.
