The Cross of Sacrifice is a monument designed by Sir Reginald Blomfield for the Imperial War Graves Commission (now the Commonwealth War Graves Commission), which is present in Commonwealth war cemeteries containing 40 or more war graves. It is one of two principal architectural features, along with the Stone of Remembrance designed by Edwin Lutyens, that characterize the Commission's cemeteries of the First and Second World Wars.

Blomfield's design balances on an octagonal base and takes the form of a stark, elongated Latin cross of Portland stone, with a bronze longsword, blade down, superimposed on its front. Over a thousand Crosses of Sacrifice stand in cemeteries from Flanders to Gallipoli, North Africa, and the Far East.

Background

The Imperial War Graves Commission was founded in 1917, with Fabian Ware as its driving force, to commemorate Commonwealth service personnel who died in the First World War. Faced with hundreds of thousands of graves spread across many countries, the Commission resolved on uniformity of headstones and on permanent architectural focal points. Three architects — Edwin Lutyens, Herbert Baker, and Reginald Blomfield — were appointed as principal architects, and in 1918–1919 the Cross of Sacrifice and Stone of Remembrance were adopted as the standard central monuments.

Design

The cross is a plain, sharply profiled Latin cross, elongated so that its vertical member dominates. Down the face of the upright runs a bronze crusader-style longsword, point downward, its crossguard aligning with the arms of the cross. The cross stands on an octagonal plinth, which in turn rises from a three-stepped octagonal base. Blomfield produced the design in four heights — 14, 18, 24, and 30 feet (approximately 4.4, 5.5, 7.3, and 9.1 m) — so that cemeteries of different sizes could receive a proportional monument. Most were carved from Portland stone, though local stone was sometimes substituted.

Symbolism

The design deliberately unites the Christian cross with the sword of military service: sacrifice and soldiering in a single emblem. The downward-pointing sword signifies a weapon laid to rest, while the cross speaks to the faith of most (though not all) of the commemorated — a duality that occasionally caused debate, since the Commission commemorated men of many religions; in practice the Cross was treated as a general emblem of sacrifice and paired with the non-denominational Stone of Remembrance.

Placement

Generally, cemeteries with at least 40 war burials received a Cross of Sacrifice, and those with more than 1,000 burials also received a Stone of Remembrance, though exceptions abound. Prominent examples stand at Tyne Cot Cemetery in Belgium (the largest Commonwealth war cemetery), at Étaples in France, and at war cemeteries across Italy, Greece, Egypt, India, and Southeast Asia.
