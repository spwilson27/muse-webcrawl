HMS Dreadnought was a Royal Navy battleship, launched in 1906, whose design so revolutionised naval warfare that her name became the generic term for all subsequent battleships of her type — "dreadnoughts" — and rendered every earlier battleship ("pre-dreadnoughts") obsolete at a stroke. She was the first battleship to combine an "all-big-gun" main armament of a single calibre with steam turbine propulsion, giving her a crushing advantage in firepower, range and speed over any ship afloat.

Origins and design

Dreadnought was the creation of Admiral Sir John "Jacky" Fisher, First Sea Lord from 1904, and his Committee on Designs. Experience from the Russo-Japanese War, especially the Battle of Tsushima (1905), together with improvements in torpedoes and fire control, argued that future naval battles would be fought at ranges where only the largest guns mattered and where a mixed battery of different calibres made spotting the fall of shot nearly impossible. Fisher's committee therefore designed a ship with ten 12-inch guns and no intermediate armament, protected by an 11-inch armour belt.

Equally radical was her propulsion: Parsons steam turbines, in place of the usual reciprocating engines, gave her 21 knots — two or three knots faster than any existing battleship — with less vibration and better reliability. She was built at Portsmouth Dockyard with unprecedented speed: laid down on 2 October 1905, launched by King Edward VII on 10 February 1906, and at sea for trials within a year and a day of her keel-laying, a propaganda triumph of British industry (though the feat owed much to stockpiling material and using guns and turrets diverted from two other ships).

Impact

Dreadnought reset the naval arms race to zero. Britain's huge fleet of pre-dreadnoughts was devalued overnight along with everyone else's, and every major navy — Germany above all — began building its own dreadnoughts, fuelling the Anglo-German naval race that became a central cause of tension before the First World War. Critics, then and since, have argued that Fisher threw away Britain's numerical advantage by levelling the field; defenders reply that the type was inevitable and that Britain was best placed to win a building race. Battlecruisers — Fisher's even faster, thinly armoured offspring of the same idea — followed from 1908, and by 1914 the dreadnought had evolved into the fast, 15-inch-gunned "super-dreadnoughts" of the Queen Elizabeth class.

Service history

Dreadnought served as flagship of the Home Fleet from 1907 to 1912 and was flagship of the 4th Battle Squadron when war came in 1914. Her war was uneventful — she was in refit during the Battle of Jutland in 1916 — except for one singular distinction: on 18 March 1915 she rammed and sank the German submarine U-29, becoming the only battleship ever to sink a submarine. Superseded by newer ships, she was relegated to coastal defence duties with the Northern Patrol as flagship of the 3rd Battle Squadron, laid up after the war, and sold for scrap in 1921, being broken up at Inverkeithing by 1923.

Legacy

Few single ships have given their name to an entire era of technology. "Dreadnought" entered the language both as a class of warship and as a metaphor for anything fearsomely powerful, from the Martin Dreadnought guitar body shape (named for the battleship in 1916) to the dreadnoughts of science fiction. The ship's name was later carried by Britain's first nuclear-powered submarine, HMS Dreadnought (S101), launched in 1960.
