Chronospecies

A chronospecies is a species defined, within a single evolving lineage, for a particular segment of geological time. The concept arises in palaeontology when a lineage changes gradually and continuously through anagenesis — evolution without branching — so that earlier and later populations differ enough that palaeontologists assign them different species names even though no speciation (splitting) event occurred.

Concept

Because the fossil record preserves populations separated by time rather than by geography or reproductive isolation, the boundaries between successive chronospecies are necessarily arbitrary conventions: a point in the sequence is chosen where the accumulated morphological change is judged sufficient to warrant a new name. Chronospecies are widely used in the biostratigraphy and evolutionary study of lineages with dense fossil records, such as foraminifera, trilobites, ammonites, and mammal lineages — hominin evolution, with names like Homo habilis, Homo erectus, and Homo heidelbergensis applied along a partly continuous lineage, is often discussed in these terms.

Debates

The concept is debated because the named units do not correspond to species in the biological (reproductive) sense, and different researchers may split the same lineage differently, affecting counts of diversity and extinction. In cladistic practice, chronospecies are sometimes treated as segments of a single metaspecies, or renamed to emphasize that they are temporal subdivisions rather than separately evolving branches.

Related ideas

Chronospecies contrast with the results of cladogenesis, in which a lineage splits into two or more contemporaneous species. Closely related notions include the chronosubspecies and the use of successive "zones" in biostratigraphy, where first and last appearances of taxa mark intervals of geological time.
