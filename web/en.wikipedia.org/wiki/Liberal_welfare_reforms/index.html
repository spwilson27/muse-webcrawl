Liberal welfare reforms

The Liberal welfare reforms were a series of social reforms enacted in the United Kingdom between 1906 and 1914 by the Liberal governments of Henry Campbell-Bannerman and H. H. Asquith. Reflecting the philosophy of "New Liberalism", they introduced old-age pensions, labour exchanges, school meals and medical inspection, and a system of national insurance — laying the foundations of the modern British welfare state.

Background

The reforms came in response to growing concern about poverty in Britain, highlighted by the social investigations of Charles Booth in London and Seebohm Rowntree in York, which showed that around a third of the population lived in poverty caused by circumstances beyond individual control. The shock of poor physical health among recruits for the Boer War, and competition from the rising Labour movement, also pressed the Liberals toward state intervention. Key ministers included David Lloyd George as Chancellor of the Exchequer and Winston Churchill at the Board of Trade.

Major reforms

  • Education (Provision of Meals) Act 1906 and school medical inspections (1907), beginning state care for children's health.
  • Old Age Pensions Act 1908: non-contributory pensions for people over 70 with low incomes, first paid in 1909.
  • Labour Exchanges Act 1909: a national network of employment exchanges, alongside trade boards setting minimum wages in "sweated" industries.
  • People's Budget 1909: Lloyd George's budget raised taxes on high incomes and land to fund the reforms, provoking a constitutional crisis with the House of Lords that ended with the Parliament Act 1911.
  • National Insurance Act 1911: contributory insurance against sickness for workers and unemployment insurance for certain trades — the centrepiece of the reforms.
  • Further measures regulated shop hours, coal mines, and housing and town planning.

Significance

The reforms marked a decisive shift from laissez-faire toward collective provision and were the seed from which the post-1945 welfare state of the Beveridge Report and the Attlee government grew, though historians debate how far they reflected ideology, electoral calculation, or administrative pressure.
