Bullion coin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: A one-ounce gold bullion coin beside its mint tube]
A modern one-ounce gold bullion coin

A bullion coin is a coin struck from precious metal — usually gold, silver, platinum, or palladium — and kept as a store of value or an investment rather than for circulation. Bullion coins are sold at a modest premium over the spot price of their metal content. Although they often carry a nominal face value as legal tender, that value is far below the worth of the metal, so they do not circulate as money.

History

The modern bullion coin era is generally dated to South Africa's Krugerrand, first issued in 1967 to market South African gold. Other national mints followed, especially after the 1970s, as private gold ownership was re-legalised in the United States and demand for tangible precious-metal investments grew. Canada's Maple Leaf (1979), China's Gold Panda (1982), the American Gold Eagle (1986), and Austria's Vienna Philharmonic (1989) became major competing issues.

Well-known issues

  • Krugerrand (South Africa): gold, alloyed with copper for durability, containing one troy ounce of fine gold in a 22-karat coin.
  • Canadian Maple Leaf: among the purest issues, at 99.99 percent (and later 99.999 percent for some editions) fine gold; also struck in silver, platinum, and palladium.
  • American Gold Eagle: 22-karat gold sourced from United States deposits; accompanied by the American Silver Eagle and the Gold Buffalo in 24-karat gold.
  • Vienna Philharmonic (Austria): a leading European issue, also produced in silver and platinum.
  • Britannia (United Kingdom): capital-gains-tax advantages for UK investors have made it popular domestically.

Characteristics

Common weights are one troy ounce and fractions (1/2, 1/4, 1/10 ounce) for gold, and one ounce for silver, with larger sizes produced by some mints. Coins are typically supplied in mint tubes or sealed packaging. Security features such as micro-engraving, radial lines, and privy marks have been introduced to combat counterfeiting, which is a recognised risk when buying outside authorised dealer networks.

Collecting and investing

Investors value bullion coins for their liquidity, recognisability, and divisibility compared with large bars. Some issues, notably the Chinese Panda with its annually changing design, also attract collectors and can command premiums beyond metal value. Tax treatment varies by jurisdiction: in the United Kingdom, Britannia and Sovereign coins are exempt from capital gains tax for residents, while in the EU investment gold coins meeting certain criteria are exempt from VAT.
