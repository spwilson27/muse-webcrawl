Letter case

Letter case is the distinction between the letters that are in larger uppercase or capitals (or more formally majuscule) and smaller lowercase (or more formally minuscule) in the written representation of certain languages. The writing systems that distinguish between the upper- and lowercase have two parallel sets of letters: each in the uppercase set has a counterpart in the lowercase set. The two case variants are alternative representations of the same letter: they have the same name and pronunciation and are treated identically when sorting in alphabetical order.

Letter case is generally applied in a mixed-case fashion, with both uppercase and lowercase letters appearing in a given piece of text for legibility. When discussing individual letters, the terms uppercase and lowercase derive from the way in which type was stored in shallow storage drawers, called cases, in traditional typesetting: capitals were kept in the upper case, while the smaller letters were kept in the lower case.

History

The distinction between uppercase and lowercase originated with the scribes of the Middle Ages. Ancient Roman inscriptions used only capital letters. Over time, scribes developed faster, rounder handwriting styles for everyday documents, giving rise to uncial and half-uncial scripts, and eventually to the Carolingian minuscule promoted under Charlemagne. By the late medieval period, the two forms coexisted: capitals were used for emphasis and decoration, and minuscules for the body of the text. With the invention of printing, the bipartite system became standardized across Europe.

Usage

In English and most languages using bicameral scripts, the first letter of a sentence is capitalized, as are proper nouns and many derived adjectives. Capital letters are also used for acronyms and initialisms, for emphasis in headings, and in certain legal and ceremonial contexts. Rules differ between languages: German capitalizes all nouns, while French generally uses lowercase for languages and nationalities used as adjectives.

Case styles

  • Sentence case — only the first word and proper nouns capitalized.
  • Title case — principal words of a heading capitalized.
  • ALL CAPS — entire text in uppercase, often perceived as shouting online.
  • Small caps — lowercase letters replaced by smaller versions of capitals.
  • camelCase and snake_case — conventions in computer programming for multiword identifiers.

Unicameral scripts

Many writing systems, such as Arabic, Hebrew, Chinese, Japanese kana, Korean hangul, and Devanagari, have no letter case distinction at all; they are described as unicameral. Among bicameral alphabets are Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, Armenian, and Georgian (in its modern form).
