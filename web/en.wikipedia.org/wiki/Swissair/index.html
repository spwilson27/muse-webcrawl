Swissair AG was the national airline of Switzerland from its formation in 1931 until its collapse in 2002. Based at Zurich Airport, it was long regarded as one of the world's most prestigious and financially solid airlines, nicknamed "the flying bank" for its profitability and conservative management. Its grounding on 2 October 2001, when its aircraft were stranded worldwide for lack of cash to pay for fuel, was a national trauma in Switzerland.

History

Swissair was formed by the merger of Ad Astra Aero and Balair in 1931. It pioneered several European firsts: it was the first European airline to employ flight attendants (1934) and among the first to operate transatlantic services (to New York, 1947) and jet aircraft such as the Douglas DC-8 and Sud Aviation Caravelle. For decades it expanded steadily, acquiring stakes in other carriers and building a reputation for punctuality, service quality and financial strength.

Collapse

In the 1990s Swissair pursued an aggressive expansion — the so-called "Hunter strategy" — buying stakes in loss-making airlines including Sabena, LOT, Air Littoral and others, accumulating heavy debts. The downturn in aviation after the September 11 attacks in 2001 pushed the group into crisis: on 2 October 2001 Swissair's fleet was grounded, stranding tens of thousands of passengers. Flights resumed days later with emergency government and bank funding, and the airline's operations were transferred to its regional subsidiary Crossair, which was relaunched as Swiss International Air Lines in 2002 and later acquired by the Lufthansa Group. The collapse provoked criminal proceedings against former executives (who were acquitted in 2007) and remains a byword for corporate overreach in Switzerland.

Accidents

Swissair's most notorious accident was Flight 111, an MD-11 from New York to Geneva that crashed into the Atlantic off Nova Scotia on 2 September 1998 after an in-flight fire, killing all 229 people aboard. Earlier accidents included Flight 330 (1970), destroyed by a bomb near Würenlingen, and Flight 306 (1963).
