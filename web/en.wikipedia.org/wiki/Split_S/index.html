Split S

The split S is an air combat manoeuvre used to disengage from combat. The pilot half-rolls the aircraft inverted and then executes a descending half-loop, converting altitude into airspeed and emerging on a reciprocal heading, much lower and travelling in the opposite direction. The manoeuvre takes its name from the shape traced by the flight path, which resembles the letter S split in half.

Description

To perform a split S, the pilot rolls the aircraft to the inverted position, then pulls back on the stick so the nose drops through the horizon in a half-loop. The aircraft loses altitude rapidly while gaining speed. Compared with the related Immelmann turn — a climbing half-loop followed by a half-roll — the split S trades height for speed, while the Immelmann trades speed for height and position.

History and use

The manoeuvre was developed and used during the Second World War. It was a standard escape tactic for German pilots early in the Battle of Britain: RAF fighters such as the Spitfire and Hurricane used early carburetted Merlin engines that would cut out under negative g, so they could not simply follow a diving half-roll, although this disadvantage was later reduced by restrictor devices and fuel injection. In modern aerobatics and flight training the split S is taught as an introduction to vertical manoeuvring, with strict minimum entry altitudes because of the height consumed in the dive.
