Butterfly effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A butterfly on the left and a distant storm on the right, connected by a swirling line]

The butterfly effect is the idea that small causes can have large effects in chaotic systems — named for the notion that a butterfly flapping its wings in Brazil might, through chains of atmospheric influence, set off a tornado in Texas. The title comes from a 1972 talk by Edward Lorenz, the meteorologist whose 1963 weather model founded modern chaos theory.

Technically it refers to sensitive dependence on initial conditions: in chaotic systems, measurement errors grow exponentially, so long-term prediction becomes impossible even though the system is perfectly deterministic.

Misreadings

The effect does not mean everything is connected to everything, or that butterflies are unusually powerful — any small disturbance, including unmeasurably tiny ones, grows just as surely; the butterfly is a stand-in for the unknowability of exact initial conditions.

Lorenz discovered the phenomenon accidentally in 1961, when re-running a weather simulation with rounded numbers produced a completely different forecast.
