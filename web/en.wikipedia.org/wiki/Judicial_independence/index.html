Judicial independence

Judicial independence is the concept that the judiciary should be independent from the other branches of government — the executive and the legislature — so that judges can decide cases impartially, on the basis of law and facts, without improper influence, pressure, or interference.

Foundations

The idea is central to the doctrine of separation of powers, developed by Montesquieu in The Spirit of Law (1748). In common-law systems it is closely linked to the rule of law. Alexander Hamilton argued in Federalist No. 78 that life tenure and fixed salaries were needed to insulate federal judges.

Safeguards

  • Security of tenure — judges serve during good behaviour or until a fixed retirement age, removable only by impeachment or similar process.
  • Financial security — salaries that cannot be reduced while in office.
  • Institutional independence — court administration, budgets, and case assignment controlled by the judiciary itself.
  • Immunity from suit for judicial acts.

International standards

The United Nations Basic Principles on the Independence of the Judiciary (1985) and the Bangalore Principles of Judicial Conduct set global norms. Indices such as the World Justice Project Rule of Law Index attempt to measure de facto independence across countries.

Challenges

Threats include political appointment processes, court-packing, intimidation, corruption, budget cuts, and public campaigns against judges. Even in established democracies, debates persist over how to balance independence with accountability for misconduct and for decisions widely seen as activist or erroneous.
