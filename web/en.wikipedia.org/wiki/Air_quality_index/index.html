An air quality index (AQI) is a scale used by government agencies to communicate how polluted the air is and what health effects may be of concern. It converts measured concentrations of major air pollutants — typically ground-level ozone, particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, and nitrogen dioxide — into a single number and color-coded category, with the highest individual pollutant sub-index usually determining the reported value.

National systems

The United States AQI, maintained by the Environmental Protection Agency, runs from 0 to 500 in six categories from "Good" (green) to "Hazardous" (maroon). Other systems differ in scale and pollutants: Canada's Air Quality Health Index uses a 1–10 scale based on health risk; the European Air Quality Index uses five bands; China's AQI resembles the US scale but with different breakpoints; India's National AQI (launched 2014) has six categories and eight pollutants. Because breakpoints differ, the same air can receive different ratings in different countries.

Health guidance

Agencies pair index values with advice: at moderate levels, unusually sensitive people may be advised to limit prolonged exertion outdoors; at unhealthy levels, children, the elderly, and people with respiratory or heart disease are urged to avoid outdoor exertion; hazardous readings may prompt school closures and emergency measures.
