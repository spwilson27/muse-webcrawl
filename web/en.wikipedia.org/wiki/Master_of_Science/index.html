A Master of Science (Latin: Magister Scientiae; abbreviated MS, M.S., MSc, or M.Sc.) is a master's degree awarded by universities in many countries for study in the sciences, engineering, technology, mathematics, and related fields. It is typically a postgraduate degree situated between a bachelor's degree and a doctorate.

Structure

In the United States, Master of Science programs usually require one to three years of study beyond the bachelor's degree, combining advanced coursework with either a research thesis or a capstone project. In the United Kingdom and countries following its model, the MSc is commonly a one-year taught program, sometimes incorporating an independent research project or dissertation. Under the Bologna Process, master's degrees in continental Europe generally take one to two years and follow a three-year bachelor's degree.

Distinction from Master of Arts

The distinction between the Master of Science and the Master of Arts (MA) varies by institution. Traditionally the MS is awarded in scientific and technical subjects and the MA in humanities and social sciences, but the boundary is porous: economics, psychology, and data science may be offered under either title depending on the university and the program's emphasis.

History

Master's degrees originated in the medieval European universities, where the magister was licensed to teach. The modern research-oriented master's degree developed in the 19th century, particularly in German and American universities, as graduate education was formalized.
