First-magnitude spring
ClassificationMagnitude 1 (Meinzer scale)
Minimum discharge100 cubic feet per second (2,800 L/s); ≈64.6 million US gallons per day
Notable examplesSilver Springs, Wakulla Springs, Big Spring (Missouri)

A first-magnitude spring is a spring that discharges water at a rate of at least 100 cubic feet per second (2.83 m³/s), or about 64.6 million US gallons per day. It is the highest class in the magnitude scale proposed by Oscar Edward Meinzer in 1927, which ranks springs by average discharge from first magnitude down to eighth magnitude (less than 1 pint per minute).

Distribution

Large springs occur where extensive soluble-rock (karst) aquifers discharge at a single point. The U.S. state of Florida has the largest concentration of first-magnitude springs in the country — commonly counted at more than thirty — fed by the Floridan aquifer, including Silver Springs, Rainbow Springs, Wakulla Springs, Weeki Wachee Springs and the springs of the Suwannee and Santa Fe rivers. Other major examples include Big Spring in Missouri (among the largest in the United States, averaging roughly 286 million gallons per day), Mammoth Spring in Arkansas, and Giant Springs in Montana, one of the largest freshwater springs in the world by some measures.

Characteristics and ecology

Because their water comes from large aquifers and travels long distances underground, first-magnitude springs typically have remarkably constant temperature and flow, supporting distinctive ecosystems with high water clarity. Florida's spring runs are refuges for manatees in winter and support endemic snails, fishes and submerged vegetation. Many such springs have declined in flow or suffered nitrate enrichment from groundwater pumping and land use in their springsheds, prompting restoration and protection efforts, including designation of several as Florida state parks.
