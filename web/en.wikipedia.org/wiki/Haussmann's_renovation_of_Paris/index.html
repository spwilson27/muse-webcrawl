Haussmann's renovation of Paris was a vast public works programme commissioned by Emperor Napoleon III and directed by Georges-Eugène Haussmann, Prefect of the Seine, between 1853 and 1870. It transformed Paris from a largely medieval city of narrow, crowded streets into a modern capital of wide tree-lined boulevards, uniform stone buildings, new parks, and modern water and sewer systems. The renovation is frequently held responsible for the distinctive appearance of central Paris today.

Origins

By the mid-nineteenth century Paris was overcrowded, disease-ridden and difficult to govern. Cholera epidemics struck in 1832 and 1849; traffic barely moved through the medieval street network; and the dense old quarters were seen as breeding grounds for the barricade fighting that had marked the revolutions of 1830 and 1848. Napoleon III, influenced by the London he had seen in exile, wanted a capital that was healthier, more beautiful and easier to police. In June 1853 he appointed Haussmann Prefect of the Seine with instructions to carry out the transformation.

The works

Boulevards and streets

Haussmann's teams demolished whole districts to cut some 130 kilometres of new avenues through the city, including the Boulevards Saint-Michel, Saint-Germain, Haussmann and Voltaire, organised around monumental focal points such as the Place de l'Étoile and the new Opéra Garnier. Strict rules governed the height, alignment and stone façades of the buildings lining the new streets, producing the harmonious cream-coloured streetscape characteristic of Paris.

Water, sewers and parks

Engineer Eugène Belgrand built a double water-supply network and a vastly expanded sewer system, which by 1870 stretched to some 600 kilometres. Landscape architect Jean-Charles Alphand created or remodelled great parks — the Bois de Boulogne and Bois de Vincennes, the Parc des Buttes-Chaumont, Parc Montsouris and Parc Monceau — and hundreds of squares and tree-lined promenades.

Public buildings and annexation

The programme added markets (Les Halles), railway stations, theatres, hospitals, schools and churches. In 1860 Paris annexed the suburban communes between the old tax wall and the Thiers fortifications, expanding from 12 to 20 arrondissements.

Finance, criticism and legacy

The works cost roughly 2.5 billion francs and were financed through novel and controversial borrowing schemes criticised by figures such as Jules Ferry ("Haussmann's fantastic accounts"). Hundreds of thousands of workers were employed, but around 350,000 Parisians were displaced, and working-class residents were pushed toward the periphery. Haussmann was dismissed in January 1870, months before the fall of the Second Empire, yet construction continued under the Third Republic. His model of urban renewal influenced cities worldwide and remains debated by historians of planning.
