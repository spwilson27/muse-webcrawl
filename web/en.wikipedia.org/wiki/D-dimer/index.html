D-dimer (or D dimer) is a small protein fragment present in the blood after a blood clot is degraded by fibrinolysis. It is so named because it contains two D fragments of the fibrin protein joined by a cross-link. D-dimer testing is widely used in the diagnostic workup of suspected thrombotic disorders.

Formation

When a clot forms, thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin, and factor XIII cross-links the fibrin strands. Subsequent breakdown of the clot by plasmin releases soluble fragments, among them D-dimer, which is specific to the degradation of cross-linked fibrin; D-dimer therefore reflects both clot formation and clot dissolution.

Clinical use

The test's principal role is exclusion: in patients with a low or intermediate clinical pre-test probability of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE) — assessed with scores such as the Wells score — a D-dimer below the cutoff makes these diagnoses unlikely and avoids imaging. Because D-dimer rises in many other conditions (infection, inflammation, cancer, surgery, trauma, pregnancy, advancing age), a raised result is not diagnostic and requires confirmation by imaging. Age-adjusted cutoffs (age × 10 µg/L in patients over 50) improve specificity in older patients. D-dimer is also a component of scoring systems for disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and is used prognostically in some settings, including severe COVID-19.

Assays and units

Results are reported in fibrinogen equivalent units (FEU) or D-dimer units (DDU), which differ by roughly a factor of two; methods include ELISA, latex agglutination and point-of-care assays. High-sensitivity assays are required for the exclusion strategy.

History

D-dimer was described in the 1970s and monoclonal-antibody-based assays entered clinical practice in the 1980s–90s, with prospective management studies in the 1990s and 2000s establishing the safety of withholding anticoagulation after a negative result in low-probability patients.
