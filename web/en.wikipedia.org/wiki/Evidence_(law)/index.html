Evidence (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The law of evidence governs the proof of facts in legal proceedings: what material may be presented to a court, by whom, and with what effect. Its rules balance accurate fact-finding against fairness, reliability, efficiency and extrinsic policies (privilege, deterrence of police misconduct). Major topics include relevance, burdens and standards of proof, competence and compellability of witnesses, hearsay, character and similar-fact evidence, opinion and expert evidence, documentary and real evidence, and illegally obtained evidence.

Core doctrines

Evidence must generally be relevant and more probative than prejudicial; hearsay (out-of-court statements offered for their truth) is presumptively inadmissible in common-law trials, subject to many exceptions and to statutory reform; confessions and identification evidence attract special safeguards; and the exclusionary rule in the US (and discretionary exclusion elsewhere) can bar the fruits of unlawful searches. Modern practice is transformed by forensic science and digital evidence — DNA profiling (from 1984), CCTV, communications data — raising authentication, deepfake and algorithmic-evidence questions that the classical rules are being stretched to answer. Civil-law systems traditionally evaluate evidence more freely ("free evaluation of evidence"), relying on professional judges rather than exclusionary rules designed for juries.
