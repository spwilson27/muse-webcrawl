Temple Bar (Irish: Barra an Teampaill) is an area on the south bank of the River Liffey in central Dublin, Ireland. The area is bounded by the Liffey to the north, Dame Street to the south, Westmoreland Street to the east and Fishamble Street to the southwest. It is promoted as Dublin's "cultural quarter" and, as a centre of Dublin's nightlife, is a tourist destination.

Name and early history

The name is generally traced to the Temple family — Sir William Temple, provost of Trinity College in the early 17th century, had his house and gardens here — with "bar" in the older sense of a barred passage or riverside walkway (compare London's Temple Bar). In the 18th century the area was a centre of commerce and of ill repute; the first performance of Handel's Messiah took place in 1742 in Neal's Musick Hall on Fishamble Street, at the district's edge.

Redevelopment

In the 1980s the state transport company CIÉ planned a bus depot on the site and let buildings cheaply to artists and small businesses, accidentally creating a bohemian quarter. The depot plan was abandoned, and after European Capital of Culture year 1991 the government backed regeneration through Temple Bar Properties, adding cultural institutions, Meeting House Square, and new housing while retaining the cobbled streets and 18th-century fabric. The quarter's concentration of pubs later made it synonymous with stag and hen-party tourism, prompting continuing debate about balance and alcohol licensing.
