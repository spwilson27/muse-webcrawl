Cotton gin

A cotton gin — "gin" being short for "engine" — is a machine that quickly and easily separates cotton fibres from their seeds, a task previously done laboriously by hand. The modern mechanical gin was invented in the United States by Eli Whitney, who received a patent in 1794.

Cotton gin
InventorEli Whitney (1793–94)
PurposeSeparating cotton fibre from seed

How it works

In Whitney's saw gin, cotton is fed through a grid of wires while rotating saws with fine teeth pull the lint through openings too small for the seeds, and a rotating brush removes the lint from the teeth. Roller gins, used for long-staple cotton, use a leather roller and blade; roller gins of various kinds had existed in India and China for centuries.

Effects

The gin made short-staple upland cotton profitable across the American South. United States cotton production soared, feeding textile mills in New England and Britain — but the profitability it created greatly expanded the demand for enslaved labour and entrenched slavery, while also driving the removal of Native American peoples from cotton lands. Whitney's patent was widely infringed and litigated, and he profited less than legend suggests.
