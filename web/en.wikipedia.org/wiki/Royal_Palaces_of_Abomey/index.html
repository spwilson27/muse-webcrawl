Royal Palaces of Abomey

The Royal Palaces of Abomey are a group of earthen palace complexes in Abomey, in present-day Benin, built by the kings of the Kingdom of Dahomey between the mid-17th and late 19th centuries. Twelve kings in succession, from Houegbadja (c. 1645) to Agoli-agbo (1894), each built his own palace within a common walled enclosure, retaining earlier palaces as temples and ancestral shrines. The site, covering about 47 hectares (116 acres), was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1985 as a unique testimony to the vanished kingdom.

Description

Each palace follows a similar plan: a series of courtyards of increasing privacy, audience halls (jalalahen), temples, and the king's private apartments and tomb, constructed in banco — a mixture of earth, laterite and organic binders — with roofs of thatch or, later, corrugated metal. The palace walls are celebrated for their polychrome bas-reliefs: hundreds of moulded and painted clay panels depicting the symbols, proverbs and military exploits of each king — sharks for Kpengla, a buffalo for Ghezo, a lion for Glele — forming a visual chronicle of the dynasty.

The palaces of Ghezo (1818–1858) and Glele (1858–1889) survive in best condition and house the Historical Museum of Abomey, founded in 1943, displaying royal thrones, weapons, textiles including the appliqué banners of Dahomey, sacred objects (asan altars and bocio figures) and items relating to the female royal guard corps (the Mino, called "Amazons" by Europeans).

History and conservation

When King Behanzin was defeated by French colonial forces in 1892–94 he set fire to his palace before fleeing; the kingdom was abolished and the surviving structures fell into decay. The site was placed simultaneously on the World Heritage List and the List of World Heritage in Danger in 1985, owing to deterioration of the earthen fabric and damage from a 1984 tornado; following extensive conservation, documentation and re-roofing programmes, it was removed from the Danger List in 2007. Fires in 2009 and 2015 damaged some structures, underlining the fragility of earthen architecture. In 2021 France returned 26 royal treasures looted from Abomey in 1892, which are destined for display in a new museum at the site.
