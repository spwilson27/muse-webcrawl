Pas-de-Calais (French pronunciation: [pɑ d(ə) kalɛ], "strait of Calais"; Picard: Pas-Calés) is a department in northern France named after the French designation of the Strait of Dover, which it borders. It is part of the Hauts-de-France region. Its prefecture is Arras and its largest city is Calais.

Cap Blanc-Nez on the Côte d'Opale.

History

Pas-de-Calais is one of the original 83 departments created during the French Revolution on 4 March 1790. It was created from parts of the former provinces of Artois and Picardy and the formerly English exclave of Calais. The region was heavily fought over in both World Wars; the Canadian National Vimy Memorial, near Arras, commemorates the Battle of Vimy Ridge of 1917.

Geography

The department is bordered by the departments of Nord and Somme, the English Channel, and the Strait of Dover. Its coast, part of the Côte d'Opale (Opal Coast), faces the white cliffs of the English county of Kent across the strait — at its narrowest point only about 34 kilometres (21 miles) separate the two coasts. Major towns include Calais, Boulogne-sur-Mer, Arras, Lens, Liévin, and Béthune. The Channel Tunnel, opened in 1994, links the department at Coquelles, near Calais, with Folkestone in England.

Economy

Historically the department was a major centre of coal mining and textiles, centred on the Lens-Béthune basin; the last collieries closed in 1990. Today the ports of Calais and Boulogne-sur-Mer, cross-Channel transport, and logistics are significant employers, and Calais is the busiest passenger ferry port in France.

Tourism

Attractions include the Grand-Place of Arras, the Nausicaä national sea centre at Boulogne-sur-Mer, the Louvre-Lens museum (opened 2012), and numerous First World War cemeteries and memorials.
