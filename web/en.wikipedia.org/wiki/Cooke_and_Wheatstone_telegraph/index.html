The Cooke and Wheatstone telegraph was an early electrical telegraph system dating from the 1830s, invented by the English inventor William Fothergill Cooke and the scientist Charles Wheatstone. It was the first telegraph system to be put into commercial service anywhere in the world, preceding the better-known Morse system in practical deployment.

Development

Cooke, who had seen a demonstration of an experimental needle telegraph while studying in Heidelberg, recognised its commercial potential for railway signalling. Lacking the theoretical knowledge to perfect it, he partnered with Wheatstone, professor of experimental philosophy at King's College London. They received a joint British patent in June 1837.

The needle instruments

The original instrument used five magnetic needles that could be deflected left or right by currents in corresponding wires. Pairs of needles pointed to letters arranged on a diamond-shaped grid board, spelling out messages letter by letter. The system required five wires (plus a return) and could transmit 20 characters. Simplified two-needle and single-needle versions followed, reducing the number of wires required; the single-needle instrument, worked with a code of deflections, remained in British railway service well into the 20th century.

Railway adoption

The first installation ran along the Great Western Railway from Paddington station to West Drayton in 1838, and was later extended to Slough. The telegraph received national publicity in 1845 when it enabled the arrest of the murderer John Tawell, who had fled by train to Paddington; the message describing him could not include the letter J or properly spell "Quaker" in its original spelling on the five-needle grid, a famous early anecdote of the technology.

Legacy

Cooke went on to co-found the Electric Telegraph Company in 1846, the world's first telegraph company, which built a national network in Britain. Although Morse code systems eventually dominated internationally, the Cooke and Wheatstone needle telegraph established the practical and commercial foundations of electrical communication.
