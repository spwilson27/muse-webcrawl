Bacteriorhodopsin

Bacteriorhodopsin is a protein used by archaea, most notably haloarchaea such as Halobacterium salinarum, to convert light energy into chemical energy. It is a light-driven proton pump: when its retinal chromophore absorbs a photon of green light (maximum absorption near 568 nm), the protein pumps a proton from the cytoplasm to the extracellular side of the cell membrane, generating a proton gradient that the cell can use to synthesize ATP via ATP synthase. The process is a form of phototrophy unrelated to chlorophyll-based photosynthesis.

Structure

Bacteriorhodopsin is an integral membrane protein of 248 amino acids whose seven alpha-helices span the membrane, with a molecule of retinal bound via a Schiff base to a lysine residue (Lys216). In the cell it forms crystalline two-dimensional arrays called the purple membrane, which can make up much of the cell surface. Its structure, determined by Henderson and Unwin by electron crystallography in 1975, was the first structure of a membrane protein and became the prototype for the large family of seven-transmembrane (GPCR-like) proteins, though it functions as a pump rather than a receptor.

Photocycle

Absorption of light isomerizes the retinal from the all-trans to the 13-cis form, driving a series of intermediates (named K, L, M, N, and O) through which the Schiff base donates its proton to Asp85 on the extracellular side and is reprotonated from Asp96 on the cytoplasmic side, moving one proton across the membrane per photon before the retinal relaxes back.

Applications

Because it is exceptionally stable, bacteriorhodopsin has been studied for use in biomolecular electronics, holographic data storage, and artificial retinas; it is also a model system for membrane-protein biophysics and a conceptual relative of the microbial rhodopsins used in optogenetics.
