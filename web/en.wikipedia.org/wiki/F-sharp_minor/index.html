F-sharp minor

Relative majorA major
Parallel majorF-sharp major
Key signature3 sharps (F♯, C♯, G♯)
Component pitchesF♯, G♯, A, B, C♯, D, E

F-sharp minor is a minor scale based on F♯, consisting of the pitches F♯, G♯, A, B, C♯, D, and E. Its key signature has three sharps: F♯, C♯, and G♯. Its relative major is A major and its parallel major is F-sharp major (or enharmonically G-flat major).

Scale forms

The natural minor form of the scale is F♯–G♯–A–B–C♯–D–E. In the harmonic minor form, the seventh degree is raised, giving E♯ instead of E natural, which produces a leading tone pulling toward the tonic. In the melodic minor form, both the sixth and seventh degrees are raised when ascending (D♯ and E♯) and lowered again when descending.

Music in F-sharp minor

F-sharp minor was a relatively uncommon key in the Baroque and early Classical eras, partly because of tuning and instrumentation constraints, but it gained a distinctive expressive profile in the Romantic period, where it was often associated with melancholy, longing, or the sublime. Notable works include:

  • Joseph Haydn's Symphony No. 45 ("Farewell"), an early and famous use of the key.
  • Frédéric Chopin's Polonaise in F-sharp minor, Op. 44, and his Barcarolle's contrasting middle section associations with the key.
  • Robert Schumann's Piano Sonata No. 1 in F-sharp minor, Op. 11.
  • Alexander Scriabin's Piano Sonata No. 3 in F-sharp minor, Op. 23.
  • Sergei Rachmaninoff's Piano Concerto No. 1 in F-sharp minor, Op. 1.
  • George Frideric Handel and later composers used it sparingly; by the twentieth century it also appears in popular music and film scores.

Characteristics

On the piano, F-sharp minor's three-sharp signature places many of its notes on black keys, giving the hand a distinctive position. For string players, the key resonates differently than open-string-friendly keys such as D major or A major, contributing to its somewhat veiled reputation in orchestral writing.
