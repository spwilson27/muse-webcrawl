Phaser (effect)

A phaser is an electronic sound processor used to filter a signal by creating a series of peaks and troughs in the frequency spectrum. The position of the peaks and troughs of the waveform being affected is typically modulated by an internal low-frequency oscillator so that they vary over time, creating a sweeping effect.

Phasers are often used to give a "synthesized" or electronic effect to natural sounds, such as human speech. The effect has been standard equipment in guitar rigs since the 1970s and is heard on recordings by artists including Eddie Van Halen, Pink Floyd, and Tame Impala.

Structure

A traditional electronic phaser splits the audio signal into two paths. One path treats the signal with all-pass filters, which alter the phase relationship between frequencies while preserving their amplitude. The number of all-pass filters (usually called stages) varies; 4, 6, 8 and 12 stages are common. The filtered signal is mixed back with the dry signal, producing comb-filter-like notches where the two signals are out of phase. A low-frequency oscillator (LFO) sweeps the filters' phase shift up and down.

Similarity to flanging

Phasing is related to flanging: both mix a signal with a phase-shifted copy of itself. In flanging, the delay time is uniform across frequencies, producing evenly spaced notches; in phasing, the notches are unevenly spaced and fewer in number, giving a subtler, more "swirling" character. Classic units include the MXR Phase 90, the Electro-Harmonix Small Stone, and the Mu-Tron Bi-Phase.

Use

Beyond guitar, phasers are applied to keyboards, drums, vocals, and whole mixes. Film and television sound uses the effect for robotic or otherworldly voices. In electronic music, the phaser is a staple of psychedelic and space rock production.
