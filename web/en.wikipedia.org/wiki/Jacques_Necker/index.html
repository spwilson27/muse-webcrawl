Jacques Necker

Born: September 30, 1732, Geneva, Republic of Geneva

Died: April 9, 1804, Coppet, Switzerland

Offices: Director-General of Finance of France (1777–1781, 1788–1789, 1789–1790)

Spouse: Suzanne Curchod

Children: Germaine de Staël

Jacques Necker (September 30, 1732 – April 9, 1804) was a Genevan banker and statesman who served three times as France's chief finance minister under Louis XVI. His dismissals and recalls were key episodes in the coming of the French Revolution; his firing on July 11, 1789, helped provoke the storming of the Bastille three days later.

Banking career

Necker was born in Geneva, son of a law professor originally from Brandenburg. Sent to Paris at fifteen as a bank clerk, he rose spectacularly, becoming a partner in the bank of Thellusson and Necker, speculating successfully in grain and in French and British funds, and amassing a fortune. He represented the Republic of Geneva at the French court and married Suzanne Curchod, whose Paris salon became one of the most celebrated of the Enlightenment; their daughter was the writer Germaine de Staël.

Finance minister

As a Protestant foreigner, Necker could not hold the title of Controller-General, but from 1777 as Director-General of Finance he effectively ran French finances. He financed France's part in the American Revolutionary War entirely by borrowing, avoided new taxes, introduced modest administrative economies, and in 1781 published the Compte rendu au roi, the first public accounting of royal finances — a bestseller that (misleadingly) showed a surplus. Forced out in 1781, he was recalled in August 1788 amid financial collapse and doubled the Third Estate's representation in the Estates-General, though he failed to grasp the political crisis unfolding around him.

Revolution and retirement

Louis XVI dismissed Necker on July 11, 1789; Paris rose in response, and the king recalled him within days of the Bastille's fall. Returning in triumph, Necker found his popularity could not master the Assembly, the deficit, or the assignats, and he resigned in September 1790. He retired to his estate at Coppet, Switzerland, writing economic and political treatises, and died there in 1804.
