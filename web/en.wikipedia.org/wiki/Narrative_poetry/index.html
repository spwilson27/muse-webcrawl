Narrative poetry

Narrative poetry is a form of poetry that tells a story, often using the voices of a narrator and characters. It is among the oldest forms of literature: the earliest surviving poems, such as the Epic of Gilgamesh, Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, and the Indian Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa, are narrative poems.

Forms

The principal forms of narrative poetry include the epic, the ballad, the lay, and the idyll. Epics are long heroic narratives; ballads are shorter narrative songs traditionally transmitted orally; medieval lays, such as those of Marie de France, were sung by minstrels. Longer narrative poems may employ any verse form, including blank verse, heroic couplets, and terza rima, as in Dante's Divine Comedy.

History

Narrative verse dominated European poetry through the Middle Ages, in works such as Beowulf, the Song of Roland, and Chaucer's Canterbury Tales. In the 19th century, poets such as Byron, Pushkin (whose Eugene Onegin is a "novel in verse"), and Longfellow revived the form. Though the rise of the prose novel displaced narrative poetry as the dominant storytelling medium, modern and contemporary poets have continued to write book-length narrative poems.

Characteristics

Unlike lyric poetry, which emphasizes a single speaker's feelings or perceptions, narrative poetry privileges plot, character, dialogue, and setting, though it retains the compression, imagery, and musical qualities of verse.
