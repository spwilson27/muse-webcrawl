Jaundice

Scleral icterus — yellowing of the whites of the eyes
SpecialtyGastroenterology, hepatology, internal medicine
SymptomsYellow skin and sclerae, itching, dark urine, pale stools
CausesExcess bilirubin: liver disease, bile duct obstruction, hemolysis

Jaundice, also known as icterus, is a yellowish or greenish pigmentation of the skin and whites of the eyes (sclerae) due to high bilirubin levels in the blood (hyperbilirubinemia). Jaundice is a sign, not a disease in itself: it indicates problems with the production, processing, or excretion of bilirubin, the yellow pigment formed when old red blood cells are broken down. It becomes visible when serum bilirubin rises above roughly 2–3 mg/dL. The word derives from the French jaune, "yellow."

Classification and causes

Causes are conventionally divided into three groups. Pre-hepatic jaundice results from excessive red-cell destruction (hemolysis), overwhelming the liver's capacity. Hepatic (hepatocellular) jaundice arises from liver diseases such as viral hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, cirrhosis, and drug toxicity, or inherited disorders of bilirubin metabolism such as Gilbert's syndrome and Crigler–Najjar syndrome. Post-hepatic (obstructive) jaundice follows blockage of bile flow, for example by gallstones, strictures, or pancreatic cancer, and is typically accompanied by pale stools and dark urine.

Signs and diagnosis

Besides yellowing of skin and sclerae, jaundice may come with itching (pruritus), fatigue, abdominal pain, and weight loss. Evaluation uses liver function tests, the pattern of conjugated versus unconjugated bilirubin, ultrasound or other imaging of the biliary tree, and tests for viral hepatitis and autoimmune disease.

Newborn jaundice

Jaundice is extremely common in newborns, whose immature livers process bilirubin slowly; most cases are physiological and resolve within two weeks. Very high bilirubin levels can damage the brain (kernicterus), so significant neonatal jaundice is treated with phototherapy and, rarely, exchange transfusion.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause: antivirals or abstinence for hepatitis, endoscopic or surgical relief of obstruction, and supportive care in liver failure.
