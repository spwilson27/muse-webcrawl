Völkisch movement

PeriodLate 19th century – 1945
RegionGermany and Austria
IdeologyEthnic nationalism, racialism, antisemitism

The Völkisch movement (German: Völkische Bewegung) was a German ethnic-nationalist movement active from the late 19th century through the Nazi era. Built on the concept of the Volk — a people defined by blood, language, and ancestral homeland — it combined romantic nationalism, racial theories, antisemitism, and a rejection of liberal modernity.

Origins

The movement grew out of German Romanticism and the nationalist ferment that followed the Napoleonic Wars. Thinkers such as Johann Gottlieb Fichte had earlier exalted the German language and spirit. In the later nineteenth century, writers like Paul de Lagarde and Julius Langbehn fused cultural pessimism with demands for a national rebirth rooted in peasant life and Germanic tradition.

Ideas and organisations

Völkisch groups ranged from folk-dance and hiking associations to occultist circles and political leagues. Common themes included racial purity, the idealisation of the Nordic peasant, Germanic mythology, and hostility toward Jews, urbanisation, and materialism. Organisations such as the Pan-German League and the Germanenorden carried these ideas into the early twentieth century.

Relation to Nazism

After the First World War, the völkisch milieu radicalised; the small German Workers' Party, in which Adolf Hitler began his career, emerged from this scene, and the Thule Society bridged occult völkisch circles and early Nazism. Many völkisch ideas — blood and soil, the Führer principle, racial antisemitism — were absorbed into Nazi ideology, though independent völkisch groups were dissolved or sidelined after 1933.

Legacy

Historians regard the völkisch movement as a key ideological precursor of Nazism. After 1945 the term fell into disrepute, though scholars continue to study the movement's influence on German far-right politics.
