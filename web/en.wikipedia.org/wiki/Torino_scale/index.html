Torino scale

TypeImpact hazard scale
Range0 (no hazard) to 10 (certain global catastrophe)
Created byRichard P. Binzel
Adopted1999, Turin, Italy

The Torino scale is a method for categorising the impact hazard associated with near-Earth objects such as asteroids and comets. It uses an integer scale from 0 to 10, combining the probability of collision with the kinetic energy of the possible impact, and is intended to communicate risk to the public in simple colour-coded terms.

The scale

A value of 0 means the object has a negligible chance of collision or is too small to survive passage through the atmosphere. Values 1 (green) denote routine discoveries meriting careful monitoring; 2–4 (yellow) are events meriting concern; 5–7 (orange) are threatening events; and 8–10 (red) represent certain collisions with local, regional or global consequences. The scale was created by Richard P. Binzel of MIT and was adopted at a conference in Turin (Torino), Italy, in 1999; a revised wording was adopted in 2005.

Notable ratings

No object has ever sustained a high rating for long, since further observations usually refine the orbit and eliminate the risk. The asteroid 99942 Apophis briefly reached level 4 in December 2004 — the highest rating ever given — before later observations ruled out an impact in 2029.
