Fire making

Fire making Methods: friction (hand drill, bow drill, fire plough), percussion (flint and steel), solar, electrical, chemical
Key materials: tinder, kindling, fuel
Earliest evidence: c. 1 million years ago (debated)

Fire making, the art of starting a fire, is the deliberate ignition of combustible material for warmth, cooking, light, signalling, or protection. Controlled use of fire was a major milestone in human evolution, enabling cooking—which increases the energy available from food—colonisation of colder climates, and protection from predators.

Friction methods

Friction methods generate an ember by rubbing wood on wood. In the hand drill, a spindle is rotated between the palms against a hearth board; the bow drill uses a bow to spin the spindle, increasing speed and pressure. The fire plough rubs a stick along a groove in a base board. Fine, dry tinder such as char cloth, bark fibres, or fungus catches the ember, which is then blown into flame.

Percussion and other methods

Striking flint or another hard stone against steel produces sparks that can ignite prepared tinder; flint-and-steel kits were standard fire-making tools for centuries. Other methods include focusing sunlight with a lens or parabolic mirror, compressing air in a fire piston, electrical sparks, and chemical reactions. Modern equivalents include matches, lighters, and ferrocerium rods, widely used in bushcraft and survival.

History

Evidence for controlled fire extends back at least 400,000 years, with some claims—such as at Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa—approaching one million years. Regular, habitual fire use became widespread among early humans during the Middle Palaeolithic.
