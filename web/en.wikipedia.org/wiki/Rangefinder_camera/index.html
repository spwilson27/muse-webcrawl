A rangefinder camera is a camera fitted with a rangefinder — a focusing mechanism that lets the photographer measure the distance to the subject and focus the lens accordingly, usually via a coincident or split image in the viewfinder. The viewfinder is separate from the lens, so what the photographer sees is not exactly what the lens captures (parallax).

History

The first practical 35 mm rangefinder cameras were the Leica I (1925) and the Contax I (1932). Rangefinders dominated professional and press photography through the 1950s, exemplified by the Leica M3 (1954), before single-lens reflex cameras took over in the 1960s and 1970s.

How it works

A coupled rangefinder links the focusing ring to a mechanism that superimposes two images of the subject; when the images coincide, focus is correct. The effective base length of the rangefinder determines focusing precision, which is why wide-angle lenses focus easily on rangefinders while long telephotos are difficult.

Advantages and limitations

Rangefinders are compact, quiet (no mirror slap), and allow the photographer to see outside the frame lines, aiding composition and anticipation. Limitations include parallax error at close distances, difficulty focusing very long or very fast lenses precisely, and the inability to preview depth of field.

Digital rangefinders

Leica continues the tradition with the digital M series (M8 through M11), combining a mechanical rangefinder with a full-frame digital sensor. The format remains popular for street and documentary photography.
