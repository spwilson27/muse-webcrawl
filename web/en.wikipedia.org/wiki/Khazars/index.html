Khazars

The Khazars were a semi-nomadic Turkic people who, from the 7th to the 10th centuries, ruled a large empire spanning the steppes between the Black Sea, the Caspian Sea and the Caucasus. Their state, the Khazar Khaganate, was a major commercial power linking the trade routes of the Silk Road with those of the Volga. The Khazars are noted for the conversion of their ruling class to Judaism, probably beginning in the 8th or 9th century — a rare instance of a major pre-modern state adopting Judaism.

History

The Khazars emerged from the Western Turkic Khaganate in the 7th century. From their base north of the Caucasus they fought a long series of wars with the Arab Caliphate, halting Arab expansion into Eastern Europe; the Second Arab–Khazar War (722–737) ended after the Khazar defeat at Marj Ardabil and the Arab campaign of Marwan ibn Muhammad. Thereafter Khazaria settled into a prosperous buffer state between Byzantium, the Caliphate and the Rus'.

Khazar power rested on tolls levied on the great trade routes passing through their capital Atil, near the mouth of the Volga. Slavic and Rus' traders carried furs, wax and slaves south, exchanging them for silver dirhams and eastern luxuries. The Khazars also dominated subject peoples including the Alans, Bulgars, Magyars (for a time) and several Slavic tribes.

Conversion to Judaism

According to Arabic, Hebrew and Rus' sources, the Khazar khagan and much of the aristocracy adopted Judaism. The date and depth of the conversion are debated; letters such as the correspondence between Hasdai ibn Shaprut and King Joseph, and the Schechter Letter, describe a kingdom ruled by Jewish kings. Ordinary Khazars remained religiously diverse, and Muslim, Christian and pagan communities lived under Khazar rule with notable tolerance.

Decline

In the 960s Sviatoslav I of Kiev destroyed Atil and broke Khazar power; by about 969 the khaganate had collapsed. The fate of the Khazars afterwards is obscure. Arthur Koestler's The Thirteenth Tribe (1976) popularised the hypothesis that Ashkenazi Jews descend largely from Khazars, but genetic and historical studies have found little support for a large-scale Khazar contribution to Ashkenazi ancestry.
