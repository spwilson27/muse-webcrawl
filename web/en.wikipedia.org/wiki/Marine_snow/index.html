Marine snow

In oceanography, marine snow is a continuous shower of mostly organic detritus falling from the sunlit upper layers of the ocean down to the deep sea. It is a significant means of transporting energy and nutrients from the productive photic zone to organisms of the aphotic zone, and a key component of the ocean's biological carbon pump.

Composition

Marine snow particles are aggregates of material including dead and dying phytoplankton and zooplankton, faecal pellets, discarded larvacean feeding structures ("houses"), mucus, dust and other inorganic matter, all bound together in a loose, mucus-rich matrix. Individual aggregates are typically a few millimetres across — much larger than their constituent particles — and sink at rates on the order of tens of metres per day.

Ecological role

Bacteria colonise the aggregates and remineralise much of their organic content during descent, so only a fraction of surface production reaches the seafloor. Even so, marine snow supports a rich food web: midwater animals consume it directly, and the material that reaches the abyssal floor sustains deep-sea benthic communities in regions far below the reach of sunlight. Seasonal pulses following phytoplankton blooms can deliver large quantities of relatively fresh material to the deep ocean within weeks.

Carbon export

By carrying photosynthetically fixed carbon into the deep ocean, where it may be stored in sediments for centuries or longer, marine snow helps regulate atmospheric carbon dioxide over geological timescales. It is studied using sediment traps, underwater cameras and autonomous profilers. See also Biological pump, Detritus, and Marine food web.
