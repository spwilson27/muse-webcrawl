Lordosis

SpecialtyRheumatology, orthopedics

Lordosis is the normal inward (lordotic) curvature of the lumbar and cervical regions of the human spine. When the term is used clinically, it often refers to an exaggerated inward curve — sometimes called hyperlordosis or, colloquially, "swayback" — which can be associated with pain and postural problems.

Normal lordotic curves

The human spine has four curves in the sagittal plane: cervical and lumbar curves that are convex anteriorly (lordotic), and thoracic and sacral curves that are convex posteriorly (kyphotic). These alternating curves help the spine absorb shock, maintain balance over the pelvis, and support the weight of the head and trunk. The lumbar lordosis typically measures between about 20 and 45 degrees by the Cobb method.

Lumbar lordosis develops in infancy as a child begins to sit, stand, and walk, and it varies considerably between individuals, with pelvic morphology (pelvic incidence) a major determinant of the normal range for a given person.

Hyperlordosis

Excessive lumbar lordosis may result from:

  • Obesity, which shifts the centre of mass forward;
  • Pregnancy (usually temporary);
  • Osteoporosis with vertebral compression;
  • Spondylolisthesis, in which one vertebra slips forward on another;
  • Achondroplasia, a form of dwarfism;
  • Hip flexion contractures or weak abdominal and gluteal muscles.

Most cases require no treatment. Where symptoms occur, management includes physiotherapy and exercise to strengthen the core and gluteal muscles, weight management, and treatment of the underlying cause. Surgery is reserved for severe deformity with neurological compromise or intractable pain.

Lordosis in other animals

In animal behaviour, "lordosis" also names the mating posture adopted by females of many mammals, in which the back is arched ventrally to facilitate copulation; the lordosis reflex is hormonally mediated and widely studied in reproductive neurobiology.
