Sense

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sense is a physiological capacity of organisms to perceive their environment and internal state, via specialised sensory receptor cells whose signals the nervous system converts into perception. The classical five senses of Aristotle — sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch — are a subset: humans also sense balance (vestibular), body position (proprioception), temperature, pain, hunger and more, and other animals possess senses we lack, such as electroreception and magnetoreception.

Each sense begins with transduction: converting a physical stimulus (photons, pressure waves, molecules) into nerve impulses. The brain then constructs perception actively — filling gaps, correcting illusions and weighting senses against one another — so what is experienced is a model of the world, not a recording.

The major senses

Vision uses the eyes and is dominant in primates; hearing detects vibrations via the ear and extends to ultrasound in bats and infrasound in elephants; smell and taste are chemical senses (dogs deploy smell professionally); touch is distributed across the skin with distinct receptors for pressure, vibration, temperature and pain.

Loss and substitution

Blindness and deafness have driven substitution technologies from Braille to cochlear implants, and sensory loss reshapes the brain — visual cortex is recruited for touch and language in the blind, an illustration of neuroplasticity.
