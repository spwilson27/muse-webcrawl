Gold plating

Gold plating is a method of depositing a thin layer of gold onto the surface of another metal, most often copper or silver, by chemical or electrochemical means. It is used in jewellery, electronics, and decorative arts to give an object the appearance and surface properties of gold at a fraction of the cost of solid gold.

Methods

Electroplating immerses the object in a gold salt solution and passes an electric current, depositing gold ions onto the surface. Typical thicknesses range from a fraction of a micrometre for decorative flash plating to several micrometres for durable wear layers. Electroless plating deposits gold by chemical reduction without current and is used for complex electronic parts. Older techniques include fire gilding with mercury amalgam, now largely abandoned because of toxicity.

Applications

In electronics, gold plating protects connectors, switch contacts, and bonding wires from corrosion while maintaining excellent electrical conductivity; gold does not tarnish or form insulating oxides. In jewellery, gold plating over sterling silver is sold as vermeil when the layer meets minimum thickness standards. Gold plating is also used on coins, medals, trophies, and architectural details.

Black gold plating

Black finishes can be produced by electroplating gold alloys containing metals such as cobalt or nickel, giving a dark decorative coating used in luxury watchmaking and jewellery.
