The glossopharyngeal nerve, also known as the ninth cranial nerve (CN IX), is a mixed cranial nerve that emerges from the medulla oblongata of the brainstem and exits the skull through the jugular foramen. Its name reflects its distribution to the tongue (glossa) and pharynx.

Functions

CN IX carries several fibre types: branchial motor fibres to the stylopharyngeus muscle, which elevates the pharynx during swallowing; visceral motor (parasympathetic) fibres that, via the tympanic branch (Jacobson's nerve) and the lesser petrosal nerve, supply the parotid gland through the otic ganglion; visceral sensory fibres from the carotid sinus and carotid body conveying blood-pressure and blood-gas information central to cardiovascular reflexes; general sensory fibres from the posterior third of the tongue, tonsils, pharynx and middle ear; and special sensory (taste) fibres from the posterior third of the tongue. The nerve also contributes the afferent limb of the gag reflex.

Clinical significance

Damage to the glossopharyngeal nerve can cause loss of taste from the posterior tongue, impaired swallowing, loss of the gag reflex and reduced salivation. Irritation of the nerve produces glossopharyngeal neuralgia, a rare disorder of severe stabbing pain in the throat, tongue and ear, sometimes triggered by swallowing and occasionally causing syncope through the nerve's connections with the carotid sinus reflex.
