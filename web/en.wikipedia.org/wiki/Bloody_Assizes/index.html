Bloody Assizes

[Hero image: Judge Jeffreys presiding over the trials of Monmouth's rebels at Taunton, 1685]

The Bloody Assizes were a series of trials held in the West Country of England in the autumn of 1685, following the defeat of the Monmouth Rebellion. Presided over by the Lord Chief Justice, George Jeffreys, the assizes dealt with some 1,400 prisoners accused of high treason. Around 250–320 were executed — hanged, drawn and quartered — and some 850 were transported to the West Indies as indentured labour. The proceedings earned Jeffreys a lasting reputation for brutality and became a byword for judicial vengeance.

Background

In June 1685 James Scott, Duke of Monmouth, the illegitimate son of Charles II, landed at Lyme Regis and raised a rebellion against James II. His largely peasant army was crushed at the Battle of Sedgemoor on 6 July 1685. In the aftermath, royal forces under the Earl of Feversham and Colonel Kirke — whose men were nicknamed "Kirke's Lambs" — carried out summary reprisals.

The trials

Jeffreys opened the Western Circuit at Winchester, where the elderly Dame Alice Lisle was convicted of harbouring rebels and beheaded. The court then sat at Dorchester, Exeter, Taunton and Wells. Trials were peremptory; defendants were bullied into pleading guilty, juries intimidated, and sentences carried out swiftly, with the quartered remains of the executed displayed in towns and villages across the region as a warning. Hundreds more prisoners died of gaol fever in overcrowded prisons.

Legacy

After the Glorious Revolution of 1688, Jeffreys was captured attempting to flee the country and died in the Tower of London in 1689. The Bloody Assizes have lived on in West Country folk memory and literature, notably in Arthur Conan Doyle's novel Micah Clarke and R. D. Blackmore's Lorna Doone.
