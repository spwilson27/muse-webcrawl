Crabs are decapod crustaceans of the infraorder Brachyura, characterised by a very short projecting tail and a reduced abdomen held folded beneath the thorax. Crabs are found in all the world's oceans, in freshwater, and on land, comprising around 7,000 species in some 70 families.

Most crabs have a thick exoskeleton and a single pair of pincers (chelae). They range greatly in size: the pea crab measures only a few millimetres across, while the Japanese spider crab has a leg span of up to 3.7 metres (12 ft). Many species are sexually dimorphic, with males having larger claws, one of which may be greatly enlarged for display, as in fiddler crabs.

Ecology and behaviour

Crabs are generally omnivores, feeding on algae, molluscs, other crustaceans, fungi, bacteria, and detritus. They typically walk sideways, a gait made efficient by the articulation of their legs, although some can move forwards. Crabs communicate by drumming or waving their pincers. The crab body plan has evolved convergently in several related groups, a phenomenon known as carcinisation.

Human use

Crabs form a major fishery: roughly 1.5 million tonnes are caught annually worldwide, including blue crab, Dungeness crab, snow crab, and king crab, and they are eaten in cuisines around the world.
