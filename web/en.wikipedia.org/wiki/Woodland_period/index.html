In the classification of archaeological cultures of the Americas, the Woodland period is a period of time in the history of the Eastern Woodlands of North America, roughly spanning 1000 BCE to 1000 CE. The term was coined in the 1930s and refers to prehistoric sites in the eastern United States between the Archaic period and the rise of the Mississippian culture. It is conventionally divided into Early, Middle, and Late Woodland sub-periods.

Early Woodland (1000–200 BCE)

The Early Woodland period is marked by the widespread adoption of pottery, the construction of burial mounds, and increasing sedentism. The Adena culture of the Ohio River valley (c. 800 BCE – 1 CE) is its best-known expression, characterised by conical burial mounds, circular enclosures, and finely made ceremonial objects. People cultivated native seed crops of the Eastern Agricultural Complex, including sunflower, squash, chenopod, and knotweed, supplementing a continuing diet of deer, nuts, and fish.

Middle Woodland (200 BCE – 500 CE)

The Middle Woodland period is dominated by the Hopewell tradition, a network of related communities across the Ohio and Illinois river valleys connected by the exchange of exotic raw materials — obsidian from the Rocky Mountains, mica from the Appalachians, copper from the Great Lakes, and marine shell from the Gulf Coast. Hopewell groups built vast geometric earthworks, such as those at Newark, Ohio, and elaborate burials containing effigy pipes, copper ornaments, and carved figurines. This era is often called the golden age of Woodland culture.

Late Woodland (500–1000 CE)

After about 500 CE the Hopewell exchange system collapsed, populations dispersed into smaller, more autonomous communities, and monumental construction declined. Long-distance trade diminished, warfare appears to have increased, and new technologies spread, notably the bow and arrow and, late in the period, the adoption of maize as a staple crop. In the centuries after 800 CE, intensive maize agriculture and new social forms paved the way for the Mississippian culture.

Subsistence and technology

Woodland peoples combined hunting, fishing, and gathering with garden agriculture. Distinctive technologies include cord-marked and stamped pottery, platform pipes, gorgets, and expanding-stemmed projectile points. Settlements ranged from seasonal camps to semi-permanent villages of circular houses along river terraces.
