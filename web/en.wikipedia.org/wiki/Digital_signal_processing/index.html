Digital signal processing (DSP) is the use of digital processing — by computers or specialised digital signal processors — to perform a wide variety of signal processing operations. The signals processed are sequences of numbers representing samples of a continuous variable (such as sound, images, temperature or radio waves), converted to digital form by analog-to-digital converters.

Core theory

DSP rests on sampling theory: a band-limited signal sampled at more than twice its highest frequency (the Nyquist rate) can be reconstructed exactly. Signals are analysed in the time and frequency domains using the discrete Fourier transform, computed efficiently by the fast Fourier transform (FFT, popularised by Cooley and Tukey in 1965), and manipulated with digital filters — finite impulse response (FIR) and infinite impulse response (IIR) — designed to specification and implemented as multiply-accumulate operations. The z-transform provides the mathematical framework for discrete-time systems.

Applications

DSP is foundational to modern technology: digital telephony and mobile networks (speech coding, echo cancellation, modulation), audio and image compression (MP3, JPEG), radar and sonar, medical imaging and ECG/EEG analysis, seismology, control systems, and software-defined radio. Dedicated DSP chips, optimised for multiply-accumulate throughput, were pioneered by parts such as the Texas Instruments TMS320 series (1983); today much DSP also runs on general-purpose processors and FPGAs.
