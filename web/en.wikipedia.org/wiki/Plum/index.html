Plum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Dark purple plums with a pale dusty bloom on their skins]

A plum is a stone fruit of the genus Prunus, most often the European plum (Prunus domestica) — oval, blue-purple and freestone — or the rounder Japanese plum (P. salicina), which supplies most red and yellow fresh plums. The family is a orchard of variety: tart damsons for jam and gin, golden mirabelles, green greengages prized in France, and the prune plums whose dried fruit, the prune, is a food in its own right. China leads world production. Plums sit at a crossroads of fresh fruit, preserve and drink, from Central European slivovitz to Japanese umeshu.

Origins and types

The European plum is an ancient hybrid, probably formed near the Caucasus from cherry-plum and blackthorn ancestry, and was cultivated by the Romans. Japanese plums, despite the name refined largely in California by breeders such as Luther Burbank from East Asian stock, transformed the fresh market with firm, shippable fruit. The Japanese apricot-relative Prunus mume is a plum in use if not in name: salted and pickled as umeboshi, it flavours rice across Japan.

Prunes and preservation

Drying turned the plum into a storable, tradable food centuries before canning. France's pruneaux d'Agen, from the d'Ente plum, are protected and famous; California became the other prune giant. Prunes' reputation as a laxative is physiologically real — they contain sorbitol and fibre — which led the US industry to rebrand them "dried plums" in 2000, an unusually successful piece of marketing. In Britain plums darken Christmas pudding and in Central Europe they fill dumplings (Zwetschgenknödel) and distill into fiery, clear brandies.
