Background check

A background check is a process a person or company uses to verify that an individual is who they claim to be, and this provides an opportunity to check and confirm the validity of someone's criminal record, education, employment history, and other activities from their past. Background checks are frequently conducted by governments and employers for a wide variety of reasons, such as pre-employment screening, security clearances, firearm purchases, and immigration procedures. The frequency, purpose, and legitimacy of background checks vary among countries, industries, and individuals.

Types of checks

Background checks can include several distinct categories of inquiry:

  • Criminal record checks — searches of criminal databases at the local, state, or federal level for arrests, convictions, and outstanding warrants.
  • Employment verification — confirmation of previous job titles, dates of employment, and eligibility for rehire.
  • Education verification — confirmation of degrees, diplomas, and professional certifications.
  • Credit history checks — review of a candidate's credit report, used particularly for positions involving financial responsibility.
  • Reference checks — interviews with former employers, colleagues, or teachers.
  • Drug testing — screening for the use of illegal drugs, often performed alongside background screening.

Pre-employment screening

Employers commonly conduct background checks before extending an offer of employment. The practice is intended to reduce the risk of negligent hiring, protect the safety of other employees and customers, and verify the accuracy of information provided by the applicant. In the United States, the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) governs background checks performed by third-party consumer reporting agencies, requiring employers to obtain written consent from the applicant and to follow specific procedures if adverse action is taken based on the report.

Government and security checks

Government agencies conduct background investigations for security clearances, law enforcement positions, and access to sensitive information. These investigations may be considerably more extensive than commercial checks, involving interviews with associates, verification of foreign contacts, and review of financial and travel records. In the United States, the National Background Investigation Bureau and its predecessors have administered personnel vetting for federal positions.

Firearms purchases

In the United States, licensed firearm dealers are required to run a background check through the National Instant Criminal Background Check System (NICS), maintained by the FBI, before transferring a firearm to an unlicensed person. The system checks whether the prospective buyer is prohibited from receiving firearms under federal or state law, for example because of a felony conviction or a domestic violence restraining order.

Controversies

Background checks have been criticized for inaccuracies in commercial databases, for perpetuating racial and socioeconomic disparities in employment, and for the difficulty individuals face in correcting erroneous records. Reform campaigns such as "ban the box" have sought to delay criminal-history inquiries until later in the hiring process. Data privacy regulations in various jurisdictions limit the retention and use of background information.
