Pope Martin V (Latin: Martinus V; born Oddone Colonna; 1369 – 20 February 1431) was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 11 November 1417 to his death on 20 February 1431. His election by the Council of Constance effectively ended the Western Schism, reuniting the Latin Church under a single, universally recognised pope after nearly four decades of rival claimants.

Early life and election

Oddone Colonna was a member of the powerful Roman Colonna family. Trained as a lawyer, he became a cardinal deacon in 1405 under Innocent VII. At Constance, the council first deposed or accepted the resignations of the three rival popes — Gregory XII of the Roman line, antipope Benedict XIII of Avignon (who refused to submit but was abandoned), and antipope John XXIII of the Pisan line. A conclave augmented by representatives of the nations of the council then elected Colonna on 11 November 1417, the feast of St Martin, from which he took his papal name.

Pontificate

Martin's first task was restoration. Rome, to which he returned in 1420 after journeys through Italy, was dilapidated and depopulated; he began rebuilding its churches, aqueducts and streets, laying the foundations for the Renaissance papacy. As required by the council's decree Frequens he convoked councils at Pavia/Siena (1423, poorly attended and quickly dissolved) and summoned the Council of Basel, which opened shortly after his death in 1431.

Martin dealt firmly with the Hussite movement in Bohemia, proclaiming crusades against Jan Hus's followers after the Council of Constance had condemned Hus to the stake in 1415. He also worked to reassert papal authority over the Papal States, relying heavily on his Colonna relatives, and negotiated concordats with several nations limiting some of the reform decrees adopted at Constance.

Death and legacy

Martin V died of a stroke in Rome on 20 February 1431 and was buried in St John Lateran. He is remembered as the pope who restored a single papacy and the city of Rome, setting the stage for the Renaissance papacy while frustrating many of the conciliar movement's hopes for institutional reform of the Church.
