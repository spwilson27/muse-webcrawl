Equestrian at the Summer Olympics

Equestrian sports have been part of the Summer Olympic Games since 1900, when events were held at Paris; after an absence in 1904 and 1908 they returned at Stockholm 1912 and have been contested at every Games since. The programme comprises three disciplines — dressage, eventing and show jumping — each with individual and team competitions. Equestrian is the only Olympic sport in which men and women compete directly against each other in individual events, and one of the few in which humans and animals compete as a team.

Disciplines

  • Dressage: horse and rider perform a sequence of predetermined movements judged on precision and grace; the freestyle to music (kür) is the individual final.
  • Eventing: a combined test of dressage, cross-country jumping over solid obstacles, and show jumping, held over several days.
  • Show jumping: horse and rider clear a course of knockable fences within a time limit; faults decide the placings.

History

Early Olympic equestrianism was dominated by military officers, and until 1952 only commissioned officers and "gentlemen" could compete; women first rode in dressage in 1952 and in all disciplines by 1964. The 1956 equestrian events were held in Stockholm, Sweden, rather than with the main Games in Melbourne, because of Australian quarantine laws — the only split-hosting of its kind in Olympic history. Notable champions include Isabell Werth of Germany, the most decorated Olympic equestrian, and Charlotte Dujardin of Great Britain in dressage.

Governance and welfare

The sport is governed internationally by the FEI (Fédération Équestre Internationale). Because horses must travel internationally and compete in heat and unfamiliar venues, veterinary inspections ("trot-ups") precede each phase, and welfare rules have tightened following incidents such as the horse deaths and the modern pentathlon riding controversy at Tokyo 2020.
