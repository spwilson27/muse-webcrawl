Five hindrances

In Buddhism, the five hindrances (Pali: pañca nīvaraṇāni) are mental states that impede concentration and insight during meditation and in daily life. They are presented in the early Buddhist texts, including the Satipatthana Sutta, as the chief obstacles to attaining the meditative absorptions (jhānas) and to developing wisdom.

The five hindrances

  1. Sensory desire (kāmacchanda) — craving for pleasure through the five senses, likened to water coloured by dyes.
  2. Ill will (byāpāda) — aversion, resentment and hostility, likened to boiling water.
  3. Sloth and torpor (thīna-middha) — heaviness of body and dullness of mind, likened to water covered in algae.
  4. Restlessness and remorse (uddhacca-kukkucca) — agitation of mind and worry over past actions, likened to water stirred by wind.
  5. Skeptical doubt (vicikicchā) — indecision and lack of confidence in the teaching, likened to muddy water.

Overcoming the hindrances

The hindrances are temporarily suppressed by the development of concentration in jhāna and are weakened or eradicated at the stages of enlightenment. Traditional practice works with them by recognising their presence, investigating their causes, and applying specific antidotes: contemplation of unattractiveness against desire, loving-kindness (mettā) against ill will, energy and attention to light against sloth, calming the breath against restlessness, and clear comprehension against doubt. In the Anapanasati and Satipatthana frameworks, simply noting a hindrance mindfully — knowing "there is sensual desire in me" — is itself the first step in loosening its grip.

Relation to other teachings

The hindrances are the negative counterpart of the seven factors of enlightenment, which flourish when the hindrances subside; they also overlap with the ten fetters that bind beings to rebirth. Within Abhidharma analysis each hindrance is treated as a mental factor present in unwholesome states of consciousness.
