Engraving

Engraving is the practice of incising a design onto a hard, usually flat surface by cutting grooves into it with a tool called a burin or graver. As a printmaking technique, engraving is an intaglio process: ink is held in the incised lines of a metal (usually copper or zinc) plate, whose surface is wiped clean before printing under high pressure. The result is characterised by precise, swelling and tapering lines that end in fine points.

History

Engraving on metal grew from the goldsmith's craft in the fifteenth century, in Germany's Upper Rhine region and in Italy. The Master of the Playing Cards and Martin Schongauer developed it as an independent art; Albrecht Dürer brought it to a peak of virtuosity around 1500, spreading his fame across Europe through printed images. In the following centuries engraving became the principal means of reproducing paintings and illustrations, supporting a vast trade in reproductive prints, book illustrations, banknotes and maps.

Technique

The engraver drives the burin through the metal with the heel of the hand, rotating the plate on a leather sandbag to curve lines. Depth and width of cut control the printed tone; cross-hatching builds darker areas. Unlike etching, where acid bites the lines, engraving produces a characteristically clean, sharp-edged groove. Steel engraving, developed in the early nineteenth century, allowed far larger print runs and dominated commercial illustration until displaced by photomechanical processes.

Other uses

Engraving is also used to decorate metal, glass, stone and wood objects, to cut seals and dies, to mark instruments, and in security printing — the fine engraved portraits on banknotes remain difficult to counterfeit. Modern practice includes hand engraving by artisans, machine and pantograph engraving, and laser engraving.
