The Cherry Orchard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The nursery of the Ranevskaya estate in spring, cherry blossom beyond tall windows — the opening set of the play as staged at the Moscow Art Theatre]

The Cherry Orchard (Russian: Вишнёвый сад) is the last play by Anton Chekhov, first performed at the Moscow Art Theatre on 17 January 1904, his 44th birthday, under Konstantin Stanislavski. Chekhov called it a comedy, 'in places even a farce'; Stanislavski staged it as a tragedy of a vanishing world, and the tension between those readings has defined its production history ever since.

The impoverished landowner Lyubov Ranevskaya returns from Paris to her estate, whose famous cherry orchard must be sold for debts. The merchant Lopakhin — son of a serf on the estate — urges her to cut the orchard for summer cottages; she cannot act, the estate is auctioned, and Lopakhin himself buys it. As the family departs for good, axes sound in the orchard and the ancient servant Firs is discovered, forgotten, in the locked empty house.

Themes

Written as serfdom's children bought the gentry's estates across Russia, the play balances elegy and comedy: the orchard is beauty, memory and uselessness at once, and Chekhov refuses villains — Lopakhin's triumph is written with sympathy, Ranevskaya's fecklessness with love. Its 'sound of a breaking string', a mysterious noise heard twice in the play, remains theatre's most famous unexplained stage effect.

Reception

The premiere, mid-jubilee for its dying author, secured the play's classic status; productions from Peter Brook to contemporary versions worldwide treat it as the definitive drama of social change, and 'cherry orchard' has become a common metaphor for a cherished, doomed inheritance.
