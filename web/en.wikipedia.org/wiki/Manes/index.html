Manes

In ancient Roman religion, the Manes (Latin: mānēs, "the good ones") were the souls of deceased loved ones, regarded as deified ancestral spirits. They belonged to the broad class of underworld gods, the di inferi, and were honoured with offerings at tombs and during festivals such as the Parentalia. Tombstones in the Roman world frequently bore the dedication D.M. — Dis Manibus, "to the divine Manes" — followed by the name of the deceased. The word also came to be used more generally for the spirits of the dead, and in literature for the underworld itself.

Etymology and nature

The name was explained by Roman writers as a euphemistic title meaning "the good" or "the kindly ones", intended to placate the dead, similar to the Greek practice of calling the Furies the Eumenides ("the kindly ones"). The Manes were not sharply distinguished in Roman thought from other spirit categories such as the lares (guardian spirits), the penates (household gods), and the lemures (hostile or restless dead).

Cult

Worship of the Manes centred on the tomb and the family. During the Parentalia (13–21 February), families visited cemeteries outside the city, brought offerings of wine, milk, honey, flowers, and food to the graves, and shared small meals in honour of their ancestors; the festival culminated in the Feralia. Every Roman household also maintained cult observances at home, and the head of the family (paterfamilias) bore responsibility for sustaining the rites of the family dead. A mundus, a pit believed to be an entrance to the underworld, was ritually opened on three days of the year, when the Manes were thought to move among the living and official business was suspended.

Roman law protected tombs as sacred places (loci religiosi), and violation of a grave was an offence against the Manes. Offerings called inferiae were made to them, and generals might vow temples or dedications to the Di Manes of fallen soldiers.
