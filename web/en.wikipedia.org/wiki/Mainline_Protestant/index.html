Mainline Protestant

The mainline Protestant churches (sometimes also known as oldline Protestant churches) are a group of Protestant denominations in the United States and Canada that contrast in history and outlook with evangelical, fundamentalist, and charismatic Protestant movements.


A mainline Protestant church interior.

Terminology and characteristics

The term "mainline" derives in part from the Main Line, a group of affluent suburbs of Philadelphia historically associated with members of these denominations. Mainline churches are generally characterised by moderate to liberal theological positions, openness to historical-critical study of the Bible, ecumenical engagement, and emphasis on social justice through movements such as the Social Gospel.

Denominations

The "Seven Sisters of the Protestant mainline" traditionally comprise:

  • United Methodist Church
  • Evangelical Lutheran Church in America
  • Presbyterian Church (USA)
  • The Episcopal Church
  • American Baptist Churches USA
  • United Church of Christ
  • Christian Church (Disciples of Christ)

Other bodies often counted as mainline include the Reformed Church in America, the Moravian Church in America, and the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers).

Demographics and decline

Mainline denominations dominated American religious life in the mid-20th century; the United Methodist Church alone had about 11 million members in the 1960s. Since then mainline membership has declined substantially as a share of the population, a trend widely studied by sociologists of religion. In 2014 the Pew Research Center estimated mainline Protestants at about 14.7% of the U.S. population.
