Kappa-opioid receptor

GeneOPRK1
TypeG protein-coupled receptor
Endogenous ligandDynorphins (especially dynorphin A)
Effects of activationAnalgesia, sedation, dysphoria, diuresis

The kappa-opioid receptor (KOR or κ-opioid receptor) is one of the four opioid receptors in humans, encoded by the OPRK1 gene. It is a G protein-coupled receptor whose principal endogenous ligands are the dynorphin peptides. Together with the mu, delta and nociceptin receptors, it mediates the effects of opioid drugs, but activation of the kappa receptor produces a distinctive profile: analgesia accompanied by dysphoria and sedation rather than euphoria.

Function

KOR is widely distributed in the brain, spinal cord and peripheral tissues, and modulates pain, mood, stress responses, appetite and water balance (activation promotes diuresis). The dynorphin–KOR system is strongly implicated in the aversive effects of stress and in relapse in addiction models.

Pharmacology

Kappa agonists such as pentazocine and nalbuphine have been used as analgesics; the peripherally restricted agonist difelikefalin is approved for treating itch in dialysis patients, and asimadoline was studied for irritable bowel syndrome. The hallucinogen salvinorin A, from Salvia divinorum, is a potent selective KOR agonist. KOR antagonists such as aticaprant have been investigated as treatments for depression and substance use disorders.
