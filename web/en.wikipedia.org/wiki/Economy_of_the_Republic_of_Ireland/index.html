Economy of the Republic of Ireland

CurrencyEuro (€)
Trade organisationsEU, WTO, OECD
GDP (nominal)Approx. $590 billion (2023 est., heavily affected by multinationals)
Main industriesPharmaceuticals, medical devices, information technology, chemicals, food products
Key policyLow corporate tax rate (12.5% historically; 15% for large firms from 2024)

The economy of Ireland is a highly developed knowledge economy, focused on services in high-tech, life sciences, financial services, and agri-food. Its growth has been driven by foreign direct investment from multinational corporations attracted by a low corporate tax rate, an English-speaking workforce, and membership of the European Union.

History

For decades after independence in 1922, Ireland was one of Western Europe's poorer countries, marked by protectionism and emigration. Liberalisation from the late 1950s, EEC entry in 1973, and investment in education laid the groundwork for the "Celtic Tiger" boom of the mid-1990s to mid-2000s. The 2008 property crash and banking crisis forced an EU–IMF bailout in 2010, followed by austerity and then a strong recovery.

Statistical distortions

Ireland's headline GDP figures are notoriously distorted by the activities of multinational companies — tax inversions, intellectual-property transfers, and aircraft leasing — a phenomenon economist Paul Krugman dubbed "leprechaun economics" after GDP was reported to grow 26% in 2015. Ireland's Central Statistics Office therefore publishes GNI*, a modified measure that better reflects the domestic economy.
