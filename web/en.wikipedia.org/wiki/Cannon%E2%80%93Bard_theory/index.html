Cannon–Bard theory

Cannon–Bard theory
Field: psychology (emotion)
Proponents: Walter Bradford Cannon; Philip Bard (1927–1930s)
Key claim: emotional experience and physiological arousal occur simultaneously
Rivals: James–Lange theory; Schachter–Singer two-factor theory

The Cannon–Bard theory of emotion is a physiological theory developed in the 1920s and 1930s by Walter Bradford Cannon and his doctoral student Philip Bard. It holds that emotion-provoking stimuli are relayed by the thalamus simultaneously to the cortex, producing the conscious experience of emotion, and to the sympathetic nervous system, producing physiological arousal — so that we feel afraid and tremble at the same time, rather than trembling first and feeling afraid because we tremble.

Objections to James–Lange

Cannon marshalled several criticisms of the earlier James–Lange theory: the visceral organs are relatively insensitive and their changes too slow to source the speed of emotional feeling; artificially inducing visceral changes (for example by injecting adrenaline) does not produce genuine emotions; the same visceral pattern accompanies quite different emotions; and animals with severed viscera still display emotional behaviour. Bard's experiments on decorticate cats, which showed rage responses coordinated by diencephalic structures, supported the thalamic account.

Legacy

The theory redirected emotion research toward the brain and away from the periphery, anticipating later work on the limbic system. It has itself been modified by appraisal and two-factor theories, which emphasize cognitive interpretation of arousal, and by modern affective neuroscience.
