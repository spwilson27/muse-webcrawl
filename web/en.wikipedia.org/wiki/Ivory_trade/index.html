The ivory trade is the commercial, often illegal trade in ivory — the dentine material of animal tusks and teeth, above all those of African and Asian elephants. Ivory has been traded for centuries for use in carved ornaments, piano keys, billiard balls, seals and jewellery. The trade's modern scale devastated elephant populations: Africa's elephants fell from an estimated 1.3 million in 1979 to about 600,000 a decade later, and renewed poaching in the 2000s–2010s killed tens of thousands of elephants per year at its peak.

History

Ivory from Africa reached Europe, India and China along ancient trade routes, and carving centres such as Dieppe, Erbach and, later, Hong Kong and Japan built industries around it. In the 19th century, demand for piano keys, billiard balls and cutlery handles drove a vast extraction trade in colonial Africa, entangled with the slave trade in East and Central Africa. In the 1970s and 1980s, high prices and armed poaching gangs, sometimes linked to insurgent groups, sharply reduced elephant herds, especially in East and Central Africa.

Regulation

The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES) listed the Asian elephant on Appendix I in 1975 and the African elephant in 1977 (Appendix II), moving the African elephant to Appendix I in a decision effective January 1990 — the "ivory ban". Controversially, CITES later authorised two "one-off" sales of government ivory stockpiles (in 1999 and 2008) to Japan and China. China, long the largest market, closed its domestic ivory trade at the end of 2017; Hong Kong followed with a phase-out completed in 2021, and the United Kingdom's Ivory Act 2018 imposed one of the world's strictest near-total bans.

Illegal trade and enforcement

Illegal ivory flows from poaching hotspots in Central and East Africa through transit hubs to consumer markets in Asia, where ivory is carved into ornaments and status goods. Enforcement tools include DNA profiling of seized tusks to identify poaching hotspots, container scanning, and destruction of stockpiles — Kenya burned 105 tonnes of ivory in 2016. Demand-reduction campaigns and the Chinese ban were followed by falls in raw ivory prices, though poaching persists, and forest elephants in Central Africa have continued to decline steeply. Mammoth ivory from Siberian permafrost is generally legal and is sometimes used to launder elephant ivory.
