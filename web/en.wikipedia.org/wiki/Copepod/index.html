Copepods (class Copepoda, from Greek kōpē "oar" + pous "foot") are a group of small crustaceans found in nearly every freshwater and marine habitat. Most are 1 to 2 mm long with a teardrop-shaped body, long antennae, and a single prominent compound eye (often bright red). They are usually the most abundant multicellular animals in the ocean and form a crucial link in aquatic food webs, grazed by fish larvae, baleen whales, and countless other animals.

A calanoid copepod, showing the single red eye and long antennae.

Biology

Free-living copepods feed on phytoplankton, bacteria, and detritus, or prey on other small zooplankton. They swim with characteristic jerky movements of their thoracic limbs and use their long first antennae as sensors and stabilisers. Development proceeds through six nauplius larval stages and five copepodid stages before adulthood. Many marine species undertake diel vertical migrations of hundreds of metres and overwinter in a dormant diapause at depth; some females carry paired egg sacs.

Ecology and importance

Copepods channel primary production to higher trophic levels: species such as Calanus finmarchicus in the North Atlantic and Calanus glacialis in the Arctic are keystone prey for cod, herring, seabirds, and right whales. Their faecal pellets contribute significantly to the biological carbon pump. Not all are free-living: many are parasites of fish and invertebrates, including the salmon louse (Lepeophtheirus salmonis), a costly pest of salmon aquaculture, and freshwater copepods serve as intermediate hosts for the Guinea worm (Dracunculus medinensis), so filtering drinking water through cloth to remove copepods is a frontline eradication measure.

Classification

About 13,000 species are described in ten orders, the best known being Calanoida (mainly planktonic marine), Cyclopoida (abundant in fresh water), and Harpacticoida (mostly benthic). Their fossil record extends to the Carboniferous.
