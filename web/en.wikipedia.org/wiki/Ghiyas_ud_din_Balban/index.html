Ghiyas ud din Balban (c. 1216 – 1287) was the ninth sultan of the Mamluk (Slave) dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate, reigning from 1266 until his death in 1287. Originally a Turkic slave purchased by Sultan Iltutmish, he rose through the powerful corps of slave officers known as the Chihalgani ("the Forty") to become the empire's chief minister (naib) under Sultan Nasiruddin Mahmud, whose daughter he married, before ascending the throne himself.

Reign

Balban consolidated royal authority after decades of weak sultans and factional strife. He destroyed the power of the Chihalgani, enforced strict court ceremonial modeled on Persian kingship (including sijda, prostration, and paibos, kissing the sultan's feet), and projected the theory that the sultan was God's shadow on earth (zill-i-ilahi). He maintained a strong standing army, an efficient spy network, and meted out severe punishments to rebels and bandits, clearing the roads around Delhi and the Doab of brigandage.

He repelled Mongol pressure on the northwestern frontier, fortifying the border under his son Prince Muhammad, whose death in battle against the Mongols in 1285 was a devastating personal blow. Balban also suppressed the rebellion of Tughral Khan in Bengal (c. 1279–1282) with notable severity.

Death and succession

Balban died in 1287, shortly after designating his grandson Kai Khusrau as heir; the nobles instead enthroned another grandson, the young Muiz ud din Qaiqabad. Within three years the Khalji dynasty replaced the Mamluks. Historians remember Balban as an authoritarian but effective state-builder who restored the prestige of the Delhi Sultanate's monarchy.
