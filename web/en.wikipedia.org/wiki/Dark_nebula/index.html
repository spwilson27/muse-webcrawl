Dark nebula

A dark nebula or absorption nebula is a type of interstellar cloud, particularly a molecular cloud, that is so dense that it obscures the visible wavelengths of light from objects behind it, such as background stars and emission or reflection nebulae. The extinction of the light is caused by interstellar dust grains located in the coldest, densest parts of molecular clouds.

Famous examples include the Horsehead Nebula in Orion, the Coalsack Nebula in Crux, and the dark band of the Milky Way known as the Great Rift.

Characteristics

Dark nebulae appear as irregular black patches against the star field. Their dust grains — sub-micrometre particles of carbon, silicon, and ices of water, ammonia and other molecules — absorb and scatter shorter wavelengths, also producing interstellar reddening. Clusters and associations of dark nebulae are concentrated in the galactic plane. The largest dark clouds, giant molecular clouds, contain enough mass to form thousands of stars.

Star formation

Though dark to visible light, these clouds radiate in the infrared and radio, revealing dense cores that collapse under gravity to form protostars. Small, isolated, roughly spherical dark nebulae are called Bok globules, after astronomer Bart Bok. E. E. Barnard catalogued over 370 dark nebulae in the early 20th century, establishing that they are material clouds rather than gaps between stars.
