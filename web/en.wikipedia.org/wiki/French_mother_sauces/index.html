In French cuisine, the mother sauces (grandes sauces) are a group of sauce recipes upon which other, 'daughter' or secondary sauces are based. The five mother sauces in their modern form are béchamel, velouté, espagnole, hollandaise, and sauce tomate.

The five sauces

Béchamel is a white sauce of milk thickened with a white roux. Velouté is a light stock (veal, chicken, or fish) thickened with roux. Espagnole is a rich brown sauce of brown stock, brown roux, tomato, and mirepoix — the base of demi-glace. Hollandaise is an emulsion of egg yolk, melted butter, and lemon or vinegar. Sauce tomate is a tomato sauce, classically thickened with roux and flavoured with pork and vegetables.

History

The concept was systematised by Antonin Carême in the early 19th century, whose four grandes sauces were béchamel, velouté, espagnole, and allemande. Auguste Escoffier revised the list in Le Guide Culinaire (1903), replacing allemande with hollandaise and adding tomate, producing the canonical five taught in culinary schools today.

Daughter sauces

From the mothers derive hundreds of small sauces: Mornay (béchamel with cheese), suprême (chicken velouté with cream), bordelaise (espagnole family), béarnaise (hollandaise with tarragon), and many more.
