Hong Kong cinema

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A neon-lit Hong Kong street at night in the rain, in the style of a Wong Kar-wai frame]
A neon-lit Hong Kong street at night in the rain, in the style of a Wong Kar-wai frame

Hong Kong cinema is the film industry of Hong Kong, historically one of the world's largest exporters of films and the global capital of martial-arts cinema. From the Shaw Brothers and Golden Harvest studios through the 'Hong Kong New Wave' and the star era of Bruce Lee, Jackie Chan, Chow Yun-fat and director John Woo and Wong Kar-wai, it shaped action filmmaking worldwide — before the 1997 handover era, piracy and mainland market gravity reshaped it.

Studio era and kung fu

Post-1949 refugee capital built the Cantonese and Mandarin studio system; Shaw Brothers' Huangmei operas and wuxia gave way, in the 1970s, to Bruce Lee's kung fu explosion (The Big Boss, Fist of Fury, Enter the Dragon, 1971–73) at Golden Harvest, then to Jackie Chan's comic kung fu and the Hui brothers' Cantonese comedies, which re-localised the industry.

Golden age and diaspora

The 1980s–early 90s were the miracle years: John Woo's heroic bloodshed (A Better Tomorrow, 1986; The Killer, 1989), Tsui Hark's spectacles, Stephen Chow's comedies, Wong Kar-wai's art films (Chungking Express, 1994; In the Mood for Love, 2000, routinely ranked among the greatest films ever made), and output second only to India and the US in volume. After 1997, talent drained to Hollywood and Beijing; the CEPA agreement folded Hong Kong into mainland co-production, while a localist revival (Ten Years, 2015; recent record-breaking local hits) asserted a distinct voice amid tightening censorship after 2020.
