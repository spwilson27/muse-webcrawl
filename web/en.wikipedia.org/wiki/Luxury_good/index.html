Luxury good

In economics, a luxury good (or upmarket good) is a good for which demand increases more than proportionally as income rises, so that expenditures on the good become a greater proportion of overall spending. In contrast, a necessity good is one for which demand rises less than proportionally with income. Luxury goods are said to have high income elasticity of demand.

Characteristics

Luxury goods are typically characterised by high price relative to comparable goods, craftsmanship, rarity, and strong brand identity. They are often used as status symbols: the economist Thorstein Veblen described "conspicuous consumption", in which goods are purchased partly to display wealth. A related concept is the Veblen good, whose demand may rise as its price increases because of its exclusivity value.

Market

The global luxury market includes fashion and leather goods, watches and jewellery, perfumes and cosmetics, wines and spirits, automobiles, yachts, and high-end hospitality. Major luxury groups include LVMH, Kering and Richemont. The market has grown strongly with rising wealth in Asia, especially China, though it remains sensitive to economic cycles.

Taxation

Governments have sometimes imposed luxury taxes on goods such as jewellery, expensive cars, boats and aircraft, both to raise revenue and on equity grounds. Economists debate their efficiency, since they may fall heavily on the workers who produce the goods rather than only on wealthy buyers.
