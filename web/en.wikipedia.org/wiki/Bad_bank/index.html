Bad bank

A bad bank is a corporate structure created to isolate illiquid and high-risk assets — typically non-performing loans and troubled securities — held by a bank or financial organisation. By transferring toxic assets into a separate entity, the original institution (the "good bank") is left with a cleaned balance sheet, restoring confidence among depositors, creditors, and regulators and allowing it to resume lending.

History and examples

The model was pioneered at scale in the United States with Grant Street National Bank, created in 1988 to liquidate the bad assets of Mellon Bank. Sweden used the approach successfully during its early-1990s banking crisis, with Securum holding distressed assets of Nordbanken. Following the 2008 financial crisis, several countries established bad banks or asset management companies, including Ireland's National Asset Management Agency (NAMA, 2009), Germany's Erste Abwicklungsanstalt for WestLB, and Spain's SAREB (2012). China had earlier created four state asset management companies in 1999 to absorb non-performing loans from its big state banks.

Operation

A key design question is the transfer price of assets: paying too much subsidises the bank's shareholders, while paying too little may fail to stabilise the bank. The bad bank then manages and sells the assets over years to maximise recovery for the state or investors. Criticisms include moral hazard — banks may lend recklessly expecting future rescues — and the fiscal cost to taxpayers when recoveries fall short.

Alternatives

Alternatives include direct recapitalisation, asset guarantee schemes (such as the UK's Asset Protection Scheme of 2009), and internal "bad bank" divisions, as used by UBS and Citigroup after 2008.
