Richard Avedon (May 15, 1923 – October 1, 2004) was an American fashion and portrait photographer. He worked for Harper's Bazaar and Vogue and is regarded as one of the most influential photographers of the twentieth century, credited with helping to define America's image of style, beauty, and culture over the last half-century of his life.

Early life

Avedon was born in New York City to a Jewish family. His father, Jacob Israel Avedon, had emigrated from Russia and founded a successful retail dress business; his mother, Anna Avedon, came from a family of dress manufacturers. Avedon studied at DeWitt Clinton High School in the Bronx, where he worked on the school literary magazine with James Baldwin. He served in the Merchant Marine during the Second World War, taking identification photographs of sailors, an experience he later credited with teaching him the fundamentals of portraiture.

Career

After the war Avedon studied photography at the New School for Social Research under Alexey Brodovitch, the art director of Harper's Bazaar. He joined the magazine's staff in 1945 and remained its chief photographer for two decades before moving to Vogue in 1966. His fashion photographs broke with the static conventions of the era: his models moved, laughed, and leapt, bringing a sense of motion and spontaneity to the genre. His 1955 photograph Dovima with Elephants, showing the model Dovima in a Dior gown between two circus elephants, became one of the most famous fashion images ever made.

In 1992 Avedon became the first staff photographer in the history of The New Yorker. His minimalist portraits, usually shot against a stark white background, exposed his sitters with unflinching directness.

In the American West

Commissioned by the Amon Carter Museum in Fort Worth, Texas, Avedon spent 1979 to 1984 travelling the American West photographing drifters, miners, cowboys, and other working people. The resulting exhibition and book, In the American West (1985), is considered a landmark of portrait photography, though it was controversial for its unsparing depictions. His portrait of beekeeper Ronald Fischer, shown shirtless and covered in bees, became one of the series' most reproduced images.

Death and legacy

Avedon died on October 1, 2004, in San Antonio, Texas, of a cerebral hemorrhage while on assignment for The New Yorker, photographing a series on democracy. The Richard Avedon Foundation, established in 2004, preserves his archive. Major retrospectives of his work have been held at the Metropolitan Museum of Art and other institutions worldwide.
