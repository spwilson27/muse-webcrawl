The Greenland ice sheet (Danish: Grønlands indlandsis) is the second-largest ice sheet in the world, after the Antarctic ice sheet. It covers about 1.7 million square kilometres — roughly 80 per cent of the island of Greenland — and averages more than 2 kilometres thick. It holds about 2.9 million cubic kilometres of ice; if it melted entirely, global sea level would rise by approximately 7.4 metres (24 feet).

History and research

The ice sheet formed and grew over the last several million years, and its deep layers preserve a continuous climate archive: ice cores such as GRIP and NGRIP have recovered records stretching back more than 120,000 years, revealing abrupt past climate changes. Ice at the base in places may be far older.

Melting and sea level

Since the 1990s the ice sheet has been losing mass at an accelerating rate — through surface melting and runoff, and through the speed-up and retreat of marine-terminating outlet glaciers such as Jakobshavn Isbræ (Sermeq Kujalleq). Satellite gravimetry (GRACE) indicates losses of several hundred gigatonnes per year in the 2010s, making Greenland one of the largest contributors to present-day sea level rise. Its long-term behaviour under continued warming is a major uncertainty in climate projections, since parts of the sheet may be subject to self-sustaining elevation–feedback collapse once thresholds are passed.
