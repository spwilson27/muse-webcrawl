Staccato

Staccato (Italian for "detached") is a form of musical articulation in which a note or series of notes is played separated from the following note by silence, and shortened in duration. It is notated by a small dot placed above or below the note head, on the opposite side from the stem. Staccato is the opposite of legato, in which notes are played smoothly connected.

Notation and degrees

A dot indicates a moderately shortened note, conventionally roughly half its written value, though interpretation varies by era and style. Stronger degrees of detachment use a wedge or vertical stroke (sometimes called staccatissimo), while the round dot combined with a slur indicates mezzo-staccato or portato — notes gently separated but sustained longer. In the 18th century dots and wedges were often used interchangeably.

Technique

On string instruments staccato is produced by stopping the bow between notes; flying staccato and spiccato use a bouncing bow. On piano, the finger lifts quickly from the key. Wind players interrupt the air stream with the tongue. On the guitar and other plucked instruments, the string is muted after sounding. The resulting crisp, detached effect is used for rhythms from Mozart's light operatic figures to the percussive chords of Stravinsky.

In other media

By extension, "staccato" describes any rapid, disconnected series of sounds or movements — staccato speech, staccato bursts of gunfire, or staccato editing in film.
