The Baltimore Plot was an alleged conspiracy in February 1861 to assassinate Abraham Lincoln, then president-elect of the United States, as he travelled through Baltimore, Maryland, en route to his inauguration in Washington, D.C. Detectives hired by railroad president Samuel Morse Felton, led by Allan Pinkerton, reported uncovering the plot and persuaded Lincoln to pass through Baltimore secretly at night, an episode his opponents mocked but which security officials defended.

Background

After Lincoln's election in November 1860, Southern states began seceding, and Baltimore—with strong Confederate sympathies—was a known flashpoint; Maryland's loyalty was uncertain, yet every rail route to Washington passed through a city where passengers had to transfer between stations by carriage across town. Felton, fearing sabotage of his Philadelphia, Wilmington and Baltimore Railroad, engaged Pinkerton's detective agency.

The alleged plot and the night journey

Pinkerton's operatives, including Kate Warne, infiltrated Baltimore secessionist circles and reported that barber Cipriano Ferrandini and associates planned to stab Lincoln during the station transfer on 23 February 1861, when a crowd and a small police escort would leave him exposed. On the night of 22–23 February Lincoln, wearing a soft cap and cloak, secretly boarded a special train from Harrisburg to Baltimore and onward to Washington, arriving unannounced. Newspapers ridiculed the "flight through Baltimore" with cartoons of a disguised, frightened Lincoln, damaging his image, though historians generally credit Pinkerton's reports as sincerely believed; whether the plot would actually have been attempted remains debated, and no conspirator was ever prosecuted.

Aftermath

Weeks later, Baltimore saw the Pratt Street Riot of 19 April 1861, in which soldiers and civilians were killed—early bloodshed of the Civil War—underscoring the danger officials had feared.
