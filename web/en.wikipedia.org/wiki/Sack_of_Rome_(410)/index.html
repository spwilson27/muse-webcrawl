Sack of Rome (410)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sack of Rome

Date: 24 August 410 AD

Location: Rome, Western Roman Empire

Attackers: Visigoths under Alaric I

Result: Visigoth victory; city plundered for three days

The Sack of Rome on 24 August 410 AD was undertaken by the Visigoths under their king, Alaric I. At the time Rome was no longer the administrative capital of the Western Roman Empire — that role had passed to Mediolanum and then Ravenna — but it remained the empire's largest and symbolically most important city. The sack was the first time in almost 800 years that Rome had fallen to a foreign enemy, the previous occasion being the Gauls under Brennus in 390 BC.

Background

Alaric, a Gothic leader who had served Rome as a federate commander, repeatedly demanded land and gold after the execution of the Roman general Stilicho in 408 threw the western court into chaos. The Senate and the emperor Honorius refused his terms in full; Alaric besieged Rome three times, starving the city and installing a rival emperor, Priscus Attalus, before negotiations finally collapsed.

The sack

On the night of 24 August 410 the Salarian Gate was opened — some sources say by slaves or disaffected servants — and the Visigoths poured in. For three days they plundered the city, seizing gold, silver and movable wealth. As Arian Christians, the Goths largely spared the great basilicas of St Peter and St Paul, and those who took refuge in churches were generally unharmed, though many Romans were killed, enslaved or ransomed. Among the captives was the emperor's half-sister, Galla Placidia.

Aftermath

The news stunned the Mediterranean world. Jerome, writing from Bethlehem, lamented that "the city which had taken the whole world was itself taken". Pagan critics blamed the abandonment of the old gods, prompting Augustine of Hippo to write The City of God in reply. Rome recovered partially, but the sack is conventionally seen as a landmark in the decline and fall of the Western Roman Empire.
