Old Red Sandstone
AgeLate Silurian – early Carboniferous, mainly Devonian (c. 419–359 Ma)
TypeContinental sedimentary facies (sandstones, conglomerates, mudstones)
DistributionBritain and Ireland, Norway, Spitsbergen, around the North Atlantic (former "Old Red Continent")

The Old Red Sandstone is an assemblage of continental sedimentary rocks of mostly Devonian age found across the British Isles and around the margins of the North Atlantic. It was laid down in rivers, lakes and floodplains on the continent of Laurussia — the "Old Red Continent" — which formed when North America, Greenland and northern Europe collided in the Caledonian orogeny. Its red colour comes from iron oxide (hematite) coatings formed in an arid, seasonally wet climate south of the equator.

History of study

The term was first used by the Scottish geologist Hugh Miller, and the rocks of the Moray Firth and the Midland Valley of Scotland became classic ground for nineteenth-century geology. Miller's popular book The Old Red Sandstone (1841) described the fossil fishes of Cromarty and made the formation famous; Roderick Murchison and Adam Sedgwick named the Devonian System partly for these strata, whose continental deposits are the time-equivalent of the marine limestones of Devon.

Palaeontology

The Old Red Sandstone contains some of the most important fossils of early vertebrate life: armoured jawless fishes (ostracoderms), acanthodians, placoderms such as Bothriolepis and the giant Dunkleosteus relatives, early lobe-finned fishes, and some of the earliest evidence of tetrapod trackways. The Rhynie chert of Aberdeenshire, a Lower Devonian deposit within the sequence, preserves an entire early land ecosystem — plants, fungi, arthropods — in cellular detail.

Use as building stone

Its durable red and brown sandstones have been quarried for centuries and give distinctive colouring to buildings in Dundee, Stirling, and the Old Town of Edinburgh, as well as the original St Magnus Cathedral in Orkney, built of red and yellow sandstone.
