Parabolic trough

A parabolic trough is a type of solar thermal collector used in concentrated solar power (CSP) plants. It consists of a long, parabolic (U-shaped) mirror that focuses sunlight onto a receiver tube running along the mirror's focal line. A heat-transfer fluid flowing through the tube is heated — often to around 400 °C — and used to generate steam and electricity. Parabolic troughs are the most commercially mature CSP technology, accounting for most of the world's installed CSP capacity.

Design and operation

Rows of troughs are aligned north–south and rotate on a single axis to track the sun from east to west. The receiver tube is typically a steel pipe coated to absorb sunlight, enclosed in an evacuated glass tube to reduce heat loss. Heat-transfer fluid — originally synthetic thermal oil, increasingly molten salt in newer designs — carries the heat to a central power block, where it drives a conventional steam turbine. Some plants store hot molten salt so generation can continue after sunset.

History and major plants

The first large commercial trough plants were the Solar Energy Generating Systems (SEGS) in California's Mojave Desert, built between 1984 and 1991 with a combined capacity of 354 MW, which operated for decades. Spain built dozens of 50 MW trough plants in the 2000s and 2010s (such as Andasol, which added molten-salt storage), and large plants followed in Morocco (Noor), South Africa, China, and the Middle East. Although photovoltaic costs have fallen faster, troughs with thermal storage remain valued for dispatchable solar power.
