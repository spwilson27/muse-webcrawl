Waring's problem

Waring's problem is a problem in number theory proposed by Edward Waring in 1770. It asks whether, for every natural number k, there exists a positive integer s such that every natural number is the sum of at most s non-negative integer k-th powers. Waring asserted without proof that every number is a sum of at most 4 squares, 9 cubes, 19 fourth powers, and so on.

Solution and the function g(k)

The existence of such an s for every k was proved by David Hilbert in 1909. The smallest such number, written g(k), has been determined for most small k: g(2) = 4 (Lagrange's four-square theorem), g(3) = 9, g(4) = 19, g(5) = 37, and g(6) = 73. A widely believed formula, verified in all computed cases, gives g(k) in terms of the integer parts of (3/2)k.

Related results

The related function G(k) asks how many k-th powers suffice for all sufficiently large integers; bounds on G(k) were obtained by Hardy and Littlewood using the circle method, with major contributions by Vinogradov, Wooley, and others. Waring's problem has many variants and generalizations, including sums of like powers in other rings and the easier problem of representing numbers as sums of powers with signs allowed.
