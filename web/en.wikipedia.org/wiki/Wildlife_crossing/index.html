Wildlife crossing

Wildlife crossings are structures that allow animals to cross human-made barriers safely. They include underpass tunnels, viaducts, overpasses (mainly for large or herd-type animals), amphibian tunnels, fish ladders, tunnels and culverts (for small mammals such as otters, hedgehogs and badgers), and green roofs (for butterflies and birds). Wildlife crossings are a practice in habitat conservation, allowing connections or reconnections between habitats and combating habitat fragmentation. They also help avoid collisions between vehicles and animals, which in addition to killing or injuring animals may cause injury to humans and property damage.

History

The first wildlife crossings were fish ladders built in 17th-century France. Dedicated animal bridges were built in France in the 1950s, and the Netherlands subsequently developed an extensive network of ecoducts, including the Natuurbrug Zanderij Crailoo, one of the longest wildlife overpasses in the world. Banff National Park in Canada constructed a series of famous overpasses and underpasses along the Trans-Canada Highway beginning in the 1990s, heavily studied by ecologists and shown to dramatically reduce wildlife-vehicle collisions while maintaining genetic connectivity between populations.

Design and effectiveness

Effective crossings are usually combined with fencing that funnels animals toward the structure, and their design is tailored to the target species: large mammals prefer wide, open overpasses with natural vegetation, while amphibians and small mammals use damp tunnels and culverts. Studies across North America and Europe show that well-designed crossings, paired with fencing, can reduce animal-vehicle collisions by 80 percent or more.
