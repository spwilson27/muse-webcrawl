Greek Revival architecture

A Greek Revival temple front with Doric columns and pediment.

The Greek Revival was an architectural movement of the late eighteenth and early-to-mid nineteenth centuries, predominantly in northern Europe and the United States, that sought to revive the forms of ancient Greek architecture, particularly the temple with its orders, pediment and columnar portico. It was the last phase of Neoclassical architecture and followed earlier revivals of Roman models.

Origins

Renewed interest in Greek antiquity came from archaeological publication — notably James "Athenian" Stuart and Nicholas Revett's The Antiquities of Athens (1762) — and from the view that Greece, as the cradle of democracy and the source of Roman art, offered purer models than Rome. The Greek War of Independence (1821–29) added political sympathy ("philhellenism") to aesthetic admiration. Early landmarks include the work of Karl Friedrich Schinkel in Berlin (the Altes Museum, 1823–30) and Leo von Klenze in Munich (the Glyptothek and the Propyläen).

In Britain

British examples include William Wilkins's National Gallery in London (1832–38), the British Museum by Robert Smirke (with its great Ionic colonnade), and a remarkable concentration in Edinburgh — "the Athens of the North" — including the Royal High School by Thomas Hamilton and the National Monument on Calton Hill, a partial Parthenon replica left unfinished for lack of funds. Churches, town halls and country houses adopted the style widely.

In the United States

In the United States the Greek Revival became the first truly national architectural style, flourishing from about 1825 to 1860. Its association with Athenian democracy suited the young republic, and pattern books by Asher Benjamin and Minard Lafever spread simplified versions to farmhouses across the country: typically a gabled front treated as a pediment, wide corner boards suggesting pilasters, and an entry framed with sidelights. Thomas U. Walter's Founders' Hall at Girard College, William Strickland's Second Bank of the United States and Tennessee State Capitol, and countless courthouses, plantation houses and churches made the style ubiquitous; it is also the look of many antebellum Southern mansions.

Decline

From the 1840s the Greek Revival was displaced by the Italianate, Gothic Revival and other picturesque styles better adapted to domestic planning and cheaper materials, though it persisted for banks, courthouses and public buildings into the twentieth century in a stricter classical form.
