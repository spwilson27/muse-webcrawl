Matzah, matzo, or maẓẓah (Hebrew: מַצָּה; plural matzot) is an unleavened flatbread that is part of Jewish cuisine and forms an integral element of the Passover festival, during which chametz (leaven and five grains that, per Jewish law, are self-leavening) is forbidden.

According to the Torah, the Israelites fled Egypt in such haste that they had no time to let their bread rise; matzah commemorates this exodus. During the Passover Seder, three matzot are traditionally used, and a piece of the middle matzah — the afikoman — is hidden, broken, and eaten as the last food of the meal.

Preparation

Matzah dough is made from flour and water only and must be baked before it begins to rise; under Orthodox supervision, the entire process from mixing to baking must be completed within 18 minutes. Matzah may be made from wheat, spelt, barley, rye or oat flour. Shmura matzah ("guarded" matzah) is made from grain supervised from the time of harvest to prevent contact with water.

Uses

Beyond the Seder, matzah is ground into matzah meal, used in dishes such as matzah balls for soup, matzah brei (fried with egg), and Passover cakes and kugels. Machine-made square matzah became widespread in the 19th and 20th centuries alongside traditional handmade round matzah.
