Frequency analysis

Typical letter frequencies in English: E is by far the most common.

In cryptanalysis, frequency analysis (also known as counting letters) is the study of the frequency of letters or groups of letters in a ciphertext. The method is used as an aid to breaking classical ciphers. Frequency analysis is based on the fact that, in any given stretch of written language, certain letters and combinations of letters occur with varying frequencies. Moreover, there is a characteristic distribution of letters that is roughly the same for almost all samples of that language.

For instance, in English, the letter E is the most common (about 12–13% of letters), followed by T, A, O, I and N, while Z, Q and J are rare. In a monoalphabetic substitution cipher, the most frequent ciphertext symbol will very likely stand for E, and patterns in digrams (such as TH, HE, IN) and trigrams (THE, AND) allow the analyst to reconstruct the key systematically.

History

The technique was first described by the Arab polymath Al-Kindi in the 9th century in his Manuscript on Deciphering Cryptographic Messages, making him a founder of cryptanalysis. It dominated codebreaking in Europe through the Renaissance and early modern period; Mary, Queen of Scots' downfall in the Babington Plot (1586) followed the frequency-based decipherment of her letters. Edgar Allan Poe dramatised the method in "The Gold-Bug" (1843).

Countermeasures

Frequency analysis works only when the cipher preserves plaintext statistics. Countermeasures include polyalphabetic ciphers (the Vigenère cipher), which flatten the distribution by using multiple alphabets; homophonic substitution, giving common letters several symbols; and transposition and fractionation. Vigenère fell in turn to the Kasiski examination and index of coincidence, which reduce it to multiple frequency-analysis problems. Modern ciphers are designed so that ciphertext is statistically indistinguishable from random noise, making frequency analysis useless against them — though it remains effective against simple ciphers in puzzles and against any system that leaks language patterns.
