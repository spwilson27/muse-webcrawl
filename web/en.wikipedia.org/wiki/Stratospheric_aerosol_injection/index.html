Stratospheric aerosol injection

Stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) is a proposed method of solar geoengineering (or solar radiation modification) that would introduce aerosols into the stratosphere to create a cooling effect on the Earth's climate. The approach is modelled on the global cooling observed after large volcanic eruptions, such as the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo, which injected sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere and lowered global temperatures by roughly 0.5 °C for about a year.

Proposals

Proposed delivery methods include high-altitude aircraft, balloons, and artillery, releasing precursor gases such as sulfur dioxide or hydrogen sulfide that form sulfate aerosols, or directly dispersing solid particles such as calcite or alumina. The aerosols would scatter a small fraction of incoming sunlight back to space, offsetting some warming from greenhouse gases.

Risks and governance

SAI would not reduce atmospheric carbon dioxide or ocean acidification, and modelling suggests it could alter precipitation patterns, delay ozone-layer recovery, and produce uneven regional effects. If deployment stopped abruptly, temperatures would rebound rapidly — the "termination shock" problem. Because SAI could in principle be deployed unilaterally at relatively low cost, its governance is a subject of international debate. See also solar geoengineering.
