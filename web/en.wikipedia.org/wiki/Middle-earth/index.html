Middle-earth is the central continent of the Earth (Arda) in J. R. R. Tolkien's fantasy works. The term is taken from the Old English middangeard and its Germanic cognates, denoting the inhabited world of men between the heavens and the underworld.

Tolkien prepared detailed geographies, histories, languages, and genealogies for Middle-earth, which serves as the setting of The Hobbit (1937), The Lord of the Rings (1954–55), and, posthumously, The Silmarillion (1977).

Geography

The northwestern portion of Middle-earth, where the famous narratives take place, includes the Shire of the Hobbits, the Elven realms of Rivendell and Lothlórien, the Dwarf-kingdom of Moria and later Erebor, and the kingdoms of Men—Arnor and Gondor—as well as the dark land of Mordor, ruled by Sauron. Geography and history are shaped by the War of the Ring and by earlier ages: the First Age centred on Beleriand, drowned at its close; the Second Age saw the rise and fall of Númenor.

Peoples and languages

Tolkien constructed full languages for his peoples, notably the Elvish tongues Quenya and Sindarin, and drew on Norse, Anglo-Saxon, Finnish, and Welsh sources for names and mythic themes. The legendarium frames Middle-earth as a mythic prehistory of our own world.

Influence

Middle-earth is widely regarded as the foundational setting of modern fantasy literature, influencing generations of authors, games, and film adaptations, including Peter Jackson's trilogies. Tolkien scholarship, fan culture, and societies devoted to his languages continue to expand its study.
