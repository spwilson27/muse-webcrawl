Sovereign immunity

Sovereign immunity shields states from suit without their consent

Sovereign immunity, or crown immunity, is a legal doctrine whereby a sovereign or state cannot commit a legal wrong and is immune from civil suit or criminal prosecution, strictly speaking in modern texts in its own courts. A similar, stronger rule applies to foreign states: a state is immune from the jurisdiction of the courts of another state. The doctrine derives from the Latin maxim rex non potest peccare ("the king can do no wrong") and from the principle that equal sovereigns have no jurisdiction over one another (par in parem non habet imperium).

Types

Sovereign immunity takes two forms:

  • Immunity from suit (jurisdiction) — a court cannot hear a claim against the state.
  • Immunity from enforcement (execution) — even where a judgment exists, state property cannot be seized to satisfy it.

Historically, immunity was absolute: a state could not be sued for any act. During the 20th century most legal systems moved to a restrictive theory, under which immunity is preserved for sovereign or governmental acts (acta jure imperii) but denied for commercial acts (acta jure gestionis), such as ordinary business transactions.

By jurisdiction

United States

In the United States, the federal government and state governments enjoy sovereign immunity unless they waive it. Congress abrogated state immunity for some purposes under statutes such as the Federal Tort Claims Act at the federal level, and state immunity in federal court is reinforced by the Eleventh Amendment. Suits against foreign states are governed by the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act of 1976, which adopts the restrictive theory with exceptions including commercial activity and terrorism-related claims.

United Kingdom

The Crown Proceedings Act 1947 largely abolished the Crown's immunity from suit in the United Kingdom, allowing actions in contract and tort against government departments. Immunity of foreign states is governed by the State Immunity Act 1978, again on restrictive principles.

International law

State immunity is also a rule of customary international law, addressed by the United Nations Convention on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and Their Property (adopted 2004, not yet in force). Debated exceptions include claims arising from grave human-rights violations; the International Court of Justice held in Jurisdictional Immunities of the State (Germany v. Italy) (2012) that immunity was not displaced even by allegations of serious violations of humanitarian law.

Waiver

A state may waive its immunity expressly, for example by treaty or by contract clause, and waivers are construed narrowly: waiver of immunity from suit does not automatically waive immunity from execution against state property.
