Personhood

Personhood is the status of being a person — a bearer of moral rights, duties, and (in law) legal standing. Defining personhood is a controversial topic in philosophy and law, implicated in debates over abortion, animal rights, artificial intelligence, and the rights of corporations and natural entities.

Philosophical accounts

Philosophers have proposed criteria including consciousness, self-awareness, rationality, the capacity to suffer, and membership in a moral community. John Locke defined a person as "a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself". Kantian ethics treats rational persons as ends in themselves, the ground of human dignity. Utilitarian thinkers such as Peter Singer distinguish being a member of Homo sapiens from being a person, arguing that some non-human animals are persons while some humans, in a purely descriptive sense, may not meet the criteria — a position sharply criticised by disability advocates and others. In bioethics, personhood underlies disputes over the moral status of embryos and fetuses ("personhood" amendments in US politics seek to extend legal rights from conception) and of patients in persistent vegetative states.

Legal personhood

In law, a "person" need not be a human being: corporations, municipalities, and in some jurisdictions ships, temples, and rivers (such as New Zealand's Whanganui River, granted legal personhood in 2017) can hold rights and duties as legal persons. Conversely, throughout history enslaved people and women were denied full legal personhood in many systems. Animal-law campaigns have sought habeas corpus and personhood status for great apes, elephants, and cetaceans, with courts in Argentina and India granting limited recognitions and US courts generally declining.
