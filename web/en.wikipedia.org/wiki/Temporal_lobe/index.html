Temporal lobe

The temporal lobe is one of the four major lobes of the cerebral cortex in the brain of mammals. It is located beneath the lateral fissure (Sylvian fissure) on both cerebral hemispheres. The temporal lobe is involved in processing sensory input into derived meanings for the appropriate retention of visual memory, language comprehension, and emotion association.

The temporal lobe of the left cerebral hemisphere

Structure

The temporal lobe contains the hippocampus and the amygdala, key structures of the limbic system involved in memory formation and emotion. Its upper surface includes the primary auditory cortex (Brodmann areas 41 and 42) and Wernicke's area (Brodmann area 22) in the dominant hemisphere, which is essential for the comprehension of spoken and written language. The lobe is divided into the superior, middle and inferior temporal gyri.

Function

The auditory cortex processes sound, allowing perception of pitch and loudness and contributing to speech recognition. The ventral stream of visual processing passes through the inferior temporal cortex, supporting object and face recognition. The medial temporal lobe, centred on the hippocampus, consolidates short-term memories into long-term memories; damage to it, as in the famous patient H.M., produces profound anterograde amnesia.

Clinical significance

Temporal lobe epilepsy is the most common form of focal epilepsy, often arising from hippocampal sclerosis and producing seizures that may involve déjà vu, automatisms and altered awareness. Damage to Wernicke's area causes receptive aphasia, characterised by fluent but meaningless speech and poor comprehension. Tumours, stroke and herpes simplex encephalitis also frequently affect this lobe.
