Bone china

Bone china is a type of porcelain composed of bone ash, feldspathic material and kaolin. It is characterised by high whiteness, translucency and mechanical strength, together with a delicate appearance, and is traditionally regarded as the finest of tableware ceramics.

History

Bone ash had been added experimentally to porcelain in England from the 1740s, notably at the Bow factory, but the standard bone china body was perfected by Josiah Spode in Stoke-on-Trent in the 1790s. The addition of bone ash made the body whiter, more translucent and far less prone to cracking, allowing English potteries to compete with Chinese and continental porcelain.

Production

Traditional bone china contains around 50% bone ash (calcined cattle bone), 25% kaolin and 25% Cornish stone. It is fired first at about 1,200–1,300 °C in a biscuit firing and then at a lower temperature after glazing — the reverse of hard-paste porcelain. The high bone-ash content gives the material its characteristic warm white colour and the ability to transmit light when thin.
