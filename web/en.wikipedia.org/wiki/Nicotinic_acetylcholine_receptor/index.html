Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor

Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) are receptor polypeptides that respond to the neurotransmitter acetylcholine. They are ligand-gated ion channels named for their sensitivity to nicotine, in contrast to muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, which are G protein-coupled. nAChRs are found in the central and peripheral nervous systems, muscle, and other tissues.

Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor

Type: Ligand-gated ion channel

Agonists: Acetylcholine, nicotine, epibatidine

Antagonists: Curare, α-bungarotoxin, mecamylamine

Subunits: α1–α10, β1–β4, γ, δ, ε

Structure

Each receptor is a pentamer of five subunits arranged around a central ion-conducting pore. The muscle-type receptor at the neuromuscular junction has the composition (α1)2β1γδ in fetal tissue, with γ replaced by ε in adults. Neuronal receptors are assembled from α and β subunits; the α7 and α4β2 subtypes are prominent in the brain. Binding of acetylcholine at subunit interfaces triggers a conformational change that opens the pore, allowing Na+ and K+ (and for some subtypes Ca2+) to flow, depolarizing the cell.

Function and clinical significance

At the neuromuscular junction, nAChRs mediate voluntary muscle contraction; antibodies against them cause the autoimmune disease myasthenia gravis. In the brain, nicotinic receptors modulate the release of many neurotransmitters and are implicated in nicotine addiction, Alzheimer's disease and schizophrenia. Many natural toxins and drugs, including curare and the anaesthetic agent tubocurarine, act at these receptors.
