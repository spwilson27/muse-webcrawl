Concurrency (computer science)

In computer science, concurrency is the ability of different parts or units of a program, algorithm, or problem to be executed out-of-order or in partial order, without affecting the final outcome. This allows for parallel execution of the concurrent units, which can significantly improve overall speed of the execution in multi-processor and multi-core systems. In more technical terms, concurrency refers to the decomposability of a program, algorithm, or problem into order-independent or partially-ordered components or units of computation.

Concurrent computations may execute sequentially by interleaving on a single processor, or in true parallel on multiple cores. (placeholder image)

According to Rob Pike, concurrency is the composition of independently executing computations, and concurrency is not parallelism: concurrency is about dealing with lots of things at once, while parallelism is about doing lots of things at once. Concurrency is a property of a program's structure; parallelism is a property of its execution.

History

The study of concurrent systems dates to the 1960s, with Edsger W. Dijkstra credited with the first paper in the field, "Cooperating Sequential Processes" (1965), which introduced the mutual exclusion problem and the semaphore. The development of operating systems, time-sharing, and later multi-core processors made concurrency a central concern of computer science. C. A. R. Hoare's Communicating Sequential Processes (CSP, 1978) and Robin Milner's Calculus of Communicating Systems (CCS) provided formal calculi for reasoning about concurrent behaviour.

Concepts

Processes and threads

A process is an instance of a program in execution with its own memory space, while threads are units of execution within a process that share memory. Concurrent programs may use several processes (multiprocessing) or several threads (multithreading). Because shared memory introduces the possibility of interference, concurrent programming requires coordination mechanisms.

Race conditions

A race condition occurs when the behaviour of a program depends on the relative timing of concurrent operations — for example, when two threads read and update a shared variable and the final value depends on the order in which their accesses interleave. A sequence of operations that must be executed atomically relative to other threads is called a critical section.

Synchronization

Synchronization primitives include locks (mutexes), semaphores, monitors, condition variables, barriers, and atomic operations. Higher-level approaches include message passing, in which processes communicate by exchanging messages, as in the actor model and CSP-style channels; this avoids shared state and the locks it requires.

Deadlock

A deadlock is a state in which each member of a group of processes is waiting for another member to release a resource. The four Coffman conditions — mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption, and circular wait — must all hold simultaneously for deadlock to occur; breaking any one of them prevents deadlock.

Models and theory

Formal models of concurrency include process calculi (CSP, CCS, the π-calculus), Petri nets, the actor model, and dataflow models. A fundamental theoretical question is the notion of consistency or memory model: sequential consistency, proposed by Leslie Lamport, requires that the result of any execution be the same as if operations of all processors were executed in some sequential order.

Performance limits

Amdahl's law bounds the speedup obtainable from parallelising a program: if a fraction p of the work can be parallelised, the maximum speedup is 1 / ((1−p) + p/n) for n processors. Gustafson's law gives a more optimistic accounting for scaled workloads. Concurrency also underlies techniques such as asynchronous I/O, event loops, and reactive programming, where a single thread juggles many tasks by never blocking.
