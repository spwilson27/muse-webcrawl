The Hawaiian bobtail squid (Euprymna scolopes) is a small species of bobtail squid native to shallow waters of the Hawaiian Islands. It is celebrated in biology for its symbiotic relationship with the bioluminescent bacterium Aliivibrio fischeri, which colonises a specialised light organ in the squid's mantle; the partnership is a leading model system for studying animal–bacterial symbiosis.

Description and behaviour

Adults reach only about 3 cm in mantle length. The squid is nocturnal: by day it buries itself in sand, glued in place with mucus, and at night it emerges to hunt shrimp and other small prey in shallow reef flats. Its bioluminescent light organ performs counter-illumination camouflage — by matching the intensity and colour of moonlight from below and venting excess bacteria to adjust brightness, the squid erases its silhouette so predators beneath cannot spot it against the surface.

The Vibrio symbiosis

Juvenile squid hatch without symbionts and must acquire A. fischeri from seawater within hours. Ciliated fields on the juvenile light organ capture the bacteria, which migrate through pores and ducts into deep crypts, triggering dramatic developmental remodelling of the organ — one of the clearest demonstrations that bacteria direct animal development. Each dawn the squid expels about 90–95% of the symbiont population, seeding the environment and keeping the culture in check; the bacteria regrow during the day. The association, developed as a research model chiefly by Margaret McFall-Ngai and Edward Ruby, illuminates how animals select beneficial microbes, with parallels to the human gut microbiome.

Research and spaceflight

E. scolopes has become a laboratory model organism, with a sequenced genome and established husbandry. Specimens have even been sent to the International Space Station to study how microgravity affects beneficial animal–microbe interactions.
