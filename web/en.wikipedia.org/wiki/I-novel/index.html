The I-novel grew out of Japanese naturalism in the first decades of the twentieth century.

The I-novel (私小説, shishōsetsu, also read watakushi shōsetsu) is a literary genre in Japanese literature in which the author narrates, in a largely autobiographical and confessional form, events from his or her own life. The "I" of the title (from the Japanese first-person pronoun watakushi) signals that the narrator is understood to be the author, and the reader is expected to read the work as a sincere, thinly veiled account of the writer's actual experiences and inner states.

Origins

The genre emerged from Japanese naturalism in the 1900s–1910s. Tayama Katai's Futon (The Quilt, 1907), a frank depiction of the author's infatuation with a young female disciple, is often cited as the first I-novel. Unlike European naturalism, which prized detached third-person observation, Japanese writers turned the naturalist method inward, treating the author's own psyche and circumstances as the most reliable material. Shimazaki Tōson's Hakai (The Broken Commandment, 1906) is an important forerunner.

Characteristics

I-novels are typically written in the first person, focus on everyday, often trivial episodes, and privilege psychological honesty and immediacy over plot, structure, or fictional invention. The genre established a durable critical expectation in Japan that serious literature should be rooted in the author's lived experience, and a "sincerity" of confession became a standard of literary value. Shiga Naoya, celebrated as a "god of the novel" (shōsetsu no kamisama), refined the form in works such as An'ya Kōro (A Dark Night's Passing). Dazai Osamu's No Longer Human (1948) is among the genre's most famous later examples.

Criticism

The dominance of the I-novel has been criticised for narrowing the ambitions of Japanese fiction, discouraging large-scale social novels and rewarding self-absorbed subject matter. Defenders reply that its disciplined attention to interior life produced works of great artistry, and that the boundary between autobiography and fiction within the genre is more playful than the confessional convention suggests. The genre's influence persists in contemporary Japanese autofiction and in debates about the relationship between author and narrator.
