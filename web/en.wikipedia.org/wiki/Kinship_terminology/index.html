Kinship terminology

Kinship terminology is the system used in languages to describe the persons to whom an individual is related through kinship. Different societies classify kinship relations differently; in particular, some kin terms used in one society may collapse several relationships distinguished in another. The systematic study of kinship terminologies began with Lewis Henry Morgan's Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity of the Human Family (1871).

The six classic systems

Anthropologists commonly distinguish six principal patterns of kin classification, named after peoples or regions where they were first described:

SystemDefining features
Eskimo (Inuit / lineal)Nuclear family distinguished; all cousins merged into one category; aunts and uncles distinguished from parents. Typical of English and most European languages.
Hawaiian (generational)Only sex and generation distinguish kin: all relatives of the parental generation are "mother" and "father"; all of ego's generation are "brother" and "sister".
Sudanese (descriptive)Nearly every relative has a distinct term; no two types of cousin share a term.
OmahaPatrilineal skewing: members of mother's patrilineage across generations are merged (mother's brother and mother's sister's son both "mother's brother").
CrowMatrilineal counterpart of Omaha, with skewing in father's matrilineage.
Iroquois (bifurcate merging)Father and father's brother merged ("father"); mother and mother's sister merged ("mother"); parallel cousins called by sibling terms, cross cousins distinct.

Dimensions of contrast

Kin terms may vary along several dimensions: generation, gender, lineal versus collateral relatives, consanguines versus affines (relatives by marriage), and relative age (for example, distinct terms for elder and younger brother in many Asian languages). Systems also differ in whether they use the same terms for cross cousins and parallel cousins, a distinction central to theories of prescriptive marriage alliance associated with Claude Lévi-Strauss.

Kinship and society

Morgan argued that terminologies reflected forms of marriage and social organisation; later scholars such as Alfred Radcliffe-Brown and David Schneider questioned how directly terminology maps onto behaviour, Schneider arguing that American kinship is a cultural system of symbols centred on blood and law. Modern work treats kin terms as part of wider systems of relatedness rather than simple reflections of genealogy.
