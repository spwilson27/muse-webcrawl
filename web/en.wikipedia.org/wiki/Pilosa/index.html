Sloths and anteaters are the living representatives of Pilosa.

Pilosa is an order of placental mammals within the superorder Xenarthra, native to the Americas. It comprises the sloths (suborder Folivora) and the anteaters (suborder Vermilingua). The name Pilosa, meaning "hairy," refers to the shaggy coats of its members, and distinguishes the group from the armoured armadillos of the sister order Cingulata.

Living members

Only ten species survive today: six sloths, in the two-toed and three-toed genera, and four anteaters, including the giant anteater and the tiny silky anteater. All are specialised feeders with low metabolic rates; sloths are arboreal leaf-eaters, while anteaters consume vast numbers of ants and termites with long sticky tongues and strong digging claws.

Evolutionary history

The fossil record of Pilosa is far richer than its present diversity. During the Pleistocene, South and North America supported giant ground sloths such as Megatherium, which stood six metres tall, and the semi-aquatic Thalassocnus. These giants, along with the tank-like glyptodonts of the related armadillo lineage, vanished in the extinctions at the end of the last Ice Age, roughly 10,000 years ago.

Characteristics

Pilosans share the xenarthran trait of additional ("xenarthrous") articulations between the vertebrae of the lower spine. Sloths have unusually variable neck vertebrae — up to nine in three-toed sloths, allowing the head to rotate through some 270 degrees — and anteaters have entirely toothless, elongated skulls in the giant species.
