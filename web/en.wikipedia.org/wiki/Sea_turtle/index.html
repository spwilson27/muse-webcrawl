Sea turtles (superfamily Chelonioidea), sometimes called marine turtles, are reptiles of the order Testudines adapted to life in the sea. Seven species survive: the green, loggerhead, hawksbill, Kemp's ridley, olive ridley, and flatback turtles (family Cheloniidae), and the leatherback (family Dermochelyidae), the largest turtle in the world. Sea turtles are found in all oceans except the polar regions and, like other turtles, are ectothermic and breathe air, surfacing regularly.

Biology

Unlike land turtles, sea turtles cannot retract their limbs into their streamlined shells; their forelimbs are flippers specialised for swimming. They have excellent underwater vision and a remarkable navigational ability: females return to nest on the same beaches where they hatched, guided in part by the Earth's magnetic field. Sex of hatchlings is determined by nest temperature. Diets vary by species: green turtles graze seagrass, leatherbacks specialise in jellyfish, hawksbills eat sponges, and loggerheads crush shellfish. Some species undertake migrations of thousands of kilometres between feeding and nesting grounds.

Life cycle

Females haul out at night to dig nests above the high-tide line, laying clutches of roughly 100 eggs before returning to the sea; they may nest several times in a season and breed only every few years. After about two months the hatchlings emerge, usually at night, and scramble to the sea, suffering heavy predation. Maturity may take 20–50 years in the wild.

Threats and conservation

Most sea turtle species are listed as vulnerable, endangered, or critically endangered. Threats include coastal development destroying nesting beaches, artificial lighting disorienting hatchlings, plastic pollution and entanglement, poaching for meat, eggs, and hawksbill "tortoiseshell", and — one of the largest causes of mortality — accidental capture (bycatch) in shrimp trawls and longlines. Turtle excluder devices, protected nesting beaches, and international agreements have helped some populations recover, notably green turtles at several rookeries.
