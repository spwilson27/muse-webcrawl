Scouting

Founded: 1907 (Brownsea Island camp); 1908 (Scouting for Boys)
Founder: Robert Baden-Powell
Members: Over 57 million (WOSM and WAGGGS combined, approx.)
World bodies: WOSM; WAGGGS

Scouting, also known as the Scout Movement, is a worldwide youth social movement employing the Scout method — a programme of informal education emphasising practical outdoor activities, including camping, woodcraft, aquatics, hiking, backpacking, and sports. Founded by British Army officer Robert Baden-Powell following an experimental camp on Brownsea Island in 1907 and the publication of Scouting for Boys in 1908, it grew into one of the largest youth movements in history.

The Scout method

Core elements include the Scout Promise and Law, learning by doing, the patrol system (small peer-led groups), a progressive programme of badges and awards, adult support, and symbolic framework — expressed in uniforms, salutes, and the fleur-de-lis emblem. Scouting aims to develop character, citizenship, leadership, and personal fitness. Younger sections (Beavers, Cubs) and older sections (Venturers, Rovers) extend the programme beyond the core 11–14 Scout age band.

Organisation

The movement is represented internationally by the World Organization of the Scout Movement (WOSM, for boys and co-educational associations) and the World Association of Girl Guides and Girl Scouts (WAGGGS). World Scout Jamborees, held every four years, draw tens of thousands of participants. National organisations include Scouting America (formerly the Boy Scouts of America) and The Scout Association in the UK. Guiding, founded in 1910 after girls demanded inclusion at the 1909 Crystal Palace rally, developed in parallel under Baden-Powell and his wife Olave, later World Chief Guide.

History and influence

Baden-Powell drew on his experiences in the Second Boer War — including the cadet corps at the Siege of Mafeking — and on Ernest Thompson Seton's Woodcraft Indians. Scouting spread rapidly through the British Empire and the world before and after the First World War. The movement has been adapted, and occasionally co-opted, in diverse political contexts; totalitarian regimes variously banned it or modelled state youth organisations on it. Modern Scouting addresses social inclusion — most national associations are now co-educational, and Scouting America admitted girls to its flagship programme in 2019 — alongside safeguarding reforms and youth leadership initiatives.
