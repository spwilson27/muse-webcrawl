The Pulitzer Prize for Fiction is one of the seven American Pulitzer Prizes awarded annually for Letters, Drama, and Music. It recognises distinguished fiction by an American author, preferably dealing with American life, published during the preceding calendar year. From 1918 to 1947 the category was known as the Pulitzer Prize for the Novel.

History

The prize was established under the will of newspaper publisher Joseph Pulitzer and is administered by Columbia University. The first winner, in 1918, was Ernest Poole for His Family. In several years — including 1977, 2012, and 2020's predecessors controversies aside — the board has declined to award the prize when juries' finalists were judged unsuitable, prompting periodic public debate.

Notable winners

Winners include Edith Wharton's The Age of Innocence (1921), Willa Cather's One of Ours (1923), Margaret Mitchell's Gone with the Wind (1937), John Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath (1940), Harper Lee's To Kill a Mockingbird (1961), Toni Morrison's Beloved (1988), Junot Díaz's The Brief Wondrous Life of Oscar Wao (2008), and Colson Whitehead, who won twice, for The Underground Railroad (2017) and The Nickel Boys (2020). William Faulkner and John Updike also each won the fiction prize twice.

Selection process

A three-member jury reads hundreds of submitted titles and submits three finalists to the Pulitzer Board, which makes the final decision. Winners receive a certificate and a cash award of $15,000.
