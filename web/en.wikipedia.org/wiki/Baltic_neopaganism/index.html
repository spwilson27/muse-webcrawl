Baltic neopaganism


A Romuva ritual gathering in Lithuania.

Baltic neopaganism is a category of autochthonous religious movements that aim to revive or reconstruct the pre-Christian religions of the Baltic peoples — the Lithuanians, Latvians, and the extinct Old Prussians. The Baltic lands were among the last in Europe to be Christianised (Lithuania officially in 1387, with pagan practices persisting for centuries afterwards), and rich folklore, folk songs (dainas), and seasonal customs preserved substantial material for revival.

Movements

  • Romuva (Lithuania) — named after a legendary Prussian sanctuary, Romuva traces its organised revival to the folklorist Jonas Trinkūnas, who held solstice celebrations from 1967 despite Soviet suppression. Re-established openly after 1988, it was registered as a religious community in 1992. Its long campaign for state recognition as a "traditional" religion in Lithuania was refused by the Seimas in 2019 and again debated thereafter; the European Court of Human Rights ruled in 2021 that the refusal process had violated principles of impartiality.
  • Dievturība (Latvia) — founded in the 1920s by Ernests Brastiņš, Dievturība ("god-keeping") systematises Latvian folk religion around the god Dievs and the ethical world of the dainas. Suppressed under Soviet occupation, it revived after independence.
  • Prussian and other revivals — smaller groups attempt reconstruction of Old Prussian religion, sometimes in cooperation with Lithuanian Romuva communities.

Beliefs and practice

Baltic neopaganism is generally polytheistic and nature-centred. Important deities include Dievas/Dievs (the sky god), Perkūnas/Perkūns (thunder), Žemyna (earth), Laima (fate), Saulė (the sun), and Mėnulis (the moon). Central values include darna — harmony with nature, community, and the divine order. Rituals mark the solstices and equinoxes (such as Joninės/Rasos at midsummer and Vėlinės for the dead), often involving fire, sacred groves and hills (alkas), folk singing, and offerings. Marriage and naming ceremonies are also conducted.

Scholars debate how far the movements represent continuity with historical Baltic religion versus modern reconstruction informed by folklore studies and national romanticism; adherents themselves vary in their views on the question.
