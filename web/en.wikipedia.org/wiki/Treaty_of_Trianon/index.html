The Treaty of Trianon (French: Traité de Trianon; Hungarian: Trianoni békeszerződés) is the peace agreement of 1920 that formally ended World War I between most of the Allies and the Kingdom of Hungary. The treaty regulated the status of the new Hungarian state and defined its new borders, radically reducing Hungary's territory and population.

Terms

Under the treaty, Hungary lost about two-thirds of its pre-war territory (from roughly 283,000 km² to 93,000 km²) and more than half of its population. Transylvania and other territories went to Romania; Slovakia and Carpathian Ruthenia to Czechoslovakia; Croatia-Slavonia and Vojvodina to the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (Yugoslavia); and Burgenland to Austria. Hungary's army was limited to 35,000 men, and it was required to pay reparations. About 3.3 million ethnic Hungarians found themselves outside the new borders.

Consequences

The treaty became known in Hungary as the "Trianon trauma" and remains a central, painful reference point in Hungarian politics and culture. The desire to revise the treaty drove Hungarian foreign policy in the interwar period and its alignment with Nazi Germany, which produced the Vienna Awards of 1938 and 1940. The borders were essentially restored after World War II by the Paris Peace Treaties of 1947.
