Nivolumab, sold under the brand name Opdivo, is a cancer immunotherapy medication. It is a fully human IgG4 monoclonal antibody that blocks the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) receptor on T cells, preventing tumour cells from switching those T cells off via PD-L1/PD-L2 and thereby restoring anti-tumour immune responses. It belongs to the class of immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Medical uses

Nivolumab is approved, alone or in combination with the CTLA-4 inhibitor ipilimumab or chemotherapy, for numerous cancers: advanced melanoma (including adjuvant treatment), non-small cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, classical Hodgkin lymphoma, squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck, urothelial carcinoma, colorectal cancer with mismatch-repair deficiency, hepatocellular carcinoma, oesophageal and gastric cancers, malignant pleural mesothelioma, and others; indications have continued to expand into perioperative and neoadjuvant settings. A fixed combination with relatlimab (anti–LAG-3) is marketed for melanoma, and a subcutaneous formulation (Opdivo Qvantig, with hyaluronidase) was approved in the United States in late 2024.

Adverse effects

Unleashing the immune system can cause immune-related adverse events affecting almost any organ: rash and itching, colitis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, thyroid and other endocrine disorders, and rarely myocarditis or neurological syndromes. These are managed by withholding the drug and giving corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. Common non-immune effects include fatigue, nausea, and decreased appetite.

History

Nivolumab was developed by Medarex using its transgenic mouse platform, working with Ono Pharmaceutical, which had rights from the PD-1 research of Tasuku Honjo (later a Nobel laureate for this work); Bristol Myers Squibb acquired Medarex in 2009. It was first approved in Japan in July 2014 for melanoma — the first PD-1 inhibitor approved anywhere — and by the U.S. FDA in December 2014. It has become one of the world's best-selling cancer drugs.
