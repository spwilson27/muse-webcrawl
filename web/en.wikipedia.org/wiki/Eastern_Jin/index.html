Eastern Jin (south) and the Sixteen Kingdoms (north), c. 382

The Eastern Jin (simplified Chinese: 东晋; traditional Chinese: 東晉; pinyin: Dōngjìn; 317–420) was a Chinese imperial dynasty and one of the Six Dynasties during the Six Dynasties era. It was founded by Sima Rui (Emperor Yuan) after the fall of the Western Jin to Han-Zhao forces in 316, when the Jin court fled south of the Yangtze River and established a new capital at Jiankang (present-day Nanjing). The dynasty ruled southern China for just over a century while the north fragmented among the Sixteen Kingdoms.

History

Founding and émigré politics

The Eastern Jin court was dominated by émigré aristocratic clans from the north — the Langya Wang clan ("Wang and Sima share the realm", in a contemporary saying), and later the Xie, Yu and Huan families — who balanced power against the emperor. The regime repeatedly faced internal revolts (Wang Dun, Su Jun) and depended on powerful frontier generals. Waves of northern refugees were settled in "commanderies in exile" (qiaoren), preserving their northern place-names in the south.

Struggle with the north

The dynasty's survival turned on the Yangtze and Huai river defences. In 383, at the Battle of Fei River, the Eastern Jin army under Xie An and Xie Xuan destroyed the vastly larger forces of Fu Jian of Former Qin — a victory often credited with preserving Chinese civilisation in the south and prolonging the division of China for two centuries. Subsequent Northern Expeditions by generals such as Liu Yu briefly recovered Luoyang and Chang'an without holding them.

Fall

In the early fifth century Liu Yu, rising from the Beifu Army, crushed internal rivals (Huan Xuan had briefly usurped the throne in 403–404), dominated the court, and in 420 forced the abdication of Emperor Gong, founding the Liu Song dynasty and ending Eastern Jin.

Culture

The Eastern Jin period was culturally brilliant. The calligrapher Wang Xizhi, whose Preface to the Orchid Pavilion (353) is the most celebrated work of Chinese calligraphy, flourished under its patronage; the painter Gu Kaizhi pioneered figure painting; the poet Tao Yuanming (Tao Qian) reinvented pastoral verse; and Buddhism spread deeply among the elite, with monks such as Huiyuan and the translator Kumārajīva (working in the north) transforming Chinese thought. The refined salon culture of "qingtan" ("pure conversation") defined aristocratic life.
