The Medici Bank (Italian: Banco dei Medici) was a financial institution created by the Medici family in Florence, Italy, in 1397. It was the largest and most respected bank in Europe during its prime, and its profits helped make the Medici the effective rulers of Florence and great patrons of Renaissance art.

History

Giovanni di Bicci de' Medici founded the bank in 1397 after managing the Rome branch of a relative's bank. Under Giovanni and his son Cosimo, the bank expanded into a network of branches in Rome, Venice, Milan, Pisa, Geneva (later Lyons), Avignon, Bruges, and London. Each branch was organized as a semi-autonomous partnership with the Florence headquarters, a structure resembling a modern holding company. The bank handled the accounts of the Papacy, dominated European foreign-exchange dealing through bills of exchange, and traded in wool and silk.

Decline and fall

After Cosimo's death in 1464, management weakened under Piero and Lorenzo de' Medici. Branches, especially Bruges and London, made bad loans to rulers such as Edward IV of England and Charles the Bold of Burgundy, and several branches were closed or liquidated. When the Medici were expelled from Florence in 1494, the bank's remaining assets were seized and the bank ceased to exist.

Innovations

The bank is credited with popularizing the bill of exchange, letters of credit, and the use of double-entry bookkeeping across its branch network, and with the partnership system that let a central firm control semi-independent branches — an organizational model influential in the history of banking.
