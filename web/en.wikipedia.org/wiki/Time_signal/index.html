Time signal


Radio-controlled clocks receive broadcast time signals

A time signal is a visible, audible, mechanical, or electronic signal used as a reference to determine the time of day. From church bells and factory whistles to cannon fire and dropping time balls, communities have long relied on public signals to synchronize clocks and coordinate daily life.

History

Public time signals became important in the nineteenth century, when railways and telegraphy made a single standard time essential. Time balls, such as the one installed at the Royal Observatory, Greenwich, in 1833, dropped at a precise hour so navigators in port could rate their chronometers. Time guns fired in many port cities served the same purpose. Telegraphic signals distributed observatory time to railway stations and jewellers.

Radio time signals

With the advent of radio, time signals were broadcast by land-based transmitters and later by dedicated stations such as WWV in the United States, WWVH in Hawaii, and MSF in the United Kingdom. These broadcasts include voice announcements, tone pulses, and coded digital information that radio-controlled clocks decode automatically. Low-frequency signals such as Germany's DCF77 can be received over continental distances.

Modern systems

Satellite navigation systems including GPS broadcast extremely precise time derived from atomic clocks, and computers synchronize over networks using the Network Time Protocol (NTP). In broadcasting, familiar audible signals such as the BBC's "pips" (the Greenwich Time Signal, introduced in 1924) still mark the hour for listeners.
