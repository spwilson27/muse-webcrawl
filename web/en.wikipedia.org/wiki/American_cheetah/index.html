American cheetah

The American cheetah is the common name for two extinct North American cat species of the genus Miracinonyx — M. inexpectatus and the later, more cheetah-like M. trumani — which lived during the Pleistocene epoch. Despite their name and their striking resemblance to the modern African cheetah (Acinonyx jubatus), genetic and anatomical studies indicate they were more closely related to the cougar (Puma concolor), making them a celebrated case of convergent evolution.

Description and evolution

Like the true cheetah, Miracinonyx had a shortened face, enlarged nasal passages, long limbs and reduced claws compared with other big cats — adaptations for fast running in open country. M. inexpectatus retained more puma-like proportions and fully retractile claws, while M. trumani was lighter and more specialised for speed. Fossils have been found across much of North America, from Florida to the Yukon border region.

The pronghorn hypothesis

The American cheetah is frequently proposed as the evolutionary explanation for the extraordinary speed of the pronghorn, which can run far faster than any living North American predator. Under this "ghost of predators past" hypothesis, pronghorns evolved their speed in an arms race with Miracinonyx, and retained it after the cheetah's extinction at the end of the Pleistocene, around 12,000 years ago, in the Quaternary extinction event that also eliminated mammoths, ground sloths and many other American megafauna.

Classification debate

Miracinonyx was once placed in or near Acinonyx, and its fossils prompted a debate over whether true cheetahs originated in the Old World or the New. Ancient DNA analysis published in the 21st century confirmed that the American cheetah belongs to the puma lineage, which also includes the jaguarundi.
