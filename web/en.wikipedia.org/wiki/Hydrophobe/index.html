In chemistry and materials science, a hydrophobe is a molecule or surface that is repelled by water. Hydrophobic molecules tend to be non-polar and thus prefer neutral molecules and non-polar solvents; they do not dissolve in water but instead aggregate, as when oil droplets coalesce in water. The phenomenon underlies the hydrophobic effect, the tendency of non-polar substances to minimise contact with water, which drives protein folding and the self-assembly of cell membranes.

Measurement

Hydrophobicity of a surface is quantified by the contact angle of a water droplet: angles above 90° indicate hydrophobicity, and above about 150° denote a superhydrophobic surface. On a molecular scale, hydrophobicity is often measured by the octanol–water partition coefficient (log P).

Natural examples

Many leaves, including the lotus, have waxy, micro-textured cuticles that shed water and dirt — the "lotus effect" exploited in self-cleaning coatings. Feathers, insect cuticles, and the feet of water striders also rely on hydrophobicity, as do the hydrophobic amino acid side chains buried in protein cores.

Applications

Hydrophobic materials and coatings are used in water-repellent clothing (e.g. fluoropolymer membranes), anti-fogging and anti-icing treatments, oil–water separation, and detergents — whose surfactant molecules have hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails and form micelles around grease.
