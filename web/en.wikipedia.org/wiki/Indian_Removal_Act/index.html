The Indian Removal Act was signed into law by President Andrew Jackson on May 28, 1830, authorizing the president to grant unsettled lands west of the Mississippi River in exchange for Indian lands within existing state borders. The act led to the forced relocation of tens of thousands of Native Americans from the southeastern United States, in a series of migrations remembered as the Trail of Tears.

Background

Since the early republic, federal policy had sought Native American land cessions in the Old Southwest, where the "Five Civilized Tribes" — the Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Seminole, Chickasaw, and Choctaw — held lands coveted by white settlers, especially after the expansion of cotton agriculture. Georgia's extension of state law over Cherokee territory, and the Supreme Court cases Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831) and Worcester v. Georgia (1832), sharpened the conflict between federal treaty obligations and state demands.

Passage

The bill was a legislative priority of the Jackson administration and passed Congress after intense debate, by 102 to 97 in the House of Representatives. Supporters presented removal as voluntary and benevolent; opponents, including many New England congressmen and missionaries, condemned it as a violation of treaties and of Native rights.

Implementation and consequences

Under the act, treaties were negotiated — often with unrepresentative minority factions, as in the Treaty of New Echota (1835) with the Cherokee. The Choctaw were removed in 1831–1834, the Creek in 1836, the Chickasaw in 1837–1838, and the Cherokee in 1838–1839, when some 16,000 Cherokee were forcibly marched west to Indian Territory; roughly 4,000 died of disease, exposure, and starvation. The Seminole resisted in the Second Seminole War (1835–1842). In total, approximately 60,000 Native Americans were removed from the Southeast under the act and its treaties.

Legacy

The Indian Removal Act remains one of the most controversial laws in American history, symbolising the federal government's dispossession of Native peoples. The Cherokee removal, in particular, is commemorated by the Trail of Tears National Historic Trail.
