Traffic collision avoidance system

A traffic alert and collision avoidance system (TCAS, pronounced "tee-kas"), also known internationally as an airborne collision avoidance system (ACAS), is an aircraft collision avoidance system designed to reduce the incidence of mid-air collisions between aircraft. It monitors the airspace around an aircraft for other aircraft equipped with a corresponding active transponder, independent of air traffic control, and warns pilots of the presence of other transponder-equipped aircraft which may present a threat of mid-air collision.

A TCAS traffic display, showing nearby aircraft and their relative altitudes.

Operation

TCAS interrogates the transponders of nearby aircraft (using the 1030/1090 MHz Mode S link) several times per second and builds a three-dimensional picture of surrounding traffic. It issues two levels of alert:

  • Traffic advisories (TAs) — "Traffic, traffic" — which alert the crew to intruding aircraft and help them acquire it visually.
  • Resolution advisories (RAs) — commands to climb, descend, or adjust vertical speed (e.g., "Climb, climb", "Descend, descend"), coordinated between the two aircraft's TCAS units so that their manoeuvres are complementary.

TCAS II, fitted to virtually all commercial airliners, provides vertical-plane resolution advisories; TCAS I, used on smaller aircraft, provides traffic advisories only. Pilots are trained to follow an RA immediately, even in preference to a conflicting air traffic control instruction — a rule reinforced after the 2002 Überlingen mid-air collision, in which one crew followed ATC instead of its RA.

History and mandates

Development followed a series of mid-air collisions, notably the 1956 Grand Canyon collision and the 1986 Cerritos collision near Los Angeles. The United States mandated TCAS II for large commercial aircraft by the early 1990s, and similar requirements were adopted in Europe (through EASA) and worldwide under ICAO standards. The current standard version, TCAS II version 7.1, refined RA logic and reversed some advisory phrasing. ACAS X is a next-generation family under development using probabilistic models and dynamic programming, intended to improve performance and accommodate drones (ACAS Xu) and other new entrants.

Limitations

TCAS can only detect aircraft with operating transponders, it provides no horizontal manoeuvre guidance in TCAS II, and its use is inhibited close to the ground. It is a last-resort safety net, complementing — not replacing — air traffic control separation and see-and-avoid.
