Shūbun

Tenshō Shūbun (天章周文, fl. early–mid 15th century) was a Japanese Zen Buddhist monk and painter of the Muromachi period, regarded as a founder of the Japanese ink painting (suibokuga) tradition. Little is documented of his life; he is believed to have been active in Kyoto, associated with the great Zen temple Shōkoku-ji, and possibly to have travelled to Joseon Korea with a Japanese diplomatic mission in 1423, encountering contemporary Korean court painting.

Work and style

Shūbun worked in the Chinese-derived ink landscape manner, adapting the styles of Song dynasty masters such as Xia Gui and Ma Yuan, and the Yuan painter Gao Ran, to Japanese tastes. His landscapes — steep peaks dissolving into mist, a small pavilion or traveller in the foreground, brushwork ranging from hard axe-cut strokes to pale washes — established the compositional template for Japanese suiboku landscape for a century. The hanging scroll Reading in a Bamboo Grove (竹林読書図, c. 1446), painted for a patron and preserved in the Tokyo National Museum, is his best-known attributed work and a National Treasure of Japan; it includes verses inscribed by Zen monks at the request of the artist.

Legacy

Shūbun is credited with teaching or influencing the next generation of ink painters, most notably his celebrated pupil Sesshū Tōyō, who travelled to Ming China and brought the tradition to maturity, and the line of professional painters at Shōkoku-ji that preceded the Kano school. Because so few works are securely attributed, "Shūbun" also serves art historians as the name for a style and a workshop milieu as much as for a single hand.
