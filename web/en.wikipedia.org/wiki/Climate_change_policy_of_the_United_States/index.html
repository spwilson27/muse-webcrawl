Climate change policy of the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

U.S. greenhouse gas emissions by sector, a central concern of federal climate policy

The climate change policy of the United States comprises the federal, state, and local laws, regulations, executive actions, and international commitments through which the United States addresses climate change. As the world's second-largest emitter of greenhouse gases, U.S. policy has global significance, and its direction has swung sharply between administrations.

Federal legislation

The most significant federal climate law is the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, which directed hundreds of billions of dollars toward clean-energy tax credits, electric-vehicle incentives, and methane-emission fees. Earlier landmarks include the Clean Air Act, under which the EPA regulates greenhouse gases following Massachusetts v. EPA (2007), and the Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007.

Executive action

Presidents have used executive authority to set vehicle fuel-economy (CAFE) standards, power-plant emission rules (the Clean Power Plan of 2015 and its successors), and methane regulations. The Paris Agreement has been a political football: the U.S. joined in 2016, withdrew in 2020, rejoined in 2021, and faced renewed withdrawal threats afterward.

State and local action

States such as California have set their own vehicle-emission standards (waived under the Clean Air Act) and cap-and-trade programs; the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative (RGGI) coordinates power-sector caps across eastern states. Many cities have adopted their own net-zero targets.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue federal policy has been inconsistent and insufficient to meet the U.S. nationally determined contribution under Paris, while supporters of deregulation contend aggressive rules raise energy costs and harm competitiveness.
