Greenhouse and icehouse Earth

Greenhouse Earth and icehouse Earth are the two broad climate states between which the Earth's climate system has alternated over geological time. In a greenhouse state there are no permanent continental ice sheets and global temperatures and sea levels are high; in an icehouse state, large ice sheets exist on one or both poles, as they do today.

Icehouse Earth

Icehouse conditions have prevailed during only a minority of Earth's history but include the Huronian glaciation, the Cryogenian "Snowball Earth" episodes, the late Ordovician glaciation, the late Palaeozoic ice age, and the current Late Cenozoic Ice Age, which began with Antarctic glaciation about 34 million years ago. Within icehouse states, climate oscillates between glacial and interglacial phases, driven in part by Milankovitch cycles and carbon dioxide feedbacks.

Greenhouse Earth

Greenhouse states dominated the Mesozoic and the early Cenozoic, with warm, equable climates, forests growing at high latitudes, and sea levels tens to hundreds of metres above today's. Episodes such as the Cretaceous Thermal Maximum and the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum mark the warm extremes; atmospheric CO2 concentrations were commonly several times modern pre-industrial values, sustained by volcanic outgassing and slow silicate weathering feedbacks.

Transitions

Transitions between the states are governed by plate tectonics, which controls volcanic CO2 supply, weathering sinks, and ocean gateways; the weathering of the rising Himalayas and the opening of Southern Ocean passages are among the factors invoked for the slide into the current icehouse. Because greenhouse intervals occupy most of the record, the present icehouse is sometimes called the anomalous state — and one that anthropogenic emissions are now warming rapidly.
