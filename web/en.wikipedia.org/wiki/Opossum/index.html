Opossums are members of the order Didelphimorphia, the largest order of marsupials in the Western Hemisphere, comprising more than 120 species in 18 genera. They are endemic to the Americas, ranging from southern Canada to Patagonia. The word opossum derives from the Powhatan (Virginia Algonquian) opassom, meaning "white dog" or "white animal".

Opossums should not be confused with the possums of Australia and New Guinea, which belong to a different marsupial order (Diprotodontia) and received their English name from their resemblance to the American animals.

Characteristics

Opossums are typically solitary, nocturnal omnivores ranging in size from the pygmy opossums (about the size of a mouse) to the Virginia opossum (about the size of a domestic cat). Many have prehensile tails, opposable thumbs on their hind feet, and — in females — a marsupium (pouch) in which the tiny newborns develop. The Virginia opossum is famous for "playing possum": an involuntary thanatosis response in which the animal collapses, appears dead, and emits a foul odour when threatened.

Evolution and distribution

Marsupials reached South America from North America in the late Cretaceous or early Paleogene, and opossums diversified there in isolation. After the formation of the Isthmus of Panama some three million years ago, during the Great American Interchange, a few species such as the Virginia opossum spread north again into Central and North America.

Diet and ecology

Opossums are opportunistic feeders eating insects, snails, rodents, fruit, carrion, and garbage; the Virginia opossum consumes large numbers of ticks and is highly resistant to rabies, partly owing to its low body temperature. Several species, notably the water opossum or yapok, are semi-aquatic.
