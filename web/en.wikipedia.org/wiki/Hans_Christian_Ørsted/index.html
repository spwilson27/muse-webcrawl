Hans Christian Ørsted (14 August 1777 – 9 March 1851) was a Danish physicist and chemist who discovered that an electric current creates a magnetic field — the first evidence of the connection between electricity and magnetism, now called electromagnetism. The CGS unit of magnetic field strength, the oersted, is named after him.

Discovery of electromagnetism

In April 1820, during a lecture demonstration at the University of Copenhagen, Ørsted noticed a compass needle deflect when an electric current from a battery was switched on and off, showing that the current-bearing wire produced a circular magnetic field around it. He published his findings in Latin in July 1820 in the short paper Experimenta circa effectum conflictus electrici in acum magneticam. The result immediately inspired André-Marie Ampère's mathematical theory of electrodynamics and, a decade later, Faraday's discovery of electromagnetic induction.

Other work

Ørsted was the first to isolate aluminium (in an impure form) in 1825 by reduction of aluminium chloride, and he also investigated the compressibility of fluids and the chemistry of alkaloids such as piperine. Influenced by Kantian philosophy and Naturphilosophie, he believed in the unity of natural forces and wrote extensively on science and aesthetics. He founded the Society for the Dissemination of Natural Science and the institution that became the Technical University of Denmark (DTU), serving as its first director. He was the elder brother of statesman Anders Sandøe Ørsted and a close friend of Hans Christian Andersen.
