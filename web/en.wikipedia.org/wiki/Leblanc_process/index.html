Leblanc process

The Leblanc process was an early industrial process for making soda ash (sodium carbonate), used throughout the 19th century and named after its inventor, the French surgeon and chemist Nicolas Leblanc. It involved two stages: making sodium sulfate from common salt, then reacting it with coal and limestone to produce sodium carbonate.

Chemistry

In the first stage, salt was treated with concentrated sulfuric acid, yielding sodium sulfate and hydrogen chloride gas: 2 NaCl + H2SO4 → Na2SO4 + 2 HCl. In the second stage, the "salt cake" was heated with crushed limestone and coal, producing sodium carbonate and calcium sulfide.

History

Leblanc patented the process in 1791 during the French Revolution; his plant was confiscated and he never profited from the invention. In Britain the process powered the great alkali works of Tyneside, Lancashire and Glasgow, supplying soda for the soap, glass, textile and paper industries.

Environmental impact and decline

The process released hydrogen chloride gas, devastating vegetation near the works, and left heaps of foul calcium sulfide waste ("galligu"). Public outcry led to Britain's Alkali Act of 1863, the world's first modern air-pollution law. From the 1860s the cleaner, cheaper Solvay process steadily displaced Leblanc soda-making, and the last Leblanc works closed in the early 1920s.
