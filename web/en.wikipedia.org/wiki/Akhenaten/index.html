Akhenaten

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The painted bust of Nefertiti, Akhenaten's queen, with tall blue crown]

Akhenaten (died c. 1335 BC) was an Egyptian pharaoh of the Eighteenth Dynasty — born Amenhotep IV — who launched one of history's most startling religious revolutions. Abandoning Egypt's traditional gods, he promoted the sun-disc Aten as sole god, moved the capital to a new city, Akhetaten (modern Amarna), closed temples, suppressed the cult of Amun, and had his agents erase the names of rival deities. His queen was the famous Nefertiti; the boy king Tutankhamun was his son or close relative. After Akhenaten's death his revolution was reversed and he was remembered, when at all, as 'the heretic king'.

The Amarna revolution

Early in his reign Akhenaten built temples to the Aten at Thebes in a revolutionary artistic style — elongated, naturalistic forms replacing idealised convention. In his fifth year he changed his name, founded Akhetaten on virgin desert ground, and progressively outlawed the old cults. The Great Hymn to the Aten, attributed to the king, praises the sun as universal creator; some scholars see Atenism as an early monotheism, others as royal absolutism in theological dress.

Aftermath

Neglect of foreign policy shrank Egypt's Asiatic influence, as the Amarna Letters — the diplomatic archive found in 1887 — reveal. Akhenaten died after a seventeen-year reign (c. 1336/1335 BC); within a few years Tutankhaten had become Tutankhamun, the court returned to Thebes, the old gods were restored, and Horemheb and the Ramessides demolished Akhetaten and struck Akhenaten from the king lists. His city, art and heresy were rediscovered only in the 19th century.
