Liturgical colours

Altar frontals and vestments change colour with the seasons of the Church year.

Liturgical colours are the colours prescribed or customary for the vestments worn by clergy and for altar hangings, lectern falls, and other church furnishings during Christian worship. Their purpose is symbolic and pedagogical: by marking the seasons of the liturgical year — Advent, Christmas, Lent, Easter, and Ordinary Time — and the nature of feasts, they teach the rhythm of the Church's life and focus the congregation's devotion.

Colours and their seasons

ColourMeaning and season
WhitePurity, joy and glory: Christmas, Easter, feasts of the Lord, the Virgin Mary, angels, and saints who were not martyrs; also weddings and (in many places) funerals
RedFire and blood: Pentecost and the Holy Spirit; feasts of martyrs; Palm Sunday and Good Friday
GreenHope and growth: the "ordinary" Sundays and weekdays outside the great seasons
Violet / purplePenitence and preparation: Advent and Lent; funerals and prayers for the dead in some traditions
GoldFestivity: may substitute for white, red, or green on the greatest feasts
BlueUsed for Advent in the Sarum tradition and, in some churches, for feasts of the Virgin Mary
BlackMourning: formerly standard for funerals and Good Friday in the West; now less common
Rose (pink)Joy within penitence: Gaudete and Laetare Sundays

Regional and denominational variation

The Latin Church standardized its colour sequence after the Council of Trent, and most Western churches that retained liturgical colour — Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist, and some Reformed bodies — follow similar patterns. Eastern Orthodox practice is less regimented: gold is the default colour, bright colours mark Pascha, blue is associated with the Mother of God, and dark colours with Lent, but local custom governs much. Some Protestant churches that once abandoned colour have reintroduced stoles and paraments coloured by season.

Origins

The medieval church developed elaborate colour symbolism: Pope Innocent III (c. 1198–1216) wrote of white for purity, red for martyrdom, green for the "middle" days, and black for mourning. Before standardization, local "uses" — Sarum, York, Parisian, and others — each had their own sequences, some including yellow, russet, and grey (the Lenten array).
