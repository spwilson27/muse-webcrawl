Animal communication

Animal communication is the transfer of information from one or a group of animals (the sender or senders) to one or more other animals (the receiver or receivers) that affects the current or future behaviour of the receivers. Information may be sent intentionally, as in a courtship display, or unintentionally, as when a prey animal's odour betrays it to a predator. The study of animal communication is a central part of ethology, sociobiology, and animal cognition research.

Modalities

Animals communicate through many channels. Visual signals include displays, colour patterns, and bioluminescence. Auditory communication includes bird song, whale song, and the alarm calls of monkeys and prairie dogs, some of which appear to refer to specific predator types. Chemical signals—pheromones—mark territory, coordinate the societies of ants and bees, and signal reproductive state. Tactile, seismic, and electrical signals are also used by various taxa; weakly electric fish, for example, navigate and communicate by modulating their electric fields.

Functions

Communication serves to attract mates, warn of predators, coordinate group hunting or defence, establish dominance and territory, and care for offspring. Classic examples include the honeybee waggle dance, which encodes the direction and distance of food sources, and firefly flash patterns used in courtship—also exploited deceptively by predatory Photuris females imitating the signals of other species.

Language debate

Whether any animal system constitutes a true language remains contested. Trained apes and parrots have learned to use symbols and signs, and dolphin and prairie dog calls show elements of reference and combination, but most researchers hold that animal systems lack the open-ended syntax and displacement characteristic of human language. The field of zoosemiotics studies these sign systems in their own right.
