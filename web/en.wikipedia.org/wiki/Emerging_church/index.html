Emerging church

The emerging church is a Christian movement of the late 20th and early 21st centuries that crosses a number of theological boundaries. Participants seek to live their faith in what they believe to be a "postmodern" society. Proponents of this movement call it a "conversation" to emphasize its developing and decentralized nature. What those involved in the conversation mostly agree on is their disillusionment with the organized and institutional church and their support for the deconstruction of modern Christian worship, evangelism, and community.

History

The movement grew during the 1990s and early 2000s, particularly in the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australasia, partly as a reaction against seeker-sensitive and charismatic megachurch models, and partly as an attempt to engage postmodern culture. Figures associated with the movement include Brian McLaren, Doug Pagitt, Dan Kimball, Tony Jones, and Rob Bell. Organizations such as Emergent Village served as informal networks for the conversation.

Characteristics

Emerging churches often emphasize narrative approaches to theology, communal and incarnational living, hospitality, creativity in worship, and social justice. Worship gatherings may incorporate ancient liturgical practices, candles, icons, and participatory art alongside contemporary music. Participants frequently describe their stance toward doctrine as generous orthodoxy, holding traditional creeds while remaining open about questions.

Criticism

Critics, including some evangelical theologians, have accused the movement of relativism, of abandoning propositional truth, and of accommodating culture at the expense of biblical authority. Others have noted that the label covers a wide and sometimes inconsistent range of communities, making generalization difficult. By the 2010s use of the term had declined, though many of its emphases persisted in progressive evangelical and missional church networks.
