Input–output model

In economics, an input–output model is a quantitative economic model that represents the interdependencies between different sectors of a national economy or different regional economies. The model was developed by Wassily Leontief, who won the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for it in 1973, building on earlier ideas including the Tableau économique of François Quesnay and Marx's reproduction schemes.

Structure

The economy is divided into sectors. An input–output table records, for each sector, how much output it sells to every other sector (as intermediate inputs) and to final demand (consumption, investment, government and exports), and how much it buys from each sector, plus value added such as wages and profits. Dividing intermediate purchases by a sector's total output gives technical coefficients, which are treated as fixed: producing one unit of a good requires fixed proportions of inputs.

The system can be written as x = Ax + y, where x is the vector of total outputs, A the matrix of technical coefficients, and y final demand. Solving gives x = (I − A)−1y; the matrix (I − A)−1 is the Leontief inverse, whose entries show the total (direct plus indirect) output of each sector required per unit of final demand for another sector's product.

Applications

Input–output analysis is widely used by statistical offices — including the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis and Eurostat — for national accounting, and for estimating the economy-wide effects of changes in demand: multiplier analysis, industrial and regional planning, and assessments of disruptions. Extensions incorporate environmental accounts, tracking energy use, emissions and resource flows per unit of output (environmentally extended input–output analysis), and multi-regional models linking trade between countries, such as the World Input–Output Database.

Limitations

The model's assumptions are restrictive: fixed input proportions with no substitution, constant returns to scale, no supply constraints, and fixed prices. Leontief himself regarded the framework as an empirical description rather than a complete theory. Nevertheless, its ability to trace indirect supply-chain effects keeps it in routine use in applied economics.
