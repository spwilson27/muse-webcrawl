Celsius

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A thermometer showing water freezing at 0 degrees and boiling at 100 degrees Celsius]
A thermometer showing water freezing at 0 degrees and boiling at 100 degrees Celsius

The Celsius scale (°C), formerly called centigrade, is a temperature scale used worldwide for most everyday and scientific purposes. In its modern form it is defined in terms of the kelvin: 0 °C equals 273.15 K, and temperature differences of 1 °C and 1 K are identical. Historically the scale was fixed by the freezing (0°) and boiling (100°) points of water at standard atmospheric pressure.

History

The Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius proposed in 1742 a scale with 100 degrees between water's freezing and boiling points — but inverted from today's, with 0° at boiling and 100° at freezing. After his death the scale was reversed, by Carl Linnaeus among others. 'Centigrade' was renamed 'Celsius' by the 9th General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1948 to honour him and avoid confusion with the centesimal angular degree.

Use

Celsius is the everyday scale in nearly all countries except the United States (and a few others), which use Fahrenheit; the two scales meet at −40°. SI-derived, it anchors weather reporting, cooking, medicine and most technical work outside physics, where kelvin dominates absolute measurement.
