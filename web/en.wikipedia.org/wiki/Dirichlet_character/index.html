Dirichlet character

Dirichlet character
FieldAnalytic number theory

In number theory, a Dirichlet character is a certain type of arithmetic function: a completely multiplicative periodic function on the integers that is zero on numbers not coprime to its modulus. Dirichlet characters were introduced by Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet in 1837 in his proof that there are infinitely many primes in any arithmetic progression whose first term and common difference are coprime.

Definition

A Dirichlet character modulo a positive integer N is a function χ from the integers to the complex numbers such that χ is periodic with period N, completely multiplicative (χ(mn) = χ(m)χ(n) for all m, n), and χ(n) = 0 whenever gcd(n, N) > 1. On residues coprime to N, χ restricts to a group homomorphism from the multiplicative group (ℤ/Nℤ)× to the nonzero complex numbers, and its values are roots of unity.

Principal and primitive characters

The principal character modulo N takes the value 1 on all integers coprime to N and 0 otherwise. A character is primitive if it is not induced from a character of smaller modulus; the smallest such modulus is its conductor. Characters also divide into even and odd according to the value χ(−1) = ±1, and into real (quadratic) and complex characters.

Applications

Dirichlet characters are the essential tool for studying primes in arithmetic progressions via Dirichlet L-functions, and they appear throughout analytic number theory, including in the proof of the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions and in character-sum estimates such as the Pólya–Vinogradov inequality.
