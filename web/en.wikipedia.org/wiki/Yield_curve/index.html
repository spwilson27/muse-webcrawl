Yield curve

In finance, the yield curve is a graph which depicts how the yields on debt instruments—such as bonds—vary as a function of their years remaining to maturity. Typically, the graph's horizontal axis (x-axis) shows the time to maturity (term), and the vertical axis (y-axis) shows the yield to maturity of the instruments, usually government securities such as U.S. Treasury bills, notes, and bonds. The shape of the curve is closely watched by economists and market participants as a signal about expectations for interest rates, inflation, and economic growth.

Shapes of the curve

Normal yield curve

A normal (or steep) yield curve slopes upward: longer-maturity bonds have higher yields than shorter-maturity ones. Investors normally demand extra compensation— a term premium—for locking up money for longer, and for bearing greater inflation and interest-rate risk.

Flat yield curve

A flat curve shows little difference between short- and long-term yields. It often appears during transitions between normal and inverted configurations, for example when a central bank raises short-term rates toward long-term rates.

Inverted yield curve

An inverted yield curve slopes downward: short-term yields exceed long-term yields. Inversions of the U.S. Treasury curve—especially the 10-year/2-year and 10-year/3-month spreads—have preceded every modern U.S. recession, typically by 6 to 24 months, and are explained by expectations that the central bank will cut rates in response to weakening growth (the expectations hypothesis), possibly combined with strong demand for long-term bonds.

Humped yield curve

A humped (bell-shaped) curve has intermediate maturities yielding more than both short and long maturities; it is comparatively rare.

Theories of the term structure

Three classic theories explain the curve's shape. The pure expectations hypothesis holds that long-term rates are averages of expected future short-term rates. The liquidity preference theory adds a positive term premium for longer maturities. The market segmentation theory treats different maturities as separate markets driven by the distinct supply and demand of investors such as banks (short end) and pension funds (long end). Modern practice combines these ideas in the concept of a term premium added to expected rates.

Uses

The yield curve is used to price bonds and derivatives, to infer market expectations of future policy rates, as a benchmark "risk-free" curve for valuing other securities, and as an economic indicator: the New York Fed publishes a recession-probability model based on the 10-year/3-month spread. Corporate and municipal curves are usually plotted as spreads over the government curve.
