USB-C

USB-C, or USB Type-C, is a 24-pin reversible connector (not a protocol) that supersedes previous USB connectors (not protocols) such as USB-A and USB-B as it supports the newer USB4 protocol (not connector). It also supersedes Mini and Micro USB as well as, sometimes, other kinds of cables such as Thunderbolt and the charging port of older phones and laptops. It is used on almost all modern Android phones, Windows computers, Chromebooks, newer Macs, newer iPads, newer Nintendo consoles and newer PC peripherals.

Type: Digital audio/video/data/power connector
Production history: Designed August 2014
Supersedes: USB Type-A, Type-B, Mini, Micro
Pins: 24

Design

The connector measures 8.4 mm by 2.6 mm and is rotationally symmetrical: it can be inserted either way up. Its 24 pins carry two SuperSpeed differential pairs (used for high-speed data, with lane swapping for reversibility), USB 2.0 lines, configuration channel (CC) pins used to detect orientation and negotiate roles, sideband use (SBU) pins, and power (VBUS) and ground. A USB-C cable connecting two devices identifies itself through the CC pins, which also advertise available power.

Capabilities

USB-C is a connector, not a data standard: a USB-C port may carry USB 2.0, USB 3.x, USB4, or Thunderbolt data. Alternate Modes allow the connector to carry non-USB signals, most notably DisplayPort video, and legacy HDMI. Under USB Power Delivery (USB PD), USB-C supports charging at up to 100 W (20 V, 5 A), extended to 240 W (48 V, 5 A) with the USB PD 3.1 Extended Power Range specification and suitably rated cables.

Adoption and regulation

First appearing on devices such as the 2015 MacBook and Nokia N1, USB-C spread to become the near-universal connector for phones, tablets, laptops and accessories. The European Union's amended Radio Equipment Directive requires most mobile phones, tablets and cameras sold in the EU to use USB-C charging from late 2024, with laptops following in 2026.
