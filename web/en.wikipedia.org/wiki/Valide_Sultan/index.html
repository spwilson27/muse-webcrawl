Valide Sultan (Ottoman Turkish for "mother of the sultan") was the title held by the mother of a reigning Ottoman sultan. The Valide Sultan was the most powerful woman in the empire, ranking above the sultan's wives and daughters; she headed the imperial harem, managed an immense household and fortune, acted as patron of major charitable and architectural works, and — particularly during the period historians call the Sultanate of Women — could wield decisive political influence, even serving effectively as regent for under-age sultans.

Role and power

On her son's accession, the Valide Sultan was escorted in a grand procession (the valide alayı) from the Old Palace to Topkapı Palace, where she occupied the largest apartments in the harem. She received the largest stipend of any member of the dynasty and controlled rich estates and tax farms whose revenues funded mosques, schools, hospitals, soup kitchens (imarets) and fountains built in her name in Istanbul and the holy cities. She corresponded with foreign rulers — Venetian doges and French kings among them — and mediated succession disputes within the dynasty.

Notable holders

Prominent Valide Sultans include Nurbanu Sultan, wife of Selim II and mother of Murad III; Safiye Sultan; and the rivals Kösem Sultan and Turhan Sultan, whose struggle ended with Kösem's murder in 1651 and Turhan's regency for Mehmed IV, during which she completed the Yeni Mosque in Istanbul and appointed the Köprülü grand viziers. Earlier, Hürrem Sultan — though never Valide — established the power of imperial women at court that the office institutionalised.
