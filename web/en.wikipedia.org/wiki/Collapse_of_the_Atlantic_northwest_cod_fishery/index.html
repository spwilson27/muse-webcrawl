Collapse of the Atlantic northwest cod fishery

The collapse of the Atlantic northwest cod fishery in the early 1990s was one of the most dramatic fishery collapses in history. After centuries of abundant harvests — by the 1960s, northern cod catches off Newfoundland peaked at some 800,000 tonnes a year — the stock crashed, and on 2 July 1992 the Canadian government declared a moratorium on the northern cod fishery, ending almost 500 years of cod fishing in Newfoundland and Labrador. About 40,000 people lost their livelihoods in what remains the largest industrial closure in Canadian history.

Causes

The collapse is attributed to severe overfishing, driven by post-war factory freezer trawlers (foreign fleets before Canada declared a 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zone in 1977, and domestic offshore fleets thereafter), optimistic stock assessments and quotas set above scientific advice, destructive bottom trawling, and possibly unfavourable cold-water environmental conditions in the late 1980s. Inshore fishers' warnings that fish were becoming smaller and scarcer were largely discounted.

Consequences and aftermath

The moratorium devastated outport communities in Newfoundland and Labrador, prompting outmigration and a federal adjustment programme (TAGS). The ecosystem shifted, with crab and shrimp becoming the new economic mainstays. Cod recovered only very slowly; a small stewardship fishery reopened, and in 2024 Canada lifted the moratorium to a modest commercial quota, though the stock remained far below historical levels.
