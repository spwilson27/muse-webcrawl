Sound bite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sound bite (or soundbite) is a short clip of speech or music extracted from a longer piece of audio, often used to promote or exemplify the full piece. In journalism and politics, the term refers to a brief, quotable excerpt of a speech or interview selected for broadcast because it captures the speaker's message in a memorable and concise form.

History

The sound bite emerged with broadcast news. As television news formats tightened, the average length of a candidate's uninterrupted speech on U.S. network news shrank dramatically — studies found it fell from about 43 seconds in 1968 to roughly 9 seconds by 1988. This compression was widely criticised for favouring slogans over argument, while politicians and their advisers adapted by crafting speeches around deliberately quotable lines.

Criticism and analysis

Critics argue that sound-bite politics rewards image and catchphrases over policy depth, and that decontextualised clips can distort a speaker's meaning. Supporters respond that a well-crafted sound bite is simply effective rhetoric — a memorable summary of a larger argument, comparable to a headline or a slogan. In the era of social media, the phenomenon has extended to short video clips engineered for sharing.

In music and other media

In music, a sound bite may be a distinctive hook or sample used in promotion. The term is also used more loosely for any short, pithy statement designed to be quoted by the media.
