Historic counties of England

The historic counties of England are areas used for geographic and cultural reference. There are thirty-nine of them, established principally in the Middle Ages, and they formed the basis of local administration until the local government reforms of the 19th and 20th centuries.

History

Many counties originated as Anglo-Saxon shires — such as Wessex's Hampshire, Wiltshire and Somerset — while others, like Lancashire and Cumberland, crystallized later; Durham was governed as a county palatine by its prince-bishops. By the 12th century the pattern of counties was essentially fixed, and counties became the framework for parliamentary representation, the lieutenancy, the assizes, and later the postal system.

Changes

The Local Government Act 1888 created administrative counties that diverged from the historic pattern, and the reforms of 1965 and 1974 (under the Local Government Act 1972) created new administrative and ceremonial counties — merging some historic counties (e.g. into Avon, Cleveland and Humberside, later abolished) and establishing metropolitan counties such as Greater Manchester and the West Midlands.

Cultural survival

Despite administrative changes, the historic counties persist in cultural identity, sport (county cricket clubs), place-name signs erected by campaign groups, and bodies such as the Association of British Counties, which advocates their use as a standard geographic reference frame.
