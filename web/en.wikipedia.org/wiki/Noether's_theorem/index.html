Noether's theorem (also called Noether's first theorem) states that every differentiable symmetry of the action of a physical system with conservative forces has a corresponding conservation law. The theorem was proven by mathematician Emmy Noether in 1915 and published in 1918. The action of a physical system is the integral over time of a Lagrangian function, from which the system's behavior can be determined by the principle of least action.

This theorem only applies to continuous and differentiable symmetries of the action, and expresses the principle of least action. It has become a fundamental tool of modern theoretical physics and the calculus of variations.

Basic idea

The basic idea behind the theorem is that if the action is invariant under a continuous transformation, then there is a quantity that is conserved. The most important examples are:

  • Invariance under time translation implies conservation of energy.
  • Invariance under spatial translation implies conservation of linear momentum.
  • Invariance under rotation implies conservation of angular momentum.
  • Invariance under gauge transformations implies conservation of electric charge.

History

Noether was invited to the University of Göttingen in 1915 by David Hilbert and Felix Klein, who wanted her expertise in invariant theory to help them understand general relativity. She proved the theorem in 1915; it was published in 1918 in the paper "Invariante Variationsprobleme" ("Invariant Variation Problems"). The result resolved an apparent paradox in general relativity concerning the conservation of energy, and went on to underpin much of modern physics, including quantum field theory and the Standard Model.

Applications

Noether's theorem is used in theoretical physics and the calculus of variations. It reveals the fundamental connection between symmetry and conservation laws, guides the construction of physical theories, and is central to the formulation of gauge theories. A generalization, Noether's second theorem, applies to infinite-dimensional (gauge) symmetries and yields identities rather than straightforward conservation laws.
