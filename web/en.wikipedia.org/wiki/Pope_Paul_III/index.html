Pope Paul III (Latin: Paulus III; Italian: Paolo III; 29 February 1468 – 10 November 1549), born Alessandro Farnese, was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 13 October 1534 until his death on 10 November 1549. He came to the papal throne in an era following the Sack of Rome (1527) and as the Protestant Reformation spread across Europe. His pontificate opened the Catholic Reformation: he approved the Society of Jesus (Jesuits), established the Roman Inquisition, and convoked the Council of Trent in 1545.

Early career

Alessandro Farnese was born at Canino in Lazio into an aristocratic family with deep ties to the Church. Educated by the humanist Pomponio Leto and at the court of Lorenzo de' Medici in Florence, he was raised to the cardinalate in 1493 by Pope Alexander VI — a promotion popularly attributed to the influence of his sister Giulia, the pope's mistress, earning him the unflattering nickname "the petticoat cardinal." In his early life he lived openly with a mistress and fathered four children, including Pier Luigi Farnese, whom he later made Duke of Parma. Farnese served under several popes, became bishop of Parma and later of Ostia, and rose to be dean of the College of Cardinals before his election in 1534.

Reform and the Counter-Reformation

Paul recognised that the survival of the old Church required internal reform. In 1536 he appointed a commission of cardinals whose report, the Consilium de Emendanda Ecclesia (1537), candidly catalogued abuses in the curia. He created cardinals of outstanding reformers such as Gasparo Contarini, Reginald Pole, and Gian Pietro Carafa (the future Paul IV). In 1540, by the bull Regimini militantis Ecclesiae, he formally approved the Society of Jesus founded by Ignatius of Loyola, which became the spearhead of Catholic renewal and missions. In 1542, with the bull Licet ab initio, he established the Supreme Sacred Congregation of the Roman and Universal Inquisition to combat heresy in Italy.

Council of Trent

After years of failed attempts frustrated by war between Emperor Charles V and Francis I of France, Paul finally convoked a general council, which opened at Trent on 13 December 1545. The Council of Trent would define Catholic doctrine against Protestant teaching — including the decree on justification (1547) — and set in motion reforms of episcopal residence, seminaries, and clerical discipline. Paul suspended the council in 1547 amid fears of plague and disputes with the emperor, transferring it briefly to Bologna; it resumed under his successors and closed in 1563.

Politics and family

Paul maintained a careful neutrality between Charles V and Francis I, meeting both at the Congress of Nice in 1538. His nepotism was conspicuous: he made two teenage grandsons cardinals and in 1545 carved the duchies of Parma and Piacenza out of papal territory for his son Pier Luigi, whose assassination in 1547 and the ensuing struggle over Parma darkened the pope's last years. His patronage of the arts was equally notable — he appointed Michelangelo to complete St Peter's Basilica and to paint The Last Judgment in the Sistine Chapel (unveiled 1541), and commissioned the famous portrait by Titian. In 1543 he also received and approved Nicolaus Copernicus's heliocentric work De revolutionibus, which was dedicated to him. Paul III died in Rome on 10 November 1549, aged 81.
