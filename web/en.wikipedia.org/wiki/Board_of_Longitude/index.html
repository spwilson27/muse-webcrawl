Overview

The Board of Longitude was a body established by the British Parliament in 1714 to administer a scheme of prizes — up to £20,000, a fortune — for a practicable and useful method of determining longitude at sea, a problem made urgent by disasters such as the Scilly naval disaster of 1707, when four warships and some 1,400–2,000 sailors were lost.

The prize and Harrison

The Board — Commissioners of Longitude including the Astronomer Royal — judged competing methods: lunar distances (supported by Maskelyne's Nautical Almanac) and timekeepers. John Harrison, a Yorkshire carpenter-clockmaker, spent decades building sea clocks H1–H4; his H4 performed superbly on trials to Jamaica (1761–62) and Barbados (1764), yet the Board, influenced by Maskelyne, demanded more and paid only in part until Parliament intervened in 1773 after George III's interest. The Board disbursed over £100,000 in total before its abolition in 1828. Dava Sobel's Longitude (1995) made the saga famous again.
