The Catholic Church in Vietnam is part of the worldwide Catholic Church in communion with the Pope. With roughly seven million adherents — about seven percent of the population — Vietnam has one of the largest Catholic communities in Southeast Asia.

History

Catholicism was introduced by Portuguese, Spanish and French missionaries in the 16th and 17th centuries. The Jesuit Alexandre de Rhodes developed chữ quốc ngữ, the Latin-based Vietnamese alphabet, and published a catechism and dictionary in 1651. The church grew despite periodic persecution under the Nguyễn dynasty, notably under emperors Minh Mạng and Tự Đức; tens of thousands of Vietnamese Catholics were martyred, of whom 117 were canonised by Pope John Paul II in 1988 as the Vietnamese Martyrs.

20th century

After the 1954 partition, some 600,000–800,000 northern Catholics fled south during Operation Passage to Freedom. In the Republic of Vietnam, President Ngô Đình Diệm, a Catholic, favoured the church, contributing to tensions that culminated in the Buddhist crisis of 1963. After reunification in 1975 the church faced restrictions under the communist government: seminaries were limited, church property seized, and bishops required state approval.

Present day

Relations between Vietnam and the Holy See have gradually improved: regular high-level meetings began in the 1990s, a non-resident papal representative was appointed in 2011, and in 2023 the two sides agreed on a resident papal representative in Hanoi. The church today runs 27 dioceses, reopened seminaries and extensive charitable work, though religious activity remains supervised under Vietnam's law on belief and religion.
