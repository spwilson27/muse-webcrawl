Crypto-Judaism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Placeholder image

Crypto-Judaism is the secret adherence to Judaism while publicly professing to be of another faith. Practitioners are known as "crypto-Jews" (also conversos, marranos, Anusim, or Xuetes depending on region and period).

History

Crypto-Judaism became widespread in Spain and Portugal following the mass forced conversions of Jews in the late Middle Ages — the pogroms and conversions of 1391 in Spain, the forced conversion of 1497 in Portugal, and the expulsion of Jews from Spain under the Alhambra Decree of 1492. Many converts outwardly practised Catholicism while maintaining Jewish customs in private: lighting candles on Friday night, observing dietary rules, celebrating Passover in secret, and fasting on Yom Kippur.

The Spanish and Portuguese Inquisitions prosecuted suspected Judaizers for centuries, and crypto-Jewish communities spread through the Iberian colonial world — to Mexico, Peru, Brazil, and later the American Southwest. In Belmonte, Portugal, a community survived in secret for some 500 years before openly returning to Judaism in the 20th century.

Other contexts

The term is also applied to the Dönmeh of the Ottoman Empire (followers of Sabbatai Zevi who outwardly converted to Islam), to Jewish communities that hid their identity under persecution in other regions, and in modern usage to descendants of conversos who rediscover and reclaim their heritage.
