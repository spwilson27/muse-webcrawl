Nguni people

The Nguni peoples are a group of closely related Bantu ethnic groups living in southern Africa who speak Nguni languages, a subgroup of the Southern Bantu languages. They include the Zulu, Xhosa, Swazi (siSwati-speaking), Ndebele and Ngoni peoples, among others, and together form a large share of the population of South Africa, Eswatini, Zimbabwe and parts of Malawi, Zambia, Mozambique and Tanzania.

History

Nguni-speaking pastoralists migrated southward over many centuries as part of the Bantu expansion, settling the well-watered coastal regions of south-eastern Africa. Their societies were traditionally organised in chiefdoms centred on cattle herding. In the early 19th century the upheavals known as the Mfecane (or Difaqane), associated with the rise of Shaka and the Zulu kingdom, transformed the region: groups such as Zwangendaba's Ngoni migrated as far north as present-day Malawi, Zambia and Tanzania, while the Ndebele under Mzilikazi established a kingdom in what is now Zimbabwe.

Languages

The Nguni languages — Zulu, Xhosa, Swazi and the Ndebele languages — are notable for their use of click consonants, adopted through contact with Khoisan languages. They are largely mutually intelligible to varying degrees and are written in the Latin alphabet.

Culture

  • Cattle are central to traditional Nguni life, serving as wealth, bridewealth (lobola) and a link to the ancestors.
  • Homesteads (umuzi/umuzi wabantu) traditionally arrange houses around a central cattle byre.
  • Rites such as the Swazi Incwala and the Zulu Umkhosi womHlanga (Reed Dance) remain prominent.
