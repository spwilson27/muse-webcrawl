Prestressed concrete

Prestressed concrete is a form of concrete used in construction. It is substantially "prestressed" (compressed) during production, in a manner that strengthens it against tensile forces which will exist when in service. This compression is produced by the tensioning of high-strength "tendons" located within or adjacent to the concrete, and is done to improve the performance of the concrete in service. Tendons may consist of single wires, multi-wire strands or threaded bars, and are most commonly made from high-tensile steels, carbon fiber or aramid fiber.

Principle

Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension. In ordinary reinforced concrete, tensile cracking under service loads is accepted and steel reinforcement carries the tension after cracking. In prestressed concrete, the concrete is pre-compressed so that the tensile stresses produced by service loads are counteracted; the entire cross-section remains effective, deflections and cracking are reduced, and longer spans can be achieved with shallower members.

Pre-tensioning

In pre-tensioning, the tendons are tensioned against external abutments before the concrete is cast. Once the concrete has gained sufficient strength, the tendons are released and the prestress is transferred to the concrete by bond. This method is typical of factory-produced precast elements such as bridge beams, railway sleepers and hollow-core slabs.

Post-tensioning

In post-tensioning, ducts or sheaths are cast into the concrete; after the concrete hardens, tendons are threaded through and jacked against the member itself, then anchored. In bonded post-tensioning the ducts are subsequently filled with grout. In unbonded post-tensioning, greased and plastic-sheathed strands are used and the tendons remain free to move relative to the concrete, anchored only at the ends; this system is widely used for flat slabs in buildings.

History

Early attempts at prestressing in the late 19th century failed because ordinary steel lost its tension through creep and shrinkage of the concrete. Eugène Freyssinet recognised in 1928 that only high-strength steel, tensioned to high strains, could retain useful prestress, and he patented the modern system, later developing conical anchorage wedges. After the Second World War, prestressed concrete expanded rapidly for rebuilding bridges, and engineers such as Gustave Magnel and Fritz Leonhardt advanced its design methods.

Applications

Prestressed concrete is used extensively in bridges (including segmental and cable-stayed construction), building frames and floor slabs, parking structures, water-retaining structures and tanks, silos, nuclear reactor pressure vessels, offshore structures, and ground anchors and rock bolts in geotechnical engineering.
