The Second Epistle to the Thessalonians, commonly referred to as Second Thessalonians (or 2 Thessalonians), is a book of the New Testament of the Christian Bible. It is framed as a letter from the Apostle Paul, together with Silvanus (Silas) and Timothy, to the church in Thessalonica in Macedonia. It is traditionally held to have been written shortly after the First Epistle to the Thessalonians, around AD 51–52, though many critical scholars question its authenticity.

Contents

The letter's dominant theme is eschatology. Chapter 1 gives thanks for the Thessalonians' faith under persecution and promises that God will repay their afflictors at the revelation of Jesus. Chapter 2 corrects a claim — apparently spread by a forged letter "as though from us" — that the day of the Lord had already come: before it, there must come a rebellion (apostasy) and the revelation of the "man of lawlessness", currently held back by a restrainer (the katechon). Chapter 3 warns against idleness within the community — "if anyone is not willing to work, let him not eat" — and closes with Paul's handwritten authentication: "I, Paul, write this greeting with my own hand. This is the mark in every letter of mine."

Authorship debate

Since the early 19th century, many scholars have argued that 2 Thessalonians is pseudepigraphal, written by a later follower of Paul: its vocabulary and style differ measurably from 1 Thessalonians, its eschatology (a timetable of events preceding the end) contrasts with the imminent expectation of the first letter, and its warning against forged letters could serve to authorise the letter itself. Other scholars defend authenticity, noting the external attestation is early (the letter was known to Irenaeus and included in Marcion's canon) and that the differences can be explained by the different questions addressed. Scholarly opinion remains divided, with critical scholarship leaning toward inauthenticity.
