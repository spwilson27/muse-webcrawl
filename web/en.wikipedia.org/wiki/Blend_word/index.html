In linguistics, a blend — also known as a portmanteau word — is a word formed by combining sounds and meanings from two or more other words, typically by joining the beginning of one word with the end of another. Familiar English examples include smog (smoke + fog), brunch (breakfast + lunch), motel (motor + hotel), and internet (international + network).

Origin of the term

The term "portmanteau" was applied to such words by Lewis Carroll in Through the Looking-Glass (1871), where Humpty Dumpty explains that words like slithy (lithe + slimy) and mimsy (flimsy + miserable) pack two meanings into one word "like a portmanteau" — the travelling bag that opens into two compartments. Carroll coined several blends, of which chortle (chuckle + snort) has entered standard English.

Formation

Blends usually combine a word's opening fragment with another's closing fragment, and their success depends on phonetic smoothness and transparency of meaning. Many blends begin as slang, trade names, or jokes and later become standard: Brexit, webinar, blog (from weblog), emoticon, staycation, and mocktail. Blending is one of the regular processes of English word formation, alongside compounding, derivation, clipping, and acronym formation.
