Northern England, often known simply as the North, is the northern part of England, considered as a single cultural and statistical region. It comprises the three statistical regions of the North East, the North West, and Yorkshire and the Humber, broadly corresponding to the area north of the River Trent and the Humber. It has a population of around 15 million and includes the great industrial cities of Manchester, Liverpool, Leeds, Sheffield, and Newcastle upon Tyne.

History

The region corresponds roughly to the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Northumbria and to the lands settled by Norse Vikings in the Danelaw. In the 19th century the North was the engine of the Industrial Revolution: cotton mills in Lancashire, woollen mills in Yorkshire, coal mining across the coalfields, shipbuilding on the Tyne, Wear, and Mersey, and steel in Sheffield. The decline of heavy industry in the late 20th century brought severe economic dislocation, and the "North–South divide" in wealth, health, and opportunity remains a major theme in British politics, periodically addressed by regeneration and "levelling up" and Northern Powerhouse initiatives.

Culture and identity

Northern England has a strong regional identity, expressed in distinctive dialects and accents (such as Scouse, Geordie, Mancunian, and Yorkshire), food traditions, music (from brass bands to the "Madchester" scene), and sport — rugby league is centred on the M62 corridor, and the region's football clubs include Manchester United, Liverpool, Manchester City, and Leeds United. Surveys consistently show a large share of northerners identify strongly with the North as a whole as well as with their county or city.

Geography

The Pennines run down the centre of the region, separating Lancashire from Yorkshire; the Lake District in Cumbria contains England's highest mountain, Scafell Pike, and its largest lakes. National parks in the region include the Lake District, the Yorkshire Dales, the North York Moors, Northumberland, and part of the Peak District.
