Flagellum

A flagellum (plural flagella) is a hairlike appendage that protrudes from certain plant and animal sperm cells, and from a wide range of microorganisms to provide motility. Many protists with flagella are termed as flagellates.

A microorganism may have from one to many flagella. A gram-negative bacterium Helicobacter pylori for example, uses its multiple flagella to propel itself through the mucus lining of the stomach to reach the stomach epithelium, where it may cause disease. In some bacteria the flagella can also serve as sensory organs.

Types

Three types of flagella have so far been distinguished: bacterial, archaeal, and eukaryotic. The main differences among these three types are summarized below:

  • Bacterial flagella are helical filaments, each with a rotary motor at its base which can turn clockwise or counterclockwise. They provide two of several kinds of bacterial motility. The filament is composed of the protein flagellin and rotates like a propeller.
  • Archaeal flagella (archaella) are superficially similar to bacterial flagella but are homologous to bacterial type IV pili, are assembled from the base, and are powered by ATP hydrolysis rather than a proton gradient.
  • Eukaryotic flagella are complex cellular projections that undulate; they are structurally the same as cilia, with a 9+2 arrangement of microtubules (the axoneme) driven by dynein motors, and are enclosed by the cell membrane.

Arrangements

Bacteria are described by the arrangement of their flagella: monotrichous (a single flagellum), amphitrichous (one at each end), lophotrichous (a tuft at one or both ends), and peritrichous (flagella all around the cell, as in Escherichia coli). Coordinated switching between counterclockwise "runs" and clockwise "tumbles" lets bacteria perform chemotaxis toward nutrients and away from repellents.
