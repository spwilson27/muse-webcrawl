Battle of Maldon

The Battle of Maldon took place on 10 or 11 August 991 AD near Maldon in Essex, England, during the reign of Æthelred the Unready. An English force under Ealdorman Byrhtnoth was defeated by a Viking raiding fleet. The battle is famous chiefly through the Old English poem The Battle of Maldon, which celebrates Byrhtnoth's heroic stand.

The battle

The Vikings — the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle gives their fleet as 93 ships — encamped on Northey Island in the Blackwater estuary, separated from the mainland by a tidal causeway. According to the poem, the Vikings demanded tribute (Danegeld); Byrhtnoth refused, declaring they would receive spears and swords instead. At low tide the Vikings attempted the causeway, held by three English warriors, and Byrhtnoth — out of ofermod (overmastering pride, or heroic excess) — allowed them to cross unmolested so the battle could be joined in earnest.

In the ensuing fight Byrhtnoth was killed. Some of his retainers fled, but his household warriors famously fought on around his body and died with him, vowing that courage must grow as strength fails — the poem's most quoted lines.

Aftermath and the poem

Following Maldon, Archbishop Sigeric advised paying the Vikings 10,000 pounds of silver — the first recorded payment of Danegeld, beginning a policy that continued for decades. The poem The Battle of Maldon, of which 325 lines survive (the manuscript was damaged in the Cotton library fire of 1731), is one of the finest monuments of Old English heroic verse and the principal source for the battle's details.
