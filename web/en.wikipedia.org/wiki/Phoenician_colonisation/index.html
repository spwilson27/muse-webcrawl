Phoenician colonisation

Phoenician colonisation refers to the expansion of Phoenician city-states, principally Tyre, Sidon and Byblos on the Levantine coast, through trading posts and colonies across the Mediterranean Sea between roughly the 10th and 6th centuries BC. Unlike the largely agricultural colonies of the Greeks, Phoenician settlements were primarily commercial entrepôts serving the great maritime trade routes.

Motives and organisation

The Phoenician cities were small, crowded and short of land, but superbly placed for trade, with cedar timber, purple dye (murex), glass and metalwork to export. Colonies secured sources of raw materials — silver and tin from Iberia, grain and slaves from North Africa and Sardinia — and safe anchorages at intervals of about a day's sailing along the coasts. Colonies typically began as seasonal trading stations, some growing into permanent cities, often on offshore islands or defensible headlands with good harbours.

Principal colonies

The greatest Phoenician colony was Carthage in modern Tunisia, traditionally founded from Tyre in 814 BC under Queen Elissa (Dido). Carthage eventually became an independent power and inherited the western Phoenician network. Other major foundations include Gadir (Cádiz) in Spain — claimed to be among the oldest cities in western Europe — Utica, Motya in Sicily, Sulci and Nora in Sardinia, Ibiza in the Balearics, and colonies in Malta and Cyprus such as Kition. In the far west, Phoenician traders reached the Strait of Gibraltar and ventured into the Atlantic along the Iberian and Moroccan coasts.

Cultural impact

The Phoenicians spread their alphabet, the ancestor of the Greek and ultimately Latin scripts, throughout the Mediterranean, along with techniques of navigation, purple-dye manufacture and metalworking. Their colonies formed the commercial skeleton upon which Carthaginian power in the western Mediterranean was later built, bringing them into conflict first with the Greeks over Sicily and later with Rome in the Punic Wars.
