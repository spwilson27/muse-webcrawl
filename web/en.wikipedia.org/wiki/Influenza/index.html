Influenza, commonly called the flu, is an infectious disease caused by influenza viruses (types A, B, C and D; A and B cause seasonal epidemics in humans). It spreads through respiratory droplets and aerosols, typically causing sudden fever, cough, sore throat, muscle aches and exhaustion lasting about a week; complications such as pneumonia can be fatal, especially in the elderly and those with chronic conditions.

Influenza A also circulates in birds and pigs, and antigenic shift can create pandemic strains — 1918 (H1N1), 1957 (H2N2), 1968 (H3N2) and 2009 (H1N1 'swine flu'). Because of continual antigenic drift, seasonal flu vaccines are reformulated annually; global surveillance through the WHO's influenza network selects the strains. Annual epidemics are associated with hundreds of thousands of respiratory deaths worldwide (commonly estimated at 290,000–650,000).
