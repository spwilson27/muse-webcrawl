Joint venture

Type: Business arrangement
Key feature: Two or more parties pool resources for a specific undertaking while remaining separate entities
Common forms: Equity joint venture; contractual joint venture

A joint venture (JV) is a business entity created by two or more parties, generally characterised by shared ownership, shared returns and risks, and shared governance. The parties form a joint venture to accomplish a specific task, such as a particular project or business activity, while each party continues to exist as a separate organisation.

Characteristics

In an equity joint venture, the parties contribute capital to a newly formed company in agreed proportions, sharing profits, losses, and control. In a contractual joint venture, the parties cooperate under a contract without creating a separate legal entity. Joint venture agreements typically specify the venture's purpose, the contributions of each party, management structure, duration, and exit arrangements such as buy-out options or dissolution.

Uses

Joint ventures are used to enter foreign markets (where local partnership may be legally required or commercially advantageous), to combine complementary technologies or distribution networks, to share the cost and risk of large projects in industries such as oil and gas, construction, pharmaceuticals, and aerospace, and to bid for contracts that neither party could undertake alone. Well-known examples include Sony Ericsson, the former mobile phone venture between Sony and Ericsson, and MillerCoors in the United States.

Risks

Joint ventures frequently fail or are wound up early because of cultural clashes between partners, unclear objectives, unequal contributions, or changes in strategy. Studies commonly estimate that a substantial proportion of joint ventures do not survive beyond a few years, though many are designed from the outset to be temporary.
