Thermonuclear weapon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Thermonuclear weapon
Also called: Hydrogen bomb, H-bomb
First full test: Ivy Mike, 1 November 1952 (USA)
Principle: Fission primary igniting a fusion secondary

A thermonuclear weapon, or hydrogen bomb, is a nuclear weapon that draws most of its energy from nuclear fusion. In the Teller–Ulam design used by all deployed thermonuclear weapons, a fission primary's radiation implodes a secondary stage containing fusion fuel (typically lithium deuteride), producing yields from hundreds of kilotons to many megatons.

Development

The United States tested the first full-scale thermonuclear device, Ivy Mike, at Enewetak Atoll on 1 November 1952 (yield about 10 megatons); it was a cryogenic experimental device rather than a deliverable weapon. The first deliverable-style dry-fuel design followed in the Castle Bravo test of 1954, whose 15-megaton yield far exceeded predictions and spread serious fallout. The Soviet Union tested its own true thermonuclear weapon in 1955, followed by the United Kingdom (1957), China (1967) and France (1968).

Significance

Because fusion fuel is comparatively cheap and the design scalable — the Tsar Bomba reached about 50 megatons — thermonuclear weapons multiplied the destructive power of arsenals during the Cold War and underpin the strategic deterrents of the nuclear-weapon states today.
