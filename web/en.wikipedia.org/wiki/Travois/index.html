Travois

A travois (French: travail, "work") is a frame structure used by Indigenous peoples of the Great Plains of North America to drag loads over land. It consists of two long poles lashed together in a V or A shape, with a platform or netting between them on which goods are placed. The narrow end of the frame rests on the shoulders of the draft animal — originally dogs, and later horses — while the butt ends of the poles drag along the ground.

History

Before the reintroduction of the horse to the Americas, Plains peoples used dogs to pull smaller travois. With the spread of horses in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, horse-drawn travois became much larger and could carry heavier loads, including the long poles of tipis, meat from hunts and household goods as camps moved. The trailing poles left distinctive parallel tracks across the prairie, some of which remain visible at historic sites.

Construction

The poles were typically lodgepole pine. Crosspieces or a woven rawhide platform formed the load bed, and rawhide straps attached the frame to the animal's harness or saddle. The design was light, could be assembled from the same poles used to raise a tipi, and coped well with rough, roadless terrain.
