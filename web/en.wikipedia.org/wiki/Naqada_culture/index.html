The Naqada culture is an archaeological culture of Chalcolithic Predynastic Egypt (c. 4000–3100 BC), named after the town of Naqada, Qena Governorate. It is the principal culture of the Predynastic period immediately preceding the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt and the beginning of the Pharaonic state. A 2013 study based on radiocarbon dates suggested the beginning of Naqada I around 3800–3700 BC.

Phases

The culture is conventionally divided into three phases, based largely on the sequence dating system devised by Flinders Petrie after his excavations at Naqada in 1894–95:

  • Naqada I (c. 4000–3500 BC), also called the Amratian — characterised by black-topped red pottery, early farming villages, and copper working.
  • Naqada II (c. 3500–3200 BC), also called the Gerzean — marked by decorated (D-ware) pottery with boat and figure scenes, wavy-handled jars, expanding trade, and the appearance of large proto-urban centres such as Hierakonpolis, Naqada, and Abydos.
  • Naqada III (c. 3200–3100 BC), also called the Semainean or Dynasty 0 — the final Predynastic stage, when regional rulers (including kings such as Scorpion and Narmer) moved toward political unification.

Material culture

Naqada pottery evolved from polished black-topped red ware to buff-coloured decorated ware painted with scenes of boats, animals, and human figures. Craft specialisation advanced markedly: finely flaked flint knives (some with carved ivory handles), slate cosmetic palettes, maceheads, copper tools, and luxury goods of gold, lapis lazuli, and ivory testify to long-distance trade with Nubia, the Levant, and Mesopotamia. Small figurines and the painted Tomb 100 at Hierakonpolis provide early evidence of imagery later central to Egyptian kingship.

Burials and society

Cemeteries at Naqada, Hierakonpolis, and Abydos show increasing social stratification: elite tombs grew larger and richer, some brick-lined and containing multiple chambers and prestige goods, while ordinary burials remained simple oval pits with a few vessels. Settlement evidence points to growing towns with craft quarters and, at Hierakonpolis, industrial-scale brewing and pottery production.

Expansion and unification

During Naqada II and III, Upper Egyptian culture spread northward, gradually supplanting the distinct Maadi culture of the Delta region. By the end of Naqada III, rulers buried at Abydos (Umm el-Qa'ab) controlled a unified valley, and the process culminated in the Early Dynastic Period and the First Dynasty, traditionally associated with Narmer and the foundation of Memphis.
