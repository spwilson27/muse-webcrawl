Charles Messier (26 June 1730 – 12 April 1817) was a French astronomer. He published an astronomical catalogue consisting of 110 nebulae and star clusters, which came to be known as the Messier objects. Messier's purpose for the catalogue was to help astronomical observers distinguish between permanent and transient visually diffuse objects in the sky — in particular, to help comet hunters like himself avoid mistaking nebulae for comets.

Life and career

Messier was born in Badonviller in Lorraine, the tenth of twelve children. His interest in astronomy was stimulated by the great six-tailed comet of 1744 and by an annular solar eclipse visible from his hometown in 1748. He became an assistant at the Marine Observatory in Paris under Joseph-Nicolas Delisle, and in 1758 observed the return of Halley's Comet — while searching for it he mistook the Crab Nebula for the comet, an experience that prompted him to begin his catalogue of fixed diffuse objects.

Comet hunting

Messier was the pre-eminent comet hunter of his age, discovering 13 comets and co-discovering several others; King Louis XV called him the "comet ferret". He observed from the Hôtel de Cluny in Paris with small refracting telescopes.

The Messier catalogue

The first version of the catalogue appeared in 1774 with 45 objects; the final version, published in 1781, listed 103, with later additions (M104–M110) made from Messier's and Pierre Méchain's notes. The objects include the Andromeda Galaxy (M31), the Orion Nebula (M42), the Pleiades (M45) and the Ring Nebula (M57). The catalogue remains in daily use by amateur astronomers, and "Messier marathons" attempt to observe all 110 objects in a single night.
