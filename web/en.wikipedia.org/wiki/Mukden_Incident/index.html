The Mukden Incident, also known as the Manchurian Incident, was a false flag event staged by Japanese military personnel as a pretext for the Japanese invasion of Manchuria in 1931. On 18 September 1931, Lieutenant Suemori Kawamoto of the Kwantung Army detonated a small quantity of dynamite close to a railway line owned by Japan's South Manchuria Railway near Mukden (now Shenyang). The explosion was so weak that it failed to destroy the track, and a train passed over it minutes later. Blaming Chinese dissidents for the act, the Imperial Japanese Army responded with a full invasion, occupying all of Manchuria within months.

Japanese troops entering Mukden (Shenyang) after the incident.

Background

Japan had held extensive economic and territorial interests in Manchuria since the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–05, including the lease of the Liaodong Peninsula and control of the South Manchuria Railway. Friction with the Chinese warlord government of Zhang Xueliang and rising Japanese militarism in the wake of the Great Depression led officers of the Kwantung Army — notably Colonel Seishirō Itagaki and Lieutenant Colonel Kanji Ishiwara — to plot an incident that would justify seizing the region, acting largely without authorisation from Tokyo.

The incident and invasion

Following the explosion on the evening of 18 September 1931, Japanese forces attacked the Chinese garrison at Beidaying barracks and seized Mukden's arsenal and airfield by the next morning. Citing self-defence, the Kwantung Army rapidly occupied cities along the railway zone and then the whole of Manchuria. The Chinese government of Chiang Kai-shek, prioritising its struggle against the Communists, ordered non-resistance, and Zhang Xueliang withdrew his forces south of the Great Wall. By February 1932 the occupation was complete.

Consequences

In March 1932 Japan established the puppet state of Manchukuo under Puyi, the last Qing emperor. The League of Nations sent the Lytton Commission, whose 1932 report rejected Japan's claim of self-defence and refused to recognise Manchukuo. Japan responded by withdrawing from the League in 1933, deepening its international isolation. The incident is widely regarded as an opening act of the conflicts that led to the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–45) and the wider Second World War in Asia. In China, 18 September is commemorated as National Humiliation Day.
