Beating the bounds

Beating the bounds is an ancient custom, still observed in parts of England and Wales, in which members of a parish walk the boundary of their parish, striking boundary markers with branches and praying for protection and blessings on the crops. The ceremony, historically held during Rogationtide — the days before Ascension Day — is known also as the Rogation procession or "perambulation".

Origins and purpose

The custom descends from Roman boundary festivals such as the Terminalia and was absorbed into Christian practice as processions led by the parish priest with cross and banners. In an age before reliable maps, the annual walk fixed the parish boundary in communal memory. Children were traditionally taken along and, at each boundary stone or tree, bumped, whipped with willow wands, or otherwise made to remember the spot — in some places boys were ducked in boundary streams or hung upside down. Parish bounds mattered because they determined liability for poor relief, tithes, church maintenance and burial rights.

Practice

The procession paused at customary points — " Gospel oaks", crosses, bridges and notable stones — where psalms and prayers were read. The accompanying blows on the markers with green boughs gave the custom its name. Disputes between neighbouring parishes over boundaries could turn violent when two processions met or encroached.

Survival

Although the enclosure movement, printed maps and civil administration removed its practical necessity, the ceremony survives or has been revived in many places. A well-known example is the beating of the bounds of the Liberty of the Tower of London by the Yeoman Warders and local children every three years, marking the Tower's boundary stones. Many Oxford and Cambridge colleges and London parishes still hold Ascension Day perambulations. Similar boundary-walking customs, such as the German Grenzbegehung, exist elsewhere in Europe.
