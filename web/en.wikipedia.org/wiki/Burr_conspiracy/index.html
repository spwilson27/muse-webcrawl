Burr conspiracy

The Burr conspiracy was a plot alleged to have been led by Aaron Burr, former Vice President of the United States, in the years following the end of his term in 1805. Burr was accused of scheming to create an independent nation in the southwestern United States and parts of Spanish territory, possibly with himself as its ruler. In 1807 he was tried for treason in Richmond, Virginia, and acquitted, though the scandal destroyed his political career.

Background

Burr's political career had collapsed after he killed Alexander Hamilton in their famous duel in July 1804 while still vice president. Indicted for murder in New Jersey and New York, he finished his term under a cloud. In 1805 he travelled west, touring the Ohio and Mississippi valleys and meeting with, among others, General James Wilkinson, commander of the US Army and governor of the Louisiana Territory.

The alleged plot

What Burr intended remains disputed. He publicly promoted a settlement scheme on lands along the Washita River in present-day Louisiana and Arkansas, and raised men and supplies for an expedition down the Mississippi. Critics alleged far grander designs: detaching the western states from the Union, invading Spanish Mexico, or carving out an empire with Burr at its head. Burr reportedly sought British support, and the British minister in Washington conveyed his overtures to London, which showed little enthusiasm.

In late 1806 Wilkinson, fearing exposure or seeking favour, betrayed the scheme to President Thomas Jefferson, portraying Burr as the leader of a treasonable conspiracy. Jefferson issued a proclamation warning citizens against the expedition and ordered Burr's arrest.

Trial and aftermath

Burr was captured in the Mississippi Territory in February 1807 and taken to Richmond, where he was tried for treason before Chief Justice John Marshall, sitting on circuit. Marshall construed the Constitution's treason clause narrowly, requiring proof of an overt act of levying war witnessed by two witnesses. The prosecution could not meet that standard, and Burr was acquitted on 1 September 1807.

Acquitted but ruined, Burr spent several years in Europe attempting to revive schemes against Spanish America, then returned to New York in 1812 and practised law quietly until his death in 1836.
