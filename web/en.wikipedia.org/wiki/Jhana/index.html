Jhana

In the oldest texts of the Pali Canon, jhāna (Sanskrit: dhyāna) is the training of the mind, commonly translated as meditation, to withdraw the mind from automatic responses to sense impressions and attain a state of perfect equanimity and awareness. The jhānas are a series of progressively deeper states of absorption concentration (samādhi) cultivated in Buddhist meditation, and form the core of "right concentration" (sammā-samādhi), the eighth factor of the Noble Eightfold Path.

The four material jhānas

  1. First jhāna — born of seclusion from sensual pleasures and unwholesome states, accompanied by applied and sustained thought (vitakka-vicāra), rapture (pīti) and happiness (sukha).
  2. Second jhāna — inner tranquillity and unification of mind; discursive thought subsides, leaving rapture and happiness born of concentration.
  3. Third jhāna — rapture fades; the meditator dwells equanimous, mindful and clearly comprehending, experiencing happiness in the body.
  4. Fourth jhāna — purity of equanimity and mindfulness; neither pleasure nor pain.

The jhāna factors develop as the "five hindrances" — sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and remorse, and doubt — are suppressed.

The formless attainments

Beyond the four material jhānas, the texts describe four immaterial dimensions (arūpa-āyatana): the sphere of infinite space, of infinite consciousness, of nothingness, and of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. Together with the four jhānas these constitute the eight attainments; a ninth state, the cessation of perception and feeling, is mentioned in some texts.

Role in the path

In the Pali suttas the Buddha regularly describes progressing through the jhānas before directing the concentrated mind to insight and the destruction of the taints. Commentarial tradition, notably the Visuddhimagga, developed detailed techniques of access concentration based on meditation objects (kasiṇa, mindfulness of breathing). Modern teachers differ on how deep jhāna must be and whether it is indispensable to awakening — a debate sometimes called the "jhāna wars". The Sanskrit term dhyāna travelled with Buddhism to China as chán and to Japan as zen.
