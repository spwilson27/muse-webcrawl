Hangul (Korean: 한글) is the native alphabet of the Korean language, created in 1443 under King Sejong the Great of Joseon and promulgated in 1446 in the document Hunminjeongeum ("The Proper Sounds for the Instruction of the People"). It was designed so that ordinary people, excluded from literacy by the difficulty of Chinese characters (hanja), could learn to read and write easily; Sejong's preface declares that a wise man can master it in a morning and even a fool in ten days.

Design

Hangul is a featural alphabet: the shapes of the basic consonant letters depict the position of the speech organs (ㄱ shows the tongue root blocking the throat, ㄴ the tongue touching the gum ridge), and related sounds are formed by adding strokes. Vowel letters are built from three elements symbolising heaven (•), earth (ㅡ) and humanity (ㅣ). Letters are not written in a line but assembled into syllable blocks — for example 한 (han) combines ㅎ, ㅏ and ㄴ — fitting the square frame of a Chinese character.

History

The script met fierce opposition from Confucian officials such as Choe Man-ri, who saw it as a betrayal of classical learning, and it was banned by King Yeonsangun in 1504 after posters in Hangul criticised him. For centuries it remained the script of women, merchants and vernacular literature while officialdom used Classical Chinese. Hangul was revived and standardised in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, becoming the sole official script of North Korea and the predominant script of South Korea; the original Hunminjeongeum Haerye is a UNESCO Memory of the World treasure, and 9 October is Hangul Day in South Korea.
