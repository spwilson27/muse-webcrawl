Cereal

Wheat, rice and maize together account for most of the world's cereal production.

A cereal is a grass cultivated for its edible grain. Cereals are the world's most important food crops: the largest cereals are maize (corn), rice, wheat, barley, sorghum, millets, oats and rye. In 2022, about 3 billion tonnes of cereals were produced worldwide. Cereal grain is a staple food providing more food energy worldwide than any other type of crop, and it is also used for animal feed, brewing, and industrial products such as biofuels and starch.

Structure and composition

The cereal grain, botanically a caryopsis, consists of the bran (outer layers), the germ (embryo) and the endosperm, which stores starch and protein. Milling often removes the bran and germ, leaving refined endosperm flour; whole-grain products retain all three parts and are richer in fibre, B vitamins and minerals.

History

Cereals were among the first plants domesticated in the Neolithic agricultural revolutions: wheat and barley in the Fertile Crescent, rice in the Yangtze valley, maize in Mesoamerica, and sorghum and pearl millet in Africa. Their storability and yield underpinned the growth of settled farming societies and cities.

Production

Maize is the most produced cereal by tonnage, much of it used for livestock feed and ethanol; rice and wheat are the most important direct human foods. The Green Revolution of the mid-20th century greatly increased cereal yields through semi-dwarf varieties, fertiliser and irrigation.
