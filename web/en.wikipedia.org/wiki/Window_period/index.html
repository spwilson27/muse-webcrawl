Window period

In medicine, the window period for a test designed to detect a specific disease is the time between initial infection and the point at which the test can reliably detect that infection. During the window period a person can test negative even though they are actually infected and potentially infectious, because the markers the test looks for — antibodies, antigens or the pathogen's genetic material — have not yet reached detectable levels.

Significance

The window period is of central importance in screening donated blood and in diagnosing infections such as HIV. A blood donation made during the window period can pass antibody screening yet transmit the infection, which is why modern blood services combine donor questionnaires with sensitive nucleic-acid testing (NAT) that shortens the window substantially.

Examples

For HIV, the window period depends on the test type: an HIV RNA test can usually detect infection about 10 to 33 days after exposure; a laboratory antigen/antibody test about 18 to 45 days; and antibody-only tests may take 23 to 90 days to detect infection. Guidelines therefore recommend repeat testing at defined intervals after a possible exposure before infection can be conclusively excluded.

Similar considerations apply to hepatitis B and C, syphilis and other blood-borne infections, each with its own window period reflecting the biology of the immune response and the target of the assay.

Related concepts

The window period is related to, but distinct from, the incubation period — the time between infection and the onset of symptoms — and the latent period, after which an infected person becomes infectious. A person may be infectious while still in the testing window period, which is why post-exposure counselling emphasises precautions until repeat testing is complete.
