Federal Rules of Evidence

The Federal Rules of Evidence are a set of rules governing the introduction of evidence at trials in United States federal trial courts. Enacted by Congress in 1975, they apply in federal civil and criminal proceedings, bankruptcy cases and magistrate proceedings.

Structure

The Rules are organised in eleven articles: general provisions, judicial notice, presumptions, relevance (Rules 401–403), privileges (501), witnesses (601–615), opinions and expert testimony (701–706), hearsay (801–807), authentication (901–903), contents of writings (1001–1008), and miscellaneous rules.

Key principles

Relevant evidence is generally admissible, but may be excluded under Rule 403 if its probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice or confusion. Hearsay is generally inadmissible subject to numerous exceptions (present sense impression, excited utterance, business records, and others), and expert testimony is governed by Rule 702 and the Daubert standard.

History and influence

Drafted by an advisory committee under the Judicial Conference and modified by Congress — notably on privilege — the Rules have been amended many times, restyled in 2011, and adopted in substance by most U.S. states.
