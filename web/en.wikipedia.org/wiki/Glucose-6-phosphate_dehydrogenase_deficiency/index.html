Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency


Red blood cells in G6PD deficiency are vulnerable to oxidative damage.

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency (G6PDD), also known as favism in its most dramatic presentation, is an X-linked genetic condition that predisposes affected individuals to haemolytic anaemia. It is the most common human enzyme deficiency, affecting roughly 400–500 million people worldwide, and arises from mutations in the G6PD gene that reduce the activity of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway.

Signs and symptoms

Most people with the deficiency are asymptomatic for most of their lives. Haemolysis is triggered by oxidative stressors: certain medications (notably the antimalarials primaquine and tafenoquine, rasburicase, and some antibiotics), infections, diabetic ketoacidosis, naphthalene (mothballs), and — classically — eating fava beans (broad beans), whose vicine and convicine metabolites generate reactive oxygen species. Episodes cause fatigue, pallor, jaundice, dark urine, back or abdominal pain and, in severe cases, acute kidney injury. In newborns, the deficiency is an important cause of severe neonatal jaundice and kernicterus if untreated. Chronic non-spherocytic haemolytic anaemia occurs in the most severe variant class.

Pathophysiology

Without adequate G6PD activity, red blood cells cannot regenerate enough reduced glutathione to neutralise oxidants. Oxidised haemoglobin denatures and precipitates as Heinz bodies attached to the cell membrane; splenic macrophages remove these inclusions (producing "bite cells") or the cells rupture intravascularly. Because older red cells have the least residual enzyme activity, haemolysis in the common variants is self-limiting as younger cells with more enzyme survive.

Distribution and diagnosis

The condition is most prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa, the Mediterranean basin, the Middle East and South and Southeast Asia — regions where malaria was historically endemic, since the trait confers some protection against severe Plasmodium falciparum infection. Diagnosis is usually by enzyme activity assay or molecular testing, though assays can be falsely normal during an acute episode when older deficient cells have already been destroyed. Management consists mainly of avoiding known triggers; the World Health Organization advises testing before primaquine therapy.
