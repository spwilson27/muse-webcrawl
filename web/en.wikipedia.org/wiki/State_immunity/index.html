State immunity

State immunity, or sovereign immunity, is a principle of customary international law by which one sovereign state cannot exercise jurisdiction over another sovereign state. It derives from the maxim par in parem non habet imperium — "equals have no power over equals" — reflecting the sovereign equality of states.

Absolute and restrictive immunity

Historically, states enjoyed absolute immunity from suit in foreign courts. Over the twentieth century, most states adopted the restrictive theory of immunity, under which a state remains immune for its sovereign or public acts (acta jure imperii) but may be sued in respect of commercial acts (acta jure gestionis). The restrictive approach is reflected in national legislation such as the United States Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act of 1976 and the United Kingdom State Immunity Act 1978.

Immunity from execution

Immunity from execution — the seizure of state property to satisfy a judgment — is generally broader than immunity from jurisdiction, so that even where a state can be sued, its diplomatic premises, central bank assets, and military property usually remain protected.

Codification

The United Nations Convention on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and Their Property, adopted in 2004, seeks to codify the customary law of state immunity, though it has not yet entered into force.
