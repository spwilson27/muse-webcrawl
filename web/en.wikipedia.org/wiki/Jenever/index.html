Jenever (Dutch: [jəˈneːvər]), also known as genièvre, genever, or peket, is the juniper-flavored traditional liquor of the Netherlands, Belgium, and adjoining areas of France and Germany, from which English gin evolved. It is made by blending a malt wine — a whiskey-like distillate of fermented grains — with a neutral spirit redistilled with juniper berries and other botanicals. Since 2008 jenever has held protected geographical status in the European Union.

History

Distillation of juniper-flavored spirits in the Low Countries is documented from the late Middle Ages, initially for medicinal purposes; by the 16th and 17th centuries jenever was a popular everyday drink produced in hundreds of distilleries in cities such as Schiedam, whose "black" jenever towns exported stoneware bottles worldwide. English soldiers who encountered it during the Dutch wars carried the taste home — the phrase "Dutch courage" is traditionally linked to jenever — and its cheaper, re-engineered descendant became London gin. In the 19th century, changes in taxation and the introduction of column-still neutral spirit created the lighter "jonge" style alongside the maltier "oude."

Styles

Traditional jenever is classified by the proportion of malt wine in the blend. Oude jenever ("old," referring to the old recipe rather than aging) must contain at least 15 percent malt wine and has a fuller, maltier flavor; jonge jenever ("young") contains no more than 15 percent and is lighter and drier. Korenwijn ("corn wine") is the richest category, with 51–70 percent malt wine, often aged in oak. Jenever is traditionally drunk neat, ice cold, from a brim-filled tulip glass — the first sip taken bent over the glass without hands — or in the kopstoot ritual, a glass of jenever taken alongside a beer.

Production centers

Historic centers include Schiedam and Amsterdam in the Netherlands, and Hasselt and Ghent in Belgium, where museums preserve the craft. Belgian jenever and French Nord gin-like spirits (genièvre de Wambrechies) share the appellation. Modern craft distillers in the Netherlands and abroad have revived barrel-aged and high-malt-wine styles.
