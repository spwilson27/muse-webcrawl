Malayan Peoples' Anti-Japanese Army

The Malayan Peoples' Anti-Japanese Army (MPAJA) was a communist-led guerrilla resistance movement that fought against the Japanese occupation of Malaya during the Second World War. Drawn largely from the ethnic Chinese community and organised under the leadership of the Malayan Communist Party (MCP), it became the largest and most effective resistance force in Japanese-occupied Malaya.

History

The MPAJA grew out of the Malayan Communist Party's preparations for resistance shortly before the Japanese invasion of December 1941. British colonial authorities, desperate for allies as the Japanese advanced down the Malay Peninsula, agreed to train and arm MCP volunteers. After the fall of Singapore in February 1942, several hundred of these trainees went into the jungle to form the nucleus of the guerrilla army.

At its height the MPAJA fielded several thousand fighters organised into regiments based in different states of the peninsula. Operating from jungle camps, the guerrillas carried out ambushes, sabotage and intelligence gathering, and maintained contact with the British through Force 136, which supplied weapons, training and liaison officers by submarine and parachute drop.

Postwar period

Following the Japanese surrender in August 1945, MPAJA units briefly emerged to administer many towns before British forces returned. The army was formally disbanded on 1 December 1945, with members receiving gratuities in exchange for their weapons. However, significant caches of arms were retained, and many veterans later formed the core of the Malayan National Liberation Army during the Malayan Emergency that began in 1948.

Legacy

The MPAJA's role in the resistance remains a significant and debated part of Malaysian history. Its sacrifices during the occupation are commemorated, while its links to the later communist insurgency have made its memory politically sensitive.
