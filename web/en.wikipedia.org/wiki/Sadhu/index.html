A sadhu (Sanskrit: साधु, IAST: sādhu, "good, virtuous") is a religious ascetic, mendicant, or holy person in Hinduism and Jainism who has renounced worldly life. Sadhus are sometimes alternatively called yogi, sannyasi, or vairagi. The female form is sādhvī. A sadhu is dedicated solely to achieving mokṣa (liberation from the cycle of death and rebirth) through meditation and contemplation of the divine.

Life and practices

Sadhus typically sever ties with family and possessions, subsisting on alms. Their practices include daily ritual bathing, prayer, yoga, meditation, and, in some traditions, severe austerities (tapas). Many wear saffron or ochre robes signifying renunciation; Shaivite sadhus often smear their bodies with sacred ash (vibhuti) and wear their hair in matted locks, while the Naga Babas famously go naked. Others, particularly Jain ascetics, wear white or no clothing. Some sadhus belong to monastic orders (akhāṛās) and maths, while others wander alone as pilgrims among holy sites.

Traditions and sects

Sadhus are divided principally between Shaiva ascetics, devoted to Shiva, and Vaishnava ascetics, devoted to Vishnu or his avatars, with smaller numbers of Shakta and other traditions. Within these are numerous orders, including the Dashanami Sampradaya attributed to Adi Shankara, and the militant Naga orders historically organized as warrior-ascetics. Jain mendicants — both Digambara and Śvetāmbara — follow strict vows of non-violence, truthfulness, and non-possession.

Sadhus and society

Sadhus are widely revered in India and Nepal; giving alms to them is considered meritorious. Large gatherings such as the Kumbh Mela draw millions of pilgrims and tens of thousands of sadhus, whose processions are a central feature of the festival. Foreigners have occasionally joined the sadhu life, and the figure remains a prominent symbol of India's spiritual heritage, though authorities also contend with fake sadhus and criminality among a small minority.
