Speech synthesis

Speech synthesis is the artificial production of human speech. A computer system used for this purpose is called a speech synthesizer, and the process of converting written text into speech is known as text-to-speech (TTS). Synthesized speech can be created by concatenating pieces of recorded speech, by generating sound from models of the vocal tract, or by statistical and neural-network models trained on speech data.

History

Mechanical speaking machines date to the eighteenth century, including Wolfgang von Kempelen's speaking machine of 1791. In the 1930s Bell Labs developed the Voder, demonstrated at the 1939 World's Fair. Electronic synthesis advanced with Homer Dudley's vocoder and, in 1961, an IBM 7094 singing "Daisy Bell" — an event later referenced in the film 2001: A Space Odyssey. Formant synthesizers such as MITalk and DECtalk (famous as the voice of Stephen Hawking) made TTS practical in the 1980s.

Approaches

Concatenative synthesis joins segments of recorded speech, either large units (words and phrases), diphones, or small units selected by unit selection algorithms from many hours of recordings. Formant synthesis generates speech from acoustic parameters modelling resonances of the vocal tract, producing intelligible but robotic speech. Statistical parametric synthesis, using hidden Markov models, trades some naturalness for small size and flexibility. Modern neural TTS systems — sequence-to-sequence models such as Tacotron paired with neural vocoders such as WaveNet (2016) — achieve near-human naturalness and enable voice cloning from limited samples.

Processing pipeline

A TTS system typically normalizes text (expanding numbers, abbreviations and dates), converts graphemes to phonemes, assigns prosody — pitch, duration and stress — and then generates the waveform. Quality is evaluated with mean opinion scores and intelligibility tests.

Applications

Applications include screen readers and communication aids for people with visual impairments or speech disabilities, navigation systems, virtual assistants, audiobook and video narration, call-centre automation and language learning. Concerns have arisen over misuse of voice cloning for fraud and disinformation.
