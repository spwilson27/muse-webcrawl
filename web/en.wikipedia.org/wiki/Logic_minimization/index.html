Logic optimization

Logic optimization (minimization)
FieldDigital electronics, electronic design automation
GoalReduce gate count, area, delay or power of a logic circuit without changing its function
Classical methodsKarnaugh maps, Quine–McCluskey algorithm, Espresso heuristic
Used inLogic synthesis within EDA tool flows

Logic optimization, also called logic minimization, is a process of finding an equivalent representation of a specified logic circuit that is simpler — using fewer logic gates, literals, or levels of logic — under some cost criterion such as area, delay or power consumption. It is a central step in the design of digital integrated circuits, performed automatically by logic synthesis tools within electronic design automation (EDA) flows, and traditionally taught through hand methods for small functions.

Two-level minimization

For functions expressed as a sum of products (or product of sums), the classical approaches are:

  • Karnaugh maps (1953) — a graphical technique in which adjacent cells of a truth-table grid that differ in one variable are grouped, visually simplifying functions of up to about six variables.
  • Quine–McCluskey algorithm (1952–1956) — a tabular, algorithmic method that finds all prime implicants and then solves a covering problem to obtain a minimal form; exact but exponential in the worst case.
  • Espresso (developed at IBM and UC Berkeley in the 1980s) — a heuristic minimizer using expand, irredundant cover and reduce operations, which handles functions with hundreds of variables and outputs near-minimal results quickly; its variants (e.g., Espresso-II) became standard in synthesis tools.

Multi-level optimization

Modern circuits are rarely implemented as two-level AND–OR networks. Multi-level synthesis factors common subexpressions, decomposes functions, and maps networks onto the cells available in a technology library (technology mapping), optimising the trade-off between area and timing along critical paths. Algebraic methods treat expressions with Boolean algebra rules, while Boolean methods exploit don't-care conditions — combinations of inputs that never occur, or outputs that are unobserved — to gain additional freedom for simplification.

Complexity and practice

Finding a provably minimal circuit is computationally hard, so practical flows rely on heuristics and on representations such as binary decision diagrams (BDDs) and AND-inverter graphs (AIGs), as in the ABC synthesis system. Optimization interacts with later steps — placement, routing and timing closure — so modern flows iterate between logical and physical information.
