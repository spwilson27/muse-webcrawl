Habeas corpus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Habeas corpus (Latin: "[we command] that you have the body") is a legal remedy by which a court orders a person holding another in custody to bring the prisoner before the court, so that the lawfulness of the detention can be examined; if no sufficient cause is shown, the prisoner is released. It is the classic common-law protection against arbitrary imprisonment — the mechanism that turns the promise of Magna Carta (no imprisonment except by the law of the land) into an enforceable order — and it has repeatedly been the battleground of liberty in emergencies, suspended in wars and invoked by the detained in every era.

History and modern use

The writ developed in medieval England and was codified in the Habeas Corpus Act 1679, which closed loopholes (gaolers evading writs, prisoners shipped beyond reach) that had let the Crown detain critics; it spread throughout the common-law world and into many civil-law systems as amparo. The United States Constitution (Article I) protects the privilege of the writ, permitting suspension only in rebellion or invasion — Lincoln suspended it during the Civil War, provoking a famous clash with Chief Justice Taney. After the attacks of 11 September 2001, habeas litigation by detainees at Guantánamo Bay produced landmark rulings (Rasul 2004; Hamdan 2006; Boumediene v. Bush 2008) affirming that even non-citizens held by the US abroad may test their detention in court. Its limits are equally instructive: the writ tests the legality of detention, not guilt, and its history in the age of mass incarceration, immigration detention and counter-terrorism remains contested terrain of the rule of law.
