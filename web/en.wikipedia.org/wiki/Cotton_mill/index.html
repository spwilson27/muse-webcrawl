Cotton mill

Cotton mill
First powered mills1770s–1780s, Derwent Valley, England
Key processesCarding, spinning, weaving
Power sourcesWater wheel, later steam engine

A cotton mill is a factory housing powered machinery for manufacturing cotton textiles, principally spinning yarn and weaving cloth. Cotton mills were the emblematic buildings of the Industrial Revolution: their multi-storey brick ranges, first driven by water power and later by steam, concentrated thousands of workers in the mill towns of Lancashire, New England and later industrialising regions worldwide.

Development

Richard Arkwright's water frame, patented in 1769, demanded purpose-built factories; his mills at Cromford in the Derwent Valley (1771 onwards) set the template: long, narrow, fire-resistant multi-storey buildings with rows of machines driven by line shafts from a central power source. Samuel Slater carried the designs to Rhode Island in 1790, and the Boston Associates developed the integrated mill at Lowell, Massachusetts, combining spinning and weaving under one roof.

Processes and labour

Raw cotton bales were opened, cleaned and carded into rovings, spun on mules or ring frames, and woven on power looms in separate weaving sheds. Mills employed large numbers of women and children under harsh conditions — long hours, noise, cotton dust causing byssinosis ("brown lung"), and dangerous machinery — provoking factory reform legislation such as the British Factory Acts and strikes from Lowell mill women to Lancashire operatives.

Decline and heritage

Competition from lower-wage producers shifted cotton manufacturing to the American South and then to Asia during the twentieth century; most Lancashire and New England mills closed, many demolished, others converted to housing, museums and workplaces. The Derwent Valley Mills are a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and surviving mills anchor the heritage of towns from Lowell to Mumbai, whose Girangaon mill lands recall a parallel Indian industry.
