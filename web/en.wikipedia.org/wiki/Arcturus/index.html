Arcturus is the brightest star in the northern constellation of Boötes and the brightest star in the northern celestial hemisphere. With an apparent visual magnitude of −0.05, it is the fourth-brightest star in the night sky, after Sirius, Canopus, and Alpha Centauri. It is designated α Boötis (Latinised to Alpha Boötis).

Located relatively close to Earth at 36.7 light-years from the Sun, Arcturus is a single red giant star of spectral type K1.5 III. It is about 7.1 billion years old and has exhausted the hydrogen in its core, evolving off the main sequence. It is currently fusing helium into carbon and oxygen in its core.

Observation

Arcturus is visible from both hemispheres and is circumpolar at far northern latitudes. Its orange hue is readily apparent to the naked eye. A common mnemonic for locating it is to "follow the arc to Arcturus" — extending the curved handle of the Big Dipper (the Plough) leads first to Arcturus and then on to Spica. The star culminates at midnight on 30 April and is an evening star through the spring.

Physical characteristics

Arcturus has a radius of about 25.4 times that of the Sun and a luminosity roughly 170 times the Sun's in visible and infrared light combined. Its mass is only slightly greater than the Sun's — about 1.08 solar masses — although its great age means it has probably lost a significant fraction of its mass. Interferometric measurements give an angular diameter of about 21 milliarcseconds.

The star is a leading member of the Arcturus moving group, a stream of old stars moving through the galaxy at high speed relative to the Sun; its high proper motion — about 2 arcseconds per year — was first noted by Edmond Halley in 1718 by comparing its position with ancient records.

Name and mythology

The name Arcturus derives from the Greek Arktouros (Ἀρκτοῦρος), meaning "Guardian of the Bear," in reference to its position near the constellations Ursa Major and Ursa Minor. The star is mentioned in the Bible's Book of Job and appeared in the mythology of many cultures; in Hawaiian tradition it is Hōkūleʻa, the "Star of Gladness" used by navigators.
