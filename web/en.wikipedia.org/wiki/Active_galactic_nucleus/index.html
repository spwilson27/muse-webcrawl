Active galactic nucleus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Hubble image of the jet of M87 emerging from its active galactic nucleus]
The relativistic jet of M87's active nucleus.

An active galactic nucleus (AGN) is a compact region at the centre of a galaxy that emits enormous amounts of energy across the electromagnetic spectrum, powered by accretion of matter onto a supermassive black hole. AGN include quasars (the most luminous persistent objects in the universe), Seyfert galaxies, radio galaxies and blazars — which unified models (Antonucci, Urry & Padovani) interpret as the same phenomenon viewed at different angles through a dusty obscuring torus, with jets pointed at the observer in the blazar case.

Accretion converts gravitational energy to radiation at up to ~40% efficiency (versus 0.7% for nuclear fusion), heating a disc that outshines the host galaxy, driving winds and (in about 10% of cases) twin relativistic jets visible in radio over millions of light-years. Feedback from AGN — heating and expelling a galaxy's gas — is thought to regulate galaxy growth and explain the tight relation between black hole mass and galaxy properties (the M–sigma relation). Nearly every large galaxy hosts a supermassive black hole; whether it appears as an AGN depends on whether it is currently fed.

The Milky Way's own

Our galaxy's black hole, Sagittarius A*, is presently starved and dim — a million times underluminous compared to a quasar — but evidence (the Fermi bubbles, ionised gas trails) shows it flared within the last few million years. AGN are therefore not exotic exceptions but a phase of ordinary galactic life.
