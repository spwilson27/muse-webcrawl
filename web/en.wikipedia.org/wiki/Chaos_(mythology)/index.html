Chaos (mythology)

The primordial void of Chaos

Chaos (Ancient Greek: Χάος, Kháos) is the mythological void state preceding the creation of the universe (the cosmos) in Greek creation myths. In Hesiod's Theogony (c. 700 BC), Chaos is the first thing to exist: "at first Chaos came to be" — a yawning gap or chasm, from which emerged Gaia (Earth), Tartarus (the abyss), and Eros (Love). Chaos itself produced Erebus (Darkness) and Nyx (Night).

Interpretations

The Greek word khaos derives from a verb meaning "to gape open"; scholars debate whether Hesiod meant a formless mass, empty space, or a dark gap separating heaven and earth. Later authors and philosophers treated Chaos variously: in the Orphic tradition it arises from Chronos (Time); Ovid, in the Metamorphoses, recast it as a "rude and undeveloped mass" of discordant elements, an image that strongly influenced the modern sense of chaos as complete disorder. The popular meaning of the English word — confusion and randomness — developed from this later interpretation, though the ancient Greek concept was closer to a vast, dark emptiness.

Legacy

Chaos stands at the head of Greek cosmogony and Greek genealogy of the gods, and its name was borrowed into science in naming, for example, the chaotic behaviour studied in chaos theory and the Kuiper belt object 19521 Chaos.
