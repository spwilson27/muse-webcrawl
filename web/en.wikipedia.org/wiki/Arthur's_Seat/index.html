Arthur's Seat (Scottish Gaelic: Suidhe Artair) is an ancient volcano which is the main peak of the group of hills in Edinburgh, Scotland, which form most of Holyrood Park. It is situated just to the east of the city centre, about 1 mile (1.6 km) from Edinburgh Castle, and rises 251 metres (823 ft) above the city, affording panoramic views in all directions. Despite its modest height, its rugged, craggy character gives it the feel of a Highland summit within the capital.

Geology

Arthur's Seat is the eroded remnant of a volcano that was active in the Carboniferous period, about 350 million years ago. The hill shows classic features studied by the pioneering geologist James Hutton, including the exposed sills and dykes of the Salisbury Crags, where Hutton found evidence that molten rock had intruded into sedimentary strata — observations that helped found modern geology. Glacial action during the Ice Ages plucked and streamlined the hill into its present crag-and-tail form.

Name

The name is traditionally connected with the legendary King Arthur, though there is no evidence he had any association with the site; alternative derivations suggest a Gaelic origin meaning "Archer's Seat" or "height of arrows" (Àrd-na-Saighead). William Maitland proposed the hill as a possible location of Camelot.

History and recreation

The hill bears traces of Iron Age hill forts, and miniature coffins discovered in a cave on the hill in 1836 remain an unsolved curiosity, sometimes linked to the Burke and Hare murders. Today Arthur's Seat is one of Edinburgh's most popular walks; a broad path from Holyrood reaches the summit in under an hour. The hill also hosts the traditional May Day dew-washing custom on its slopes and is a Site of Special Scientific Interest for its geology and rare plant species.
