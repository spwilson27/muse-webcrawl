Sirloin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The sirloin is a cut of beef from the back of the animal, between the short loin in front and the round behind. The name derives from the Middle English surloine, from Old French surlonge — sur "above" and longe "loin". (A persistent folk tale claims an English king "knighted" a joint of beef as "Sir Loin"; this is a myth, though it is recorded as early as the seventeenth century.)

British and Commonwealth usage

In British butchery, the sirloin is the upper portion of the loin, corresponding roughly to the American strip loin and top sirloin together. Boned and rolled, it is a classic roasting joint; cut into steaks it gives sirloin steaks with a strip of fat along one side. The corresponding bone-in section below it, towards the rump, is treated separately.

American usage

In the United States, the sirloin is divided into subprimals. The top sirloin (top butt) is the leaner, more tender part and yields top sirloin steaks and the picanha-style cap (culotte) cut. The bottom sirloin is less tender and includes the tri-tip (popular in California), ball-tip and flap meat (bavette). American sirloin steaks are typically boneless and contain portions of the hip bone in whole-cut form. Because it combines good flavour with moderate price, sirloin is among the most popular steakhouse and retail cuts; it is less marbled and generally less tender than ribeye or tenderloin but leaner and more economical.

Cooking

Sirloin is usually grilled, pan-fried or roasted and is best served rare to medium-rare; the leaner bottom-sirloin cuts benefit from marinating or careful slicing against the grain.
