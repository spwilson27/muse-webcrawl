Big Three (American television)

The Big Three refers to the three major traditional commercial broadcast television networks in the United States: the American Broadcasting Company (ABC), the Columbia Broadcasting System (CBS), and the National Broadcasting Company (NBC). From the late 1940s until the mid-1980s, these three networks dominated American commercial television, controlling the vast majority of prime-time programming and audience share.

Background

NBC and CBS originated as radio networks in 1926 and 1927 respectively, and both began experimental television broadcasts in the 1930s. ABC was formed in 1943 from the NBC Blue Network, which the Federal Communications Commission had forced NBC to divest. A fourth network, the DuMont Television Network, competed from 1946 until its closure in 1956, leaving the Big Three unchallenged for three decades. The networks built their schedules around affiliate stations across the country and supplied news, entertainment, and sports programming.

Dominance and decline

During the "Golden Age of Television" and for decades after, the Big Three were the default viewing choice for American households; their evening newscasts, anchored by figures such as Walter Cronkite (CBS), defined the national news agenda. Their dominance began to erode with the rise of cable television in the 1980s and the launch of the Fox Broadcasting Company in 1986, which eventually joined them as a fourth major network. The spread of The WB and UPN in the 1990s (later merged into The CW), cable channels, and ultimately streaming services further fragmented audiences, though ABC, CBS, and NBC remain among the most-watched broadcasters in the United States, especially in news and live sports.
