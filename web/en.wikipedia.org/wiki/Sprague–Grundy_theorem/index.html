Sprague–Grundy theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Impartial game positions coloured by Grundy value

In combinatorial game theory, the Sprague–Grundy theorem states that every impartial game under the normal play convention is equivalent to a nim-heap, or equivalently to a nimber. The theorem is named after Roland Sprague, who published it in 1935, and Patrick Michael Grundy, who discovered it independently and published it in 1939.

Statement

The theorem asserts that any finite impartial game in which the last player to move wins is equivalent to a nim-heap whose size is called the game's Grundy value (or Grundy number). The Grundy value of a position is defined recursively as the mex (minimum excluded value) of the set of Grundy values of all positions reachable in one move. A terminal position, from which no move is possible, has Grundy value 0.

A position is a losing position for the player about to move (a P-position) if and only if its Grundy value is 0. Otherwise it is an N-position, and a winning move consists of moving to a position with Grundy value 0.

The disjunctive sum

The theorem's power comes from its treatment of the disjunctive sum of games: when several impartial games are played together and a move consists of moving in exactly one component, the Grundy value of the sum is the bitwise exclusive or (XOR) of the Grundy values of the components. This operation, also called the nim-sum, reduces the analysis of compound games to the analysis of their individual parts.

Applications

  • Nim variants such as Wythoff Nim, Moore's Nimk, and turning games are analysed by computing Grundy values.
  • Subtraction games, in which tokens are removed from a heap in fixed amounts, have eventually periodic Grundy sequences.
  • Octals and other codes describe splitting games such as Treblecross, Dawson's Kayles, and Grundy's game.
  • The theory extends to Green Hackenbush and many other impartial games.

History

The result was proved independently by R. P. Sprague in 1935 and P. M. Grundy in 1939. The earlier theory of Nim, due to Charles L. Bouton in 1901, is a special case. Grundy introduced the arithmetic of nimbers and showed that the crucial property is the mex rule combined with the XOR rule for sums of games.
