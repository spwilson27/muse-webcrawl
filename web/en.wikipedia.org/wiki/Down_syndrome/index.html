Down syndrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Down syndrome (trisomy 21) is a genetic condition caused by the presence of all or part of a third copy of chromosome 21. It is the most common chromosomal cause of intellectual disability, occurring in roughly 1 in 700–1,000 births, with probability rising with maternal age. The extra chromosome usually arises from nondisjunction in meiosis; rarer forms involve translocations or mosaicism.

Characteristic features include upward-slanting eyes with epicanthic folds, a flattened facial profile, low muscle tone, a single palmar crease and short stature — alongside increased rates of congenital heart defects, thyroid disease, hearing and vision problems, and early Alzheimer's disease (driven by the extra copy of the APP gene on chromosome 21). The condition is named for John Langdon Down, who described it in 1866; its chromosomal basis was identified by Jérôme Lejeune's group in 1959.

Life and care

Life expectancy has risen from about 10 years in the early 20th century to around 60 in wealthy countries, reflecting cardiac surgery, infection control and inclusion. Early intervention, education in mainstream settings where possible, and supported employment have changed expectations of independence and participation.

Screening

Prenatal screening (blood tests, ultrasound, cell-free DNA) and diagnostic tests (amniocentesis, CVS) can detect trisomy 21 in pregnancy; newborn diagnosis is by examination and karyotype. Screening policy and its ethics — informed choice, disability rights — are actively debated.
