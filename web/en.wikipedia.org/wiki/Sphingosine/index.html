Sphingosine

IUPAC name: (2S,3R)-2-aminooctadec-4-ene-1,3-diol
Formula: C18H37NO2
Molar mass: 299.50 g/mol

Sphingosine (2-amino-4-trans-octadecene-1,3-diol) is an 18-carbon amino alcohol with an unsaturated hydrocarbon chain, which forms the primary part of sphingolipids, a class of cell membrane lipids that include sphingomyelin, an important phospholipid.

Biosynthesis

Sphingosine is synthesised from palmitoyl-CoA and the amino acid serine in a condensation catalysed by serine palmitoyltransferase, yielding 3-ketosphinganine. This is reduced to sphinganine (dihydrosphingosine), which is then acylated to dihydroceramide and desaturated to ceramide. Sphingosine itself is formed mainly by the breakdown of ceramide by ceramidases, and it is not normally re-synthesised directly as a free base in de novo synthesis.

Functions

Sphingosine can be phosphorylated by sphingosine kinases to form sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), a potent signalling lipid involved in calcium mobilisation, cell growth, survival, and immune cell trafficking. Within cells, sphingosine is converted back to ceramide by ceramide synthases in the salvage pathway. It also acts as a signalling molecule in its own right, including inhibition of protein kinase C, and is generally associated with growth arrest and apoptosis, in contrast to the pro-survival effects of S1P — a balance sometimes called the "sphingolipid rheostat."

History

The compound was named by the German chemist Johann Ludwig Wilhelm Thudichum in the 1880s after the Sphinx, in reference to the enigmatic nature of the brain lipids he was studying.
