Grindhouse

A grindhouse is an American term for a theatre that mainly shows low-budget, sensational exploitation films — horror, sexploitation, martial arts, blaxploitation, splatter, and similar fare — often in continuous double or triple features. The name is said to derive from the "grind policy" of running films around the clock, grinding out show after show, though it has also been linked to the bump-and-grind burlesque acts once staged in the same venues.

History

Grindhouses flourished in American city centres from the 1920s through the 1980s. Famous concentrations included New York's 42nd Street, where theatres such as those along "the Deuce" showed kung fu, Italian horror, and sexploitation films to a transient audience. Many were converted burlesque houses or ageing picture palaces.

Decline and legacy

The rise of home video in the 1980s, urban redevelopment, and the cleanup of districts like Times Square in the 1990s destroyed the grindhouse ecosystem. The aesthetic was celebrated and pastiched in the 2007 double feature Grindhouse by Robert Rodriguez and Quentin Tarantino — comprising Planet Terror and Death Proof with fake trailers — which inspired spinoffs such as Machete and Hobo with a Shotgun. Directors associated with the original era, including Roger Corman, Herschell Gordon Lewis, and Russ Meyer, are now the subjects of academic and fan reappraisal.
