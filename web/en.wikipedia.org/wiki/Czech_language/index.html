Czech language

Czech (Czech: čeština), historically also known as Bohemian, is a West Slavic language of the Czech–Slovak group, written in the Latin script. Spoken by over 12 million people, it serves as the official language of the Czech Republic. Czech is closely related to Slovak, to the point of a high degree of mutual intelligibility, and is also considerably similar to Polish.

Czech is a fusional language with a rich system of morphology and a relatively free word order. Its vocabulary has been extensively influenced by Latin and German. Stress always falls on the first syllable of a word.

History

Old Czech developed from Common Slavic in the late first millennium AD. Written Czech emerged in the 13th–14th centuries; Jan Hus is credited with orthographic reforms, including the use of diacritics, in the early 15th century. After the Battle of White Mountain (1620), Czech declined under Habsburg Germanization, but was revived during the Czech National Revival of the late 18th and 19th centuries by scholars such as Josef Dobrovský and Josef Jungmann.

Classification

Czech is a member of the West Slavic subgroup of the Slavic languages, which in turn belong to the Balto-Slavic branch of Indo-European. It forms a dialect continuum with Slovak.

Phonology

Czech has ten vowel phonemes (five short and five long) and a distinctive raised alveolar trill, spelled ř, found in few other languages. Consonant clusters can form entire words without vowels (e.g. prst "finger", and the famous tongue-twister Strč prst skrz krk).

Grammar

Nouns are inflected for three genders (masculine, feminine, neuter), two numbers, and seven cases. Verbs conjugate for person, number, tense and aspect, with a perfective/imperfective distinction.

Dialects and registers

Alongside Standard Czech (spisovná čeština), a widely spoken interdialect known as Common Czech (obecná čeština) is used in everyday speech, especially in Bohemia; regional dialects persist in Moravia and Silesia.
