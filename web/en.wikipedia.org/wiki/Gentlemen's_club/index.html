Gentlemen's club

A gentlemen's club is a private social club of a type originally established by men in 18th-century Britain, and popularised by English upper-class men and other elites in the 19th century. Such clubs typically provide a comfortable, private space for members to dine, drink, read, play games, and socialise, and many historically restricted membership to men. In North America, the term can also refer to a strip club, an unrelated usage that became common in the late 20th century.

History

The gentlemen's club grew out of the coffee houses and chocolate houses of 17th- and 18th-century London. Clubs such as White's (founded 1693), Boodle's, and Brooks's became centres of political and social life, many of them located in the St James's area of London, which became known as "clubland". In the Victorian era the number of clubs proliferated, catering to political affiliations (such as the Reform Club and the Carlton Club), the military, the professions, the universities, and the arts.

Characteristics

Members are typically elected, often after nomination by existing members, and pay an annual subscription. Traditional clubs occupy grand townhouses with dining rooms, libraries, bars, and sometimes bedrooms for members. Many clubs maintain reciprocal arrangements with similar clubs overseas. From the late 20th century, most traditional gentlemen's clubs began admitting women as full members, though a few remained single-sex.

Notable clubs

Famous examples include White's, Brooks's, the Reform Club, the Athenaeum, and the Garrick Club in London, and city and university clubs in the United States, Canada, India, Australia, and elsewhere, modelled on the London pattern.
