Compass and straightedge

[Image: Constructing a regular hexagon]

Compass-and-straightedge construction is the construction of lengths, angles, and other geometric figures using only an idealised ruler and a pair of compasses. The ruler, called a straightedge, is assumed to be infinite in length, to have only one edge, and to have no markings on it. The compass is assumed to collapse when lifted from the page, so it cannot be used to transfer distances directly — though this restriction makes no difference in power, as Euclid showed.

History

The rules descend from Euclid's Elements, whose first three postulates license drawing a line through two points, extending a segment, and drawing a circle with a given centre and radius. Greek geometers constructed regular polygons, bisected angles and segments, and erected perpendiculars and parallels, and posed three famous problems: trisecting an arbitrary angle, doubling the cube, and squaring the circle.

Possible and impossible constructions

Algebraically, a point is constructible exactly when its coordinates can be obtained from the rationals by addition, subtraction, multiplication, division and the taking of square roots — equivalently, when it lies in a field extension of the rationals whose degree is a power of two. Using this criterion, Pierre Wantzel proved in 1837 that angle trisection and doubling the cube are impossible, and that a regular n-gon is constructible only if n is a power of two times a product of distinct Fermat primes (a result first obtained by Gauss, who constructed the 17-gon in 1796). Ferdinand von Lindemann's 1882 proof that π is transcendental settled the impossibility of squaring the circle.

Related results

The Mohr–Mascheroni theorem states that anything constructible with compass and straightedge can be constructed with a compass alone; the Poncelet–Steiner theorem shows that a straightedge alone suffices if a single circle and its centre are given. Origami and marked-ruler (neusis) constructions are strictly more powerful and can trisect angles and double the cube.
