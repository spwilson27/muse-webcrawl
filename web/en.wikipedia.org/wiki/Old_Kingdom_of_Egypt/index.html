Old Kingdom of Egypt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The pyramids of Giza with the Great Sphinx in the foreground]

The pyramids of Giza with the Great Sphinx in the foreground

The Old Kingdom (c. 2686–2181 BC) is the period of ancient Egyptian history spanning the Third through Sixth Dynasties, when Egypt attained its first continuous peak of civilisation — the 'Age of the Pyramids'. From the capital at Memphis, god-kings (pharaohs) of unprecedented power organised the labour that built Djoser's Step Pyramid at Saqqara (designed by the vizier Imhotep, history's first named architect) and then the Great Pyramids of Giza under Khufu, Khafre and Menkaure — the largest of which remained the tallest human-made structure on Earth for more than 3,800 years.

The pyramid age

Djoser's step pyramid (c. 2670 BC) translated royal tombs from mudbrick mastabas into stone eternity; Sneferu, founder of the Fourth Dynasty, experimented his way to the true pyramid (Meidum, the Bent Pyramid, the Red Pyramid), and his son Khufu raised the Great Pyramid — some 2.3 million blocks averaging 2.5 tonnes, aligned to true north within a fraction of a degree. The labour system behind them was corvée: rotating levies of peasants fed, by the evidence of the builders' town and bakeries at Giza, on bread, beer and beef, and buried with honour near the tombs they built.

Decline

From the Fifth Dynasty, sun temples and the growing wealth of provincial governors (nomarchs) diluted royal power; the Sixth Dynasty's Pepi II, reigning (by tradition) 94 years — the longest reign in history — outlived his heirs and his kingdom's cohesion. Drought (the 4.2-kiloyear aridification event) and low Nile floods are implicated in the collapse around 2181 BC into the First Intermediate Period of regional rulers.
