Rheingau (wine region)

The Rheingau is one of Germany’s most prestigious wine-growing regions, extending along the north bank of the Rhine west of Wiesbaden. A relatively compact area, it is internationally identified with the balance of ripeness and acidity in its dry and sweet Rieslings.

Country Germany
Principal grape Riesling
Notable centres Rüdesheim, Geisenheim, Eltville, Hochheim and Johannisberg

Geography and climate

At this point the Rhine turns from its generally northerly course and flows west for a stretch, creating south-facing slopes on its northern bank. The Taunus hills shelter vineyards from cold northern winds, while the broad river moderates temperature and reflects light. Soils include slate, quartzite, loess, clay and gravel, producing marked differences among villages and individual sites.

History

Monasteries played a major role in developing viticulture. Schloss Johannisberg is traditionally associated with the discovery of late-harvest wine after a delayed harvest permit in 1775 allowed noble rot to concentrate the grapes. Rheingau estates later helped pioneer vineyard classification and the reputation of German Riesling abroad.

Wines

Riesling dominates plantings, supplemented by Spätburgunder (Pinot noir), especially around Assmannshausen. Wines range from bone-dry Grosses Gewächs bottlings to Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese and rare Trockenbeerenauslese. The VDP classification promotes top vineyard sites, including wines labelled Rheingau Riesling GG.

Culture

The region hosts the Rheingau Musik Festival and maintains historic wine villages, river promenades and research institutions at Geisenheim. Its combination of steep vineyards, monasteries, castles and Rhine scenery forms part of the broader cultural landscape of the Middle Rhine.
