Disciples of Christ

The Disciples of Christ, officially the Christian Church (Disciples of Christ), is a mainline Protestant Christian denomination in the United States and Canada. It arose from the Stone-Campbell Restoration Movement on the American frontier in the early 19th century, which sought to restore the beliefs and practices of the New Testament church and to unite Christians by discarding denominational creeds. The Disciples are known for weekly celebration of the Lord's Supper, believer's baptism by immersion, congregational autonomy, and a commitment to Christian unity and ecumenism.

Weekly communion at an open table is central to Disciples worship.

History

The movement had two principal sources: Barton W. Stone's "Christians" in Kentucky, which emerged from the Cane Ridge Revival of 1801, and the "Disciples" led by Thomas Campbell and his son Alexander Campbell in western Pennsylvania and Virginia, whose 1809 Declaration and Address called for unity on a biblical basis. The two groups united in 1832. Over time the movement divided: the Churches of Christ separated in the late 19th and early 20th centuries over instrumental music and missionary societies, and a further conservative wing formed independent Christian churches during the 20th century. In 1968 the Disciples formally adopted a denominational structure and the name Christian Church (Disciples of Christ).

Beliefs and practices

Disciples have no formal creed, holding to the confession of Jesus as Christ as their only test of fellowship — a heritage summed up in slogans such as "No creed but Christ" and "Where the Scriptures speak, we speak; where the Scriptures are silent, we are silent." Communion is celebrated weekly and open to all Christians; baptism is by immersion of believers. Each congregation is self-governing, calling its own ministers and owning its property, while participating in regional and general church bodies. The denomination ordains women, has been active in the ecumenical movement, and maintains a formal partnership of full communion with the United Church of Christ. Its symbol, adopted in 1971, is a red chalice bearing the white X-shaped cross of St Andrew.
