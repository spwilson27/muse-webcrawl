Tomato sauce (also known as salsa roja in Spanish and sauce tomate in French) can refer to many different sauces made primarily from tomatoes, usually to be served as part of a dish rather than as a condiment. Tomato sauces are common for meat and vegetables, but they are perhaps best known as bases for Mexican salsas or sauces for Italian pasta dishes.

History

The tomato is native to the Americas and was used in Mesoamerican cooking long before European contact; Aztec markets sold prepared tomato sauces. After the tomato reached Europe in the 16th century, tomato-based sauces developed in Spain and Italy. The first Italian cookbook to include tomato sauce, L'Apicio Moderno by Francesco Leonardi, was published in 1790. Tomato sauce became a defining element of Italian cuisine, particularly in Naples, where it was paired with pasta and, later, pizza.

Preparation

A basic tomato sauce is made by cooking tomatoes — fresh, canned or as passata — with olive oil, garlic and herbs such as basil, until thickened. Common variants include marinara (a quick-cooked sauce), arrabbiata (with chilli), amatriciana (with guanciale and pecorino) and puttanesca (with olives, capers and anchovies). Long-simmered meat sauces such as ragù alla bolognese also use tomato as a base ingredient.

Uses

Tomato sauce is used worldwide as a base for pasta dishes, pizza, shakshuka, curries, and as an ingredient in soups and stews. In the United States, "tomato sauce" also denotes a canned, lightly seasoned tomato purée used as a cooking ingredient.
