Yoshiharu Tsuge (つげ 義春, Tsuge Yoshiharu, born 31 October 1937) is a Japanese manga artist and essayist regarded as one of the most influential figures in alternative manga. Debuting in the rental-manga (kashihon) market in the 1950s, he came to prominence in the 1960s in the avant-garde magazine Garo, where his surreal, dreamlike, and deeply personal stories transformed the possibilities of the medium.

Career

Tsuge began publishing in 1954, working in the gekiga style of dramatic manga for adults. His breakthrough came with stories published in Garo beginning in 1966, including "The Swamp" (Numa), "Chiko", and "Lee-san's Family". His most famous work, "Nejishiki" ("Screw Style", 1968), is a surreal narrative that opens with a man bitten by a jellyfish seeking a doctor, and remains one of the most analyzed short works in manga history. In the 1970s Tsuge turned to travel writing and autobiographical stories of rural Japan, drawing on journeys to remote inns and villages. His later semi-autobiographical works, culminating in The Man Without Talent (Munō no Hito, 1986), portray a would-be artist drifting through odd jobs; he ceased publishing new work in 1987 amid anxiety and depression.

Reputation and influence

Reclusive in later life, Tsuge nevertheless became a cult figure: his works have been translated into many languages, The Man Without Talent was adapted into an award-winning film in 1991, and an English edition of Nejishiki drew wide acclaim in the 2020s. His younger brother Tadao Tsuge is also a noted manga artist. Critics credit Yoshiharu Tsuge with pioneering autobiographical and oneiric comics, influencing generations of artists in Japan and abroad.
