Spinach

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A fresh bunch of dark-green spinach leaves with their stems]

Spinach (Spinacia oleracea) is a leafy green of the amaranth family, native to Persia, eaten for its mild, mineral leaves — raw in salads as tender baby spinach, or cooked down in quantities that famously shrink ("a kiss in the pan"). From Iran it travelled to China by the 7th century as the "Persian vegetable", reached Europe through Arab Sicily and Spain, and became a Lenten staple. It is forever linked with Popeye the Sailor, whose spinach-fuelled strength from 1931 onward sent consumption climbing in the United States.

Popeye and the iron question

Spinach's iron reputation exceeds its chemistry: it contains a moderate amount of iron, much of it poorly absorbed because spinach is also rich in oxalic acid. The beloved story that a misplaced decimal point in 1870s German data inflated spinach's iron tenfold — and thus inspired Popeye — has itself been debunked: a myth about a myth. Popeye's creator, E. C. Segar, actually praised spinach's vitamin A in the strip. The cartoon still measurably boosted sales; Crystal City, Texas, calls itself the Spinach Capital of the World and raised a statue to Popeye in 1937.

Use

Spinach anchors dishes across its whole range: Indian saag and palak paneer, Greek spanakopita, Persian ash reshteh, French épinards à la crème, and the "Florentine" garnish of eggs and fish — said to honour Catherine de' Medici's Florentine cooks, a story more repeated than proven. Baby-leaf spinach built the modern bagged-salad trade; frozen spinach, pioneered by the Birds Eye quick-freezing industry, fills quiches and dips. Few greens are as versatile or as quick to cook: spinach is done almost as soon as it wilts.
