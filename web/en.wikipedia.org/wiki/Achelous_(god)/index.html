Achelous (Ancient Greek: Ἀχελώιος Akhelṓios) was a river god in Greek mythology, the deity of the Achelous River, the largest river in Greece, which flows through Aetolia into the Ionian Sea. He was considered the eldest and chief of the three thousand river gods, the sons of the Titans Oceanus and Tethys. As a water deity he was a shapeshifter, able to assume the forms of a bull, a serpent, or a man with a bull's head.

Wrestling with Heracles

Achelous is best known for his contest with the hero Heracles for the hand of Deianira, daughter of King Oeneus of Calydon. During the wrestling match Achelous transformed first into a serpent and then into a bull; Heracles broke off one of his horns and defeated him. The broken horn, filled with fruits and flowers by the Naiads, became the cornucopia, the horn of plenty. Achelous recovered his horn by trading for it the horn of the goat Amalthea, according to some accounts. The story is told in Sophocles' Trachiniae and at length in Ovid's Metamorphoses.

Offspring and myths

By the Muse Melpomene or Terpsichore, Achelous was said to be the father of the Sirens. He was also father of the spring nymphs called the Acheloides and, in some traditions, of the fountain nymphs of Dirce and Castalia. When the nymphs neglected to sacrifice to him, Achelous swept their islands, the Echinades, out to sea.

Cult and iconography

Achelous was widely worshipped throughout the Greek world, and his name was sometimes used generically for fresh water in poetry and religious language. The oracle of Zeus at Dodona reportedly instructed petitioners to sacrifice to Achelous. In art he appears as a bull with a man's bearded face, a type copied for many lesser river gods across Greek colonies in Italy and Sicily, where his image appears on coins of numerous cities.
