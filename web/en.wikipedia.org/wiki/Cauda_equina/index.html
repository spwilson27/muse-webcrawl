Cauda equina

The cauda equina (Latin for "horse's tail") is a bundle of spinal nerves and nerve rootlets consisting of the second through fifth lumbar nerve pairs, the first through fifth sacral nerve pairs, and the coccygeal nerve, all of which arise from the lumbar enlargement and the conus medullaris of the spinal cord.

Anatomy

The spinal cord ends around the first or second lumbar vertebra, but the nerve roots continue downward within the lumbar cistern of cerebrospinal fluid before exiting the vertebral canal at their respective levels. This leash of roots below the conus medullaris resembles a horse's tail, from which it takes its name. Because the nerves float in cerebrospinal fluid, a lumbar puncture can be performed safely below the end of the cord, typically at the L3–L4 or L4–L5 level, with the roots pushed aside by the needle.

Clinical significance

Compression of the cauda equina, most often by a large central lumbar disc herniation, tumour, infection or trauma, produces cauda equina syndrome: a surgical emergency characterised by low back pain, saddle anaesthesia, bilateral sciatica, lower-limb weakness and bladder or bowel dysfunction. Delayed decompression can result in permanent neurological damage, so urgent MRI and neurosurgical referral are indicated.
