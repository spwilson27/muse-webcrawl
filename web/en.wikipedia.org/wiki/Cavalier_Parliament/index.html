The Cavalier Parliament of England was the first Parliament of Charles II, sitting from May 1661 until January 1679 — a period of nearly eighteen years that makes it the longest-lasting English Parliament. Dominated by royalist Anglican landowners ("Cavaliers"), it consolidated the Restoration settlement after the Civil War and Interregnum.

Character and early legislation

Elected amid a royalist reaction, the Parliament was more royalist than the king. It enacted the so-called Clarendon Code — the Corporation Act (1661), Act of Uniformity (1662), Conventicle Act (1664) and Five Mile Act (1665) — which re-established the Church of England and penalised Nonconformists. It also passed the Licensing of the Press Act 1662, settled the king's revenue, and abolished feudal tenures, confirming the work of the Convention Parliament.

Later years

The Parliament survived the Second and Third Anglo-Dutch Wars, the Great Plague and the Great Fire of London, impeaching the Earl of Clarendon in 1667. In the 1670s it became the arena for the emerging Court and Country parties, the Test Acts against Catholics (1673, 1678), and the fabricated Popish Plot crisis. Charles dissolved it in January 1679 in an attempt to protect his brother James from exclusion from the succession, and new elections produced the Habeas Corpus Parliament.

Legacy

The Cavalier Parliament's longevity and its shift from royalist zeal to opposition helped shape the modern party system and Parliament's role as a check on the Crown in the later Stuart period.
