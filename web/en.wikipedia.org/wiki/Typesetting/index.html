Typesetting

Typesetting is the composition of text by arranging physical type (in traditional printing) or its digital equivalents. A person who sets type is a typesetter or compositor. Historically the craft defined the appearance of every printed book and newspaper; in the digital era it is performed with software that positions glyphs from a typeface according to rules of spacing, alignment and hyphenation.

Typesetting
Era15th century – present
Key technologiesHand composition, Linotype, phototypesetting, desktop publishing

History

From Gutenberg's time until the nineteenth century, compositors picked individual metal sorts from type cases and arranged them, mirror-reversed, in a composing stick, justifying lines with spaces before locking them into a chase. The mechanisation of the 1880s — Ottmar Mergenthaler's Linotype, which cast whole lines of type from a keyboard, and the Monotype system — transformed newspaper production. Phototypesetting replaced metal with film in the mid-twentieth century, and desktop publishing software of the 1980s (PageMaker, QuarkXPress, later InDesign, and Donald Knuth's TeX for scientific work) put typesetting on personal computers.

Principles

Typesetting concerns itself with the choice and size of typeface, line length and leading (line spacing), kerning and letter-spacing, justification or ragged-right setting, hyphenation and the avoidance of widows and orphans — all in the service of legibility and an even texture of type on the page. Modern digital typesetting also handles complex scripts, OpenType features and automated pagination.
