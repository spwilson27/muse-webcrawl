Megalithic tomb

A megalithic tomb is a prehistoric burial chamber built of large stones (megaliths), usually during the Neolithic and early Bronze Age. Megalithic tombs are found across Europe, Asia, Africa, and elsewhere, and represent some of the earliest surviving monumental architecture built by farming communities.

Types

  • Dolmen: a simple chamber of upright stones supporting one or more capstones, originally often covered by an earthen mound.
  • Passage grave: a chamber reached by a stone-lined passage, covered by a round mound or cairn; examples include Newgrange in Ireland and Maeshowe in Orkney.
  • Gallery grave: an elongated chamber, sometimes with a portico or transepts, as in the allées couvertes of France.
  • Court tomb: an Irish type with an open forecourt before the burial gallery.
  • Portal tomb: tall portal stones forming an entrance, common in Ireland and Wales.
  • Wedge tomb: a later Irish type, narrower and lower at the back.

Distribution and date

In Europe, megalithic tombs cluster along the Atlantic façade — Iberia, Brittany, Britain, Ireland, Scandinavia, and northern Germany — dating broadly from about 4500 to 2000 BCE, though megalith-building burial traditions also developed independently in the Caucasus, the Levant, India, Korea (with thousands of dolmens), and elsewhere.

Use

Most megalithic tombs were collective burial places used over generations, containing disarticulated or cremated remains of many individuals, together with pottery, tools, and ornaments. Their construction required substantial communal labor, and many appear to have served as territorial markers and ritual centers as well as tombs. Some, such as Newgrange, are aligned on astronomical events like the winter solstice sunrise.
