Fenske equation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Fenske equation in continuous fractional distillation is an equation used for calculating the minimum number of theoretical plates required for the separation of a binary feed stream by a fractionation column that is being operated at total reflux (i.e., no overhead distillate product is withdrawn from the column). It was derived by Merrell R. Fenske in 1932, then a professor of chemical engineering at Pennsylvania State University.

The equation

A commonly used form of the Fenske equation, written for a binary mixture, is:

Nmin = log[ (xd / (1 − xd)) · ((1 − xb) / xb) ] / log αavg

where:

  • Nmin is the minimum number of theoretical plates (equilibrium stages), including the reboiler;
  • xd is the mole fraction of the more volatile component in the overhead distillate;
  • xb is the mole fraction of the more volatile component in the bottoms;
  • αavg is the average relative volatility of the more volatile component to the less volatile component throughout the column.

The average relative volatility is often taken as the geometric mean of the relative volatilities at the top and bottom of the column: αavg = √(αtop · αbottom).

Use in design

The Fenske equation provides one of the three pillars of the classic Fenske–Underwood–Gilliland (FUG) shortcut design method for distillation columns. Fenske gives the minimum number of stages at total reflux; the Underwood equations give the minimum reflux ratio; and the Gilliland correlation (or the more modern Kirkbride equation) links these limits to estimate the number of stages required at a finite, economically practical reflux ratio. Shortcut methods remain widely used for preliminary design and for checking rigorous simulation results.

Versions of the equation exist for multicomponent mixtures, in which the split of a pair of "key" components (the light key and heavy key) is specified and the distribution of other components between distillate and bottoms is estimated from their relative volatilities.

Assumptions

The derivation assumes constant molal overflow and a relative volatility that can be represented by a single average value. It applies strictly at total reflux, a condition approached in practice during column start-up but never sustained in production, since no product is withdrawn.
