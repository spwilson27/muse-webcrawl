Provinces of Spain

Spain is divided into fifty provinces (Spanish: provincias), which form the second level of its territorial organization, below the seventeen autonomous communities and above the municipalities. The provincial system dates mainly from the territorial division of 1833 designed by Javier de Burgos, which remains, with minor changes, the basis of Spain's provinces today.

Government

Each province is governed by a provincial council (diputación provincial), except in the single-province autonomous communities (such as Madrid, Asturias and Cantabria), where the regional government exercises provincial functions, and in the island provinces, where island councils (cabildos and consells) govern. Provinces are used as electoral districts for the Congress of Deputies and as the framework for postal codes, telephone codes and much statistical reporting.

History

The 1833 division created 49 provinces, most named after their capital cities. Subsequent changes included the division of the Canary Islands into two provinces (Las Palmas and Santa Cruz de Tenerife) in 1927, bringing the total to 50. The 1978 Constitution retained the provinces while superimposing the autonomous communities.

Characteristics

The most populous provinces are Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia and Alicante; the largest by area is Badajoz. Island provinces are the Balearic Islands, Las Palmas and Santa Cruz de Tenerife. Several provinces share their name with their capital, while others, such as Gipuzkoa, Araba/Álava and Bizkaia in the Basque Country, carry distinct traditional names.
