Torsion spring

A mousetrap's torsion spring
TypeSpring
Working principleElastic resistance to twisting (torque)
ExamplesClothespins, mousetraps, garage doors, torsion bar suspension

A torsion spring is a spring that works by twisting its end along its axis; that is, a flexible elastic object that stores mechanical energy when it is twisted. When it is twisted, it exerts a torque in the opposite direction, proportional to the angle of twist. The spring's torque is proportional to the amount (angle) it is twisted, in accordance with a rotational form of Hooke's law.

Types

Helical torsion springs

The most familiar form is the coil (helical) torsion spring, wound from wire, with two legs that are pushed apart or together. Examples include the spring in a clothespin, a mousetrap, a clipboard clip, and the large counterbalance springs of sectional garage doors.

Torsion bars

A torsion bar is a straight bar of metal or rubber that is subjected to twisting about its axis by torque applied at its ends. Torsion bar suspensions were widely used in automobiles — famously in the original Volkswagen Beetle and many post-war cars — and in armoured vehicles. The longer and thinner the bar, the softer the spring rate.

Torsion fibres and pendulums

Very fine torsion fibres, in which a mass hangs from a thin wire or quartz fibre, are extremely sensitive torque sensors. They were used in the Cavendish experiment to measure the gravitational constant, in early galvanometers, and in the torsion pendulum of clocks and balances.

Physics

For a torsion spring, torque τ = −κθ, where θ is the twist angle and κ is the torsion spring constant (torsional stiffness), with units of torque per unit angle. The stored potential energy is U = ½κθ². In a helical torsion spring the wire itself is primarily stressed in bending rather than torsion — a common point of confusion given the name.

Applications

  • Clothespins, mousetraps, and spring-loaded hinges and lids
  • Garage door counterbalance systems
  • Vehicle suspensions (torsion bars)
  • Watches and clocks (balance springs are spiral torsion springs)
  • Precision instruments and torsion balances
