Price skimming

Price skimming is a pricing strategy in which a firm launches a product at a high price to capture the maximum revenue from the segment of customers willing to pay most, then progressively lowers the price to "skim" successive, more price-sensitive layers of the market — like skimming cream from milk. It is the temporal counterpart of price discrimination and the opposite of penetration pricing, where a low launch price aims for rapid market share.

Conditions for success

Skimming works best when the product is new and distinctive, early adopters are relatively price-insensitive, demand is uncertain, and the firm needs to recover heavy research and development costs quickly. High launch prices can also signal quality and exclusivity for prestige goods. The strategy requires that competitors cannot quickly enter with cheaper substitutes — through patents, brand strength or production complexity — because conspicuously high margins invite imitation.

Examples

Consumer electronics is the classic field: manufacturers launch smartphones, games consoles and televisions at premium prices and cut them over the product's life. Publishers traditionally skim by releasing hardback editions before cheaper paperbacks; film studios sequence cinema, premium streaming and standard release windows at descending prices; pharmaceutical firms price new patented drugs highly before generic competition arrives at patent expiry.

Advantages and risks

Skimming maximises early revenue, funds further development, and lets a firm test demand at the top of the market before committing to mass volumes. Its risks include alienating later customers who learn the pattern and delay purchases, leaving room for low-price rivals to establish themselves, and provoking public or regulatory backlash where the product is a necessity — as debates over launch prices for new medicines illustrate. The optimal speed of price reductions balances the margin earned on each layer against the danger of losing the next layer to competitors.
