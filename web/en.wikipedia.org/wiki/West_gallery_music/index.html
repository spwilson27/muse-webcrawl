West gallery music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

West gallery music, also called Georgian church music or "gallery psalmody", is the sacred music sung and played in English parish churches — mostly rural ones — during the 18th and early 19th centuries. Because the musicians were seated in a wooden gallery at the west end of the church, their repertoire took its name from that location.

Practice

At a time when most churches had no organ, services were accompanied by a choir of local singers supported by a small band of instruments: fiddles, cello, clarinet, flute, bassoon and serpent. Their repertoire centred on metrical psalms and hymn tunes, many written by local composers and circulated in manuscript tunebooks, along with lively "fuguing tunes" in which the voices enter in imitation, and verse anthems for choir and soloists. Some tunes were sung at Christmas by travelling groups of carollers — a tradition echoed by the village choir in Thomas Hardy's Under the Greenwood Tree.

Decline and revival

From the 1840s, reformers influenced by the Oxford Movement regarded gallery music as unruly and irreverent; choirs were moved to the chancel in surplices and organs replaced the bands. The tradition survived longest in a few places — notably in the Sacred Harp shape-note singing of the American South, which descends from related sources. Since the late 20th century, West gallery music has been revived by specialist choirs and societies in England.
