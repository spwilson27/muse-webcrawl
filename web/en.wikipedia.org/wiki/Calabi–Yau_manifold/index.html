Calabi–Yau manifold

A Calabi–Yau manifold, also known as a Calabi–Yau space, is a particular type of manifold in algebraic and differential geometry. In its most common formulation it is a compact Kähler manifold with a trivial canonical bundle and vanishing first Chern class; by Yau's proof of the Calabi conjecture, such a manifold admits a Ricci-flat Kähler metric. Calabi–Yau manifolds are named after Eugenio Calabi, who conjectured the existence of such metrics in the 1950s, and Shing-Tung Yau, who proved the conjecture in 1977–78.

Definitions and properties

Equivalent characterisations include a compact complex manifold with holonomy contained in SU(n), and the existence of a nowhere-vanishing holomorphic n-form. The simplest examples occur in low dimensions: in complex dimension one, a Calabi–Yau manifold is an elliptic curve (torus); in complex dimension two, it is a K3 surface. In complex dimension three, thousands of examples are known, including the quintic threefold in complex projective 4-space, and complete intersection Calabi–Yau manifolds more generally. Their topology is studied through Hodge numbers, arranged in the Hodge diamond; for threefolds, mirror symmetry is reflected in the exchange of the Hodge numbers h1,1 and h2,1.

Applications in physics

Calabi–Yau manifolds acquired major importance in theoretical physics through string theory. In superstring compactifications proposed in 1985 by Candelas, Horowitz, Strominger and Witten, the ten-dimensional spacetime of superstring theory splits into four-dimensional Minkowski space times a tiny six-dimensional (complex three-dimensional) Calabi–Yau manifold, whose shape determines the particle spectrum and couplings of the resulting four-dimensional physics. Mirror symmetry — the pairing of distinct Calabi–Yau manifolds giving equivalent physics — led to striking predictions in enumerative geometry, including counts of rational curves on the quintic threefold.
