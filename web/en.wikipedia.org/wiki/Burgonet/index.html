Burgonet

A burgonet with peak, comb and cheek pieces

The burgonet is a type of open-faced helmet of the Renaissance period, widely used by infantry and light cavalry in the 16th and early 17th centuries. It is characterised by a skull usually rising to a comb or point, a projecting peak or brim over the eyes, hinged cheek pieces that fasten under the chin, and often a flared tail protecting the neck.

Development

The burgonet developed in the early 16th century, its name deriving from Burgundy. It evolved from the sallet and kettle helm and was influenced by contemporary fashionable caps: the peak imitated civilian headwear while giving shade and deflecting downward blows. German and Italian armouries — in Nuremberg, Augsburg, Milan and Greenwich — produced examples ranging from plain munitions pieces to lavishly etched and gilded parade helmets for princes.

Use

The burgonet offered a good balance of protection, visibility and breathability, and was adopted across Europe: by Landsknechts, Spanish tercios, English troops in Ireland, and the horsemen of the Anglo-Scottish borders. Some versions, such as the Savoyard burgonet or "totenkopf", added a grotesque face defence. The type declined in the mid-17th century as firearms made heavy armour less worthwhile, surviving longest in the lighter "spider" and zischägge forms in Eastern Europe.
