A medium tank is a classification of tanks, particularly prevalent during World War II, which represented a compromise of firepower, mobility, and protection. A medium tank's classification is not actually based on weight, but on tactical usage and intended purpose; for instance, Germany's later medium tank, the Panther, had a mass similar to contemporary Allied heavy tanks.

The medium tank class was designed to form the bulk of an army's armoured forces, combining sufficient armour and armament to engage enemy armour and support infantry, with enough mobility to exploit breakthroughs. The concept emerged in the interwar period as armies distinguished between slow, heavily armoured infantry tanks and fast, lightly armed cruiser or cavalry tanks.

History

The first tanks to carry the "medium" designation appeared in British service during and just after the First World War, including the Medium Mark A Whippet. In the interwar years, tanks such as the Soviet BT series and the German Panzer III and IV developed the concept. During World War II, medium tanks like the Soviet T-34 and the American M4 Sherman were produced in enormous numbers and proved to be the war's most influential armoured fighting vehicles.

Role

The medium tank filled the role of the general-purpose main armoured vehicle: mobile enough for exploitation, armed well enough for anti-tank combat, and cheap enough to build in quantity. After the Second World War, the medium tank's role was absorbed by the universal or main battle tank, which combined the mobility of the medium with the firepower and protection of the heavy tank.

Examples

  • M4 Sherman (United States)
  • Panzer IV and Panther (Germany)
  • Cromwell and Comet (United Kingdom)
