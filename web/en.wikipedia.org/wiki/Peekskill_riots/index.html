Peekskill riots

The Peekskill riots were two episodes of anti-communist and racist violence near Peekskill, New York, in the late summer of 1949, surrounding outdoor concerts headlined by the singer and activist Paul Robeson. The riots are remembered as emblematic events of the early Cold War Red Scare and of the suppression of the American left.

Background

Robeson, blacklisted for his civil-rights activism and sympathy for the Soviet Union, had been quoted in 1949 (he said inaccurately reported) to the effect that Black Americans would not fight in a war against the USSR. A benefit concert for the Civil Rights Congress was scheduled for 27 August 1949 in Peekskill, a summer resort area with Jewish and left-wing holiday colonies. Local veterans' organisations announced a protest parade intended to stop the concert.

The riots

On 27 August, before Robeson arrived, a mob burned crosses, attacked concert organisers with stones and clubs, and burned chairs and songbooks; thirteen people were injured and the concert was cancelled. Robeson rescheduled for 4 September at a nearby golf course, guarded by some 2,500 union members and volunteers. The concert itself — attended by about 25,000 people, with performers including Pete Seeger — passed peacefully, but afterwards attackers lined the roads out of the area and stoned buses and cars for miles; more than 140 people were injured. Woody Guthrie and Lee Hays composed the song "Hold the Line" about the defence of the concert.

Aftermath

Westchester County officials blamed the victims, and a grand jury report largely endorsed the rioters, a finding challenged in the pamphlet Eyewitness at Peekskill edited by Howard Fast, himself a participant. No attackers were convicted. The riots intensified the climate of anti-communist repression: Robeson's passport was revoked the following year, and his career in the United States was destroyed.
