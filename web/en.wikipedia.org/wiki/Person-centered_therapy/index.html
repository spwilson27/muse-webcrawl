Person-centered therapy, also known as person-centered psychotherapy, person-centered counselling, client-centered therapy and Rogerian psychotherapy, is a form of psychotherapy developed by psychologist Carl Rogers beginning in the 1940s and extending into the 1980s. Person-centered therapy seeks to facilitate a client's self-actualizing tendency — "an inbuilt proclivity toward growth and fulfilment" — via acceptance (unconditional positive regard), therapist empathy and genuineness. Its proponents consider it, along with psychoanalysis and cognitive behavioural therapy, one of the major schools of psychotherapy, and it strongly influenced counselling practice and the wider culture of listening skills.

The core conditions

In a landmark 1957 paper Rogers proposed that six conditions are necessary and sufficient for therapeutic personality change, of which three therapist-provided attitudes are the practical heart of the approach:

  • Unconditional positive regard — the therapist accepts and values the client without judgement or conditions.
  • Empathic understanding — the therapist accurately senses the client's private world "as if" it were their own, and communicates that understanding.
  • Congruence (genuineness) — the therapist is authentic and transparent rather than hiding behind a professional façade.

The remaining conditions require psychological contact, a client who is in a state of incongruence, and that the client perceives the therapist's regard and empathy at least to a minimal degree.

Theory

Rogers held that people develop distress when "conditions of worth" imposed by others distort the self-concept away from the person's own organismic experience, producing incongruence. In a relationship free of threat, the client can reintegrate disowned experiences and move towards becoming a "fully functioning person". The therapist is deliberately non-directive: rather than interpreting, diagnosing or advising, they reflect and clarify the client's feelings, trusting the client's actualising tendency to direct growth.

History and influence

Rogers published his system in Counseling and Psychotherapy (1942) and Client-Centered Therapy (1951), and summarised his wider view in On Becoming a Person (1961). He pioneered the empirical study of therapy, recording sessions and testing hypotheses about outcomes. The approach influenced education (student-centred learning), conflict resolution — Rogers facilitated encounter groups between divided communities — and later developments such as motivational interviewing and emotion-focused therapy. In later life Rogers renamed the approach "person-centered" as he applied it beyond the therapy room.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether the core conditions are truly sufficient for all disorders, the approach's limited structure for clients wanting guidance, and its cultural assumptions about the autonomous self. Meta-analyses nevertheless find person-centered and other humanistic therapies comparably effective to other bona fide therapies for many common difficulties.
