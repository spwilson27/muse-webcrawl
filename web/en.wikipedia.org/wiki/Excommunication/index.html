Excommunication

[Image: Medieval ceremony of excommunication with bell, book and candle, by which the sentence was solemnly pronounced]

Excommunication is an institutional act of religious censure used to deprive, suspend or limit the membership rights of a person within a religious community. In its classic Roman Catholic form it excludes the offender from receiving the sacraments and from communion with the Church, and in medieval society carried serious civil disabilities, since excommunicates could be shunned, lose legal standing and — if rulers — release their subjects from obedience.

Catholic canon law

Under the 1983 Code of Canon Law, excommunication is a medicinal penalty intended to bring about repentance. It may be imposed by a sentence of an ecclesiastical court (ferendae sententiae) or incurred automatically (latae sententiae) for specified grave offences, including apostasy, heresy, schism, desecration of the Eucharist, violence against the pope, and procuring a completed abortion. The excommunicated are barred from celebrating or receiving the sacraments and from holding church office, but are not expelled from the Church and remain bound by its obligations; the censure can be lifted through absolution, often reserved to higher authority.

Historical significance

Excommunication was one of the papacy's principal weapons in its contests with secular rulers. Pope Gregory VII excommunicated and deposed the Emperor Henry IV in 1076, precipitating Henry's penitential journey to Canossa; Innocent III placed England under interdict and excommunicated King John; Martin Luther was excommunicated by the bull Decet Romanum Pontificem in 1521, and Elizabeth I by Regnans in Excelsis in 1570. The penalty's force declined as states grew stronger and religious pluralism spread, and rulers learned to withstand or ignore it.

Other traditions

Analogous practices exist elsewhere: Orthodox churches may exclude offenders from communion; many Protestant bodies practise church discipline up to removal from membership; Anabaptist communities historically practised the ban and shunning, as do some Amish groups today; the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints used "excommunication" (renamed "withdrawal of membership" in 2019) as its most serious sanction. In Judaism, the herem was a ban of exclusion, famously imposed on Baruch Spinoza by the Amsterdam Portuguese congregation in 1656.
