Secretary of State for the Colonies

The Secretary of State for the Colonies or Colonial Secretary was the British Cabinet minister in charge of managing the United Kingdom's various colonial dependencies. The position was created in 1768 in response to the growing troubles in the American colonies, abolished in 1782 after the loss of the Thirteen Colonies, and revived in 1854 as a separate office from the Secretary of State for War. The office was finally abolished in 1966, when the Colonial Office was merged into the Commonwealth Office.

History

The first Secretary of State for the Colonies was appointed in 1768, when the American Department was created to deal specifically with colonial affairs in North America. The post was held first by Wills Hill, 1st Earl of Hillsborough. Following the American War of Independence, the office was abolished in 1782 and responsibility for the remaining colonies passed to the Home Secretary and later to the Secretary of State for War.

In 1854, as part of the reorganisation of government during the Crimean War, the offices of Secretary of State for War and Secretary of State for the Colonies were separated, with George Grey becoming the first holder of the revived colonial post. The Colonial Secretary was supported by the Colonial Office in Whitehall and by a network of colonial governors.

During the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the Colonial Secretary was one of the most senior positions in the government, overseeing an empire that at its height covered roughly a quarter of the world's land surface. Notable holders included Joseph Chamberlain (1895–1903), who pursued an ambitious programme of colonial development, and Winston Churchill (1921–1922).

Abolition

As decolonisation accelerated after the Second World War, the importance of the office declined. In 1966 the Colonial Office was merged with the Commonwealth Relations Office to form the Commonwealth Office, and the separate post of Secretary of State for the Colonies ceased to exist. The last holder was Frederick Lee. Responsibility for the remaining dependent territories eventually passed to the Foreign and Commonwealth Office.
