The River Forth (Scottish Gaelic: Abhainn Foirthe) is a river in central Scotland. It rises in Loch Ard in the Trossachs, west of Aberfoyle, and flows generally eastward through Stirling to the Firth of Forth at Kincardine. Although only about 47 kilometres long, its meandering tidal reach has for centuries been one of the most strategically important waterways in Scottish history: Stirling, at the lowest reliable crossing point, was long described as the key to the kingdom.

Course

From Loch Ard the river, there known as the Duchray Water's outflow, flows east past Aberfoyle and through the flat carse lands, where it is joined by the River Teith near Drip. It winds in broad loops — the Links of Forth — past Cambuskenneth Abbey to Stirling, below whose castle rock the Old Bridge of 1507 once formed the principal crossing. Below Stirling the river becomes tidal, passing Fallin, Alloa and Kincardine before widening into the Firth of Forth, whose great crossings at Queensferry include the Forth Bridge (1890), the Forth Road Bridge (1964) and the Queensferry Crossing (2017).

History

Control of the Forth crossing decided several of Scotland's most famous battles. At the Battle of Stirling Bridge in 1297, William Wallace and Andrew de Moray defeated an English army as it crossed the narrow bridge over the river. In 1314 the Battle of Bannockburn was fought near its tributary the Bannock Burn. The river also bounded the effective reach of Roman power; the Antonine Wall ran westward from its firth.

Ecology and use

The inner firth's mudflats are an important habitat for wintering wildfowl and waders. The river once supported salmon and sea-trout fisheries and the heavy industry of the Forth Valley — shipbuilding, coal and the petrochemical complex at Grangemouth — though water quality has improved markedly since the late twentieth century. The port of Grangemouth, on the firth, remains Scotland's largest container port.
