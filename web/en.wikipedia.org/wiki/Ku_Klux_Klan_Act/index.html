Ku Klux Klan Act

The Enforcement Act of 1871, also known as the Ku Klux Klan Act, is an Act of the United States Congress that empowered the President to intervene and suspend the writ of habeas corpus to combat the Ku Klux Klan and other white supremacy organizations during Reconstruction. It is formally titled "An act to enforce the Provisions of the Fourteenth Amendment to the Constitution of the United States, and for other Purposes".

Legislation details Enacted: 20 April 1871
Signed by: President Ulysses S. Grant
Citation: 17 Stat. 13
Codification: 42 U.S.C. § 1983 (in part)

Provisions

The Act made persons acting under colour of state law liable in federal court for depriving others of their constitutional rights — the ancestor of the modern civil rights statute 42 U.S.C. § 1983. It criminalized conspiracies to obstruct the execution of federal law or to intimidate voters, officeholders and jurors, authorized the President to deploy federal troops, and in Section 3 permitted the suspension of habeas corpus in areas deemed to be in a state of insurrection.

Enforcement

President Grant used the Act vigorously in South Carolina in 1871, suspending habeas corpus in nine counties and overseeing hundreds of prosecutions that effectively crushed Klan violence there. Federal prosecutions under the Act largely ceased after Reconstruction ended in 1877.

Modern use

The civil action created by the Act, now 42 U.S.C. § 1983, was revived by the Supreme Court in Monroe v. Pape (1961) and is today the principal vehicle for civil rights litigation against state and local officials. Section 1985(3), derived from the Act's conspiracy provisions, was applied in Griffin v. Breckenridge (1971).
