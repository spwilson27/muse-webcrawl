Polycythemia

Polycythemia (also spelled polycythaemia) is a laboratory finding in which the haematocrit (the volume percentage of red blood cells in the blood) and/or the haemoglobin concentration are increased in the peripheral blood. It reflects either a true increase in red cell mass (absolute polycythemia) or a reduction in plasma volume (relative polycythemia). The closely related term erythrocytosis is often used interchangeably, though strictly it refers to the increased red cell count itself.

Classification and causes

Primary polycythemia

The main primary form is polycythemia vera (PV), a myeloproliferative neoplasm in which the bone marrow produces red cells autonomously, almost always driven by an acquired mutation in the JAK2 gene (JAK2 V617F, found in about 95% of cases, or exon 12 mutations). Serum erythropoietin (Epo) levels are low in PV. Rare congenital primary forms arise from mutations in the erythropoietin receptor.

Secondary polycythemia

Secondary polycythemia results from increased erythropoietin, either appropriately — as a response to chronic hypoxia from living at high altitude, chronic lung disease, cyanotic heart disease, sleep apnoea, or heavy smoking (carboxyhaemoglobin) — or inappropriately, from Epo-secreting tumours (e.g., renal cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, cerebellar haemangioblastoma) or exogenous Epo/androgen use, including blood doping in sport.

Relative polycythemia

Relative (apparent) polycythemia occurs when plasma volume falls — through dehydration, diuretics, or the chronic "stress" (Gaisböck) pattern associated with hypertension, obesity, smoking, and alcohol — without a true rise in red cell mass.

Signs and symptoms

Many cases are found incidentally on a blood count. Symptoms of hyperviscosity include headache, dizziness, visual disturbance, tinnitus, and a ruddy complexion. Polycythemia vera characteristically causes aquagenic pruritus (itching after hot showers), erythromelalgia (burning red extremities), splenomegaly, gout, and an increased risk of arterial and venous thrombosis and bleeding.

Diagnosis and treatment

Evaluation includes blood counts, JAK2 mutation testing, serum Epo, oxygen saturation, and sometimes red cell mass measurement and bone marrow examination, applying WHO diagnostic criteria. Treatment of PV centres on keeping haematocrit below 45% by venesection (phlebotomy), low-dose aspirin, and cytoreductive therapy (e.g., hydroxycarbamide, interferon, or the JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib) for higher-risk patients. Secondary forms are managed by treating the underlying cause.
