Hundred Flowers Campaign


"Let a hundred flowers bloom" — a poster of the campaign.

The Hundred Flowers Campaign (Chinese: 百花齐放、百家争鸣; pinyin: Bǎihuā Qífàng, Bǎijiā Zhēngmíng) was a period in 1956 in the People's Republic of China during which the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), led by Mao Zedong, encouraged citizens to express freely their opinions of the communist regime. The campaign's name derives from Mao's slogan: "Let a hundred flowers bloom; let a hundred schools of thought contend."

Course of the campaign

After initial hesitation, intellectuals, students, and party members voiced increasingly sharp criticism of the regime in the spring of 1957 — of bureaucracy, corruption, censorship, and the privileged status of cadres. The unexpectedly sweeping response alarmed the leadership.

Aftermath

In June–July 1957 Mao reversed course and launched the Anti-Rightist Campaign. Hundreds of thousands of people (estimates range from 300,000 to over 500,000) who had spoken out were labeled "rightists" and subjected to persecution, imprisonment, labor camps, or exile; many were not rehabilitated until after Mao's death. Historians debate whether the campaign was a sincere attempt at liberalization that went further than intended, or a deliberate trap to expose critics.
