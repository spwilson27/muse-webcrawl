Divergent series

The partial sums of a divergent series do not settle at a limit.

In mathematics, a divergent series is an infinite series that is not convergent, meaning that the sequence of partial sums of the series does not have a finite limit. If a series converges, the individual terms must approach zero; the converse is not true, as shown by the harmonic series:

1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + … = ∞

whose terms tend to zero yet whose sum grows without bound.

Summability methods

Although divergent series have no sum in the ordinary sense, mathematicians have developed summability methods that assign values to certain divergent series in a consistent way. Cesàro summation, Abel summation, Borel summation and Ramanujan summation can each assign finite values to series such as Grandi's series 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + …, which Cesàro summation values at 1/2. The famous assignment 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + … = −1/12 arises from analytic continuation of the Riemann zeta function rather than from ordinary summation.

History

Divergent series were used freely by mathematicians such as Leibniz and Euler, who obtained correct results by manipulating them. Nineteenth-century analysis placed convergence on a rigorous footing and treated divergent series with suspicion, before twentieth-century work on summability rehabilitated them as useful tools in number theory, physics and asymptotic analysis.
