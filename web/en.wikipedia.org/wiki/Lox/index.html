Lox

Lox served on a bagel with cream cheese
TypeCured salmon
Main ingredientSalmon belly

Lox is a fillet of brined salmon. The term derives from the Yiddish word for salmon, laks, cognate with the German Lachs. Traditionally, lox is made from the belly of the salmon, which is cured in salt brine but not smoked. It is a staple of Ashkenazi Jewish-American cuisine, most famously served on a bagel with cream cheese, often accompanied by tomato, sliced red onion, and capers.

Types

Several related products are commonly sold as lox. Regular lox is brined only. Nova lox (or Nova Scotia lox) is first brined and then lightly cold-smoked, giving it a milder flavour. Gravlax, the Scandinavian preparation, is cured with salt, sugar, and dill rather than plain brine. Although lox is sometimes confused with smoked salmon, true lox itself is never smoked.

History

Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe brought lox to the United States in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, where barrels of salted salmon shipped from the Pacific Northwest and Nova Scotia supplied New York delicatessens and "appetizing stores." Paired with the bagel and cream cheese — another immigrant food that became widespread in the same period — lox became an iconic item of New York Jewish food culture and, eventually, of American breakfast and brunch menus nationwide.
