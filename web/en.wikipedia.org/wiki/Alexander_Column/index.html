The Alexander Column (Russian: Александровская колонна) is a monumental triumphal column standing at the centre of Palace Square in Saint Petersburg, Russia. Erected between 1830 and 1834 to a design by Auguste de Montferrand, it commemorates Emperor Alexander I and the Russian victory over Napoleonic France in the Patriotic War of 1812.

Construction

The column's shaft is the largest single-piece granite monolith ever quarried for a modern monument: a single block of red Finnish granite, 25.45 metres (83.5 ft) long and weighing about 600 tonnes, quarried at Virolahti in Finland, shipped by barge to St Petersburg, and raised into place in just 105 minutes on 30 August 1832 using a wooden scaffold, capstans and the labour of some 2,000 soldiers and workmen. Remarkably, the column is held upright by gravity alone, with no fixings to its pedestal.

The square granite pedestal is decorated with bronze bas-reliefs of military trophies and allegories of the victory, executed by Russian and French sculptors. The column is crowned by a statue of an angel — said to bear the features of Alexander I — trampling a serpent and holding a cross, symbolising the triumph of peace over war.

Significance

At 47.5 metres (156 ft) including pedestal and statue, the Alexander Column is the tallest triumphal column of its kind in the world, exceeding both the Vendôme Column in Paris and Trajan's Column in Rome in the height of its monolithic shaft. It remains the focal point of Palace Square and one of the most recognisable landmarks of Saint Petersburg.
