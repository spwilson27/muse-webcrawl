Oxbridge

Universities: University of Oxford; University of Cambridge

Term first recorded: 1849 (satirical usage); popularised 1950s

Shared features: Collegiate structure; tutorial/supervision teaching

Oxbridge is a portmanteau of Oxford and Cambridge, the two oldest universities in the English-speaking world, used to refer to them collectively, especially in contrast to other British universities. The word appears in Thackeray's Pendennis (1849–50) as the name of a fictional university and entered general use in the mid-twentieth century.

Similarities

Both are collegiate universities: students belong to semi-autonomous colleges that provide accommodation, dining and small-group teaching — tutorials at Oxford, supervisions at Cambridge — alongside central lectures and laboratories. Both operate highly selective admissions with subject interviews, shared application restrictions (candidates normally cannot apply to both in the same year), and similar three-term years.

Rivalry

The universities are long-standing rivals. Their annual contests include the Boat Race on the Thames (first held 1829), the Varsity Match in rugby union, and the lighter "Gods" competitions; Cambridge was founded in 1209 by scholars who left Oxford after a dispute — traditionally after the hanging of two clerks by townsmen.

Criticism and access

"Oxbridge" is also used critically, to denote the disproportionate presence of the two universities' graduates in British public life and the social exclusivity associated with them. Both universities have expanded access programmes, and their intakes from state schools have risen to roughly two-thirds of UK entrants in recent years.
