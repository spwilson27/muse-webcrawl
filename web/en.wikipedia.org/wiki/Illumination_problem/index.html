Illumination problem

The illumination problem is a resolved mathematical problem first posed in the early 1950s by Ernst G. Straus. It asks whether a room with mirrored walls can always be fully illuminated by a single point light source, allowing reflections of light off the mirrored walls.

Equivalently, the problem can be restated as: given a polygonal room with mirrored walls and a point source of light, is every point in the room reachable by a ray from the source after some number of reflections? In physical terms it corresponds to placing a billiard ball on a billiard table shaped like the room and asking about the geometry of reflected paths.

Solutions

In 1958, Roger Penrose produced the first counterexample: the Penrose unilluminable room, a curved room constructed from elliptical arcs in which some regions remain dark regardless of where the light source is placed. In 1969, the problem was also solved in the negative for polygonal rooms by a construction involving a 26-sided polygon, later simplified by George Tokarsky in 1995 to a 24-sided orthogonal polygon.

On the positive side, for certain classes of polygons — such as rational polygons whose angles are rational multiples of π — results have been established about the illumination of almost every point, and for a point source placed at particular positions the room can be fully lit.

Related problems

The illumination problem is closely related to the study of mathematical billiards, in which the trajectory of a point mass reflecting off the walls of a region is analysed, and to problems of visibility in computational geometry, such as the art gallery problem.
