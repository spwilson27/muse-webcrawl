Bogie exchange

Bogie exchange is a system for operating railway vehicles across two railways with different track gauges. It involves lifting a railway vehicle (or its body) off its bogies (wheel assemblies, called trucks in North America), replacing them with bogies of the other gauge, and lowering the vehicle back down. Bogie exchange allows through running of freight wagons and passenger coaches without the time and labour of transloading goods or transferring passengers at break-of-gauge stations.

A coach body raised on jacks during bogie exchange

Process

At a bogie exchange facility, the vehicle is uncoupled and its body is lifted by jacks or an overhead crane. The original bogies are rolled away along the track, and replacement bogies of the new gauge are rolled into position, after which the body is lowered and connections — brakes, electrical jumpers, couplers — are re-made. Special arrangements are needed for brake systems, which may differ between railways, and for securing the body to the new bogies. A well-organized facility can change the bogies of a wagon in a matter of minutes to tens of minutes.

Usage

Bogie exchange has been used extensively at break-of-gauge points between the standard-gauge networks of Western and Central Europe and the broad (1,520 mm) gauge of the former Soviet Union — for example at Brest (Belarus/Poland) and at the Slovakia–Ukraine and Romania–Moldova borders — and between China (standard gauge) and Mongolia/Kazakhstan (1,520 mm), notably at Erenhot and Dostyk/Alashankou. In Australia, bogie exchange was historically used between the standard, broad (1,600 mm), and narrow (1,067 mm) gauge systems of different states, for example at Port Pirie and Peterborough in South Australia. Passenger trains between China and Russia/Mongolia, such as the Trans-Mongolian services, undergo bogie exchange at the border, with passengers remaining aboard while the coaches are lifted.

Alternatives

Alternatives to bogie exchange include transloading (moving goods between wagons), carrying complete road vehicles on trains, variable-gauge axles (such as the Spanish Talgo RD and CAF Brava systems, and the Polish SUW 2000), which allow a train to change gauge at low speed through a gauge changer, and dual-gauge track. Variable-gauge systems avoid the lifting operation but are more complex and, for freight, bogie exchange remains common in Eurasian traffic.
