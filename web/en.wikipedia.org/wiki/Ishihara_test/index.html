Ishihara test

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An Ishihara plate of coloured dots forming a number visible to normal colour vision
An Ishihara plate of coloured dots forming a number visible to normal colour vision

The Ishihara test is a colour vision test for red-green colour deficiencies, designed by Dr Shinobu Ishihara and published in 1917. Its plates consist of circles of dots in varied colours and sizes forming numbers or paths visible to people with normal colour vision but invisible (or differently visible) to those with red-green colour blindness. It remains the world's standard screening test for congenital colour-vision deficiency.

Design

The full test has 38 plates: demonstration, transformation (the colour-blind see a different number), vanishing, hidden-digit and tracing designs, exploiting the confusion lines of Protanopia and Deuteranopia. The test screens but cannot grade severity precisely, and it does not detect blue-yellow (tritan) defects; definitive classification uses anomaloscopes.

Use and limits

Ishihara plates gate occupations from train driving to military service and electrical trades, appear in most school health screenings, and are the image laypeople mean by 'colour-blind test'. Prints fade and online versions vary with screens, so formal screening uses controlled editions — and acquired colour defects (from drugs, diabetes, eye disease) demand fuller testing than the little book alone.
