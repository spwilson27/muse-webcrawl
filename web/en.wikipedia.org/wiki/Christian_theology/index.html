Christian theology

Christian theology is the theology — the systematic study of the divine and religion — of Christian belief and practice. It concentrates primarily upon the texts of the Old Testament and the New Testament, as well as on Christian tradition. Christian theologians use biblical exegesis, rational analysis and argument to articulate the doctrines of the faith.

Branches

Systematic theology organises doctrine by topic: theology proper (the doctrine of God), Christology, pneumatology (the Holy Spirit), soteriology (salvation), ecclesiology (the church), and eschatology (last things). Biblical theology studies the theology of the biblical texts themselves; historical theology traces doctrine through the church's history; practical theology addresses preaching, pastoral care and ministry.

Key doctrines

Central to Christian theology is the doctrine of the Trinity — that God is one being in three persons, Father, Son and Holy Spirit — defined at the Councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381). The Incarnation holds that Jesus Christ is both fully God and fully human, a formula defined at Chalcedon (451). Soteriology examines how Christ's death and resurrection accomplish salvation, with theories of atonement including Christus Victor, satisfaction, and penal substitution.

History

Theology developed through the writings of the Church Fathers, the scholastic synthesis of Thomas Aquinas in the Summa Theologiae, the Reformation challenges of Martin Luther and John Calvin, and modern movements including liberal theology, neo-orthodoxy, and liberation theology.
