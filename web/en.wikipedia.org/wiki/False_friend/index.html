False friend

In linguistics, a false friend is a word in a language that bears a deceptive resemblance to a word in another language but signifies something different. That is, a word pair in two languages that seem to be cognates but have different meanings, whether they are genuinely cognate or not. The term is a calque of the French faux-ami, used by Maxime Koessler and Jules Derocquigny in their 1928 book Les faux amis ou les trahisons du vocabulaire anglais ("False friends, or the betrayals of English vocabulary").

Origins

False friends arise in three main ways. Two words may share a common etymology but diverge semantically over time: English actually and French actuellement ("currently") both descend from a word meaning "present," but their meanings shifted apart. Second, chance resemblance may produce homographs with no shared history, such as English gift and German Gift ("poison"). Third, loanwords may be borrowed with a narrower or altered meaning, as with English-derived Smoking in German, which means a dinner jacket or tuxedo.

False cognates and partial false friends

A false friend differs from a false cognate: false friends may be real cognates whose meanings diverged, whereas false cognates only appear related but have entirely separate etymologies. Partial false friends overlap in some senses but not others — for example, Spanish carpeta means "folder" but never "carpet," while French demander means "to ask" but not "to demand" in the forceful English sense.

Significance

False friends are a classic source of error in language learning, translation, and interpreting, and feature prominently in language teaching materials. They also matter in international branding and law, where mistranslations can be costly or embarrassing; professional translators are trained to consult usage rather than rely on surface resemblance.
