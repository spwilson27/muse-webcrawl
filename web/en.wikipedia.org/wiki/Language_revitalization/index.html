Language revitalization

Language revitalization

Language revitalization, also termed language revival or reversing language shift, is an effort to halt or reverse the decline of a language, or to revive an extinct one. Such efforts are undertaken when a language's level of use is declining, when its speaker population is shrinking, or when intergenerational transmission — children learning the language from their parents — has been interrupted. Revitalization programs are carried out by linguists, community activists, educators, and governments, and range from full national policies to grassroots projects in a single village or family.

Of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken in the world today, a large proportion are considered endangered. The UNESCO Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger classifies languages along a scale from "vulnerable" to "extinct". Revitalization seeks to move a language back up that scale, typically by increasing the number of speakers, expanding the domains in which the language is used, and restoring its transmission to children.

Degrees of endangerment

Linguist Joshua Fishman proposed the Graded Intergenerational Disruption Scale (GIDS), an eight-stage model describing how disrupted a language's transmission has become, from Stage 8 (only a few elderly speakers remain, and the language must be reassembled from documentation) to Stage 1 (the language is used in higher education, government, and national media). Fishman argued that Stage 6 — the language being spoken in the home and passed to children — is the crucial threshold: without it, gains in schools or media are unlikely to be sustained. Later scholars, including the authors of the Expanded Graded Intergenerational Disruption Scale (EGIDS), refined the model to thirteen levels.

Methods

Immersion education

The most widely used strategy is immersion schooling, in which children are educated partly or entirely through the target language. Famous models include the Māori kōhanga reo ("language nests") established in New Zealand from 1982, the Hawaiian Pūnana Leo preschools founded from 1984, Basque ikastolak, and Welsh-medium schools. Language nests, in which fluent elders care for young children in the language, have been adopted by many Indigenous communities in North America, Australia, and the Pacific.

Master-apprentice programs

Developed in California by Leanne Hinton and the Advocates for Indigenous California Language Survival, the Master-Apprentice Language Learning Program pairs a fluent elder (the "master") with a younger learner (the "apprentice") for extended sessions of ordinary activities conducted entirely in the language, aiming to produce new conversational speakers where no child transmission remains.

Documentation and technology

Where few speakers survive, linguists work with communities to record, transcribe, and archive the language so that teaching materials can be built from documentation. Modern revitalization also uses dictionaries and grammar apps, online courses, social media, text messaging, and speech technology. The Wikimedia movement supports editions of Wikipedia in hundreds of smaller languages, and community archives such as FirstVoices host recordings and lessons.

Notable cases

Hebrew

The revival of Hebrew is the most famous success. By the late nineteenth century Hebrew had not been a vernacular for roughly two millennia, surviving as a liturgical and literary language. Activists such as Eliezer Ben-Yehuda promoted its everyday use in Ottoman and Mandate Palestine; it became an official language of Israel in 1948 and today has millions of native speakers — the only example of a language with no native speakers being fully revived as a national vernacular, though scholars debate how continuous its tradition was.

Irish, Welsh, Māori, Hawaiian

Irish has been supported by the Irish state since 1922, is compulsory in schools, and retains native-speaking districts (the Gaeltacht), though daily use outside education remains limited. Welsh declined through the twentieth century but has stabilised, aided by Welsh-medium education, the S4C television channel, and the Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011; the Welsh government aims for a million speakers by 2050. Te reo Māori gained official status in 1987 and a growing immersion sector. Hawaiian, reduced to a few thousand native speakers outside Niʻihau by the 1980s, has rebuilt a speaker base through the Pūnana Leo movement and Hawaiian-medium degrees.

Criticism and debate

Revitalization raises questions about which variety of a language should be taught, how "authentic" revived speech must be, and whether resources should prioritise documentation or new speakers. Some linguists caution that revived languages inevitably differ from their ancestral forms, influenced by the dominant languages of their new speakers; community advocates generally regard a living, changed language as preferable to a perfectly documented dead one.
