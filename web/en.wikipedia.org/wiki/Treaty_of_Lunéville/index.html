Treaty of Lunéville

Signed9 February 1801
LocationLunéville, France
SignatoriesFrench Republic, Holy Roman Empire

The Treaty of Lunéville was signed in the town of Lunéville (in Lorraine, France) on 9 February 1801 between the French Republic and the Holy Roman Emperor Francis II, in the name both of himself as ruler of the hereditary Austrian dominions and of the Holy Roman Empire. It ended the War of the Second Coalition phase of the French Revolutionary Wars for Austria.

Background

French victories at Marengo (June 1800) by Napoleon Bonaparte and at Hohenlinden (December 1800) by Jean Moreau forced Austria to sue for peace. Negotiations were conducted by Joseph Bonaparte for France and Count Ludwig von Cobenzl for Austria.

Terms

The treaty largely reconfirmed the Treaty of Campo-Formio of 1797:

  • Austria recognized French possession of the left bank of the Rhine and of Belgium;
  • The French client republics in Italy — the Cisalpine, Ligurian, Helvetic and Batavian republics — were recognized;
  • Austria retained Venice, Istria and the Dalmatian coast as gained at Campo-Formio;
  • The Grand Duke of Tuscany was dispossessed and compensated in Germany, and his grand duchy became the Kingdom of Etruria under a Spanish Bourbon prince;
  • The German princes dispossessed on the left bank of the Rhine were to be compensated within the Empire — a process carried out in the great territorial reorganization (the Reichsdeputationshauptschluss) of 1803.

Significance

Lunéville left Britain as France's only remaining major enemy, a situation resolved briefly by the Treaty of Amiens in 1802. It also set in motion the mediatization and secularization of the small German states, drastically simplifying the map of the Holy Roman Empire shortly before its dissolution in 1806.
