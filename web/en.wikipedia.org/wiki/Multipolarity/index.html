Multipolarity

In international relations, multipolarity is a distribution of power in which more than two nation-states have nearly equal amounts of economic, military, and cultural influence. It contrasts with unipolarity (one dominant power) and bipolarity (two great powers).

Historical examples

The European state system of the 19th century, governed by the Concert of Europe among Britain, France, Russia, Austria, and Prussia, is the classic multipolar order. The interwar period is also often described as multipolar.

Theory

Scholars debate the stability of multipolarity. Classical realists often view multipolar balance-of-power politics as a workable route to stability, while neorealists such as Kenneth Waltz argued bipolar systems are more stable because there is less uncertainty about who threatens whom. Others warn that multipolarity encourages miscalculation and buck-passing among several rivals.

Contemporary debate

Since the end of the Cold War, analysts have debated whether the unipolar moment of United States dominance is giving way to a multipolar world shaped by the rise of China, India, and other regional powers.
