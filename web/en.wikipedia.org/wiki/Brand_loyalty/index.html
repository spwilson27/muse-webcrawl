Brand loyalty

Brand loyalty is the positive association consumers attach to a particular product or brand, demonstrated by their repeat purchases and preference for that brand over competitors, even in the face of price differences or persuasive efforts by rivals. It is a central concept in marketing and consumer behaviour, and is generally considered one of a firm's most valuable intangible assets because loyal customers are cheaper to retain than new customers are to acquire.

Repeat purchasing is the most common behavioural measure of brand loyalty

Dimensions

Researchers commonly distinguish two dimensions of loyalty. Behavioural loyalty is observed repeat purchasing of the same brand over time. Attitudinal loyalty is a psychological commitment to the brand — a preference and intention to repurchase that persists despite situational influences. Combining the two produces a widely used classification: true loyalty (high behaviour and high attitude), latent loyalty (high attitude but low behaviour, often due to cost or availability), spurious loyalty (high repeat purchase but low commitment, e.g. due to habit or lack of alternatives), and no loyalty.

Antecedents

Brand loyalty is generally modelled as an outcome of customer satisfaction, perceived quality, brand trust, and brand image, reinforced by positive consumption experiences. Switching costs — financial, procedural, and emotional — also sustain loyalty. Research by scholars such as Richard Oliver proposed a phased development from cognitive loyalty (based on information) through affective and conative loyalty to action loyalty.

Loyalty programs

Firms attempt to build and reward loyalty through loyalty programs such as frequent-flyer schemes, supermarket loyalty cards, and points-based rewards. Evidence on their effectiveness is mixed: programs can increase purchase frequency and share of wallet, but critics note they may reward behaviour that would have occurred anyway and can devolve into price competition.

Criticism and challenges

Empirical research associated with the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, notably Byron Sharp's How Brands Grow (2010), argues that loyalty metrics largely follow market share (the "double jeopardy" pattern), that most buyers are polygamously loyal to several brands in a category, and that growth comes mainly from increasing penetration rather than deepening loyalty. Digital markets and comparison tools have also been said to weaken loyalty by reducing search and switching costs.
