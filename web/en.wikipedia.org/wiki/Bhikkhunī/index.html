Bhikkhunī

A bhikkhunī (Pali) or bhikṣuṇī (Sanskrit) is a fully ordained female monastic in Buddhism. Male monastics are called bhikkhus. Bhikkhunis live by the Vinaya, the Buddhist monastic code: the Theravada bhikkhunis traditionally observe 311 precepts, while the Dharmaguptaka lineage followed in East Asia has 348.

History

According to tradition, the first bhikkhuni was Mahapajapati Gotami, the Buddha's aunt and foster mother, whose ordination the Buddha granted after the intercession of his attendant Ananda. The order of nuns flourished in ancient India and was carried to Sri Lanka in the 3rd century BCE by Sanghamitta, daughter of the emperor Ashoka.

Survival and revival

The Theravada bhikkhuni lineage died out in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia around the 10th–13th centuries, while the Dharmaguptaka lineage survived in China, Taiwan, Korea and Vietnam. Since the late 20th century there has been a movement — controversial among some traditional authorities — to revive full ordination for women in the Theravada countries, using ordination with the assistance of East Asian bhikkhunis, and new bhikkhuni communities have been established in Sri Lanka, Thailand, and Western countries. In Tibetan Buddhism, where the bhikshuni lineage was never transmitted, the Dalai Lama and others have supported research toward establishing full ordination for nuns.
