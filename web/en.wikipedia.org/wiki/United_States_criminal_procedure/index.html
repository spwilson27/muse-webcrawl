United States criminal procedure

United States criminal procedure comprises the rules governing investigation, arrest, prosecution, trial, sentencing and appeal in federal and state courts. It is shaped principally by the Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments to the Constitution.

Investigation and arrest

The Fourth Amendment prohibits unreasonable searches and seizures and generally requires warrants supported by probable cause. Evidence obtained in violation may be excluded under the exclusionary rule, subject to exceptions such as good faith and inevitable discovery. Interrogation is constrained by Miranda v. Arizona warnings for custodial questioning.

Charging and preliminary proceedings

Federal felonies generally require a grand jury indictment; states may proceed by information. Defendants receive arraignment, counsel under Gideon v. Wainwright, bail determinations, discovery, and pretrial motions. Most cases resolve by plea bargaining.

Trial

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a speedy, public trial by an impartial jury, confrontation of witnesses, compulsory process, and assistance of counsel. The prosecution must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. Jury verdicts in criminal cases must be unanimous following Ramos v. Louisiana.

Sentencing and appeals

Sentencing considers statutes, guidelines, and constitutional limits on cruel and unusual punishment. Convicted defendants may appeal legal errors and seek post-conviction relief, including habeas corpus.
