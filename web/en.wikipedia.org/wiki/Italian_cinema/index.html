Italian cinema

The cinema of Italy has had a major influence on world film since the early 20th century. Italian filmmakers were pioneers of the historical epic in the silent era, created the neorealist movement after the Second World War, and produced internationally celebrated auteurs such as Federico Fellini, Michelangelo Antonioni, Luchino Visconti and Sergio Leone. Production is centred on the Cinecittà studios in Rome, founded in 1937.

History

Early Italian features such as Cabiria (1914) set standards for spectacle. Under fascism the industry was reorganised around Cinecittà and the Venice Film Festival (founded 1932). After 1945, neorealism — Roberto Rossellini's Rome, Open City (1945) and Vittorio De Sica's Bicycle Thieves (1948) — redefined world cinema with location shooting and non-professional actors. The 1950s–1970s brought Fellini's La Dolce Vita (1960) and 8½ (1963), the spaghetti westerns of Sergio Leone, the giallo thrillers of Dario Argento, and popular commedia all'italiana.

Recognition

Italy has won more Academy Awards for Best Foreign Language Film than any other country in the award's early history, with winners including Shoeshine, Yesterday, Today and Tomorrow, Cinema Paradiso (1988) and Life Is Beautiful (1997). Recent internationally acclaimed directors include Paolo Sorrentino (The Great Beauty, 2013), Matteo Garrone and Alice Rohrwacher.
