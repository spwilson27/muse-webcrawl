Rhuddlan Castle

Rhuddlan Castle (Welsh: Castell Rhuddlan) is a ruined medieval castle in Rhuddlan, Denbighshire, Wales. Edward I began it in 1277 during his first Welsh war, and it became one of the major royal fortresses through which England asserted control over north Wales.

Built
1277–1282
Builder
Edward I; work associated with Master James of St George
Design
Concentric castle with a distinctive diamond-shaped inner ward

Construction

Rhuddlan was planned as a concentric castle with strong twin-towered gates and an inner ward laid out as a lozenge rather than the more usual rectangle. To let ships reach it, engineers altered and canalised a stretch of the River Clwyd, an enormous undertaking that made the site accessible from the sea despite being inland.

Role in the conquest of Wales

Rhuddlan served as Edward’s headquarters and administrative centre. In 1284 the Statute of Rhuddlan was issued there, organizing the government of the conquered principality and introducing English criminal law while retaining some Welsh civil customs. The castle withstood tensions during later Welsh revolts but its strategic importance declined as English administration became established.

Later history

During the English Civil War the castle was held for the Royalists and surrendered to Parliament in 1646. It was subsequently slighted to prevent further military use, leaving substantial but roofless walls and towers. The ruin is now protected and managed as a historic monument by Cadw.

Architecture

Rhuddlan is notable for its compact geometry, double-towered gateways and layered defenses. Although less complete than Conwy or Caernarfon, it was among the first great Edwardian castles in Wales and illustrates the combination of fortress, royal residence and supply base supported by river transport.
