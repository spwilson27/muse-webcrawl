Frederick II (24 January 1712 – 17 August 1786) was King of Prussia from 1740 until his death in 1786. Known as Frederick the Great (German: Friedrich der Große), he transformed Prussia into a major European power through brilliant military campaigns and enlightened domestic reforms.

Early life

Frederick's youth was dominated by conflict with his authoritarian father, Frederick William I, who despised his son's interest in music and philosophy. Frederick's attempted flight to England in 1730 ended with the execution of his friend Hans Hermann von Katte before his eyes and his own imprisonment; the trauma marked him for life.

The Silesian Wars and Seven Years' War

Immediately on his accession in 1740, Frederick invaded the rich Austrian province of Silesia, winning and holding it through the Wars of the Austrian Succession. In the Seven Years' War (1756–63) he faced a coalition of Austria, Russia, France and Sweden, winning celebrated victories at Rossbach and Leuthen (1757) but suffering terrible defeats such as Kunersdorf (1759). Prussia survived, exhausted, thanks in part to the death of Tsarina Elizabeth in 1762 — the "Miracle of the House of Brandenburg" — and kept Silesia.

Domestic rule

An "enlightened absolutist", Frederick reformed the judiciary, curtailed torture, promoted religious toleration, encouraged immigration and agriculture (famously championing the potato), and corresponded with Voltaire, whom he hosted at Potsdam. He rebuilt Berlin and Potsdam, where his palace of Sanssouci embodies his taste. He wrote extensively in French, played the flute, and composed music. In 1772 he took part in the First Partition of Poland, gaining West Prussia.

Legacy

Frederick left Prussia greatly enlarged and possessed of one of Europe's finest armies, and he remains one of the most studied military commanders and state-builders of the 18th century.
