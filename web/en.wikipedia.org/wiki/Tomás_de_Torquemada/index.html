Tomás de Torquemada OP (14 October 1420 – 16 September 1498) was a Castilian Dominican friar and the first Grand Inquisitor of the Spanish Inquisition. He is known for his campaign against the unconverted Jews and Muslims of Spain, and for organizing the Inquisition into an efficient, centralized institution. The Spanish Inquisition's activities under his direction led to thousands of deaths; estimates of those executed during his tenure vary, commonly around 2,000.

Early life

Torquemada was born in the town of Torquemada, or possibly Valladolid, in the Crown of Castile, into a family that included conversos (converted Jews) among its ancestors, according to the contemporary chronicler Hernando del Pulgar. He entered the Dominican monastery of Santa Cruz la Real in Segovia, rising to become its prior, and later became confessor to the Infanta Isabella, the future Queen Isabella I of Castile, whose marriage to Ferdinand of Aragon he supported.

Grand Inquisitor

In 1478 Ferdinand and Isabella obtained papal authorization to establish an Inquisition in Castile, ostensibly to police the sincerity of Jewish converts to Christianity. In 1483 Torquemada was appointed Inquisitor General, extending the institution into Aragon and creating a supreme council (the Suprema) under royal control. He drafted the Inquisition's procedural guidelines (Instrucciones), which regulated arrest, secret accusations, confiscation of property, and the use of torture, and presided over tribunals staged with public autos-da-fé. Torquemada is credited by historians with pressing the monarchs toward the Alhambra Decree of 1492, which expelled practicing Jews from Spain.

Death and reputation

Torquemada's health failed in the 1490s, and Pope Alexander VI appointed four assistant inquisitors in 1494, in part to curb complaints about his severity. He died at the monastery of Saint Thomas Aquinas in Ávila in 1498. His name became a byword for fanatical religious persecution, invoked in literature from Dostoevsky's "Grand Inquisitor" legend to modern popular culture. Historians note that the Inquisition he built long outlived him, persisting until the 19th century.
