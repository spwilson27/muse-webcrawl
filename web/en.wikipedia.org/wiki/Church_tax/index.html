Church tax

A church tax is a tax imposed on members of certain religious congregations, collected by the state in some countries and remitted to the churches to finance their activities. Systems of church tax exist in Germany, Austria, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Sweden, Switzerland (in most cantons) and, in a different form, in Italy and Spain, where taxpayers may assign a fraction of their income tax to a church or to the state.

Germany

The best-known system is the German Kirchensteuer. Under the Weimar Constitution of 1919, whose articles were incorporated into the Basic Law of 1949, religious societies constituted as public-law corporations may levy taxes on their members according to civil tax lists. The tax amounts to 8% (Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg) or 9% (elsewhere) of the member's income-tax liability, withheld by the employer together with income tax and transferred to the churches by the state for a small collection fee. Together the Catholic and Protestant churches receive on the order of €13 billion annually. Members may escape the tax only by formally leaving the church (Kirchenaustritt) before a state authority; exit numbers rose sharply in the 2010s and 2020s, exceeding half a million in some years.

Debate

Supporters argue church taxes fund extensive welfare, educational and cultural services and give churches independence from wealthy donors. Critics object to state involvement in religious finance, to disclosure of religious affiliation to employers, and to exit requirements, and dispute the practice on church–state separation grounds.
