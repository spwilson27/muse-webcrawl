Illiteracy is the inability to read and write. UNESCO defines an illiterate adult as a person who cannot, with understanding, both read and write a short, simple statement about their everyday life. A related concept, functional illiteracy, describes reading and writing skills that are insufficient to manage daily living and employment tasks that require reading beyond a basic level.

Scale and distribution

Global adult literacy has risen from roughly 68 per cent in the late 1970s to around 86–87 per cent in recent UNESCO estimates, yet several hundred million adults — about two-thirds of them women — remain unable to read and write. Rates are lowest in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia, and correlate strongly with poverty, rural residence, and lack of schooling. Illiteracy in wealthy countries is concentrated among early school-leavers, some migrant populations, and people with learning disabilities such as dyslexia.

Causes and consequences

The main causes are lack of access to primary education, poverty and child labour, conflict and displacement, and the absence of written material in a person's language. Illiteracy is associated with poorer health outcomes, lower earnings, reduced political participation, and intergenerational disadvantage, since children of illiterate parents are less likely to become literate themselves.

Campaigns and policy

Historic mass literacy campaigns include the Soviet likbez programme of the 1920s, Cuba's 1961 literacy campaign, and Nicaragua's 1980 crusade. Contemporary efforts focus on universal primary and lower-secondary education, adult education classes, mother-tongue instruction, and digital literacy programmes. International Literacy Day is observed on 8 September.
