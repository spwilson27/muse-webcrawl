Coffin ship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Coffin ship is the name given to the overcrowded, disease-ridden vessels that carried emigrants from Ireland to North America during the Great Famine of 1845–1852, and, by extension, to later unseaworthy or overloaded ships whose owners risked passengers' lives for profit.

The famine crossings

Fleeing starvation, hundreds of thousands of Irish emigrants sailed for British North America and the United States, many on converted cargo vessels with minimal provisions, water, or sanitation. Typhus ("ship fever") spread rapidly in the cramped steerage decks; in the worst year, 1847, thousands died at sea or in quarantine stations such as Grosse Isle in the St. Lawrence River, where over 5,000 Irish emigrants are buried. Mortality on some voyages exceeded 20–30 percent. Parliament responded with tightened Passenger Acts regulating space, food, and water.

Later usage

The term was revived in the 1870s by Samuel Plimsoll's campaign against overloaded, insured "coffin ships" sent to sea by unscrupulous owners, which led to the Merchant Shipping Act 1876 and the Plimsoll load line. The famine crossings are commemorated by memorials in Ireland and Canada and by the replica barque Jeanie Johnston.
