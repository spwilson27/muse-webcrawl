Babylonian Talmud

The Babylonian Talmud (Hebrew: תַּלְמוּד בַּבְלִי, Talmud Bavli) is the central text of Rabbinic Judaism and the primary source of Jewish religious law (halakha) and theology. Compiled in Sasanian Babylonia over the 3rd to 6th centuries CE, it consists of the Mishnah (c. 200 CE) together with the Gemara, an extensive rabbinic commentary analysing and expanding the Mishnah. It is the more authoritative and more frequently studied of the two Talmuds, the other being the Jerusalem Talmud.

Structure

The Talmud covers the six orders (sedarim) of the Mishnah, though Babylonian Gemara exists for 37 of the 63 tractates. Its discussions range across civil and criminal law, ritual, ethics, folklore, medicine and aggadic narrative; legal debate typically proceeds by question, objection and resolution across generations of sages (the amoraim, followed by the editorial savoraim). Rav Ashi and Ravina are traditionally credited with its redaction around 500 CE.

Study and influence

Surrounded on the printed page by the commentaries of Rashi and the Tosafists, the Talmud has been the core curriculum of yeshiva study for centuries. The cycle of Daf Yomi — studying one folio a day — completes the whole work in about seven and a half years. Full English translations include the Soncino, Steinsaltz and ArtScroll (Schottenstein) editions, and the text is freely available online through Sefaria.
