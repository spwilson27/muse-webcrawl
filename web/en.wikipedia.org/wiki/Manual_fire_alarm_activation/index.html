Manual fire alarm activation

Manual fire alarm activation is the process of triggering a fire alarm system by a person, typically by operating a manual call point (also known as a pull station in North America, or a break glass call point in the United Kingdom and Commonwealth countries). Such devices are installed on walls along escape routes so that occupants who discover a fire can raise the alarm before automatic detectors respond.

Design

In Europe and much of the world, call points are red boxes with a frangible element (glass or plastic) which is pressed or broken to operate a switch; modern units use resettable plastic elements and may be protected by a hinged cover to discourage misuse. In the United States and Canada, pull stations are operated by pulling down a handle; "dual-action" designs require a second motion (such as lifting or pushing) to reduce false alarms. Stations are usually placed near exits, at a height of about 1.2–1.4 m (4 ft), and spaced so that travel distance to one does not exceed limits in codes such as NFPA 72 or BS 5839.

False alarms

Malicious or accidental activation is a common cause of false alarms, which burden fire services and can produce alarm fatigue. Protective covers with local sounders, CCTV, and penalties for malicious activation are used as countermeasures; deliberate false activation is an offence in many jurisdictions.
