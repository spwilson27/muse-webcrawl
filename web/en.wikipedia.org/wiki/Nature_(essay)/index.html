Nature is an essay published anonymously in 1836 by Ralph Waldo Emerson. It is regarded as the founding document of Transcendentalism, the American philosophical and literary movement, laying out in poetic prose the doctrine that divinity suffuses nature and that human beings can know truth directly through intuition and communion with the natural world, rather than through institutions, tradition, or books alone.

Content

The essay opens with the famous demand that "our age is retrospective" and asks, "Why should not we also enjoy an original relation to the universe?" After an introduction, Emerson considers nature under the heads of Commodity, Beauty, Language, and Discipline, arguing that nature serves human material needs, offers aesthetic delight, supplies the symbols of all human language, and finally disciplines the mind toward moral and intellectual truth. In the essay's most famous passage he describes becoming "a transparent eye-ball; I am nothing; I see all; the currents of the Universal Being circulate through me; I am part or particle of God."

Publication and influence

Published in Boston by James Munroe and Company in a small edition, Nature sold slowly at first but became the manifesto of the Transcendental Club, which included Margaret Fuller, Bronson Alcott, and Henry David Thoreau, whose Walden (1854) is its most famous literary descendant. It marked the beginning of a distinctly American literature and philosophy, and anticipated later American nature writing and environmental thought.
