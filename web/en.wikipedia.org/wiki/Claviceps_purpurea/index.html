Claviceps purpurea

Claviceps purpurea is an ergot fungus that grows on the ears of rye and related cereal and forage plants. Consumption of grains or seeds contaminated with the survival structure of this fungus, the ergot sclerotium, can cause ergotism in humans and other mammals.

Life cycle

The fungus overwinters as dark, hardened sclerotia in the soil. In spring the sclerotia germinate, producing small mushroom-like stromata that release ascospores. Wind-borne spores infect the ovaries of flowering grasses and cereals. The infected ovary produces a sticky "honeydew" full of conidia, which insects carry to other flowers. The ovary is then replaced by the sclerotium — the purple-black "ergot" body that gives the species its name.

Effects on humans

Ergot sclerotia contain a complex mixture of alkaloids, including ergotamine and ergoline derivatives. Eating bread made from contaminated rye flour historically caused epidemics of ergotism, known in the Middle Ages as "St Anthony's fire". Symptoms take two forms: convulsive ergotism, with spasms and hallucinations, and gangrenous ergotism, in which vasoconstriction cuts off blood flow to the extremities.

Medical uses

Ergot alkaloids have long been used in medicine. Ergotamine is used in the treatment of migraine, and ergometrine (ergonovine) was historically used to control postpartum haemorrhage. The ergot alkaloids also provided the chemical starting point from which Albert Hofmann synthesised LSD in 1938. Modern grain-cleaning and inspection have made ergotism rare, though the fungus remains an agricultural concern.
