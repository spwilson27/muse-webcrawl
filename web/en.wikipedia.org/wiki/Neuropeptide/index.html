Neuropeptide

Neuropeptides are chemical messengers made up of small chains of amino acids that are synthesized and released by neurons. Neuropeptides bind to cell surface receptors, mainly G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), and act as neurotransmitters, neuromodulators, or neurohormones. They are typically 3 to 36 amino acids long and are larger than classic small-molecule neurotransmitters such as glutamate or serotonin.

Biosynthesis and release

Neuropeptides are derived from larger precursor proteins called prepropeptides, which are cleaved by proteases and chemically modified within the secretory pathway. They are packaged into large dense-core vesicles and released from nerve terminals, often co-released alongside fast neurotransmitters. Unlike classical transmitters, peptides are not recycled at the synapse; after release they are broken down by extracellular peptidases or diffuse away from their site of release.

Examples and functions

Well-known neuropeptides include the opioid peptides (endorphins, enkephalins, and dynorphins), substance P, neuropeptide Y, oxytocin, vasopressin, somatostatin, cholecystokinin, and the hypocretins (orexins). Neuropeptides regulate a wide range of physiological processes, including pain perception, appetite and feeding, stress responses, sleep, mood, social bonding, and circadian rhythms. Because their receptors are widely targeted in pharmacology, neuropeptide systems are of major interest in drug development.
