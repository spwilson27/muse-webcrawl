The Alexander Romance is an account of the life and exploits of Alexander the Great written in a legendary, romantic style rather than a historical one. Composed in Greek, probably in Alexandria, and falsely attributed in some manuscripts to Alexander's court historian Callisthenes (hence its traditional author "Pseudo-Callisthenes"), it became one of the most widely read and translated works of the pre-modern world.

Content

The Romance presents Alexander as the secret son of the exiled Egyptian pharaoh-magician Nectanebo II, who visits Olympias in the shape of the god Ammon. It narrates his precocious childhood, the taming of Bucephalus, his campaigns — embroidered with wonders — and adds fantastic episodes absent from the historical sources: Alexander's descent to the sea floor in a glass diving bell, his ascent into the heavens in a chariot drawn by griffins, his journey to the Land of Darkness and the Water of Life, his encounters with talking trees, dog-headed men and the naked philosophers of India, and his enclosure of the unclean nations of Gog and Magog behind a great gate. The work closes with his death at Babylon, omens, and his testament.

Transmission and versions

The Greek text survives in several recensions (conventionally called alpha, beta, gamma and others). It was translated into Latin — notably by Julius Valerius (c. 320 CE) and in the 10th-century version of Leo of Naples, the Historia de preliis — and into Armenian, Syriac, Coptic, Ethiopic, Hebrew, Arabic, Persian, Turkish, Slavonic and most European vernaculars. Each culture reshaped the hero: in Persian tradition Iskandar becomes a prophet-king and is identified in later Islamic tradition with Dhu al-Qarnayn of the Qur'an; in medieval Christian Europe he is a model knight and one of the Nine Worthies; in Jewish versions he is a sympathetic figure received at Jerusalem.

Significance

Second perhaps only to the Bible in the number of its medieval translations and copies, the Alexander Romance shaped the image of Alexander for more than a millennium and fed episodes into works ranging from the Shahnameh to the Old French Roman d'Alexandre and the Middle English Kyng Alisaunder. Modern scholarship distinguishes its strata to recover the traditions, some possibly Hellenistic in origin, from which it grew.
