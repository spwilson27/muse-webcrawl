Pyroclastic rocks (derived from the Greek pyr, "fire", and klastos, "broken") are clastic rocks composed of rock fragments produced and ejected by explosive volcanic eruptions. The individual fragments are called pyroclasts. Pyroclastic rocks are a type of volcaniclastic deposit, formed when volcanic material is explosively fragmented, transported through the air or by ground-hugging density currents, and deposited and lithified.

Pyroclasts

Pyroclasts are classified by size. Particles finer than 2 mm are ash; fragments between 2 and 64 mm are lapilli; and fragments larger than 64 mm are blocks (angular, solid when ejected) or bombs (rounded or streamlined, molten or partly molten when ejected). Pyroclasts may consist of juvenile material — pumice, scoria and glass shards derived from the erupting magma — together with crystals and accidental lithic fragments torn from the conduit walls and older rocks.

Classification of the rocks

Unconsolidated accumulations of pyroclasts are called tephra; once consolidated, the rocks are named according to the dominant grain size: tuff (mainly ash), lapilli tuff or lapillistone (mainly lapilli), and pyroclastic breccia or agglomerate (mainly blocks or bombs, respectively). Tuffs are further described by their composition, for example as vitric (glass-rich), crystal or lithic tuffs.

Modes of deposition

Pyroclastic rocks form from three principal transport mechanisms. Pyroclastic fall deposits settle from eruption plumes and are typically well sorted and mantle the topography. Pyroclastic density currents — flows and surges — produce deposits that pond in valleys; when hot enough to weld on deposition, the resulting rock is an ignimbrite, characterised by flattened pumice fragments (fiamme) in a glassy ash matrix. Ballistic emplacement accounts for coarse blocks and bombs near the vent.

Occurrence and significance

Pyroclastic rocks are widespread around explosive volcanoes, especially the subduction-zone volcanoes of the Ring of Fire and caldera systems such as Yellowstone and Taupō, whose enormous ignimbrite sheets record super-eruptions. Because individual ash beds can be traced over vast areas and dated radiometrically, they serve as valuable marker horizons (tephrochronology) in stratigraphy, archaeology and palaeoclimate studies. Lightweight pyroclastic materials such as pumice and tuff have been used since antiquity as building stone and in pozzolanic cements.
