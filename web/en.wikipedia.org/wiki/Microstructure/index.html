Microstructure

The microstructure of a metal revealed by etching

Microstructure is the very small-scale structure of a material, defined as the structure of a prepared surface of the material as revealed by an optical microscope at magnifications above about 25×. The microstructure of a material (metals, polymers, ceramics, or composites) strongly influences physical properties such as strength, toughness, ductility, hardness, corrosion resistance, and high-temperature behaviour.

Features

Crystalline materials consist of grains — small crystals of differing orientation — separated by grain boundaries. Within and between grains there may be multiple phases, precipitates, inclusions, pores and defects such as dislocations and vacancies. Grain size, commonly measured by metallographic methods described in ASTM standards, is quantified by intercept lengths and is related to yield strength through the Hall–Petch relationship: finer grains generally give stronger, tougher materials.

Characterisation

Microstructure is revealed by cutting, mounting, polishing and chemically etching a specimen, then examining it by optical microscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), or transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for finer scales. Techniques such as electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) map crystal orientations, while X-ray diffraction identifies phases. Metallurgists manipulate microstructure through alloying and thermal processing — annealing, quenching, tempering and precipitation hardening — to tailor properties, as in the martensitic structures of hardened steels.
