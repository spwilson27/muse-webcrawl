Karamanids

The Karamanids (Turkish: Karamanoğulları) were a dynasty of Turkish beys who ruled the Karaman Beylik, one of the Anatolian beyliks, in south-central Anatolia from the mid-13th century until 1487. Centred on Karaman (formerly Larende) and at times controlling Konya, the former Seljuk capital, they were the most powerful of the Turkish principalities and the longest-standing rivals of the rising Ottoman Empire.

Origins

The dynasty descended from Karaman Bey, a Turkmen leader of the Afshar tribe, who established himself in the Taurus foothills around Ermenek in the mid-1200s as Seljuk authority in Anatolia weakened after the Mongol victory at Köse Dağ (1243). His son, Mehmed I of Karaman, famously seized Konya in 1277 during a revolt and issued a decree making Turkish the official language of government — an act still commemorated in Turkey as a landmark in the history of the Turkish language.

History

From their mountain strongholds, the Karamanids expanded across the Konya plain and at times reached the Mediterranean coast, controlling ports such as Alanya. They fought repeatedly with the neighbouring beyliks, the Ilkhanate, the Mamluks of Egypt, and above all the Ottomans. Sultan Bayezid I annexed much of their territory in the 1390s, but Timur restored the Karamanids after the Battle of Ankara in 1402, and the rivalry resumed for most of the 15th century. The Karamanids under Pir Ahmed and Kasim Bey resisted Mehmed II, but Ottoman campaigns under Mehmed II and Bayezid II progressively absorbed the beylik; the last Karamanid resistance ended by 1487, and much of the Karamanid Turkmen population was deported to the Balkans and Cyprus.

Legacy

The Karamanids left a notable architectural legacy in Karaman, Konya, Ermenek and Niğde, including mosques, madrasas and caravanserais such as the Hatuniye Madrasa in Karaman, built for the mother of Mehmed II. The modern Turkish province of Karaman bears their name, and the Karamanlides — Turkish-speaking Orthodox Christians of the region — are often (though debatably) linked to their era.
