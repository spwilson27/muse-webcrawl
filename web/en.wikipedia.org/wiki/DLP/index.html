Digital Light Processing (DLP) is a set of chipsets based on optical micro-electro-mechanical technology that uses a digital micromirror device (DMD). Developed by Texas Instruments, beginning with Larry Hornbeck's invention of the DMD in 1987, DLP technology is used in display devices including video projectors, digital cinema projection, rear-projection televisions, interactive displays, and pico projectors in mobile devices, as well as in 3D printing and other industrial applications.

A DMD chip, the heart of DLP technology.

How it works

The DMD is an array of hundreds of thousands — or millions — of microscopic mirrors, one per pixel, mounted on tiny hinges above CMOS memory cells. Each mirror can tilt thousands of times per second between an "on" position that reflects light from the source through the projection lens and an "off" position that deflects it away. Grayscale is produced by pulse-width modulation of the mirrors. Colour is generated either by a spinning colour wheel in single-chip systems or by separate red, green and blue light paths (often LEDs or lasers) in three-chip systems used in high-end and digital cinema projectors.

Applications

DLP projectors became a dominant technology in business and home-theatre projection and are the basis of the DLP Cinema standard used in most digital cinema installations worldwide. Beyond displays, the rapid switching of the mirrors is exploited in structured-light 3D scanning, spectroscopy, and resin 3D printers that cure whole layers of photopolymer at once.

Advantages and disadvantages

Strengths include high contrast, smooth pixel structure, good brightness, and durability. Single-chip designs with colour wheels can show a "rainbow effect" of momentary colour fringes to some viewers, and sequential colour can limit colour brightness compared with three-chip designs.
