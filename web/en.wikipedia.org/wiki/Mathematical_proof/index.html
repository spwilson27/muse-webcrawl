Mathematical proof

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Euclid's Elements, the classical model of axiomatic proof]

A mathematical proof is a deductive argument for a mathematical statement, showing that the stated assumptions logically guarantee the conclusion. Unlike empirical evidence, a proof establishes certainty: a proved statement — a theorem — remains true forever, provided the logic is sound.

Proofs build on axioms and previously proved results using rules of inference. The ideal was set by Euclid's Elements (around 300 BC), which derived geometry from five postulates and dominated mathematical education for two millennia.

Techniques

Standard proof methods include direct proof, proof by contradiction (reductio ad absurdum), proof by mathematical induction, proof by contraposition, constructive and non-constructive existence proofs, and exhaustive case analysis. Famous applications include Euclid's proof of the infinitude of primes and the irrationality of √2.

Modern practice accepts informal but rigorous proofs in journals; formal proofs, checked by computer proof assistants such as Lean and Coq, are a growing supplement. The four-colour theorem (1976) sparked debate by requiring computer checking of hundreds of cases.

Foundations

What counts as a proof depends on foundational choices; Gödel's incompleteness theorems limit what any fixed axiom system can prove. Axiomatic set theory (ZFC) is the conventional foundation of most mathematics.
