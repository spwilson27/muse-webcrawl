Formalism (philosophy of mathematics)

In the philosophy of mathematics, formalism is the view that mathematics is essentially the manipulation of strings of symbols according to agreed formal rules, and that mathematical statements need not refer to any external reality or meaning beyond that manipulation. It is associated above all with David Hilbert and his program of placing mathematics on a secure axiomatic foundation in the early 20th century.

Hilbert's program

Hilbert proposed that all of mathematics be formalized in axiomatic systems and that the consistency of those systems be proved using only finitary methods — reasoning about finite symbol manipulations. The program was undermined by Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931), which showed that any sufficiently strong consistent formal system cannot prove its own consistency and contains true statements it cannot prove.

Variants and criticism

Formalism comes in several forms: term formalism (mathematical terms have no meaning beyond being symbols), game formalism (mathematics as a game with symbols, analogous to chess), and deductivism (the content of a theorem is a conditional: if axioms hold, the theorem holds). Critics object that formalism struggles to explain the applicability of mathematics to the physical world and the apparent objectivity of mathematical truth. Rival schools include Platonism (mathematical objects exist abstractly), intuitionism, and logicism.
