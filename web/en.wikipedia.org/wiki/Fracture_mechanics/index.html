Fracture mechanics

Fracture mechanics is the field of mechanics concerned with the study of the propagation of cracks in materials. It uses methods of analytical solid mechanics to calculate the driving force on a crack and those of experimental solid mechanics to characterize a material's resistance to fracture.

History

Theoretically, the concept behind fracture mechanics began in the work of Alan Arnold Griffith during the First World War, who derived the energy balance criterion for brittle fracture. After the Second World War, George Irwin extended the theory by introducing the stress intensity factor and the concept of energy release rate.

Linear elastic fracture mechanics

In modern materials science, fracture mechanics is an important tool used to improve the performance of mechanical components. It applies the physics of stress and strain behaviour of materials to predict failure, distinguishing three modes of crack opening: mode I (opening), mode II (sliding) and mode III (tearing).

Applications

Fracture mechanics is used in the design and life assessment of aircraft structures, pressure vessels, pipelines, bridges and other safety-critical components, where the growth of fatigue cracks must be predicted and managed.
