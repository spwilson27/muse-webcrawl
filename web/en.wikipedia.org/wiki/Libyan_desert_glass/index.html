Libyan desert glass
TypeImpact glass (impactite)
Composition~98% silica (SiO2), lechatelierite inclusions
LocationGreat Sand Sea, western Egypt / eastern Libya
Age~29 million years

Libyan desert glass (LDG) is a naturally occurring impact glass found in areas of the northeastern Sahara, in the Great Sand Sea along the border between western Egypt and eastern Libya. Pieces are scattered over an area of several thousand square kilometres of sand dunes and are typically pale yellow to greenish-yellow in colour.

Formation

The glass is composed of almost pure silica and was formed about 29 million years ago when sand was melted at extremely high temperatures. The prevailing explanation is that a meteorite impact — possibly connected with the Kebira crater in Libya — or a large atmospheric airburst supplied the energy to fuse the desert sand into glass. The glass contains inclusions of the high-temperature silica polymorph cristobalite and grains of lechatelierite, evidence of temperatures exceeding 1,700 °C.

Human use

Libyan desert glass was knapped into tools by prehistoric peoples of the region. Its most famous ancient use is a carved scarab of LDG set in a pectoral (breast ornament) belonging to the pharaoh Tutankhamun, whose tomb was discovered in 1922; the identification of the scarab's material as desert glass was confirmed by geological analysis in the 1990s. The glass was formally described for Western science after the explorer Patrick Clayton surveyed the area in the 1930s.
