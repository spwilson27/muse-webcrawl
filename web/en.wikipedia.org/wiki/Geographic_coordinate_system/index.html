Overview

A geographic coordinate system is a spherical coordinate system that enables every location on Earth to be specified by a set of numbers. In its common form it uses two angles: latitude, the angle north or south of the Equator, and longitude, the angle east or west of the prime meridian. A third value, height above sea level, is often added.

The network of parallels and meridians drawn on globes and maps is called the graticule.

Reference surfaces and datums

Because Earth is not a perfect sphere but an oblate ellipsoid, flattened at the poles, coordinates must be defined relative to a chosen mathematical model of the planet. Such a model together with its anchor to the real Earth is called a datum. The datum used by GPS, WGS 84, differs from older regional datums by up to a few hundred metres, so the same physical place can have slightly different coordinates in different systems.

The science of measuring Earth's shape and gravity field to support all this is geodesy. Coordinates may be written in degrees-minutes-seconds or as decimal degrees.

History

The idea of fixing positions by latitude and longitude goes back to Hellenistic astronomy: Eratosthenes drew an early graticule, Hipparchus systematised coordinates, and Ptolemy's Geography listed thousands of places by their coordinates.
