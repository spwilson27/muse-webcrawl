European federalism

Image: European flag (placeholder)

European federalism is a political philosophy and movement advocating a federal union of European states, in which sovereignty is shared between a central European government and constituent member states. Federalists played a key role in the early European integration project after the Second World War, and the idea continues to shape debate about the future of the European Union.

History

Federalist thought in Europe draws on earlier proposals from thinkers such as Victor Hugo, who called for a "United States of Europe". During the war, the Italian anti-fascist Altiero Spinelli co-authored the 1941 Ventotene Manifesto, arguing that only a federal Europe could secure peace. The Union of European Federalists was founded in 1946, and the 1950 Schuman Declaration launched practical integration, although federalists' more ambitious goals — such as the European Political Community of 1953 — were not realised.

Concepts and debate

Federalists argue that challenges such as climate change, defence, and economic policy exceed the capacity of individual states, and they favour stronger roles for the European Parliament and Commission, majority voting, and a common fiscal capacity. Critics, including sovereigntists and intergovernmentalists, object that a federal Europe would lack democratic legitimacy and undermine national self-government. In practice, the EU is often described as a hybrid: neither a classic federation nor a mere confederation.
