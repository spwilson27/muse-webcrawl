Functional extinction

Functional extinction describes the condition of a species whose population has fallen so low that it no longer plays a significant role in its ecosystem, or whose remaining individuals can no longer reproduce successfully. A functionally extinct species may persist for years or decades before its final extinction.

Mechanisms

Functional extinction can arise from a severely reduced population, a skewed sex ratio, inbreeding depression, or the loss of a critical ecological interaction. Examples often discussed include the baiji (Yangtze river dolphin), declared functionally extinct in 2006 after an expedition found none, and the northern white rhinoceros, reduced to two non-breeding females. Pollinators or seed dispersers can also become functionally extinct while still locally present, with cascading effects on their ecosystems.

Significance

Conservation biologists use the concept to warn that waiting until the last individual dies misjudges urgency: by the time extinction is formally declared, the species' ecological function — and often any realistic chance of recovery — has already been lost.
