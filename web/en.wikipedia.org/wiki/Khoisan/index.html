Khoisan

San hunter-gatherers of the Kalahari
RegionsNamibia, Botswana, South Africa, Angola, Tanzania (Sandawe, Hadza)
PeoplesKhoikhoi (Khoi), San (Bushmen)
LanguagesKhoe–Kwadi, Tuu, Kxʼa; isolates Sandawe and Hadza
Defining featureClick consonants

Khoisan (also spelled Khoesaan or Khoi-San) is a catch-all term for the indigenous peoples of Southern Africa who traditionally speak (or spoke) languages with click consonants and do not speak Bantu languages. It combines two groups: the Khoikhoi (Khoi, formerly called "Hottentots"), historically pastoralists, and the San ("Bushmen"), historically hunter-gatherers. The term was proposed in the early 20th century and popularised by anthropologist Isaac Schapera.

Languages

The label "Khoisan languages" was originally proposed as a single genetic family by Joseph Greenberg, centred on their shared use of clicks. Most specialists now reject the grouping as a family: the languages are instead divided into three unrelated families — Khoe–Kwadi, Tuu and Kxʼa — plus the isolates Hadza and Sandawe in Tanzania, which may be entirely unrelated. Clicks were likely once far more widespread in Africa and survive also in some neighbouring Bantu languages (such as Zulu and Xhosa) through contact.

History

The Khoisan peoples descend from some of the earliest modern human populations of southern Africa; genetic studies indicate their ancestors diverged from other human lineages over 100,000 years ago. They occupied most of southern Africa until Bantu-speaking farming peoples expanded southwards in the first millennium CE, followed by European colonisation from the 17th century, which brought dispossession, disease and violence — particularly against the Khoikhoi of the Cape. Many San groups retreated into the Kalahari Desert, where some communities retain hunter-gatherer lifeways today.

Culture

San culture is famous for its rock art, found across southern Africa, and for trance-healing dances. Traditional social organisation is egalitarian and band-based, with strong norms of sharing. The Khoikhoi kept cattle and sheep and lived in larger chiefdoms. Since the late 20th century, Khoisan activists in South Africa, Namibia and Botswana have pressed for land rights and recognition as First Peoples under international indigenous-rights frameworks.
