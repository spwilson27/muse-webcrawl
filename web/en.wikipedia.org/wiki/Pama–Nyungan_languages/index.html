The Pama–Nyungan languages are the most widespread family of Australian Aboriginal languages, forming a single grouping spread across some seven-eighths of the Australian mainland — every part of the continent except the far north (Arnhem Land and the Kimberley), where several other families are found. The family comprises around 300 languages, of which a large majority are endangered or extinct following European colonisation.

The name compounds two of the family's branches: the Paman languages of Cape York Peninsula in the northeast, and the Nyungan languages of the southwest (from the word for "man/person" in languages at the two extremes of the continent, pama and nyungar).

Classification and validity

The family was first sketched by Wilhelm Schmidt in 1919 and substantially developed by Kenneth L. Hale in the 1960s–1980s, who argued on the basis of shared vocabulary and morphology that most Australian languages descend from a single proto-language. The hypothesis was controversial: R. M. W. Dixon argued that Australia's languages were the product of millennia of diffusion in a linguistic area and that deep genetic grouping was unprovable. Computational phylogenetic work in the 2010s, notably by Claire Bowern and colleagues, has supported the reality of the family and dated its expansion to roughly 4,000–6,000 years ago.

Proto-Pama–Nyungan

Reconstructed proto-Pama–Nyungan is thought to have had a typical Australian phoneme inventory — three vowels, a set of laminal and apical stop/nasal contrasts, and no fricatives — together with monosyllabic verb roots, suffixed tense marking, and pronominal prefixes or second-position clitic pronouns. Its homeland has been placed variously in the Gulf of Carpentaria region or in south-central Queensland.

Phonology and grammar

Member languages are famous among linguists for features such as ergative–absolutive case marking, mother-in-law (avoidance) speech registers, and elaborated kinship systems. Dyirbal (a Pama–Nyungan language of northeast Queensland) is known for its four noun classes, popularised in the phrase "women, fire, and dangerous things".

History and expansion

The mid-Holocene expansion of Pama–Nyungan replaced earlier language families over most of the continent, possibly in association with new stone tool technologies (backed artefacts and points), the introduction of the dingo, and population growth. Non-Pama–Nyungan families survived only in the tropical north.

Status and revitalisation

Of the roughly 250–300 languages spoken at the time of British settlement in 1788, most Pama–Nyungan languages now have few or no fluent speakers. Some, such as Warlpiri and Pitjantjatjara in Central Australia, remain vigorously transmitted to children. Others, such as Kaurna of the Adelaide Plains — reconstructed entirely from missionary records — have been revived as spoken languages of identity and ceremony.
