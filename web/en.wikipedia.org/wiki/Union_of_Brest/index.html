Union of Brest

The Union of Brest (Polish: Unia brzeska; Ukrainian: Берестейська унія) was the 1595–96 decision of the Ruthenian Orthodox Church eparchies (dioceses) in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth to break relations with the Eastern Orthodox Church and to enter into communion with the Holy See of the Roman Catholic Church, while keeping the Byzantine Rite. The union created the Ruthenian Uniate Church, the ancestor of today's Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church and Belarusian Greek Catholic Church.

Brest, where the union was proclaimed in 1596.

Background

The Orthodox Church in the Commonwealth faced internal difficulties and pressure from the expanding Catholic Church and the Protestant Reformation. Orthodox bishops hoped union with Rome would secure legal equality for their clergy and protection of their rite; King Sigismund III Vasa supported the union as a means of religious unification of the state. Negotiations with Rome were conducted by bishops Hypatius Pociej and Cyril Terlecki; Pope Clement VIII accepted the union in 1595.

Terms

The articles of the union guaranteed that the Byzantine liturgy, Church Slavonic language, married clergy, Julian calendar, and existing church structures would be preserved, while the Ruthenian Church recognized the primacy of the Pope. The union was solemnly proclaimed at a council in Brest in October 1596.

Opposition and consequences

The union was opposed by many Orthodox nobles, brotherhoods, Cossacks, and some bishops; a rival council at Brest condemned it. The resulting division of the Ruthenian Church into Uniate (Greek Catholic) and Orthodox jurisdictions shaped religious and national identities in Ukraine and Belarus for centuries. In the Russian Empire and later the USSR, the Greek Catholic Church was forcibly suppressed (notably in 1946), only to re-emerge publicly in 1989–91.
