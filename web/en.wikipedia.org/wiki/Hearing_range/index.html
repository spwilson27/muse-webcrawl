Hearing range

Hearing range describes the range of frequencies that can be heard by humans or other animals, though it can also refer to the range of levels (loudness). The commonly stated range for humans is 20 to 20,000 Hz, although there is considerable variation between individuals, especially at high frequencies, and the range gradually declines with age (presbycusis).

Humans

Under ideal laboratory conditions humans can hear sound as low as 12 Hz and as high as 28 kHz, though the threshold rises sharply below 20 Hz, which is felt as much as heard. The upper limit in early adulthood may reach 20 kHz but commonly falls to 15–17 kHz by middle age, and to around 12 kHz or less in older adults — a fact exploited by "mosquito" devices and ultrasonic ringtones audible mainly to teenagers. Hearing is most sensitive between about 2 and 5 kHz. Sound pressure level thresholds are measured against 0 dB SPL (20 micropascals) near 1–4 kHz.

Other animals

Many species hear far beyond the human range. Dogs hear up to roughly 45–67 kHz; cats to about 64–79 kHz; bats and dolphins use ultrasound up to and beyond 100 kHz for echolocation. Elephants, by contrast, detect infrasound below 20 Hz and communicate over long distances with low-frequency rumbles, as do some whales. Birds have a narrower range than humans, while frogs and insects often specialise in the frequencies of their own calls or predators.

Measurement

Hearing range and sensitivity are measured with audiometry, producing an audiogram; behavioural audiograms and auditory brainstem responses are used for animals and infants.
