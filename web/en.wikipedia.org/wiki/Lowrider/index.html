Lowrider

Lowrider
OriginLos Angeles, California, United States
EraLate 1940s–present
Key featuresLowered suspension, hydraulics, custom paint, wire wheels

A lowrider or low rider is a customized car with a lowered body, prized for its elaborate paintwork, upholstery, and hydraulic or air-bag suspension systems that allow the driver to raise and lower the vehicle — and even make it hop or dance. Lowrider culture is closely associated with Chicano communities in the southwestern United States, particularly Los Angeles.

History

The style emerged in the late 1940s and 1950s among Mexican-American youths who lowered their cars with sandbags and cut springs, favouring cruising "low and slow" in contrast to the speed-oriented hot rod scene. In 1959, Ron Aguirre developed a hydraulic system using aircraft parts that let drivers adjust ride height at the touch of a switch, partly to circumvent California laws against cars riding too low. The 1964 Chevrolet Impala became the iconic lowrider platform.

Features

Typical modifications include candy and metalflake paint with murals and pinstriping, chrome wire-spoke wheels with whitewall tyres, plush or velour interiors, chain-link steering wheels, and powerful sound systems. Hydraulic setups powered by multiple batteries and pumps can raise one corner of the car at a time; competitive "hopping" contests measure how high a car's front wheels can jump.

Culture

Lowriding is organised around car clubs and cruise nights, and celebrated in publications such as Lowrider magazine (founded 1977). The culture spread internationally, notably to Japan, and has been recognised as an art form: lowriders have been exhibited in museums including the Smithsonian. In 2023 California repealed laws that had allowed cities to ban cruising.
