International variety


International varieties serve communication across national boundaries

In linguistics, an international variety of a language is a form of that language adapted for communication between speakers of different national or regional varieties, often functioning as a lingua franca. The term is used particularly of English — as in International English — but applies to other pluricentric world languages such as Spanish, French, Arabic and Portuguese.

Concept

International varieties emerge when a language is spoken across many countries, each with its own standard (British, American and Australian English, for example). An international variety typically avoids strongly localised idioms, spellings and grammatical forms, instead favouring widely understood conventions. In the study of World Englishes, Braj Kachru's "three circles" model distinguishes the Inner Circle (where the language is native), the Outer Circle (where it is an institutionalised second language) and the Expanding Circle (where it is learned as a foreign language); international varieties cut across these circles.

English as an international language

English as an international language (EIL) and English as a lingua franca (ELF) research studies how non-native speakers — now the large majority of English users — negotiate meaning without reference to native-speaker norms. Simplified or controlled forms codify this idea for specific purposes: Ogden's Basic English, Voice of America's Special English, and Globish. In technical fields, controlled standards such as ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English govern documentation for international audiences.

Other languages

Similar concepts apply elsewhere: español neutro ("neutral Spanish") is used in pan-Hispanic media and dubbing to avoid regionalisms; Modern Standard Arabic serves as the international and formal variety across the Arab world, distinct from regional dialects; and international organisations often maintain their own house styles to keep French, Spanish and other official languages mutually intelligible worldwide.

Debates

Scholars debate whether an international variety is a variety in its own right or simply a register of accommodation, whether standardisation threatens linguistic diversity, and how teaching should treat native-speaker norms. Critics of a single international standard note that intelligibility is usually achieved by negotiation between speakers rather than by conformity to a codified form.
