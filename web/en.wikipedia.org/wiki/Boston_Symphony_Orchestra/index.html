The Boston Symphony Orchestra (BSO) is an American orchestra based in Boston, Massachusetts. Founded in 1881 by the banker and philanthropist Henry Lee Higginson, it is one of the five major American orchestras referred to as the "Big Five". The BSO performs most of its concerts at Boston's Symphony Hall — widely regarded as one of the finest concert halls in the world acoustically — and its summer home at Tanglewood in the Berkshire Hills of western Massachusetts.

History

Higginson single-handedly financed the orchestra for its first 37 years, hiring its first conductor, Georg Henschel. A succession of German and Austrian conductors, including Wilhelm Gericke and Karl Muck, gave the ensemble a Central European profile. In 1924 Serge Koussevitzky began a 25-year music directorship that transformed the orchestra: he championed new music, commissioned landmark works (including Bartók's Concerto for Orchestra), founded the Berkshire Music Center at Tanglewood in 1940, and built the BSO's national reputation through recordings and broadcasts.

Later music directors included Charles Munch (1949–1962), Erich Leinsdorf, William Steinberg and Seiji Ozawa, whose 29-year tenure (1973–2002) was the longest of any BSO music director. James Levine followed (2004–2011), and the Latvian conductor Andris Nelsons became music director in 2014.

Affiliated ensembles and activities

The Boston Pops Orchestra, founded in 1885 and drawn largely from BSO players, performs lighter repertoire and is famous for its Fourth of July concerts on the Charles River Esplanade. The Tanglewood festival, established in 1937, and its teaching arm — the Tanglewood Music Center — are central to American orchestral training. The orchestra also maintains an extensive recording catalogue, from the early Victor recordings under Koussevitzky to Nelsons's Grammy-winning Shostakovich cycle with Deutsche Grammophon.
