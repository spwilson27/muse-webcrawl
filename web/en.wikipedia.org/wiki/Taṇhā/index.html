Taṇhā

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Taṇhā (Pali: तण्हा; Sanskrit: tṛṣṇā) is a Buddhist concept that literally means "thirst", and is commonly translated as craving, desire or longing. In Buddhism, taṇhā is the cause of suffering (dukkha) as presented in the Four Noble Truths, and it is one of the links in the chain of dependent origination (paṭicca-samuppāda), arising from feeling (vedanā) and giving rise to clinging (upādāna).

Types of craving

The early texts analyse craving threefold: kāma-taṇhā, craving for sensual pleasures; bhava-taṇhā, craving for existence and becoming; and vibhava-taṇhā, craving for non-existence or annihilation. Craving is also described in relation to the six sense bases — craving for sights, sounds, smells, tastes, tactile objects and mental phenomena.

Role in doctrine

In the Buddha's first sermon, the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, taṇhā is identified as the origin of suffering in the Second Noble Truth: it is "this craving which leads to renewed existence, accompanied by delight and lust". Because craving conditions clinging and becoming, its complete cessation — through the Noble Eightfold Path, the subject of the Third and Fourth Noble Truths — is identified with liberation, nibbāna. Taṇhā is distinguished from wholesome desire (chanda), such as the aspiration to practise, which is not in itself a cause of suffering.

Comparisons

The concept is sometimes compared with desire in Western philosophy and psychology, though scholars caution that taṇhā specifically denotes a self-centred, unsatisfiable thirst driven by ignorance of impermanence, rather than all wanting whatsoever.
