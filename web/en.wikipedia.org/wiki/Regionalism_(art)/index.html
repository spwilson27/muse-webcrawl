Regionalism (art)

Regionalism is an American art movement that flourished from about 1925 to 1945, in which artists depicted realistic scenes of rural and small-town America, especially the Midwest, in reaction against European modernist abstraction and in response to the Great Depression. Its three best-known proponents — Grant Wood of Iowa, Thomas Hart Benton of Missouri and John Steuart Curry of Kansas — are sometimes called the "Regionalist Triumvirate" after their joint appearance on the cover of Time magazine in 1934.

Characteristics and works

Regionalist works use clear modelling, earthy palettes and narrative detail to celebrate (and sometimes satirise) agrarian life. Grant Wood's American Gothic (1930) and Daughters of Revolution (1932) exemplify the movement's polished, ironic realism; Benton's muscular mural cycles, such as America Today (1930–31) and the Missouri State Capitol murals (1936), narrate American social history; Curry painted Kansas tornadoes, baptisms and hog killings with dramatic intensity. Benton's pupil Jackson Pollock later helped lead the abstract movement that displaced Regionalism in critical esteem.

Context

The movement overlapped with the broader "American Scene" painting and with New Deal art programmes (the Section of Painting and Sculpture, the PWAP and other Treasury and WPA projects), which commissioned murals for post offices and public buildings across the country. Regionalists argued that American artists should paint American subjects in an accessible style; critics charged the movement with provincialism, chauvinism and, in Benton's case, theatrical excess. After the Second World War the centre of the art world shifted to New York abstraction, and Regionalism's reputation declined sharply, though it has since been reappraised as an important record of Depression-era culture and identity.

Regional schools

Beyond the Midwest, Regionalist tendencies appeared in the Texas work of Jerry Bywaters and Alexandre Hogue (the "Dallas Nine"), in California watercolour scenes, in the Southern paintings of Lamar Dodd, and in the lithographs that many Regionalists used to reach a mass audience through dealers such as Associated American Artists.
