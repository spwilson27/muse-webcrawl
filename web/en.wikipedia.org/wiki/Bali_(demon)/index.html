Bali (Sanskrit: बलि), also known as Mahabali ("great Bali"), is a king of the asuras (daityas) in Hindu scriptures, celebrated despite his demonic lineage for his generosity, devotion and righteousness. Grandson of the saintly Prahlada and son of Virochana, he conquered the three worlds from the devas, but lost them to Vishnu's dwarf avatar, Vamana, in one of the most beloved episodes of Hindu mythology. Unusually for an asura king, Bali is revered as a virtuous ruler and is honoured annually in Kerala's Onam festival.

The Vamana legend

After Bali, advised by his guru Shukracharya, defeated Indra and seized heaven, the gods appealed to Vishnu, who was born as Vamana, a dwarf Brahmin. At Bali's great sacrificial rite, Vamana asked for just three paces of land. Despite Shukracharya's warning that the dwarf was Vishnu in disguise, Bali agreed — honouring a gift once promised. Vamana then grew to cosmic size: with one step he covered the earth, with the second the heavens, and there was nowhere left for the third. Bali offered his own head; Vamana placed his foot upon it and pressed Bali down to Sutala, the netherworld, which Vishnu granted him to rule, promising also that Bali would become Indra in a future age. Moved by Bali's humility, Vishnu himself became the doorkeeper of Sutala.

Onam and Bali Pratipada

In Kerala tradition, Mahabali's reign is remembered as a golden age when "all men were equal", and Vishnu granted him leave to visit his subjects once a year. That visit is celebrated as Onam, Kerala's great harvest festival, with flower carpets (pookalam), feasts (sadya), boat races and dances. The fourth day of Diwali is observed in much of India as Bali Pratipada (Bali Padyami), marking Bali's return and his defeat of pride.

Family and other traditions

Bali's wife was Vindhyavali; his sons included Banasura, the thousand-armed devotee of Shiva. The monkey king Sugriva of the Ramayana was sometimes described as Bali's counterpart or, in other tellings, his enemy was Bali's brother Vali — a distinct figure, the vanara king of Kishkindha, not to be confused with the asura Bali. Bali also appears in the story of the churning of the ocean of milk.
