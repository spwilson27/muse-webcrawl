Tilted Arc was a controversial public art installation by the American sculptor Richard Serra, built in 1981 in Federal Plaza in Manhattan, New York City. The sculpture consisted of a gently curved wall of raw Cor-Ten steel, 120 feet (37 m) long and 12 feet (3.7 m) high, which bisected the plaza. It was removed in 1989 following years of litigation, becoming the most famous case study in debates over public art, site-specificity, and artists' moral rights.

Commission and reception

Serra was commissioned in 1979 by the U.S. General Services Administration's Art-in-Architecture programme. He maintained the work was conceived for that specific site and that to move it was to destroy it. Many office workers in the plaza, however, petitioned against it as an oppressive barrier that obstructed the space, and a 1985 public hearing voted for its relocation. Serra sued, arguing breach of contract and First Amendment violations, but lost.

Removal and aftermath

On the night of 15 March 1989 the sculpture was cut into three pieces and removed to storage, where it remains. The case influenced the Visual Artists Rights Act of 1990 in the United States and remains a standard reference point in discussions of who decides what art the public must live with.
