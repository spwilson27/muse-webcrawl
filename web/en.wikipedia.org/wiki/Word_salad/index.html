Word salad

Word salad is a term used in psychiatry and linguistics for speech consisting of a confused, seemingly random mixture of words and phrases that lacks coherent meaning. The individual words may be grammatical and pronounceable, but their combination is nonsensical: syntax and semantics break down so that the listener cannot extract a message. The German psychiatric term Schizophasie and Emil Kraepelin's descriptions of incoherence in schizophrenia are closely related concepts.

Clinical significance

Word salad is a feature of formal thought disorder and may occur in the disorganised speech of people with schizophrenia, in mania with flight of ideas taken to an extreme, in delirium, and in severe dementias. It also arises in the receptive (Wernicke's) aphasias following stroke or head injury, where fluent but empty, paraphasic and neologism-filled speech is produced — though in aphasia the disturbance is primarily linguistic rather than a disorder of thought. Clinicians distinguish word salad from merely tangential or circumstantial speech: in word salad the associations between words are so loose, and neologisms so frequent, that comprehension essentially fails.

Differential diagnosis

Because word salad can signal psychosis, intoxication, delirium, or acute brain injury, its sudden appearance in someone previously coherent is a medical warning sign. Assessment considers accompanying symptoms, onset, medication, and neurological findings. Delirious patients typically fluctuate in attention, while aphasic patients show characteristic patterns on language testing, and patients with schizophrenia typically show other positive and negative symptoms of the disorder.

Other uses

Outside medicine, "word salad" is used colloquially for any pompous, evasive, or meaningless verbiage — for example political rhetoric that strings together impressive-sounding phrases without content. In computing and online culture, generated spam text and nonsense output from early chatbots have also been described with the term. Such uses are metaphorical and are distinguished from the clinical symptom.
