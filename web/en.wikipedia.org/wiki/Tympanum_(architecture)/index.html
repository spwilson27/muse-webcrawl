Tympanum (architecture)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tympanum (pl. tympana; from Greek τύμπανον, "drum") is the semi-circular or triangular decorative wall surface over an entrance, door or window, which is bounded by a lintel and an arch. It often contains pedimental sculpture or other imagery or ornaments.

History

In ancient Greek and Roman architecture, the tympanum was the triangular field within a pediment, frequently filled with sculpture depicting mythological scenes. The form was revived in classical and neoclassical building.

Medieval use

In Romanesque and Gothic architecture the tympanum over a churchportal became a principal field for monumental sculpture. Romanesque examples, such as those at Vézelay and Autun, often depict Christ in Majesty or the Last Judgment, surrounded by a mandorla and flanked by figures in the archivolts. Gothic tympana, framed by pointed arches, frequently show the Virgin Mary or scenes from the lives of saints.

Later periods

Renaissance and Baroque architects returned the tympanum to the classical pediment, while in domestic architecture the term is also applied to the area above a door enclosed by a semi-circular fanlight.
