Accelerated approval

Accelerated approval is a regulatory pathway of the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) that allows drugs and biological products for serious or life-threatening conditions to be approved earlier, on the basis of a surrogate endpoint — a laboratory measurement, radiographic image or other marker thought to predict clinical benefit — rather than direct evidence that patients live longer or feel better. In exchange, manufacturers must conduct post-approval confirmatory trials to verify the predicted benefit; if the trials fail, the approval can be withdrawn.

History

The pathway was established in 1992 in the FDA's "Subpart H" regulations, driven by the HIV/AIDS crisis and demands for faster access to antiretroviral drugs. It was later codified by the FDA Safety and Innovation Act of 2012, which extended the approach, now called accelerated approval, across drugs and biologics (Subpart E for biologics). Most accelerated approvals have been in oncology, where tumour response rates serve as surrogates for survival, but the pathway has also been used for HIV, rare diseases and neurology.

Controversies

The pathway has been debated because confirmatory trials have sometimes been delayed for years, leaving medicines marketed without proven clinical benefit. High-profile cases include the cancer drug bevacizumab's breast-cancer indication (approved 2008, withdrawn 2011 after confirmatory trials failed) and the Alzheimer's disease antibody aducanumab (Aduhelm), approved in 2021 on the surrogate of amyloid plaque reduction despite opposition from an FDA advisory committee; several advisers resigned in protest and the drug was later discontinued by its manufacturer. Some sponsors have also voluntarily withdrawn indications after failed confirmatory trials, a category sometimes called "dangling" indications.

Reform

The Food and Drug Omnibus Reform Act of 2022 (FDORA) strengthened the FDA's hand: the agency may require confirmatory trials to be under way before approval, set enrolment targets and deadlines, receive progress reports every 180 days, and use an expedited procedure to withdraw approvals when sponsors fail to comply or trials fail to confirm benefit.
