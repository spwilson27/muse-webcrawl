Acer saccharum, the sugar maple, is a species of flowering plant in the soapberry and lychee family Sapindaceae. It is native to the hardwood forests of eastern Canada and the eastern United States, from Nova Scotia west to Ontario and south to Georgia and Texas. It is best known as the primary source of maple syrup and for its brilliant autumn foliage.

Description

The sugar maple is a deciduous tree normally reaching heights of 25–35 m (80–115 ft), exceptionally up to 45 m. The leaves are palmate, 8–15 cm long, typically with five lobes and smooth, rounded sinuses between them; in autumn they turn brilliant shades of yellow, orange, and red. The bark of young trees is smooth and grey, becoming darker and broken into long plates with age. The seeds are paired samaras ("maple keys") that ripen in autumn.

Ecology

Sugar maple is one of the most shade-tolerant of the large deciduous trees; seedlings can persist for years in deep forest shade and respond rapidly when a canopy gap opens. It favours deep, rich, well-drained soils and is a dominant species of the northern hardwood forest. The species is sensitive to road salt, air pollution, and drought stress, and has suffered dieback associated with acid rain in parts of its range.

Uses

In late winter and early spring, the clear, sweet sap is collected by tapping the trunk and boiled down to produce maple syrup; roughly 40 litres of sap yield one litre of syrup. The hard, close-grained wood is valued for flooring, furniture, bowling pins, and musical instruments. Sugar maple is the state tree of New York, Vermont, West Virginia, and Wisconsin, and its leaf is the central emblem of the flag of Canada.
