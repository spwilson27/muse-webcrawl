Battle of Arras (1917)

The Battle of Arras was a British-led offensive on the Western Front of the First World War, fought from 9 April to 16 May 1917 near the town of Arras in northern France. British, Canadian, Australian, New Zealand and South African troops of Allenby's Third Army and Byng's First Army attacked the German defences to support the French Nivelle Offensive further south. The opening assault achieved some of the deepest advances of any Allied attack since trench warfare began — most famously the Canadian Corps' capture of Vimy Ridge — but the offensive degenerated into costly attritional fighting, including the disastrous attacks at Bullecourt, and ended without a strategic breakthrough.

Preparations

The attack was preceded by an immense artillery programme — over 2.8 million shells — and innovative preparations including new fuzes for cutting wire, counter-battery work directed by sound-ranging and aerial observation, and the use of the medieval tunnels and quarries beneath Arras to shelter some 24,000 men who emerged directly into the assault. A short, intense "hurricane" bombardment replaced the week-long preparations of the Somme, achieving greater surprise.

Course of the battle

On 9 April, in snow and sleet, the First Army's Canadian Corps captured Vimy Ridge in four days — a defining moment in Canadian national history — while Third Army divisions advanced up to 5.5 km, taking the village of Monchy-le-Preux and breaching the Hindenburg Line defences near Wancourt. Momentum then stalled against fresher German reserves and elastic defence in depth. Subsequent phases — the Second Battle of the Scarpe (23–24 April), the Battle of Arleux (28–29 April), the Third Battle of the Scarpe (3–4 May) and the two battles of Bullecourt, where Australian divisions suffered heavily against uncut wire and failed tank support — ground on into mid-May at a daily casualty rate higher than that of the Somme.

Assessment

Arras demonstrated major technical progress in artillery-infantry cooperation and produced important early successes, but commanders could not exploit them before German reserves arrived. The battle nonetheless drew German forces away from the French front during the Nivelle Offensive and its aftermath of mutinies. The tank, still unreliable, played a minor role; air fighting above the battle ("Bloody April") cost the Royal Flying Corps dearly against the German Albatros fighters.
