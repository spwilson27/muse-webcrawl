Contact mechanics

Contact mechanics is the study of the deformation of solids that touch each other at one or more points. It is fundamental to mechanical engineering, tribology, and materials science, underpinning the design of bearings, gears, wheels and rails, seals, and electrical contacts. A central distinction in contact mechanics is between stresses in conforming and non-conforming contacts.

FieldContinuum mechanics, tribology
FounderHeinrich Hertz (1882)
Key topicsHertzian contact stress, friction, adhesive contact, rough surfaces

Hertzian contact

The classical theory was published by Heinrich Hertz in 1882, solving the elastic contact between two spheres and related geometries. For a sphere pressed against a half-space, Hertz's theory predicts a circular contact area whose radius grows with the cube root of the load, and a pressure distribution that is maximum at the centre. The theory assumes frictionless, non-adhesive, linear elastic bodies with small strains.

Extensions

Later work extended the theory to adhesive contacts (the JKR and DMT models), viscoelastic materials, plastic deformation, and rough surfaces, notably the statistical models of Greenwood and Williamson and the fractal/persson theories of rough contact. Tangential loading and partial slip were analysed by Cattaneo and Mindlin, forming the basis of fretting analysis. Numerical methods, especially finite element analysis and boundary element methods, are now standard for complex contact problems.

Applications

Contact mechanics informs hardness testing (indentation), gear tooth stress calculations, rolling-element bearing life, rail-wheel wear, and the design of microelectromechanical systems where adhesion can dominate.
