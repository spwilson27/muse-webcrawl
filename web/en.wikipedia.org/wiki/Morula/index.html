Morula

A morula (Latin: morus, mulberry) is an early-stage embryo consisting of 16 cells (called blastomeres) in a solid ball contained within the zona pellucida. It is produced by a series of cleavage divisions of the early embryo, beginning with the single-celled zygote. Once the zygote has divided into 16 cells, it begins the process of differentiation into an inner cell mass and an outer layer, which will become the blastocyst.

Development

In human development, fertilisation occurs in the fallopian tube, and cleavage begins about 30 hours afterwards. After the third cleavage, the blastomeres maximise their contact with each other, forming a compact ball held together by tight junctions — a process called compaction. About three to four days after fertilisation, the dividing cells, still contained in the zona pellucida, acquire a mulberry-like shape, hence the name morula. The internal cells of the morula give rise to the inner cell mass and are totipotent in character, while the external cells form the surrounding trophoblast. The morula enters the uterine cavity, where continued division and fluid accumulation convert it into the blastocyst around day five.

Clinical significance

The morula stage is one of the checkpoints in assisted reproduction: embryos transferred in IVF are typically transferred at the cleavage (morula) or blastocyst stage. Morphological assessment of morulae, together with time-lapse imaging, is used to predict developmental competence.
