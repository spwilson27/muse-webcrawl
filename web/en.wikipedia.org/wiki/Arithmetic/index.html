Arithmetic

[Image: an abacus with wooden beads, beside handwritten addition and multiplication sums on paper]

Arithmetic is the oldest and most elementary branch of mathematics, concerned with numbers and the basic operations on them: addition, subtraction, multiplication and division, along with powers, roots, fractions, percentages and ratios. It is the mathematics of everyday counting and calculation, taught from primary school, and the foundation on which algebra, analysis and the rest of mathematics are built.

The word comes from the Greek arithmos, 'number'. For most of history arithmetic was done with counting boards, the abacus and written algorithms; mechanical calculators and then electronic computers automated it in the twentieth century.

Operations and number systems

Arithmetic is usually developed first for the natural numbers (1, 2, 3, …) and zero, then extended to integers, rational numbers (fractions), real numbers and complex numbers. Core ideas include place value — the Hindu–Arabic numeral system spread by scholars such as Al-Khwarizmi — prime numbers, divisibility, and the properties of operations (commutative, associative and distributive laws).

Modular arithmetic, in which numbers wrap around after a fixed modulus (as on a clock), underlies modern cryptography; it was systematised by Carl Friedrich Gauss in his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801).

In education and daily life

Arithmetic underpins personal finance, commerce, measurement and statistics. Difficulties with arithmetic (dyscalculia) are studied in education and psychology, and mental arithmetic feats and competitions remain popular worldwide.

Within pure mathematics, 'arithmetic' also names the study of the integers themselves — number theory, once called 'higher arithmetic'.
