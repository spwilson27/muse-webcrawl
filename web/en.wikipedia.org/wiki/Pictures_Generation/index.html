The Pictures Generation is the loose grouping of American artists, active from the late 1970s through the 1980s, who came to prominence by appropriating images from mass media — advertising, film, television, and magazines — and re-presenting them to expose how pictures construct meaning, identity, and desire. The name derives from "Pictures," a small but landmark exhibition organised by critic Douglas Crimp at Artists Space in New York in 1977, featuring Troy Brauntuch, Jack Goldstein, Sherrie Levine, Robert Longo, and Philip Smith; Crimp's accompanying essay (revised for October journal in 1979) gave the emerging tendency its theoretical frame.

Approach and major figures

Raised on television and saturated in advertising imagery, these artists treated the mediated image — not nature — as their primary reality, and asked how representations shape the self. Sherrie Levine's rephotographs of Walker Evans's Depression-era photographs (1981) posed a frontal challenge to modernist notions of originality and authorship. Richard Prince cropped and rephotographed Marlboro cigarette advertisements, isolating the cowboy as a ready-made icon of fabricated masculinity. Cindy Sherman's Untitled Film Stills (1977–80) — black-and-white photographs of the artist herself costumed as generic film heroines — anatomised the stereotypes available to women in cinema. Barbara Kruger combined found photographs with aggressive second-person texts ("I shop therefore I am"; "Your body is a battleground") in the graphic idiom of advertising itself. Louise Lawler photographed artworks in collectors' homes and auction houses to expose the institutional framing of value; Jack Goldstein and Robert Longo worked with film loops and monumental drawings of media figures; Dara Birnbaum remixed television footage in video works such as Technology/Transformation: Wonder Woman (1978–79).

Context and reception

The Pictures artists emerged from the New York downtown scene and from the CalArts conceptual programme of John Baldessari, in dialogue with French poststructuralist theory (Barthes's "death of the author," Baudrillard's simulacra) and with feminist critiques of representation. Galleries such as Metro Pictures (founded 1980) marketed the group as the cutting edge of postmodernism, and by the late 1980s its members were central — and highly priced — figures of the contemporary art world, an irony of anti-commodity critique absorbed by the market that critics noted at the time and since.

Legacy

The term was canonised by the Metropolitan Museum of Art's 2009 exhibition "The Pictures Generation, 1974–1984." The group's strategies — sampling, re-staging, and the critique of images through images — are widely seen as anticipating the remix culture of the internet age, and its members (Sherman, Prince, Kruger, Lawler) remain among the most influential living artists.
