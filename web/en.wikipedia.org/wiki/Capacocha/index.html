Capacocha (from Quechua qhapaq hucha, "solemn crime or sacrifice") was an important sacrificial rite of the Inca Empire in which children were sacrificed to the gods, typically on high mountain summits. The rite was performed on significant occasions, such as the death or illness of the Sapa Inca, natural disasters, or major imperial events.

The rite

Children chosen for capacocha were selected for their physical perfection and came from communities throughout the empire. They were brought to Cusco, feasted, and then sent in procession back toward their home regions or to designated sacred mountains. At the summit shrines, after being given maize beer (chicha) and coca, the children were killed — by a blow to the head, strangulation, or exposure — and buried with miniature figurines of gold, silver and Spondylus shell, textiles, and pottery.

Archaeological evidence

The frozen, remarkably preserved bodies of capacocha victims have been recovered from peaks including Llullaillaco in Argentina, where the "Llullaillaco children" were found in 1999 at over 6,700 metres, and Cerro El Plomo in Chile. Analysis of hair samples has revealed changes in diet and the administration of coca and alcohol in the months before death.
