Anti-aliasing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anti-aliasing refers to a family of techniques in digital signal processing and computer graphics used to reduce aliasing — the distortion that occurs when a high-resolution signal is represented at a lower resolution, producing jagged edges ("jaggies"), moiré patterns or shimmering. In graphics, anti-aliasing smooths the appearance of edges by partially shading pixels along boundaries in proportion to their coverage.

Methods

Supersampling (SSAA) renders the image at a higher resolution and downsamples it — the most accurate but most expensive method. Multisampling (MSAA), widely used in real-time 3D graphics, samples geometry coverage at multiple points per pixel while shading once. Post-processing approaches such as FXAA and SMAA detect and blur edges in the finished image, trading accuracy for speed. Temporal anti-aliasing (TAA) combines information from previous frames, and machine-learning methods such as NVIDIA's DLSS reconstruct high-resolution images from lower-resolution renders. In typography, font hinting and grayscale or subpixel rendering (such as ClearType) serve the same smoothing purpose for text.

Background

Aliasing arises from the sampling theorem: signals containing detail finer than half the sampling rate cannot be represented faithfully. Anti-aliasing filtering before sampling, or coverage-based shading afterwards, minimises the visible artefacts.
