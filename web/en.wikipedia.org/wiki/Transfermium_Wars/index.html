Transfermium Wars

Superheavy element synthesis at a heavy-ion accelerator.

The Transfermium Wars were a series of controversies and rival claims about the discovery of the transfermium elements — the chemical elements with atomic numbers 104 to 109, beyond fermium (element 100) — that pitted research groups in the United States and the Soviet Union against one another during the Cold War. The disputes concerned both priority of discovery and the names the new elements should bear.

The disputes

Laboratories at Berkeley, California, and at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna, USSR, announced competing syntheses of elements 104 and 105 in the late 1960s. Berkeley proposed the names rutherfordium and hahnium; Dubna proposed kurchatovium and nielsbohrium. Similar clashes followed for elements 106–109, involving also the GSI laboratory at Darmstadt, Germany, which synthesised elements 107, 108 and 109 in the 1980s.

Resolution

In 1992 an IUPAC/IUPAP Transfermium Working Group assessed the competing claims and apportioned credit. A compromise set of names was finally adopted by IUPAC in 1997: rutherfordium (104), dubnium (105), seaborgium (106), bohrium (107), hassium (108) and meitnerium (109) — names honouring both the places and the scientists of the rival laboratories.

Legacy

The affair exposed the weakness of discovery claims based on a handful of atoms and led to more rigorous, internationally agreed criteria for recognising new elements, applied to all subsequent additions to the periodic table.
