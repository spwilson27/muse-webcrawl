The Battle of Dunbar was fought on 3 September 1650 near Dunbar, Scotland, between the English New Model Army under Oliver Cromwell and a Scottish Covenanter army under David Leslie. It was one of the most remarkable victories of the Wars of the Three Kingdoms: a hungry, sick and outnumbered English army, apparently trapped against the sea, destroyed the Scots in a dawn attack.

The dawn assault from the Broxburn defile, 3 September 1650.

Background

After the execution of Charles I, Scotland proclaimed his son Charles II king. Cromwell invaded in July 1650, but Leslie refused battle, held the strong position on Doon Hill, and cut English supplies until Cromwell's army, weakened by sickness, retreated to Dunbar, seemingly cornered. On 2 September, Leslie descended from the hill to close in for the kill, abandoning his unassailable position.

The battle

Cromwell saw the error and, on the night of 2–3 September, secretly redeployed his best troops across the Brox Burn to strike the Scottish right wing at dawn. In a short, violent fight the English horse and foot rolled up the Scottish line before much of it could deploy. Around 3,000 Scots were killed and some 10,000 captured, at a cost of only a few dozen English lives. Cromwell called it "one of the most signal mercies God hath done for England and His people".

Aftermath

Edinburgh and most of lowland Scotland fell to the English. Many Scottish prisoners were marched south to Durham, where thousands died of disease and starvation in the cathedral; others were transported to the American colonies and Bermuda. The defeat led, a year later, to Charles II's invasion of England and his final defeat at Worcester — fought, by coincidence, on 3 September 1651.
