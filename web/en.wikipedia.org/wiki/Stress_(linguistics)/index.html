In linguistics, and particularly phonology, stress or accent is the relative emphasis or prominence given to a certain syllable in a word or to a certain word in a phrase or sentence. That emphasis is typically caused by such properties as increased loudness and vowel length, full articulation of the vowel, and changes in tone.

Realisation

Stress is realised through a combination of acoustic cues. In English, stressed syllables tend to be pronounced with higher pitch, greater duration, greater intensity, and an unreduced ("full") vowel, while unstressed syllables often reduce their vowel to schwa /ə/. The precise weighting of these cues differs between languages, and some languages (such as French) have only weak or phrase-final stress, while tonal languages may rely on pitch in other ways.

Lexical stress

In languages with lexical stress, the position of stress is part of the specification of a word and can distinguish meaning. English has many such pairs, usually noun–verb contrasts: REcord (noun) versus reCORD (verb), or IMport versus imPORT. In other languages stress is fixed: on the first syllable in Czech and Finnish, on the penultimate syllable in Polish, and typically on the final syllable in French phrases.

Prosodic stress

Beyond individual words, speakers place emphasis on particular words in an utterance for focus or contrast ("I never said she stole my money" changes meaning depending on which word is emphasised). This sentence-level prominence is studied under prosody and intonation.

Notation

The International Phonetic Alphabet marks primary stress with a raised vertical line ˈ before the stressed syllable, and secondary stress with ˌ: e.g. photograph /ˈfoʊtəɡrɑːf/, photographic /ˌfoʊtəˈɡræfɪk/.
