The Angora goat is a breed of domestic goat named after Ankara, the capital of Turkey (historically Angora). It is bred principally for its fleece, mohair — a long, lustrous, curly fibre taken from no other goat breed. Angora goats should not be confused with Angora rabbits, which produce Angora wool.

Description

Angoras are relatively small, slender goats with long, drooping ears and, in both sexes, horns — the bucks' horns being heavy, twisted, and swept back. Apart from the face and legs, the body is covered in a dense coat of white (occasionally coloured) ringlets that grow continuously and are shorn twice a year, yielding roughly 2–3 kg of mohair per shearing. The finest fibre comes from kids; fibre coarsens as the animal ages.

History

The breed was developed in Anatolia over centuries; mohair cloth was traded from the region from at least the early modern period, and the Ottoman authorities long restricted export of the live animals to protect the industry. In the 19th century goats were exported to South Africa (from 1838) and to the United States (from 1849). South Africa's Karoo region and the Edwards Plateau of Texas became the world's leading mohair districts, and South Africa now produces about half of world output.

Husbandry

Angoras are less hardy than most goats: their fleece offers poor protection when newly shorn against cold rain, and they are susceptible to parasites and nutritional stress, requiring good feeding — particularly in late pregnancy and lactation — to keep fibre production up. They browse shrubs and are sometimes used for rangeland brush control.
