Evening Prayer (Anglican)

TraditionAnglican

Evening Prayer is a liturgy in use in the Anglican Communion (and other traditions), one of the two main daily offices in the Book of Common Prayer, the other being Morning Prayer. The service in its traditional form — often sung as Choral Evensong — is largely derived from the medieval offices of Vespers and Compline, combined by Thomas Cranmer in the first Prayer Book of 1549 and revised in 1552 and 1662.

Structure

The traditional order of Evening Prayer includes opening sentences of Scripture, an exhortation, a general confession and absolution, the Lord's Prayer, versicles and responses ("O Lord, open thou our lips"), psalms appointed for the day, two lessons from the Bible (one from the Old Testament and one from the New), the canticles Magnificat (after the first lesson) and Nunc Dimittis (after the second), the Apostles' Creed, prayers including the lesser litany and collects for the day and for peace and aid against perils, and — in choral services — an anthem.

Choral Evensong

When sung by a choir, most of the service is chanted or sung to settings by composers in a long tradition that includes Byrd, Tallis, Stanford, Howells, and many others. The preces and responses, psalm chants (Anglican chant), and paired settings of Magnificat and Nunc Dimittis form the core of the repertoire. Choral Evensong remains a daily feature of many British cathedrals and college chapels, such as those of King's College, Cambridge, and has been broadcast weekly by the BBC since 1926.

Modern revisions

Modern Anglican liturgies, such as the Church of England's Common Worship (2000), provide Evening Prayer in both traditional and contemporary language, with more flexible structure, while the 1662 order continues in widespread use.
