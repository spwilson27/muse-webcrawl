Methodism is a group of historically related Christian denominations deriving from the 18th-century revival movement within the Church of England led by John Wesley and his brother Charles. Its name came from the "methodical" devotions of the Wesleys' Oxford "Holy Club". John Wesley's itinerant preaching, organisation of converts into classes and societies, and Arminian theology — emphasising free grace available to all, assurance of salvation, and growth toward Christian perfection — created a disciplined lay movement that, after his death, separated from the Church of England.

History and spread

Carried to America by missionaries and circuit riders such as Francis Asbury, Methodism became the largest Protestant tradition in the 19th-century United States and a powerful force in Britain's working-class communities, Welsh and Cornish life, and worldwide missions to Africa, Asia and the Pacific, where Methodist churches remain major presences (notably in Korea, Fiji and South Africa). The movement spawned many bodies — in Britain the Methodist Church of Great Britain; in the US the United Methodist Church (formed 1968), African Methodist Episcopal and other historically Black denominations (born partly of resistance to segregation), and the Wesleyan and Nazarene holiness churches. The Salvation Army grew from Methodist roots.

Beliefs and ethos

Methodists share classical Protestant doctrine, adding Wesleyan emphases: prevenient grace, justification and new birth, and sanctification. The "Wesleyan Quadrilateral" — Scripture, tradition, reason and experience as theological sources — is a later summary of Wesley's practice. Methodism is associated with hymn-singing (Charles Wesley wrote thousands of hymns, including "Hark! The Herald Angels Sing"), temperance, education and social action.
