Shock tactics

Shock tactics are military tactics designed to overwhelm an enemy by sudden, concentrated force — breaking their cohesion and will to resist through the psychological shock of the blow as much as through physical destruction. The attacker seeks a rapid decision before the defender can react or bring up reserves.

Historical forms

The archetypal shock weapon was heavy cavalry: from the cataphracts and knights of antiquity and the Middle Ages, whose couched-lance charge could shatter infantry formations, to the cuirassiers and lancers of the Napoleonic era. Infantry could also deliver shock, as in the Macedonian phalanx's advance, the bayonet charge, and the "forlorn hopes" thrown against breaches. Shock depended on morale: a charge that faltered usually failed, while steady troops who received it — like British squares at Waterloo — could defeat it.

Firepower era

Rifled firearms and artillery made frontal shock costly, pushing armies toward fire tactics; yet commanders repeatedly sought to restore shock, through infiltration tactics in the First World War and then through mechanised warfare. The German Blitzkrieg of 1939–41 applied shock with tanks, motorised infantry, and close air support concentrated at a breakthrough point (Schwerpunkt). The United States' 2003 "shock and awe" doctrine in Iraq was an explicit modern formulation, aiming at rapid dominance through massive, simultaneous strikes.

Shock troops

Units specially organised and trained for shock action include historical assault detachments such as the German stormtroopers of 1917–18 and modern special forces and airborne units used to seize key points by sudden attack.
