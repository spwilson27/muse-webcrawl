Consociationalism

Consociationalism is a form of democratic power-sharing government that guarantees group representation and is often adopted to manage deep social, ethnic, religious, or linguistic divisions. The concept was formalised by political scientist Arend Lijphart, drawing on the pillarised Netherlands and other divided societies.

Characteristics

Lijphart identified four key features: (1) government by a grand coalition of leaders of all significant segments; (2) mutual veto for minorities on vital issues; (3) proportionality in representation, public appointments, and funding; and (4) segmental autonomy — each group running its own internal affairs, often through federalism or separate schools and media.

Examples

  • Netherlands (1917–1967 pillarisation), Belgium, Switzerland
  • Northern Ireland under the 1998 Good Friday Agreement
  • Bosnia and Herzegovina under the Dayton Agreement
  • Lebanon's confessional system
  • Post-2003 Iraq (muhasasa system, often described as consociational in practice)

Criticism

Critics argue consociationalism entrenches ethnic identities, rewards elite cartels, produces gridlock (e.g., repeated collapses of the Northern Ireland Executive), and fits poorly where groups are not clearly bounded. Defenders reply it can prevent violence and give minorities security that simple majoritarianism cannot.
