Kenneth Frampton CBE (born 20 November 1930) is a British architect, critic, historian, and scholar of modernist and contemporary architecture. He is Ware Professor of Architecture at the Graduate School of Architecture, Planning and Preservation at Columbia University in New York, where he has taught since 1972. His survey Modern Architecture: A Critical History (1980) is one of the most widely used histories of the modern movement.

Early life and career

Frampton was born in Woking and trained at the Architectural Association in London, graduating in 1956. He worked in architectural practice in London, including with the Middlesex County Council and Douglas Stephen and Partners, and spent a period in Israel. From 1962 to 1965 he edited the journal Architectural Design. He taught at the Royal College of Art and the Architectural Association before moving to the United States in 1965, teaching at Princeton University and then Columbia.

Critical regionalism

In his 1983 essay "Towards a Critical Regionalism: Six Points for an Architecture of Resistance," published in Hal Foster's anthology The Anti-Aesthetic, Frampton proposed a mediation between universal modernisation and local culture: an architecture grounded in topography, climate, light, and tectonic form that resists both placeless international modernism and sentimental vernacular revival. Drawing on the phenomenology of Martin Heidegger and the Frankfurt School, the concept became highly influential in architectural theory and practice worldwide.
