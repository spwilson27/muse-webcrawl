Beau Brummell

George Bryan "Beau" Brummell (1778–1840), arbiter of men's fashion in Regency England.

George Bryan "Beau" Brummell (7 June 1778 – 30 March 1840) was an important figure in Regency England and, for many years, the arbiter of British men's fashion. At one time he was a close friend of the Prince Regent, the future King George IV, but after the two quarrelled, and Brummell fell into debt, he fled to France, eventually dying poor and insane in Caen.

Influence on fashion

Brummell revolutionised men's dress by abandoning the elaborate, decorated styles of the eighteenth century in favour of understated, perfectly tailored clothing: dark coats, full-length trousers rather than knee breeches, immaculate linen shirts, and elaborately knotted cravats. He reputedly took five hours a day to dress and recommended that boots be polished with champagne. His doctrine of studied simplicity — that a gentleman should be noticed for being well dressed rather than for dressing noticeably — shaped the modern ideal of the men's suit.

Later life and death

Gambling debts forced Brummell to flee England for Calais in 1816. He later obtained, through friends, a consulship in Caen, but the post was abolished and he fell into deep poverty and mental illness, dying in an asylum in 1840. He remains the archetype of the dandy, a figure celebrated in literature and in the philosophy of dandyism developed by writers such as Barbey d'Aurevilly and Baudelaire.
