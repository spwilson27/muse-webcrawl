The FAIR principles are guidelines to improve the Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reusability of digital assets, particularly research data. They were formulated at a Lorentz Center workshop in Leiden and published by Mark Wilkinson and co-authors in the journal Scientific Data in 2016. A central goal is that data should be understandable and actionable by machines as well as humans, enabling computational reuse at scale.

The principles

Findable: data and metadata are assigned globally unique persistent identifiers (such as DOIs), described with rich metadata, and registered in searchable resources. Accessible: data can be retrieved by identifier using standardized, open protocols — possibly with authentication where necessary — and metadata remain available even if the data are withdrawn. Interoperable: data use formal, shared languages for knowledge representation, FAIR vocabularies, and qualified references to other data. Reusable: data carry clear licences, detailed provenance, and conform to domain-relevant community standards.

Adoption

The principles have been endorsed by the G7, the European Commission (which requires FAIR data management in Horizon Europe), national funders, publishers, and infrastructure initiatives such as the European Open Science Cloud and GO FAIR. FAIR applies to datasets, metadata, software, and other research objects. FAIR is frequently conflated with open data, but the principles explicitly allow restricted access where legal or ethical constraints require it — "as open as possible, as closed as necessary." Complementary frameworks, such as the CARE Principles for Indigenous data governance, address collective benefit and authority to control.
