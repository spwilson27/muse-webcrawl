Self-categorization theory

Self-categorization theory is a theory in social psychology developed by John Turner and colleagues, describing the conditions under which people perceive themselves as individuals or as members of a social group. First set out in the 1980s, notably in the 1987 book Rediscovering the Social Group, it extends Henri Tajfel's social identity theory by specifying the cognitive process — self-categorization — that underlies group behaviour.

Core claims

People categorize themselves at different levels of abstraction: as unique individuals (personal identity), as members of social groups (social identity), or as human beings. Which level is salient depends on the comparative context and the perceiver's readiness. Categorization follows the meta-contrast principle: a set of people is likely to be perceived as a group when differences between them are smaller than differences between them and others in the context. When a social identity is salient, self-perception becomes depersonalized — people see themselves less as idiosyncratic individuals and more as interchangeable exemplars of the group prototype — which produces phenomena such as conformity to group norms, stereotyping, group cohesion, polarization, and shared leadership.

Applications and influence

Together with social identity theory, self-categorization theory forms the "social identity approach" that has been applied to leadership, organizational behaviour, health, crowds, politics, and intergroup conflict. Unlike models that treat deindividuation as a loss of self, the theory argues that crowds and group members shift from personal to social identity, remaining rule-governed — but by group norms rather than personal ones. Critics have questioned the testability of the meta-contrast principle and the theory's treatment of motivation, but it remains one of the most influential accounts of the psychological group.
