Perseids

Perseids
Parent body: Comet Swift–Tuttle
Radiant: Perseus
Active: mid-July to late August
Peak: 12–13 August
Zenithal hourly rate: ~60–100

The Perseids are a prolific meteor shower associated with the comet Swift–Tuttle. The meteors are called the Perseids because their radiant — the point from which they appear to originate — lies in the constellation Perseus, named after the Greek hero.

Observing

The shower is active from mid-July to late August each year, with the peak around 11–13 August, when rates can reach 60 to over 100 meteors per hour under dark skies. The Perseids are popular with amateur astronomers because they occur during warm Northern Hemisphere summer nights. They are best viewed in the pre-dawn hours, away from light pollution.

Origin

The meteors are debris shed by Comet Swift–Tuttle, a periodic comet with a 133-year orbit that last passed perihelion in 1992. Each August, Earth passes through the stream of particles along the comet's orbit; the grains, most no larger than sand, burn up in the atmosphere at about 59 km/s. Historical records of the shower go back roughly two thousand years, with early observations in China, and in European folklore the Perseids were known as the "tears of Saint Lawrence", whose feast day falls on 10 August.

Outbursts

Enhanced activity occurred in the early 1990s around the parent comet's return, and smaller outbursts are sometimes predicted when Earth encounters denser filaments of the stream.
