Buryats

Placeholder image: Buryat people in traditional dress.

The Buryats (Buryat: Буряад, Buryaad) are a Mongolic ethnic group native to southeastern Siberia who speak the Buryat language. They are one of the two largest indigenous groups in Siberia, the other being the Yakuts. The majority of the Buryat population lives in Russia in the Republic of Buryatia, a federal subject south and east of Lake Baikal. Significant communities also live in northern Mongolia, where they are recognized as a Mongolian subgroup, and in China's Inner Mongolia region.

History

The Buryats historically inhabited the lands around Lake Baikal and were closely related to the Mongols. Following the expansion of the Russian Empire in the 17th century, the Buryat lands were incorporated into Russia, a process that separated many Buryats from other Mongolic peoples. Some Buryats migrated south into Mongolia during the Russian Civil War and the Stalinist period. During the Soviet era, the Buryat-Mongolian Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic was established; its name was shortened to the Buryat ASSR in 1958.

Culture

Traditionally nomadic herders, Buryats kept cattle, sheep, goats, camels and horses. Their religious life blends Tibetan Buddhism — introduced from Mongolia from the 17th century onward — with indigenous shamanic practices centred on sacred sites around Lake Baikal, especially Olkhon Island and the sacred mountain Burkhan. The traditional dwelling is the ger (yurt), and epic poetry, throat-influenced singing styles and the festival of Naadam-like games remain part of cultural life.

Language

The Buryat language belongs to the Mongolic language family and is written in the Cyrillic script in Russia, while Buryats in Mongolia may use the traditional Mongolian script or Cyrillic. UNESCO classifies Buryat as endangered, as Russian dominates education and public life in Buryatia, though language-revival efforts continue.
