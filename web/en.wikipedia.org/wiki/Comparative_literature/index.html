Comparative literature

Works from many languages and traditions side by side.

Comparative literature is the academic study of literature across national and linguistic boundaries, and frequently across media and disciplines as well. Comparatists study translation, influence and reception, genres and movements, themes and motifs, and the relations between literature and other arts or fields such as philosophy, history, film, and music—situating texts within an international, sometimes global, frame that later came to be discussed under the heading of world literature.

History

The discipline coalesced in nineteenth-century Europe amid the comparative methods then flourishing in philology and anatomy; Goethe's notion of Weltliteratur (1827) is a frequent point of reference. Distinct "schools" emerged: a French school emphasizing documented influence and factual relations, and a postwar American school (associated with René Wellek's 1959 manifesto "The Crisis of Comparative Literature") favoring parallel and interdisciplinary study. Repeated reports on the state of the discipline have provoked debates about its Eurocentrism and methods.

Contemporary directions

From the late twentieth century, comparative literature absorbed postcolonial studies, translation studies, and cultural studies, extending its corpus to non-Western literatures and to media such as film and digital texts. Scholars such as Edward Said, Gayatri Spivak, and David Damrosch reshaped discussions of empire, translation, and world literature, while critics periodically question the field's institutional identity.
