Constantine II (Medieval Gaelic: Causantín mac Áeda; c. 874–952) was an early King of Scotland, then known as Alba. His reign, like those of his predecessors, was dominated by the actions of the Viking rulers of the British Isles, particularly the Uí Ímair ("Grandsons of Ímar"). During Constantine's long reign, the words Scots and Scotland (Old English: Scottas, Scotland) came into use in Scotland for the first time.

Reign

Constantine became king in 900 after the death of his cousin Donald II. His reign of 43 years was one of the longest in Scottish history. In 904 he defeated a Viking army at Strathearn. In 918 he led the Scots in the Battle of Corbridge against Norse-Gael forces, retaining his throne despite a bloody battle.

In 927 Constantine acknowledged Æthelstan of England as overlord at Eamont Bridge, but relations deteriorated; in 934 Æthelstan invaded Scotland. Constantine joined a coalition with Olaf Guthfrithson of Dublin and Owain of Strathclyde, invading England in 937. The coalition was crushed by Æthelstan at the Battle of Brunanburh, one of the most celebrated battles of early medieval Britain.

Abdication and death

In 943, old and possibly blind, Constantine abdicated and retired to the monastery of St Andrews, where he became abbot of the Céli Dé (Culdees). He died in 952. He was succeeded by Malcolm I. Constantine's long reign consolidated the kingdom of Alba between the Forth and the Moray Firth and saw the church reforms associated with the Céli Dé.
