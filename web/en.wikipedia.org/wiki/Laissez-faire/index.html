Laissez-faire

[Image: a busy 19th-century free-trade port scene with merchants and unloaded goods]

Laissez-faire is the doctrine that governments should minimise interference with economic life — 'let do' (the phrase is French, associated with the circle around Vincent de Gournay in the 1750s and championed by the Physiocrats). It prescribes low taxes, minimal regulation, free markets and Free trade, on the grounds that order emerges spontaneously from self-interest coordinated by prices.

History and influence

The doctrine drew strength from Adam Smith's qualified defence of the 'system of natural liberty' and from 19th-century British practice — repeal of the Corn Laws, Gladstonian finance — and the Manchester School. It was progressively displaced by labour law, social insurance and Keynesian demand management, revived as an ideal (if never fully in practice) by Friedrich Hayek, Milton Friedman and the deregulation era of the 1980s. As rhetoric, laissez-faire denotes one pole of economic argument; as history, no substantial state has practised it completely, since markets themselves rest on state-enforced property, contract and money.
