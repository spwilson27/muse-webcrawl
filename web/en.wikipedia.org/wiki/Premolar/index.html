The premolars, also called premolar teeth or bicuspids, are transitional teeth located between the canine and molar teeth. In humans, there are two premolars per quadrant in the permanent set, making eight premolars in total in the mouth. They have at least two cusps — hence the name "bicuspid" — although the second mandibular premolar commonly has three cusps. Premolars combine the tearing function of canines with the grinding function of molars.

Human anatomy

The premolars are the maxillary first and second premolars and the mandibular first and second premolars. They are permanent teeth that erupt around ages 10–12, replacing the deciduous molars; there are no premolars in the primary dentition.

Maxillary first premolar

The maxillary (upper) first premolar has two cusps, buccal and lingual, with the buccal cusp notably larger. It typically has two roots or a single root with two canals, a feature that complicates root canal treatment. Its crown shape resembles that of the adjacent canine from the buccal aspect.

Maxillary second premolar

The maxillary second premolar has two cusps of more nearly equal size and usually a single root. It often shows less anatomical variation than the first premolar.

Mandibular premolars

The mandibular (lower) first premolar strongly resembles a canine, with one large buccal cusp and a tiny, non-functional lingual cusp. The mandibular second premolar is more molar-like, usually with three cusps — one buccal and two lingual — and a squared occlusal outline. Both mandibular premolars normally have a single root.

Function

Premolars assist the canines in cutting and tearing food and assist the molars in grinding. Their intermediate crown size and cusp arrangement make them important for maintaining the vertical dimension of the bite and for guiding jaw movements.

Other animals

Premolars are a defining feature of mammalian dentition. Their number varies widely among species and is recorded in dental formulas; they are the teeth immediately in front of the molars in both jaws. In many herbivores they are molarised for grinding plant material, while in carnivorans the upper fourth premolar forms part of the carnassial pair used for shearing.
