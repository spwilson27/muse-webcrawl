Custom (law)

[Image: A village assembly applying customary law under a tree in Africa]

Custom, in law, is a practice or rule that is followed by the members of a community so consistently that it acquires the force of law, even without enactment by a legislature or decision by a court. Customary law is one of the oldest sources of law and remains important in many legal systems.

Requirements

For a custom to be recognised in English common law it traditionally must have existed since time immemorial (fixed by statute at 1189), have been continuous, peaceable, and reasonable, and must be certain and obligatory in character. Local customs — such as rights of way or fishing rights peculiar to a district — could override the general common law if these tests were met.

Customary international law

In international law, customary law is one of the principal sources listed in the Statute of the International Court of Justice. It arises from two elements: general state practice, and opinio juris — the belief that the practice is legally obligatory. Rules such as diplomatic immunity and much of the law of the sea developed this way before being codified in treaties. A state that persistently objects to an emerging rule may avoid being bound by it.

Customary systems today

Customary law governs land, marriage, and succession for millions of people, particularly in Africa, Asia, and the Pacific, where constitutional systems often recognise it alongside state law. Trade usage and the historical law merchant (lex mercatoria) are further examples of custom crystallising into binding rules.
