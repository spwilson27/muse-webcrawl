Hypogonadism

Hypogonadism
Specialty: Endocrinology
Symptoms: Low libido, infertility, reduced muscle mass, delayed puberty
Types: Primary (hypergonadotropic), secondary (hypogonadotropic)
Treatment: Hormone replacement therapy

Hypogonadism is a medical condition in which the gonads — the testes in males and the ovaries in females — produce little or no sex hormones and, in the case of the testes and ovaries, impaired gamete production. In males the condition usually refers to deficient testosterone production; in females, deficient estrogen and progesterone. Hypogonadism may be congenital or acquired, and may present at puberty (delayed or absent pubertal development) or in adulthood.

Classification

Primary hypogonadism results from failure of the gonads themselves; gonadotropin levels (LH and FSH) are elevated because the pituitary receives no negative feedback. Causes include Klinefelter syndrome, Turner syndrome, gonadal injury, chemotherapy or radiation, infection (e.g., mumps orchitis), and autoimmune damage.

Secondary (central) hypogonadism results from dysfunction of the hypothalamus or pituitary gland, with low or inappropriately normal gonadotropins. Causes include Kallmann syndrome (congenital GnRH deficiency, often with anosmia), pituitary tumors, hyperprolactinemia, hemochromatosis, opioid or glucocorticoid use, severe obesity, and chronic illness. Late-onset hypogonadism in aging men ("andropause") is common and debated as a diagnosis.

Signs and symptoms

In adult males: reduced libido, erectile dysfunction, infertility, loss of muscle mass and body hair, gynecomastia, fatigue, depressed mood, and decreased bone density leading to osteoporosis. In females: amenorrhea, infertility, hot flashes, and low bone mass. In adolescents, the hallmark is delayed or incomplete puberty.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis requires consistently low morning serum testosterone (in men) together with symptoms, plus LH and FSH to localize the defect; prolactin, iron studies, and pituitary imaging may follow. Treatment addresses the underlying cause where possible; otherwise hormone replacement is used — testosterone preparations in men, estrogen/progestogen in women. When fertility is desired in secondary hypogonadism, gonadotropin therapy or pulsatile GnRH can restore gamete production.
