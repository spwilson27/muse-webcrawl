The umbilical vein is a blood vessel of the developing fetus that carries oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood from the placenta to the fetus. It is a single vein — the paired embryonic right umbilical vein normally regresses — and it runs within the umbilical cord alongside the two umbilical arteries.

Cross-section of the umbilical cord: one large umbilical vein and two smaller arteries in Wharton's jelly.

Course and function

Unusually for a vein, the umbilical vein carries oxygenated blood. From the placenta it travels in the cord to the fetal umbilicus, then upward within the free edge of the falciform ligament to the liver. At the porta hepatis about half of its blood passes through the ductus venosus directly into the inferior vena cava, bypassing the hepatic circulation, while the remainder perfuses the liver and reaches the vena cava through the hepatic veins. Oxygen saturation in the umbilical vein is about 80 per cent, the highest in the fetal circulation.

After birth

After the cord is clamped, the umbilical vein obliterates and becomes the round ligament of the liver (ligamentum teres hepatis), running in the falciform ligament to the umbilicus; the ductus venosus becomes the ligamentum venosum. In adults with portal hypertension the paraumbilical veins near the round ligament may recanalise, contributing to caput medusae.

Clinical significance

The umbilical vein is used in neonatal resuscitation and intensive care for emergency vascular access (umbilical venous catheterisation), allowing infusion of fluids and drugs. Cord blood collected from the umbilical vein after delivery is a source of haematopoietic stem cells for transplantation.
