Potts model

In statistical mechanics, the Potts model, a generalization of the Ising model, is a model of interacting spins on a crystalline lattice. It was described by Renfrey Potts in his 1951 PhD thesis, building on a suggestion by his advisor Cyril Domb, who had in turn credited the underlying idea to a 1948 paper context and the related Ashkin–Teller work. By studying the phase transitions of the model, insights can be gained into the behaviour of ferromagnets and certain other phenomena of solid-state physics.

Definition

In the standard Potts model, each lattice site carries a spin that can take one of q values, σ = 1, …, q. The Hamiltonian rewards alignment of neighbouring spins:

H = −J Σ⟨i,j⟩ δ(σi, σj),

where the sum runs over nearest-neighbour pairs, δ is the Kronecker delta, and J > 0 is a ferromagnetic coupling. Unlike the Ising model, all q states are treated symmetrically and there is no notion of "up" versus "down".

Phase transitions

On the two-dimensional square lattice, the model has a phase transition at a critical coupling known exactly through duality: (eK − 1)(eK* − 1) = q, giving a self-dual critical point eKc = 1 + √q. The transition is continuous (second order) for q ≤ 4 and first order for q > 4. The case q = 3 in two dimensions is exactly solvable in part and has been used to model structural transitions, while q = 1 corresponds to percolation.

Related models and applications

The Potts model is equivalent, via the Fortuin–Kasteleyn random-cluster representation, to a generalisation of percolation theory, which supplies much of its mathematical theory. The planar Potts model maps onto the six-vertex and other vertex models. Beyond ferromagnetism, Potts-type Hamiltonians appear in models of grain growth, biological cell sorting (the cellular Potts model), image segmentation, and community detection in networks.
