The Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians (Cherokee: ᏣᎳᎩᏱ ᏕᏣᏓᏂᎸᎩ) is a federally recognized tribe of Cherokee people based in western North Carolina. Its citizens descend primarily from Cherokee who avoided or resisted the forced removal of the 1830s (the Trail of Tears), under which most of the Cherokee Nation was relocated to Indian Territory in present-day Oklahoma.

History

A small number of Cherokee remained in their Appalachian homeland, including a group associated with the trader William Holland Thomas, who purchased land on their behalf that became the nucleus of the Qualla Boundary. Others hid in the mountains from federal troops, and some who had been removed later returned. The Eastern Band organized under a constitution in the 19th century; its land, the Qualla Boundary, is not a reservation in the usual sense but land purchased and held in trust by the federal government, comprising some 57,000 acres adjacent to the Great Smoky Mountains National Park.

Government and economy

The tribe is governed by an elected Principal Chief and a twelve-member Tribal Council representing six communities, including Big Cove, Birdtown, Painttown, Snowbird, Wolftown and Yellowhill. Tourism centred on the town of Cherokee — including the outdoor drama Unto These Hills, the Oconaluftee Indian Village and the Museum of the Cherokee People — long sustained the economy, and since 1997 gaming revenue from Harrah's Cherokee Casino Resort has funded extensive tribal services, infrastructure and per-capita distributions.

Language and culture

The Eastern Band maintains the Kituwah dialect of the Cherokee language. Language revitalisation efforts include the New Kituwah Academy immersion school and master-apprentice programmes. The tribe is one of three federally recognised Cherokee tribes, alongside the Cherokee Nation and the United Keetoowah Band in Oklahoma.
