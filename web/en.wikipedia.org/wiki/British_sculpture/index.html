British sculpture

British sculpture has a history stretching from prehistoric carvings and the anthropomorphic art of the Iron Age Celts, through medieval church carving and the effigies and monuments of the Tudor and Stuart periods, to Britain's leading international role in modernist sculpture in the twentieth century with figures such as Henry Moore and Barbara Hepworth.

Early and medieval sculpture

Surviving early work includes Neolithic and Bronze Age carved stones, Celtic metalwork, Anglo-Saxon crosses such as the Ruthwell Cross, and the wealth of Romanesque and Gothic carving in cathedrals and parish churches — capitals, tympana, misericords and alabaster panels, of which Nottingham alabasters were exported across Europe. Reformation iconoclasm destroyed much medieval religious imagery.

Seventeenth to nineteenth centuries

Funerary monuments dominated post-Reformation sculpture; the eighteenth century saw the rise of the professional sculptor, with artists such as Louis-François Roubiliac, and John Flaxman's neoclassical designs for Wedgwood. In the Victorian era public statuary proliferated, and sculptors such as Alfred Gilbert (the Shaftesbury Memorial Fountain, "Eros", in Piccadilly Circus) led the "New Sculpture" movement toward greater naturalism and symbolism.

Modern and contemporary sculpture

Jacob Epstein's controversial carvings opened British sculpture to modernism. Henry Moore's reclining figures and Barbara Hepworth's pierced abstract forms made Britain central to international modern sculpture after 1945; both are celebrated at the Yorkshire Sculpture Triangle, the Hepworth Wakefield and Tate St Ives. The "Geometry of Fear" generation — Lynn Chadwick, Reg Butler, Eduardo Paolozzi among them — showed at the 1952 Venice Biennale. Later figures include Anthony Caro, Richard Long, Tony Cragg, Anish Kapoor, Damien Hirst, Antony Gormley (creator of the Angel of the North) and Rachel Whiteread, the first woman to win the Turner Prize (1993) with House. Public art commissions such as the Fourth Plinth in Trafalgar Square keep sculpture prominent in national debate.
