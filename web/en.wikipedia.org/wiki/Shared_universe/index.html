Shared universe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A shared universe or shared world is a fictional universe from a set of creative works where more than one writer can contribute works that operate independently of one another but fit into the joint development of the storyline, characters, or world of the overall project. It is common in genres such as science fiction, fantasy, horror and superhero fiction, and is a standard organising structure of modern media franchises.

Characteristics

In a shared universe, works by different creators — novels, films, television series, comics, games — are understood to take place in a single continuity, so that events in one work can affect others, characters cross over between series, and a body of shared "canon" accumulates. The concept overlaps with, but is distinct from, a fictional universe created by a single author, collaborative fiction, and simple crossovers between otherwise separate franchises.

History

Early examples include the mythological cycles of antiquity and the interconnected novels of Balzac's La Comédie humaine and Zola's Rougon-Macquart cycle. In popular fiction, the Cthulhu Mythos developed by H. P. Lovecraft and his circle, and the superhero universes of DC Comics (from the 1940s) and Marvel Comics (from the 1960s), established the modern model in comics. Book publishing developed shared worlds such as the Dragonlance and Forgotten Realms settings and Robert Asprin's Thieves' World anthologies. In film and television, the Universal Monsters cycle and later the Marvel Cinematic Universe — whose box-office success from The Avengers (2012) onward triggered many imitations — demonstrated the commercial power of planned, interlocking continuities.

Criticism and challenges

Maintaining consistency across many creators and years of material requires editorial management, continuity databases ("story bibles") and occasional retroactive continuity changes ("retcons") or reboots. Critics argue that dense interconnection can alienate new audiences and subordinate individual works to franchise maintenance; supporters cite the depth of world-building and long-form storytelling possibilities.

Notable examples

  • Marvel Universe and Marvel Cinematic Universe
  • DC Universe and DC Extended Universe
  • Star Wars expanded continuity ("Legends" and canon)
  • Star Trek
  • The Cthulhu Mythos
  • Stephen King's interconnected "multiverse" of novels
  • The Alien vs. Predator franchise
