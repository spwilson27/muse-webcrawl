Pansori

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pansori (Korean: 판소리) is a Korean genre of musical storytelling performed by a singer (sorikkun) and a drummer (gosu). The term pansori is derived from the Korean words pan (판, "a place where many people gather") and sori (소리, "sound"), meaning a song performed in a public place. It is characterized by expressive singing, stylized speech, and a repertoire of dramatic narratives.

Designated as a National Intangible Cultural Heritage of South Korea and inscribed by UNESCO on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, pansori is regarded as one of the most distinctive performing arts of Korea.

History

Pansori originated in southwestern Korea in the 17th century, probably from shamanic songs and folk entertainment performed at markets and village festivals. During the 18th and 19th centuries it was refined and patronized by the literati, and singers developed long, technically demanding cycles accompanied by a single barrel drum (buk). In the late Joseon period, pansori singers performed for both commoners and aristocratic audiences, and master singers were celebrated figures.

In the 20th century pansori influenced the development of changgeuk, a theatrical form in which the narrative is divided among several singers. Famed 20th-century masters include Song Man-gap and Yi Dong-baek. Pansori was designated Important Intangible Cultural Property No. 5 by the South Korean government in 1964, and UNESCO proclaimed it a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2003.

Performance

A pansori performance consists of a singer and a drummer. The singer narrates, sings, and acts out all the characters of the story using a folding fan as a prop, while the drummer provides rhythmic accompaniment and shouts of encouragement called chuimsae (such as "Eolssigu!" and "Jotda!"). The audience also participates with chuimsae. A full performance of one story may last three to eight hours.

The singer employs a rough, powerful vocal timbre produced through years of rigorous training, traditionally including practice beside waterfalls to strengthen the voice.

Repertoire

Twelve song cycles (madang) were once known, but only five survive in performance today:

  • Chunhyangga — the tale of Chunhyang, a story of love and fidelity
  • Simcheongga — the tale of Simcheong, a filial daughter who sacrifices herself for her blind father
  • Heungbuga — the tale of Heungbu, a kind poor man rewarded for mending a swallow's broken leg
  • Sugungga — the tale of the Dragon King, the turtle, and the rabbit's liver
  • Jeokbyeokga — drawn from the Battle of Red Cliffs in the Romance of the Three Kingdoms

Musical features

Pansori music uses rhythmic cycles called jangdan, ranging from the slow jinyangjo to the fast hwimori, and melodic modes (jo) such as gyemyeonjo, associated with sorrow, and ujeok. Narrative passages mix sung sections (chang) with spoken narration (aniri).
