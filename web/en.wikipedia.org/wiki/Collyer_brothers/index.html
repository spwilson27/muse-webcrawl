Collyer brothers

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Workers clearing the Collyer brownstone in Harlem in 1947.

Homer Lusk Collyer (November 6, 1881 – March 21, 1947) and Langley Wakeman Collyer (October 3, 1885 – c. March 9, 1947), collectively known as the Collyer brothers, were two American brothers who became notorious for their bizarre natures and compulsive hoarding. For decades they lived as recluses in their brownstone mansion at Fifth Avenue and 128th Street in Harlem, New York City, which was packed with over 100 tons of debris, newspapers, furniture, musical instruments, and other accumulated items.

Background

The brothers were born into a wealthy and distinguished family; their father, Herman Livingston Collyer, was a Manhattan gynecologist, and their mother Susie was a descendant of the Livingston family. Both brothers graduated from Columbia University — Homer in law, Langley in engineering and chemistry — and Langley was an accomplished pianist. After their parents' separation and deaths, the brothers withdrew from society. Homer lost his eyesight to hemorrhages in 1933, and Langley devoted himself to caring for him, hoping to restore his sight with a diet of oranges and peanut butter, while roaming the city at night collecting objects.

Deaths and discovery

In March 1947, police, acting on a report of a foul smell, forced their way into the booby-trapped house and found Homer dead in a chair amid tunnels of debris. A manhunt for Langley — feared to be a fugitive — ended weeks later when his body was found only ten feet from Homer's, crushed by one of his own traps; he had apparently died earlier, and rats had disturbed the remains. Langley had been bringing food to his blind brother through the tunnels when a trap fell on him; Homer subsequently starved to death.

Legacy

The Collyer brothers became a byword for extreme hoarding; the term "Collyer syndrome" is sometimes used, and New York firefighters use "Collyers' mansion" as a term for a dwelling crammed with hoarded material and dangerous to enter. Their story inspired works including E. L. Doctorow's 2009 novel Homer & Langley. The site of their house is now a small pocket park named for them.
