Buddhist crisis

Buddhist crisis
DateMay – November 1963
LocationSouth Vietnam
CauseGovernment discrimination against the Buddhist majority under President Ngo Dinh Diem
OutcomeEscalating protests; contributed to the November 1963 coup in which Diem was overthrown and killed

The Buddhist crisis (Vietnamese: Biến cố Phật giáo) was a period of political and religious tension in South Vietnam between May and November 1963, characterised by a series of repressive acts by the government of President Ngo Dinh Diem — a Catholic ruling a country with a Buddhist majority — and a campaign of civil resistance by Buddhist monks and laypeople. The crisis began with the Huế Phật Đản shootings of 8 May 1963, when government forces fired on Buddhists protesting a ban on flying the Buddhist flag on the Buddha's birthday, killing nine people.

Self-immolation and raids

On 11 June 1963 the monk Thích Quảng Đức burned himself to death at a busy Saigon intersection in protest; Malcolm Browne's photograph of the self-immolation became one of the most iconic images of the 20th century and gravely damaged Diem's standing in the United States. In August, special forces loyal to Diem's brother Ngo Dinh Nhu raided pagodas across the country (the Xá Lợi Pagoda raids), arresting hundreds of monks and killing an unknown number.

Consequences

The crackdown destroyed the Kennedy administration's confidence in Diem; the ambiguous "Cable 243" of August 1963 signalled American acquiescence in a change of government. On 1–2 November 1963 South Vietnamese generals overthrew the regime, and Diem and Nhu were assassinated — an event that deepened American involvement in the Vietnam War.
