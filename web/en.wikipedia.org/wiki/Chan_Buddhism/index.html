Chan Buddhism

Chan Buddhism (Chinese: 禪; pinyin: Chán; Japanese: Zen) is a Chinese school of Mahayana Buddhism that developed during the Tang dynasty, influenced by Taoist philosophy. Chan emphasises direct insight into one's own true nature, meditation (dhyana), and the transmission of awakening from master to student outside the written scriptures.

History

Tradition credits the Indian monk Bodhidharma with bringing Chan to China in the 5th or 6th century CE and with a nine-year period of wall-gazing meditation at the Shaolin Monastery. The school took shape over the following centuries through figures such as Huineng, the Sixth Patriarch, whose Platform Sutra became a foundational text.

During the Song dynasty, Chan split into houses including the Linji (Japanese: Rinzai) and Caodong (Japanese: Soto) schools. The Linji school became known for koan practice, while Caodong emphasised silent illumination (mozhao), later shikantaza in Japan.

Practice

Central practices include seated meditation (zuochan, Japanese zazen), koan study under a teacher, and direct pointing to the mind. Chan teaching stresses that awakening is sudden and cannot be attained through conceptual study alone, summarised in the famous phrases "a special transmission outside the scriptures" and "not founded upon words and letters."

Influence

Chan spread to Vietnam (Thiền), Korea (Seon), and Japan (Zen), profoundly influencing East Asian art, calligraphy, tea culture, and martial arts. In the 20th century it attracted wide interest in the West.
