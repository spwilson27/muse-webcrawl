Doggerland

Doggerland
RegionNow submerged beneath the southern North Sea
PeriodMesolithic, c. 10,000–6,500 BC
Named afterDogger Bank; Dutch fishing boats called doggers

Doggerland was an area of land, now submerged beneath the southern North Sea, that connected Great Britain to continental Europe. During the last Ice Age it was a landscape of hills, marshes, wooded valleys and rivers inhabited by Mesolithic hunter-gatherers, sometimes described as a heartland of early post-glacial settlement in north-west Europe.

Submergence

As the climate warmed and sea levels rose after the end of the last glacial period, Doggerland was gradually inundated. The Storegga Slide tsunami, around 6,200 BC, may have devastated remaining coastal settlements. By roughly 6,500–6,000 BC Britain had become an island. The last remnant, around the present Dogger Bank, survived longest as an island before disappearing beneath the waves.

Archaeology

Evidence comes from artefacts and bones dredged up by fishing boats — including a barbed antler point found in 1931 that first demonstrated the land's former habitation — and from seismic surveys conducted for the oil and wind-farm industries, which have mapped its buried river systems, such as the Shotton River. The University of Bradford's "Europe's Lost Frontiers" project has reconstructed its environment in detail from sediment cores and DNA.
