Henry L. Stimson

Died20 October 1950 (aged 83), Huntington, New York
OfficesSecretary of War (1911–1913; 1940–1945); Secretary of State (1929–1933)

Henry Lewis Stimson (September 21, 1867 – October 20, 1950) was an American statesman, lawyer and Republican politician. Over an extraordinary career spanning the first half of the 20th century he served as Secretary of War under Presidents William Howard Taft and Franklin D. Roosevelt, as Governor-General of the Philippines, and as Secretary of State under Herbert Hoover.

Early career

A graduate of Yale (where he joined Skull and Bones) and Harvard Law School, Stimson became a partner in the New York firm of Root and Clark and served as U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of New York under Theodore Roosevelt, prosecuting antitrust cases. As Taft's Secretary of War (1911–1913) he reorganised the Army's general staff. He served as a colonel of artillery in the First World War.

Secretary of State and the Stimson Doctrine

As Hoover's Secretary of State (1929–1933), Stimson negotiated at the London Naval Conference and confronted Japan's invasion of Manchuria in 1931. His response — the Stimson Doctrine of 1932 — declared that the United States would not recognise any territorial changes effected by force, a principle that became a lasting element of international law.

Second World War

In 1940 Roosevelt, seeking bipartisan unity, appointed the 72-year-old Stimson Secretary of War. He oversaw the vast mobilisation of the U.S. Army, the Manhattan Project and the strategic direction of the war, and he supervised the deliberations that led to the use of atomic bombs against Japan, defending that decision in a famous 1947 Harper's article. He also argued — on the moral-grounded advice he gave against fire-bombing Kyoto, a city he knew from his travels — for removing it from the target list. Retiring in September 1945, he wrote his memoir On Active Service in Peace and War (1948) with McGeorge Bundy.

Legacy

Stimson is remembered as the archetype of the bipartisan American establishment statesman. The Henry L. Stimson Center, a Washington think tank, bears his name.
