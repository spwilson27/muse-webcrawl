Abyssal gigantism

In zoology, abyssal gigantism (also called deep-sea gigantism) is the tendency for species of deep-sea dwelling animals, particularly invertebrates, to grow larger than their shallower-water relatives. Examples include the giant isopod, the Japanese spider crab, the colossal squid, and the oarfish.

Examples

Well-known cases include the giant isopod Bathynomus giganteus, which can reach 50 cm in length compared with a few centimetres for typical isopods; the Japanese spider crab, with a leg span of up to 3.7 m; the giant amphipod Alicella gigantea; and the colossal squid, estimated at up to 10–14 m. In polar waters a related phenomenon, polar gigantism, is seen in sea spiders and other invertebrates.

Proposed explanations

Several factors have been proposed. Colder temperatures slow metabolism and may delay sexual maturity, allowing longer growth (consistent with Bergmann's rule). Scarce food may favour larger bodies with lower mass-specific metabolic rates and the ability to store energy and survive long fasts. Reduced predation pressure in the deep sea may also permit longer lifespans and continued growth.
