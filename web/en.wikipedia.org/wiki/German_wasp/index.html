German wasp

Scientific name: Vespula germanica
Family: Vespidae
Order: Hymenoptera
Native range: Europe, North Africa, temperate Asia
Diet: Insects, nectar, fruit, carrion, sugary foods

The German wasp (Vespula germanica), also called the German yellowjacket, is a species of social wasp native to Europe and widely introduced elsewhere, including North and South America, South Africa, Australia, and New Zealand. It is a well-known pest species because of its aggressive defence of food and its sting.

Description

Queens measure about 18 mm in length and workers 12–14 mm. The abdomen bears the classic black-and-yellow banding of yellowjackets; in V. germanica the yellow bands typically carry black spots and the face has three small black dots, helping distinguish it from the common wasp (Vespula vulgaris). Like other vespines it can sting repeatedly.

Life cycle

Colonies are annual in the native range. A fertilised queen overwinters, founds a paper nest in spring — often underground in an abandoned rodent burrow, but also in walls and roof spaces — and rears sterile female workers. By late summer a nest may contain several thousand wasps; new queens and males are produced in autumn. In mild climates in New Zealand, colonies sometimes overwinter and grow much larger, making the species a serious invasive problem.

Behaviour and impact

Workers hunt other insects to feed the larvae and scavenge widely for carbohydrates, including ripe fruit and human food and drinks, bringing them into conflict with people at picnics and vineyards. In introduced ranges the wasp competes with native birds and insects for honeydew and prey.
