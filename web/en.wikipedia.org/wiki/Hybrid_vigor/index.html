Hybrid vigor

Hybrid vigor, or heterosis, is the improved or increased function of any biological quality in a hybrid offspring. An offspring is heterotic if its traits are enhanced as a result of mixing the genetic contributions of its parents — for example, a hybrid plant may grow faster, taller or with greater yield than either inbred parent line, and hybrid animals may show increased size, fertility or disease resistance.

Genetic mechanisms

Three main hypotheses have been proposed to explain heterosis. The dominance hypothesis attributes it to the masking of harmful recessive alleles from one parent by dominant alleles from the other. The overdominance hypothesis holds that heterozygosity at some loci is itself advantageous. Epistasis — favourable interactions between genes at different loci — also contributes. Modern research indicates that all three mechanisms, together with epigenetic effects, play roles depending on the trait and organism.

Applications

Heterosis is exploited extensively in agriculture. Hybrid seed, developed in maize breeding in the early twentieth century by workers such as George Harrison Shull and Edward East, underlies the large yield gains of hybrid corn, and hybrid varieties are widely used in crops such as rice, sorghum, sunflower and many vegetables, as well as in livestock crossing schemes. The opposite phenomenon, reduced fitness from crossing, is called outbreeding depression, while the decline seen in inbred lines is inbreeding depression.
