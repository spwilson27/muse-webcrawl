Armillaria ostoyae, commonly known as the dark honey fungus, is a species of fungus in the family Physalacriaceae. It is a plant pathogen that causes Armillaria root disease in many coniferous and hardwood forests of western North America and Europe. The species is notable because a single individual of A. ostoyae in Malheur National Forest in eastern Oregon is among the largest and oldest known living organisms on Earth.

The Malheur National Forest individual

A widely reported individual in the Blue Mountains of Oregon covers roughly 3.4 square miles (about 8.9 km², or some 2,200–2,400 acres) and is estimated to be between about 2,000 and 8,500 years old. DNA sampling and vegetative compatibility testing showed that samples collected across this area belong to a single genetic individual connected by an underground network of mycelium. By mass and area it is often cited as the largest organism in the world, although comparably large clonal organisms such as the quaking aspen clone Pando and the seagrass Posidonia oceanica are also cited, depending on the criterion used.

Description

The fruiting bodies are medium-sized mushrooms that appear in clusters at the bases of trees and stumps in autumn. The cap is brownish to honey-coloured with darker scales, and the stem bears a prominent white ring (annulus). The gills are white and become spotted with age. Beneath the bark of infected roots and butts, the fungus forms white mycelial fans, and it spreads through soil by black, shoestring-like rhizomorphs.

Pathogenicity

A. ostoyae lives as a parasite on living trees and as a saprotroph on dead wood. It causes white rot of roots and butt, weakening and eventually killing host trees, including commercially important conifers such as Douglas-fir and true firs. Spread occurs mainly by rhizomorph growth and root-to-root contact, creating expanding pockets of dead and dying trees in managed and natural forests. It is considered one of the most damaging root pathogens of conifers in western North America.

Edibility

Honey fungi are eaten in some regions after thorough cooking, but A. ostoyae can cause gastrointestinal upset in some people, and identification to species within the Armillaria complex is difficult; consumption is generally not recommended for beginners.
