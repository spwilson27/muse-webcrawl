Anther

The anther is the pollen-bearing part of a flower's stamen, the male reproductive organ of flowering plants. It is typically borne on a slender stalk called the filament, and consists of one or two lobes (thecae) joined by connective tissue, each lobe containing pollen sacs (microsporangia) in which pollen grains develop.

Structure

A typical angiosperm anther is tetrasporangiate, with four microsporangia arranged in two lobes. The wall of the anther includes specialised layers — the epidermis, endothecium, middle layers and the tapetum, which nourishes the developing pollen. Inside the sporangia, microspore mother cells undergo meiosis to produce tetrads of haploid microspores, which mature into pollen grains.

Dehiscence

At maturity the anther opens (dehisces) to release pollen, most commonly by longitudinal slits, though some families open by pores (poricidal dehiscence, as in the Ericaceae and in tomatoes, where buzz pollination by bees shakes the pollen free) or by valves. Released pollen may then be carried to a stigma by wind, water or animal pollinators, completing pollination.
