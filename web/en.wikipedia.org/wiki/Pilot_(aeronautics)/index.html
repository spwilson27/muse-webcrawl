Pilot (aeronautics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Two airline pilots in uniform in an airliner cockpit]
An airline flight crew at work.

An aircraft pilot (aviator) is a person who controls the flight of an aircraft by operating its directional controls. Airline pilots hold professional licences (ATPL in most countries, ATP in the US) after progressing through private (PPL) and commercial (CPL) licences, instrument ratings and type ratings for specific aircraft. The crew of an airliner consists of a captain (pilot in command) and a first officer, both fully qualified, supported on long flights by relief pilots.

Training and licensing

Student pilots solo after basic training, then build hours and pass theory and flight tests. Instrument ratings qualify pilots to fly in cloud by reference to instruments; simulator-based type rating teaches a specific aircraft. Airline cadet programmes (e.g. integrated MPL courses) shortcut the traditional hour-building route.

The job

Pilots perform pre-flight checks, brief with the crew and dispatchers, fly mostly on autopilot while monitoring systems, and handle takeoffs and landings manually in most operations. Flight-time limitations cap duty hours to manage fatigue; seniority by company and fleet governs pay and rosters.

History and demography

Early aviators were daredevils and airmail contractors; professionalisation followed the accident investigations of the 1930s and the jet age. Women were long excluded — Helen Richey became the first woman airline pilot in the US in 1934, and women remain a small minority (roughly 5–7%) of airline pilots worldwide.
