Schwarzschild metric

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Einstein's theory of general relativity, the Schwarzschild metric (also known as the Schwarzschild solution) is an exact solution of the Einstein field equations that describes the gravitational field outside a spherical mass, on the assumption that the electric charge of the mass, its angular momentum, and the universal cosmological constant are all zero. It is named after Karl Schwarzschild, who found the solution in 1915, only a few weeks after the publication of general relativity; it was the first exact solution of the field equations other than the trivial flat-spacetime one.

Form of the metric

In Schwarzschild coordinates (t, r, θ, φ), the line element is

ds2 = −(1 − rs/r) c2 dt2 + (1 − rs/r)−1 dr2 + r2(dθ2 + sin2θ dφ2),

where rs = 2GM/c2 is the Schwarzschild radius of the mass M. For the Sun, rs is about 3 kilometres; for the Earth, about 9 millimetres.

Properties

  • The solution is static and spherically symmetric, and by Birkhoff's theorem it is the unique such vacuum solution — so it describes the exterior field of any non-rotating spherical body, even one that is pulsating.
  • At large r the metric approaches flat Minkowski spacetime, and its weak-field limit reproduces Newtonian gravity.
  • The surface r = rs is a coordinate singularity that marks the event horizon when the mass is compressed within its Schwarzschild radius, forming a Schwarzschild black hole. The singularity at r = 0 is a genuine curvature singularity.
  • The metric predicts gravitational time dilation, the bending of light, the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion (43 arcseconds per century) and the Shapiro time delay — the classical tests of general relativity.

History

Schwarzschild derived the solution in late 1915 while serving in the German army on the Russian front; he sent it to Einstein, who presented it to the Prussian Academy of Sciences in January 1916. Schwarzschild died a few months later, in May 1916. Johannes Droste independently obtained the same solution. Later work by Eddington, Lemaître, Kruskal, Szekeres and others clarified the nature of the horizon and produced coordinate systems (Eddington–Finkelstein and Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates) that extend the geometry across r = rs.

Applications

The Schwarzschild solution is an excellent approximation for slowly rotating, weakly charged bodies such as stars and planets, and it underlies the parametrised post-Newtonian formalism used to test general relativity in the Solar System. Its interior counterpart, the Schwarzschild interior solution, models a static sphere of incompressible fluid. Rotating and charged generalisations are given by the Kerr and Reissner–Nordström metrics.
