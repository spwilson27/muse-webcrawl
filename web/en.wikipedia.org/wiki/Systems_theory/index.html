Systems theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Systems theory is the interdisciplinary study of systems — cohesive groups of interrelated and interdependent parts that can be natural or human-made. Every system has causal boundaries, is influenced by its context, is defined by its structure, function and role, and is expressed through its relations with other systems. A system is "more than the sum of its parts" when it expresses synergy or emergent behaviour.

Changing one part of a system may affect other parts or the whole system. It may be possible to anticipate these changes by understanding patterns of behaviour and feedback. Systems theory provides frameworks used across biology, engineering, psychology, sociology, management and many other fields.

History

The field is commonly traced to Ludwig von Bertalanffy's general system theory, developed from the 1930s and set out in his 1968 book General System Theory. Bertalanffy sought common principles governing open systems across disciplines. Around the same time, Norbert Wiener's cybernetics (1948) formalised feedback and control in animals and machines, and Claude Shannon's information theory supplied a quantitative account of communication. Later developments include Stafford Beer's management cybernetics, Niklas Luhmann's social systems theory, Peter Checkland's soft systems methodology, and contemporary work on complex adaptive systems.

Key concepts

Central notions include emergence (properties of the whole not present in the parts), feedback loops (negative/balancing and positive/reinforcing), equifinality (open systems can reach the same end state from different beginnings), homeostasis, hierarchy and nested subsystems, boundaries, and the distinction between open systems, which exchange matter, energy or information with their environment, and closed systems, which do not.

Applications

Systems approaches underlie fields as diverse as ecology and Earth system science, control engineering, family therapy, operations research, systems engineering and software design, and public policy — where "systems thinking" is used to address problems whose causes are entangled and whose interventions produce unintended side effects.
