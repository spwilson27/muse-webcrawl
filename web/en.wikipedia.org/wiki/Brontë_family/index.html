Brontë family

The Brontës were a 19th-century literary family associated with the village of Haworth in the West Riding of Yorkshire, England. The sisters Charlotte (1816–1855), Emily (1818–1848) and Anne (1820–1849) are among the most celebrated novelists and poets in English literature, writing Jane Eyre, Wuthering Heights and The Tenant of Wildfell Hall respectively.

Background

Their father, Patrick Brontë (1777–1861), an Irish-born Anglican clergyman, became perpetual curate of Haworth in 1820; the family lived at Haworth Parsonage, now the Brontë Parsonage Museum. Their mother Maria died in 1821, and the two eldest sisters, Maria and Elizabeth, died in childhood after illness contracted at the Clergy Daughters' School at Cowan Bridge. The surviving children, raised by their aunt Elizabeth Branwell, immersed themselves in writing, inventing the imaginary kingdoms of Angria and Gondal in tiny hand-made books.

Literary careers

In 1846 the sisters published a volume of poems at their own expense under the pseudonyms Currer, Ellis and Acton Bell. Charlotte's Jane Eyre (1847) was an immediate success; Emily's Wuthering Heights and Anne's Agnes Grey followed the same year, and Anne's radical The Tenant of Wildfell Hall in 1848. Their brother Branwell, a painter and would-be writer, died of tuberculosis aggravated by alcohol and laudanum in September 1848; Emily died that December and Anne the following May.

Aftermath

Charlotte, the last survivor, married her father's curate Arthur Bell Nicholls in 1854 and died in 1855, probably from complications of pregnancy. Elizabeth Gaskell's Life of Charlotte Brontë (1857) fixed the family's legend: genius flourishing in isolation on the moors — an image modern scholarship qualifies but which continues to draw pilgrims to Haworth.
