Friedrich Miescher

Johannes Friedrich Miescher (1844–1895).

Johannes Friedrich Miescher (13 August 1844 – 26 August 1895) was a Swiss physician and biologist who discovered nucleic acids, the molecules that carry genetic information. In 1869, working as a young researcher in Felix Hoppe-Seyler's laboratory at Tübingen, he isolated a substance from the nuclei of white blood cells that he called "nuclein" — later recognised as deoxyribonucleic acid, DNA.

Discovery

Miescher obtained pus from bandages at the nearby surgical clinic, washed the cells and treated them with acid and alkali, separating the nuclei and isolating a phosphorus-rich substance unlike any known protein. He showed it was an acid present in the nuclei of all kinds of cells, studied its composition in salmon sperm — where sperm heads are almost pure nuclei — and distinguished nuclein from proteins in papers of 1871 and 1874. Although he speculated that such molecules might transmit heredity, the idea was not pursued, and the genetic role of DNA was only established in the 1940s and 1950s.

Later work

Miescher became professor of physiology at Basel in 1872, founded the university's physiological institute, and studied the physiology of breathing, the chemistry of nutrition and the development of salmon. He died in 1895, aged 51, before his most important discovery was understood; his work was honoured later by the naming of the Friedrich Miescher Institute for Biomedical Research in Basel.
