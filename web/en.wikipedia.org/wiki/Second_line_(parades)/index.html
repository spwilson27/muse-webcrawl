Second line (parades)

The second line is a tradition in New Orleans, Louisiana, in which a brass band leads a parade and the crowd of followers who join in behind it — dancing, twirling parasols and handkerchiefs, and celebrating in the street — forms the "second line", as distinct from the "first line" of the band and the sponsoring organisation's official members.

Origins

The tradition grew out of the jazz funerals and parades of New Orleans' African American community in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Social aid and pleasure clubs — mutual aid societies formed by Black working-class residents — sponsored parades marking their anniversaries and funerals for members, hiring brass bands to play. Participants dressed in club colours, carried banners, and danced behind the band. The form draws on West and Central African ring dances, French and Spanish brass band traditions, and the city's parade culture.

The parade

A second line parade typically features a grand marshal, often waving a decorated fan or wearing a sash, followed by the club members in matching outfits and the brass band playing upbeat hymns, dirges (for funerals), and popular tunes. Behind them streams the second line itself: neighbours, visitors, and passers-by who dance along, frequently waving handkerchiefs and twirling decorated umbrellas in a distinctive stepping style. Parades follow set neighbourhood routes with planned stops at bars, clubhouses, and the homes of members.

Modern tradition

Social aid and pleasure clubs organise second lines most Sundays of the year in different neighbourhoods, pausing in the heat of midsummer. The tradition has become one of the most recognisable symbols of New Orleans culture; second lines also appear at weddings, festivals, and other celebrations, and were a powerful expression of communal recovery after Hurricane Katrina in 2005.
