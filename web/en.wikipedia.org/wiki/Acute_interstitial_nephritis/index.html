Acute interstitial nephritis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Acute interstitial nephritis (AIN) is an inflammation of the renal interstitium — the tissue between the kidney tubules — accompanied by acute deterioration of kidney function. It accounts for a significant minority of cases of acute kidney injury, particularly in hospitalised patients, and is most often caused by an allergic-type reaction to medication.

Causes

Drugs are responsible for the majority of cases. Common culprits include antibiotics (especially beta-lactams), non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), proton pump inhibitors, diuretics and allopurinol. AIN can also arise from infections (such as Legionella, leptospirosis and streptococcal infection), autoimmune diseases (sarcoidosis, Sjögren syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus), and the syndrome of tubulointerstitial nephritis and uveitis (TINU).

Presentation and diagnosis

The classic triad of fever, rash and eosinophilia is present in only a small minority of patients. Typically there is a rising creatinine days to weeks after starting an offending drug, often with sterile pyuria, white cell casts and sometimes eosinophiluria. Definitive diagnosis is by kidney biopsy, showing interstitial oedema and infiltration by lymphocytes, monocytes, plasma cells and eosinophils, sometimes with granulomas.

Treatment

The key step is prompt identification and withdrawal of the causative drug, after which many patients recover. Corticosteroids may speed recovery if given early, though evidence is largely observational. Some patients require temporary dialysis, and a proportion are left with chronic kidney impairment.
