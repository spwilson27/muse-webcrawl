Dugout (shelter)

A sod dugout dwelling on the Great Plains

A dugout or dug-out, also known as a pit-house or earth lodge variant, is a shelter formed by a hole or depression dug into the ground and roofed over, typically with timber and earth or sod. Dugouts exploit the insulating and protective properties of the earth: they are cool in summer, warm in winter, resistant to fire and storm, and cheap to build where timber or stone is scarce.

Historical use

Pit dwellings are among humanity's earliest houses, appearing in many Neolithic cultures. On the North American Great Plains in the nineteenth century, settlers who lacked lumber built dugouts into hillsides and ravine banks, often as the first stage before a sod house; such dwellings are a staple of pioneer literature. In the Rocky Mountains of Canada and the western United States, prospectors and trappers wintered in dugouts. The zemlyanka of eastern Europe is a cognate form, used by peasants, forest workers, partisans and soldiers.

Military use

In military usage, a dugout is an underground shelter or bunker excavated within or behind trench systems, used for protection from shellfire, as headquarters, dressing stations or accommodation. In the First World War, deep mined dugouts in the Western Front — some, in chalk country, descending many metres with electric light and multiple chambers — protected whole companies; German dugouts were particularly noted for their depth and engineering. The term also passed into civilian air-raid shelters in the Second World War.
