The Ohio Shale is a Late Devonian geologic formation in the Appalachian Basin of the eastern United States, exposed in Ohio, Kentucky, and Indiana and present in the subsurface of Pennsylvania and West Virginia. It consists largely of organic-rich black and brown shales deposited in a deep, oxygen-poor epicontinental sea, and it is an important source rock — and, in places, a self-sourced reservoir — for oil and natural gas.

Stratigraphy

The formation is commonly divided, from oldest to youngest, into the Huron Member (the thick, most organic-rich black shale), the Chagrin Member (grey silty shale, prominent in northeastern Ohio), and the Cleveland Member (black shale near the top, along Lake Erie). It overlies the Olentangy Shale and is overlain by the Bedford Shale and Berea Sandstone.

Deposition and fossils

The black shales record slow deposition in stratified, anoxic bottom waters under a productive surface ocean, preserving abundant organic matter. Large calcareous and pyritic concretions, especially in the Cleveland and Huron members, contain exceptionally preserved fossils, including the giant arthrodire placoderm Dunkleosteus, sharks, conodonts, and the enigmatic Prototaxites-like fossil Foerstia.

Economic significance

Organic-rich intervals of the Ohio Shale (notably the Huron) have produced gas since the 19th century in the Big Sandy field of Kentucky and adjacent areas, and the formation was historically evaluated as an oil shale and as a uranium-bearing unit. It is broadly correlative with other Devonian black shales such as the Marcellus Shale and the New Albany Shale.
