Ancestor veneration in China

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ancestor veneration in China
Chinese敬祖 (jìngzǔ); 拜祖 (bàizǔ)
Key conceptsFilial piety (孝), spirit tablets, ancestral halls
Major occasionsQingming Festival, Ghost Festival, Chinese New Year, death anniversaries

Ancestor veneration in China is the complex of beliefs and rituals by which Chinese families honour their deceased ancestors, seeking their protection and blessings and affirming the continuity of the lineage. Rooted in the Confucian virtue of filial piety (xiào), it holds that the obligations of children to parents do not end at death, and that the spirits of ancestors, properly nourished with offerings, remain active members of the family. It is one of the oldest continuous features of Chinese religion, attested already in the oracle-bone divinations of the Shang dynasty (c. 1200 BC), in which kings sought the guidance of royal ancestors.

Practices

The physical focus of veneration is the ancestral altar, at home or in a clan ancestral hall (cítáng), where spirit tablets (páiwèi) inscribed with ancestors' names are kept. Offerings include incense, candles, tea, wine, fruit, and prepared foods; paper replicas of money, houses and goods (joss paper) are burned to transmit them to the spirit world. Families report important events — births, marriages, successes — to the ancestors and consult them before major decisions.

Festivals

At the Qingming Festival families sweep ancestral tombs, repair graves and make offerings outdoors. During the seventh lunar month, the Ghost Festival honours ancestors and wandering spirits with feasts and burned offerings. Chinese New Year observances typically begin with rites welcoming the ancestors to share the reunion dinner, and offerings are also made on the ancestor's death anniversary and birthdays.

Beliefs and social role

Traditional belief holds that a person has multiple souls; one may reside in the grave, another in the spirit tablet, and another in the afterlife bureaucracy. Neglected ancestors risk becoming hungry ghosts, while well-tended ancestors protect descendants' health and fortune. Genealogy books (jiāpǔ), lineage halls and graves knit extended families and villages together, and the rite has historically been central to Chinese identity in China and the diaspora. The practice was suppressed during the Cultural Revolution but has revived strongly since the 1980s.
