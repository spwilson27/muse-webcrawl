The Aztec Empire, formally the Triple Alliance (Classical Nahuatl: Ēxcān Tlahtōlōyān, "Three Places Speak"), was an alliance of three Nahua city-states — Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan — in the Valley of Mexico. Formed in 1428, the alliance dominated central Mexico until it was destroyed by Spanish conquistadores and their indigenous allies in 1521.

Formation

The alliance emerged from the Tepanec War of 1427–1428. The Mexica of Tenochtitlan under Itzcoatl, allied with Nezahualcoyotl of Texcoco, rebelled against the Tepanec empire of Azcapotzalco ruled by Maxtla. After the Tepanec defeat, the victorious cities joined with the smaller Tepanec city of Tlacopan to form the Triple Alliance, dividing the spoils of their joint conquests in shares of two-fifths to Tenochtitlan, two-fifths to Texcoco, and one-fifth to Tlacopan.

Expansion

Through the fifteenth century, successive rulers — Moctezuma I, Axayacatl, Tizoc, Ahuitzotl, and Moctezuma II — expanded the empire by conquest from the Gulf of Mexico to the Pacific Ocean and south to the borders of present-day Guatemala. Subject provinces were typically left under their own rulers but required to pay regular tribute in goods such as cacao, cotton mantles, feathers, jade, and warriors. Tenochtitlan gradually overshadowed its partners, and by the early sixteenth century the alliance functioned in practice as an empire ruled by the Mexica tlatoani.

Some states, notably the Purépecha (Tarascan) empire to the west and the republic of Tlaxcala to the east, resisted Aztec expansion; the regulated "flower wars" (xōchiyāōyōtl) with Tlaxcala and other neighbours provided captives for ritual sacrifice.

Society and administration

The empire's capital Tenochtitlan, built on islands in Lake Texcoco, grew into one of the largest cities in the world, with a population estimated at 200,000 or more. The alliance had no standing bureaucracy in conquered provinces; administration rested on tribute collection recorded in pictorial registers such as the Matrícula de Tributos, and on garrisons and tribute collectors (calpixque) in strategic provinces.

Fall

In 1519 Hernán Cortés landed on the Gulf coast and marched inland, recruiting enemies of the Aztecs — above all the Tlaxcalans — as allies. After the death of Moctezuma II and the expulsion of the Spanish from Tenochtitlan in 1520 (La Noche Triste), Cortés besieged the city with brigantines on the lake and a large indigenous army. Devastated by a smallpox epidemic, Tenochtitlan fell on 13 August 1521 with the capture of the last tlatoani, Cuauhtémoc, ending the empire.
