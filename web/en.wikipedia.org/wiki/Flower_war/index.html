Flower war

A flower war or flowery war (Nahuatl: xōchiyāōyōtl, Spanish: guerra florida) was a type of ritual warfare practised in Mesoamerica, most famously between the Aztec Triple Alliance and its traditional enemies, including Tlaxcala, Huejotzingo and Cholula, during the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries. Unlike ordinary wars of conquest, flower wars were pre-arranged, fought at an agreed time and place, and aimed not at destroying the enemy state but at capturing prisoners for human sacrifice, training warriors and demonstrating military prowess.

Origins

According to Aztec and Tlaxcalan historical traditions recorded after the Spanish conquest, the flower wars were instituted in the mid-fifteenth century, during the reign of the Aztec tlatoani Moctezuma I and under the influence of the cihuacoatl Tlacaelel. One traditional account links their beginning to a severe famine (c. 1450–1454), after which priests declared that the gods demanded more sacrificial victims, and arranged battles with neighbouring states provided a steady supply.

Practice

Flower wars were fought with comparatively small numbers of elite warriors on designated battlefields. Combat emphasised individual capture rather than killing: a warrior won prestige by seizing enemy captives, who were later sacrificed on the temple pyramids. The battles also served as military exercises, tested weapons and tactics, and allowed both sides to display strength without the costs of full-scale invasion. Some scholars have argued that the institution also had political purposes — gradually exhausting Tlaxcala and keeping it in a state of isolation — while others stress its religious significance or question how sharply it can be distinguished from ordinary warfare.

Consequences

The perpetual, contained conflict with the Aztecs left Tlaxcala resentful and militarily experienced; when Hernán Cortés arrived in 1519, the Tlaxcalans became the Spaniards' most important indigenous allies in the conquest of the Aztec Empire.
