William Paley (July 1743 – 25 May 1805) was an English Anglican clergyman, Christian apologist, philosopher and utilitarian. He is best remembered for his exposition of the teleological argument for the existence of God in his work Natural Theology (1802), which made use of the watchmaker analogy.

Life and career

Paley was educated at Giggleswick School and Christ's College, Cambridge, where he was senior wrangler in 1763 and later a fellow and tutor. Ordained in the Church of England, he held livings in Cumberland and Lincolnshire, became Archdeacon of Carlisle, and ended his career as sub-dean of Lincoln. His Cambridge lectures formed the basis of The Principles of Moral and Political Philosophy (1785), a standard textbook that defended a theological utilitarianism.

Natural Theology and the watchmaker

Paley's A View of the Evidences of Christianity (1794) argued for the historical reliability of the New Testament. His last and most famous book, Natural Theology; or, Evidences of the Existence and Attributes of the Deity (1802), opens with the analogy of finding a watch upon a heath: its intricate arrangement of parts implies a watchmaker. Likewise, Paley argued, the adaptations of living organisms imply a divine designer, cataloguing examples from the eye to the hinge of a bivalve shell. The book strongly influenced Charles Darwin as a young man, who later supplied natural selection as the alternative mechanism explaining biological adaptation.

Legacy

Paley's works remained core texts into the Victorian era; his design argument is still cited — and criticised — in philosophy of religion and debates about evolution.
