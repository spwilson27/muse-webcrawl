Outwash plain

An outwash plain, also called a sandur (plural sandar), is a plain formed of glacial sediment deposited by meltwater emerging from the terminus of a glacier. As the water leaves the ice front it slows, spreads out — often in shifting braided channels — and drops its load, so that the deposits are sorted: coarse gravel near the glacier gives way to progressively finer sands and silts downstream.

Formation

Outwash plains develop in front of end moraines where abundant meltwater carries eroded debris away from the glacier. Meltwater streams commonly issue from an ice portal or across the whole ice margin, then fan out across the plain. Because discharge and sediment supply fluctuate daily and seasonally, and catastrophically during glacial outburst floods (jökulhlaups), the braided channels constantly shift, reworking the surface. Pitted outwash, or a pitted sandur, forms where stranded blocks of buried ice melt to leave kettle depressions.

Examples

The classic sandar are found in Iceland, where the term originates — Skeiðarársandur, formed largely by floods from the Vatnajökull ice cap, is among the largest active outwash plains in the world, destroyed bridges in the 1996 jökulhlaup. Large Pleistocene outwash plains fringe the former ice-sheet margins of northern Europe and North America, where they form important aquifers and sources of sand and gravel aggregate, and their sandy soils often support dry grassland or pine barrens.
