The Finnish Civil War was a conflict in 1918 between the forces of the Senate of Finland — the "Whites" — and the socialist Finnish People's Delegation and its Red Guards — the "Reds" — over control of the newly independent state of Finland. It formed part of the wider upheaval following the First World War and the Russian Revolutions, and it remains the most divisive event in modern Finnish history.

Background

Finland had been a grand duchy of the Russian Empire since 1809. The collapse of tsarist authority in 1917 left a power vacuum: parliament dissolved, food shortages deepened, and both the bourgeois parties and the Social Democrats raised armed guards. After the Bolshevik October Revolution, the Finnish Senate declared independence on 6 December 1917, recognised by Soviet Russia on 4 January 1918. Neither side would disarm, and in late January 1918 the Reds seized Helsinki while the Senate withdrew to Vaasa.

Course of the war

The Whites, commanded by C. G. E. Mannerheim and stiffened by Finnish Jägers trained in Germany, held the rural north and centre; the Reds held the industrial south. The decisive battle was fought at Tampere (March–April 1918), the largest battle in Finnish history, ending in White victory. A German expeditionary force under Rüdiger von der Goltz landed in April, took Helsinki, and hastened the Red collapse; the last Red forces surrendered in early May 1918.

Terror and prison camps

Both sides committed acts of terror — the Reds murdered around 1,600 people; the Whites executed roughly 10,000 Reds during and after the fighting. Some 80,000 captured Reds were held in prison camps where about 12,000–13,500 died of hunger, disease, and executions during the summer of 1918, in one of Finland's worst humanitarian catastrophes.

Aftermath

The victorious Senate sought to make Finland a kingdom under a German prince, Friedrich Karl of Hesse, but Germany's defeat in November 1918 ended the project; Finland became a republic in 1919 with K. J. Ståhlberg as president. Most rank-and-file Reds were amnestied by the mid-1920s, and democratic reforms — land reform (Lex Kallio, 1922), universal suffrage already granted in 1906 — gradually reintegrated the working class into national politics.

Legacy

The war left deep scars: for decades the official narrative was that of a "War of Liberation" against Russia, while Red memory was suppressed. Since the 1960s, scholarship and literature — notably Väinö Linna's novel Under the North Star — have broadened public understanding, and the centenary in 2018 emphasised remembrance of victims on all sides.
