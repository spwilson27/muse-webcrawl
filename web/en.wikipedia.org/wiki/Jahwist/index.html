Jahwist

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Jahwist, or Yahwist, often abbreviated J, is one of the most widely recognised sources of the Pentateuch (Torah) in biblical scholarship, together with the Elohist (E), the Deuteronomist (D) and the Priestly source (P). According to the documentary hypothesis, these originally independent documents were combined by redactors to form the first five books of the Hebrew Bible.

Name and characteristics

The Jahwist is named for its characteristic use of the personal name of God, YHWH (traditionally vocalised "Jehovah" in German scholarship, hence Jahwe and the siglum J). In the Jahwist material, God is depicted in strikingly anthropomorphic terms: God walks in the garden of Eden, speaks directly with human beings, regrets decisions, and closes the door of Noah's ark.

The Jahwist narrative begins with the creation story of Genesis 2 and includes the Eden narrative, Cain and Abel, the flood (interwoven with the Priestly account), the Tower of Babel, and the stories of the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. The source shows a particular interest in the southern kingdom of Judah, in the land and its cultivation, and in etiological stories explaining names, customs and natural features.

Date

Classical scholarship associated with Julius Wellhausen dated the Jahwist to the 10th century BCE, the time of the early monarchy or "Solomonic enlightenment". Later scholars have proposed dates ranging from the 9th century BCE to the Babylonian exile (6th century BCE) or even the Persian period. A number of contemporary scholars, particularly in Europe, question whether the Jahwist ever existed as a continuous independent document, preferring fragmentary or supplementary models of the Pentateuch's formation, while many North American and Israeli scholars continue to defend a distinct Yahwistic work.
