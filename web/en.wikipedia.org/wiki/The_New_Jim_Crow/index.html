The New Jim Crow

AuthorMichelle Alexander
SubjectRace and the U.S. criminal justice system, mass incarceration
PublisherThe New Press
Published2010

The New Jim Crow: Mass Incarceration in the Age of Colorblindness is a book by legal scholar and civil rights litigator Michelle Alexander, published in 2010. It argues that the U.S. criminal justice system functions as a contemporary system of racial control, comparable to the Jim Crow laws that enforced segregation in the South, primarily through the War on Drugs and the disproportionate imprisonment of African American men and the civic disabilities that follow felony conviction.

Thesis

Alexander contends that although mass incarceration is officially colorblind, its design and enforcement produce a racial undercaste: people convicted of felonies — overwhelmingly poor men of color swept up in drug enforcement despite comparable rates of drug use across races — are legally denied voting rights, employment, housing, education, and public benefits, much as Jim Crow once did by law. She traces a pattern in American history in which systems of racial control (slavery, then Jim Crow) are redesigned rather than abolished in response to social movements.

Reception

The book became a New York Times bestseller, remained in print in a 10th-anniversary edition (2020), and is credited with reshaping public debate about criminal justice reform. Critics such as political scientist James Forman Jr. have argued it understates violent crime's role in incarceration growth and the share of Black support for tough policies; others dispute elements of the drug-war emphasis, since drug offenders are a minority of state prisoners. Alexander has responded that drug enforcement drives arrests, policing patterns, and the system's political economy even where prison populations are counted differently.
