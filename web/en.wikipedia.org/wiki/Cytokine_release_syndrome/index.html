Cytokine release syndrome

Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) is a form of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) arising when large numbers of white blood cells are activated and release inflammatory cytokines, which in turn activate yet more white blood cells. CRS is a well-recognised adverse effect of some immunotherapies, in particular CAR T-cell therapy and T-cell-engaging bispecific antibodies, and can also occur in severe infections. In severe cases it overlaps with a cytokine storm and can be life-threatening.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically begin with fever, chills, fatigue, headache, muscle and joint pain, nausea and rash. More severe cases progress to low blood pressure, rapid heart rate, difficulty breathing from capillary leak and pulmonary oedema, kidney and liver dysfunction, coagulopathy, and neurological toxicity (ICANS) with confusion, seizures or cerebral oedema. Onset is usually within days of an infusion and severity is graded (for example by the ASTCT consensus criteria) from grade 1 (fever alone) to grade 4 (life-threatening).

Mechanism and treatment

Activated T cells and macrophages release large quantities of cytokines including interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-1, interferon-γ and TNF, driving systemic inflammation and endothelial activation. Management is supportive (fluids, oxygen, vasopressors) plus immunosuppression targeted at the cascade: the IL-6 receptor blocker tocilizumab is the standard treatment for CAR-T-associated CRS, with corticosteroids added for severe, refractory or neurological cases; the IL-1 antagonist anakinra is also used. A notorious early example was the 2006 TGN1412 trial, in which a CD28 superagonist antibody caused severe CRS in all six healthy volunteers.
