The Vredefort crater, also known as the Vredefort impact structure, is the largest verified impact crater on Earth. It lies in the Free State province of South Africa, near the town of Vredefort, about 120 kilometres south-west of Johannesburg. The crater was formed just over two billion years ago when an asteroid estimated at 10–15 kilometres across struck the Earth.

Geology

The original crater rim is estimated to have been 250 to 300 kilometres in diameter, though two billion years of erosion have removed the original bowl. At its centre stands the Vredefort Dome, a ring of uplifted hills about 70 kilometres across where deep Archaean basement granite was pushed upward by the rebound of the crust after impact. Evidence of impact includes shatter cones, shocked quartz with planar deformation features, impact breccias and pseudotachylite veins, and the stishovite and coesite high-pressure minerals. The structure was long thought volcanic until this shock evidence accumulated in the 1960s–1990s.

Significance

The crater was excavated into the Witwatersrand basin, and the impact is credited with tilting and preserving the basin's gold-bearing reefs — the source of a large fraction of all gold ever mined — that might otherwise have eroded away. The Vredefort Dome was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2005. The crater's age, 2.023 billion years, makes it the oldest large impact structure known, and it is one of only a few multi-ring impact basins identified on Earth.
