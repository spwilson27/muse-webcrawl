Trace amine

Trace amines are a group of endogenous compounds structurally related to the classical monoamine neurotransmitters (dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin) but present in the nervous system at much lower — "trace" — concentrations. They are produced by decarboxylation of amino acids.

Members

The principal mammalian trace amines include β-phenethylamine, tyramine, tryptamine, octopamine, synephrine, and thyronamine derivatives. Amphetamine and some other substituted phenethylamines act partly through the same pathways, and several trace amines occur in foods such as cheese and chocolate.

Receptors and function

In 2001 a family of trace amine-associated receptors (TAARs) was identified; the best studied, TAAR1, is expressed in the brain and peripheral tissues and modulates dopaminergic, serotonergic, and glutamatergic neurotransmission. Trace amines are rapidly metabolised by monoamine oxidases, and dysfunction of trace-amine signalling has been investigated in relation to schizophrenia, depression, ADHD, and the actions of stimulant drugs. In invertebrates, octopamine and tyramine function as major neurotransmitters analogous to adrenaline.
