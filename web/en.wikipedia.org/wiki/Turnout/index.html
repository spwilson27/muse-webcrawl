Voter turnout

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Voter turnout is the proportion of eligible voters who cast a ballot in an election. It is a standard indicator of democratic engagement and of a system's inclusiveness, measured as a share of registered voters, of the voting-age population, or of the voting-eligible population — differences that account for much cross-national confusion.

Patterns and determinants

Turnout is highest in established democracies with compulsory voting (Australia and Belgium exceed 90% of registered voters), proportional systems, and high-stakes, competitive national elections; it is lower in local, second-order (European Parliament) and midterm elections, and declines in many democracies since the 1990s are a major research theme. Individual turnout rises with age, education, income, residential stability and social connectedness, and falls with registration burdens — US turnout in presidential elections (~60% of eligible voters) trails most OECD peers, and midterm turnout much lower. Weather, campaign contact, competitiveness and mobilisation by parties and civic groups all matter at the margin.

Reform debates

Proposed remedies include automatic and same-day registration, postal and early voting, election-day holidays, lowering the voting age to 16 (adopted in Austria and for some elections elsewhere), and compulsory voting — which raises turnout most reliably but raises liberty objections and produces some informal/donkey voting. Whether higher turnout changes outcomes is empirically contested.
