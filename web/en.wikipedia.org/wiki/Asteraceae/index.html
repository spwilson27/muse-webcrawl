Asteraceae

Scientific classification
Kingdom: Plantae
Order: Asterales
Family: Asteraceae
Type genus: Aster

Asteraceae is a large family of flowering plants that consists of over 32,000 known species in over 1,900 genera within the order Asterales. The number of species in Asteraceae is rivalled only by the Orchidaceae, and which is the larger family is unclear as the quantity of extant species in each family is unknown.

Commonly referred to as the aster, daisy, composite, or sunflower family, Compositae (the original name for the family) were first described in the year 1740. Most species of Asteraceae are annual, biennial, or perennial herbaceous plants, but there are also shrubs, vines, and trees. The family has a widespread distribution, found in almost all regions and habitats except Antarctica.

Description

The defining characteristic of Asteraceae is the inflorescence, a densely packed cluster of many small individual flowers, called a capitulum or floral head, which superficially resembles a single flower. The individual flowers within the head are called florets, and may be of two types: tubular disc florets in the centre and strap-shaped ray florets around the margin. The fruit is an achene, often crowned by a pappus of hairs or scales that aids wind dispersal, as in the dandelion.

Distribution and ecology

Members of the family occur on every continent except Antarctica, from polar regions to the tropics, and are especially diverse in dry and mountainous regions. Many species are important in their ecosystems as sources of nectar and pollen for insects.

Uses

The family includes many economically important plants. Food crops include the sunflower, lettuce, chicory, artichoke, and safflower; others are grown as ornamentals, such as chrysanthemums, dahlias, marigolds, and zinnias. Some species are used in traditional medicine, and a few, such as ragweed, are significant sources of allergenic pollen.

Taxonomy

The family is divided into a number of subfamilies and tribes. Molecular phylogenetic studies have greatly revised its internal classification, recognising subfamilies such as Asteroideae, Cichorioideae, and Carduoideae, among others.
