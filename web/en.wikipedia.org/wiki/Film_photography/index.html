Film photography (also known as analogue photography) is the practice of capturing images on light-sensitive photographic film—typically a plastic strip coated with a silver halide emulsion—which is then chemically developed to produce negatives or transparencies that can be printed or scanned. It was the dominant photographic technology for most of the 20th century.

Process

When film is exposed in a camera, light forms an invisible latent image in the emulsion. Development converts exposed silver halide crystals to metallic silver, producing a negative image; a fixer removes the remaining halide. Colour films use layered emulsions with dye couplers, processed in chemistries such as C-41 (colour negative) and E-6 (colour reversal/slide film). Black-and-white film remains widely developed at home by enthusiasts.

Decline and revival

Digital cameras overtook film in the 2000s; Kodak entered bankruptcy in 2012 and many films were discontinued. From the mid-2010s, demand revived among younger photographers drawn to film's aesthetic and deliberate process. Manufacturers including Kodak Alaris and Fujifilm reintroduced or expanded film stocks, prices rose, and new film cameras and instant products (such as Fujifilm's Instax) found a large market.

Formats and equipment

Common formats include 35mm (135), medium format (120/220), large-format sheet film, and instant films. Classic film cameras from Leica, Nikon, Canon, Hasselblad and Pentax remain in extensive use, serviced by a specialised repair trade.
