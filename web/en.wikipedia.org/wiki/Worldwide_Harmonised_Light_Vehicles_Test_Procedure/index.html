The Worldwide Harmonised Light Vehicles Test Procedure (WLTP) is a global standard for measuring the fuel consumption, carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions, pollutant emissions and energy consumption of light-duty vehicles, including the electric range of hybrid and battery-electric cars. It was developed under the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE) and adopted in 2014 as Global Technical Regulation No. 15. In the European Union it replaced the New European Driving Cycle (NEDC), becoming mandatory for new car types in September 2017 and for all new registrations a year later.

The test cycle

The WLTP driving cycle (WLTC) lasts 30 minutes and covers about 23.25 km divided into low, medium, high and extra-high speed phases, with a maximum speed of 131 km/h. Compared with the NEDC it is longer, faster and more dynamic, with more realistic acceleration and gear-shift behaviour and a greater variety of driving situations. Test conditions were also tightened: vehicles are tested with more equipment fitted, at a defined temperature of 23 °C, and with individual values declared for each vehicle configuration rather than for a model range alone.

Effects

Because the cycle is more demanding, official CO2 figures under WLTP are typically about 20–25% higher than under the NEDC for the same car, narrowing — though not closing — the long-criticised gap between laboratory results and real-world fuel consumption. In the EU the change affected vehicle taxation and manufacturers' fleet-average CO2 targets, and it was accompanied by the Real Driving Emissions (RDE) on-road test using portable emissions measurement systems. Japan and India have also adopted WLTP-based procedures, while other markets retain their own cycles.
