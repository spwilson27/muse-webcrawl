Greek underworld

Charon ferries the souls of the dead across the Styx.

The Greek underworld, or Hades, is the realm of the dead in ancient Greek religion and mythology — a shadowy kingdom beneath the earth, ruled by the god Hades (also called Pluto) and his consort Persephone. To it came the shades (psychai) of all who died, irrespective of their merit, though later Greek thought developed notions of judgment, punishment and reward.

Description

In Homer's Odyssey (Book 11), the underworld lies at the edge of Ocean, in a land of mist and gloom. The dead are insubstantial wraiths who retain their appearance and memories but lack strength; Achilles famously declares he would rather be a living labourer than king of all the dead. By the classical period the geography had become more elaborate: the newly dead were guided by Hermes Psychopompos to the river Styx (in other accounts the Acheron), where the ferryman Charon carried them across for a fare of one obol, which mourners placed in the mouth of the corpse. Those without burial or fare wandered the bank. The three-headed dog Cerberus guarded the entrance, letting shades in but never out.

Regions

  • Asphodel Meadows — the destination of ordinary souls, who neither sinned greatly nor excelled.
  • Elysium / the Isles of the Blest — a paradise of the heroic and virtuous, located at the ends of the earth in Homer and Hesiod and later placed within the underworld.
  • Tartarus — the deepest pit, as far below Hades as earth is below heaven, prison of the Titans and place of torment for great sinners such as Tantalus, Sisyphus, Ixion and Tityos.

The underworld's rivers included the Styx (sworn upon by the gods), the Acheron (of woe), the Lethe (forgetfulness), the Phlegethon (fire) and the Cocytus (wailing).

Judgment and later beliefs

Plato's myths — in the Gorgias, Phaedo and the Myth of Er at the end of the Republic — describe judges of the dead (Minos, Rhadamanthus and Aeacus), rewards and punishments, and reincarnation. Mystery cults such as the Eleusinian Mysteries and Orphic sects promised initiates a better lot after death; small inscribed gold tablets ("Orphic" lamellae) buried with the dead gave instructions for the journey, warning the soul to drink from the spring of Memory (Mnemosyne) rather than Lethe.

Mythological visitors

Several heroes descended alive into Hades (a katabasis): Odysseus, who consulted the shade of Tiresias; Heracles, whose final labour was to capture Cerberus; Orpheus, who nearly rescued Eurydice with his music; Theseus and Pirithous, who were trapped when they tried to abduct Persephone; and Aeneas in Virgil's Roman reworking. Persephone herself spent part of each year in the underworld, her return marking the seasons in the myth told in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter.
