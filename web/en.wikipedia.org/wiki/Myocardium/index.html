Myocardium is the muscular middle layer of the wall of the heart. It is composed of cardiac muscle cells (cardiomyocytes) and makes up the bulk of the heart wall. It lies between the outer epicardium and the inner endocardium, and is supplied with blood through the coronary circulation. Contraction of the myocardium pumps blood through the heart and into the great vessels.

Muscle within the myocardium is arranged in sheets that wrap around the chambers of the heart, so that contraction squeezes the chambers in a coordinated, wringing fashion. The myocardial wall of the left ventricle is about three times thicker than that of the right ventricle, reflecting the higher pressures of the systemic circulation.

The myocardium is the thick muscular middle layer of the heart wall.

Structure

Histology

Cardiomyocytes are short, branched muscle fibres joined end to end by intercalated discs, which contain gap junctions that allow electrical impulses to spread rapidly from cell to cell so that the myocardium contracts as a functional syncytium. Unlike skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle cells usually have a single central nucleus, are involuntary, and have an intrinsic rhythmicity modulated by the cardiac conduction system.

Layers and orientation

The ventricular myocardium is commonly described in terms of subepicardial, mid-wall, and subendocardial fibres with differing helical orientations, which together produce both circumferential shortening and longitudinal twisting of the ventricles during systole. Myocardial architecture continues to be refined by imaging methods such as diffusion tensor MRI.

Development

The myocardium develops from the primary heart field of splanchnic mesoderm in the cardiogenic region of the early embryo. Progenitor cells form the primitive heart tube, whose myocardial layer drives the peristaltic contractions of the early embryonic circulation before valves and septa form.

Function

The myocardium generates the force for the cardiac cycle. During systole, rising ventricular pressure closes the atrioventricular valves and ejects blood into the aorta and pulmonary artery; during diastole the myocardium relaxes and the chambers refill. Its performance depends on preload, afterload, contractility, and heart rate, summarised in the Frank–Starling relationship.

Clinical significance

  • Myocardial infarction — death of myocardium from prolonged coronary ischaemia ("heart attack").
  • Myocarditis — inflammation of the myocardium, usually viral.
  • Cardiomyopathies — diseases of the heart muscle including dilated, hypertrophic, and restrictive types.
  • Myocardial stunning and hibernation — reversible contractile dysfunction after ischaemia.
  • Myocardial bridging — a segment of coronary artery that runs within, rather than on, the myocardium.
