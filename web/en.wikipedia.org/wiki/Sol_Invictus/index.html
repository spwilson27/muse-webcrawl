Sol Invictus

Sol Invictus (Classical Latin: [ˈsoːl ɪnˈwɪk.tʊs], "Unconquered Sun") was the official sun god of the late Roman Empire and a patron of soldiers. In 274 AD the emperor Aurelian made it an official cult alongside the traditional Roman deities, elevating the sun god to a central place in Roman public religion.

Origins

The god Sol had long been worshipped at Rome, but the epithet Invictus gained prominence in the third century. Aurelian, fresh from victories over Zenobia of Palmyra in the East, credited the sun god of Emesa with his triumph. He built a magnificent temple to Sol Invictus in Rome, dedicated on 25 December 274, established a college of priests (pontifices dei Solis), and instituted quadrennial games in the god's honour.

The cult under later emperors

Sol Invictus figured prominently on imperial coinage through the reigns of Probus, Diocletian's Tetrarchy and Constantine I, who initially presented himself as the sun god's companion (comes) and continued striking Sol coinage for years after his turn toward Christianity. The radiant crown became a standard attribute of emperors on coins of the period.

Dies Natalis Solis Invicti

The Calendar of Philocalus (354 AD) records a festival, the Dies Natalis Solis Invicti ("Birthday of the Unconquered Sun"), on 25 December, near the winter solstice as reckoned in the Julian calendar. The proximity of this feast to Christmas has often been noted; whether the Christian feast influenced, was influenced by, or was calculated independently of the solar festival remains debated among historians.

Relationship to other cults

Sol Invictus absorbed traits of Apollo, Helios and the Syrian Elagabal, and coexisted with Mithraism, whose god Mithras was frequently identified with the unconquered sun. The cult declined as Christianity became dominant under Theodosius I in the late fourth century, though solar imagery persisted in Roman and early Christian art.
