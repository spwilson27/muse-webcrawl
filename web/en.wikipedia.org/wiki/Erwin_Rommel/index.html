Erwin Rommel

Johannes Erwin Eugen Rommel (15 November 1891 – 14 October 1944) was a German field marshal during World War II. Popularly known as the Desert Fox (Wüstenfuchs), he served in the Wehrmacht of Nazi Germany, commanding the Afrika Korps in North Africa and later the defences of Normandy. His reputation as a brilliant, chivalrous tactician — cultivated by both Axis and Allied propaganda — has been reassessed by modern historians.

Early career

Rommel won the Pour le Mérite as a junior officer in World War I for his actions at Caporetto, and wrote the influential infantry tactics book Infanterie greift an (1937). In 1940 his 7th Panzer Division — the "Ghost Division" — raced across France.

North Africa and Normandy

From 1941 he won striking victories at Gazala and Tobruk, but was defeated at El Alamein (1942) and driven from Africa by May 1943, hampered by supply lines and Allied air and naval power. In 1944 he fortified the Atlantic Wall and was wounded by an Allied fighter days after D-Day.

Death and the Rommel myth

Suspected of complicity in the 20 July plot to kill Hitler, Rommel was forced to take cyanide in October 1944 in return for his family's safety and a state funeral. Postwar, the "Rommel myth" — the clean, apolitical genius — was promoted in the West; scholarship now stresses his loyalty to Hitler for much of the war and the propaganda machinery behind his image, while acknowledging his operational skill.
