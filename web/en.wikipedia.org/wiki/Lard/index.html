Lard

Lard
SourceRendered fat from pigs
Fatty acids~39% saturated, ~45% monounsaturated
Melting point~30–40 °C
UsesCooking, baking, soap, historically lubricants and candles

Lard is a semi-solid white fat obtained by rendering the fatty tissue of the pig. Once the principal cooking fat of Europe and North America, it is prized in baking for producing exceptionally flaky pastry, and remains essential in many cuisines, from Mexican refried beans and tamales to Hungarian and Spanish cooking.

Production and types

Rendering melts the fat out of adipose tissue by wet or dry heat, then strains off the cracklings. Grades differ by source: leaf lard, from the visceral fat around the kidneys, is the finest — neutral in flavour and preferred for pastry; fatback lard and caul fat serve other purposes. Partially hydrogenated industrial lard, sold in blocks, keeps longer but contains trans fats in older formulations. Lard contains somewhat less saturated fat and more monounsaturated fat than butter.

History and decline

Lard powered traditional cooking for centuries and was also used for soap, candles, and machinery lubrication. In the 20th century it lost ground to vegetable shortening (Crisco, from 1911) and to health campaigns against animal fats, becoming a byword for unhealthy food. Since the 1990s, chefs and food writers have partially rehabilitated it for its flavour and performance, and it is a staple of some traditional and "nose-to-tail" cooking revivals.

Non-culinary uses

Lard is used in soap-making, as a cutting fluid and biofuel feedstock, and in some traditional leather and metalworking applications. Because it is a pork product, it is prohibited in Jewish and Islamic dietary law.
