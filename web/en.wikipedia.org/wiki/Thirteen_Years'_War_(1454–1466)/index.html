The Thirteen Years' War (German: Dreizehnjähriger Krieg; Polish: wojna trzynastoletnia), also called the War of the Cities, was a conflict fought from 1454 to 1466 between the Prussian Confederation — an alliance of Prussian cities, towns, and gentry — allied with the Kingdom of Poland, against the Teutonic Order, which ruled Prussia as a monastic state. It ended with the Second Peace of Thorn (1466), under which the western part of Prussia passed to Poland as Royal Prussia, while the Order kept the eastern lands only as a Polish fief.

Background

Discontent with the Teutonic Order's rule — heavy taxation, trade restrictions, and political exclusion — led Prussian cities such as Danzig (Gdańsk), Elbing (Elbląg), and Thorn (Toruń), together with the landed gentry, to form the Prussian Confederation in 1440. In early 1454 the Confederation renounced its allegiance to the Order, rose in revolt, and appealed to King Casimir IV of Poland, who issued an act of incorporation of Prussia into the Polish Crown and declared war on the Order.

Course of the war

The confederates quickly seized most Teutonic castles, but a Polish noble levy was crushed by the Order at the Battle of Chojnice in September 1454, prolonging the war. Unable to pay its largely mercenary armies, the Order fell into financial crisis; in 1457 its unpaid Bohemian mercenaries sold Marienburg (Malbork) — the Order's headquarters — to Poland. The war increasingly turned on naval strength and sieges: Danzig's fleet defeated the Order's Danish allies, and Polish–Prussian forces won decisive victories at the Battle of Świecino (1462), which broke the Order's field army, and at the Battle of Vistula Lagoon (1463), which destroyed its fleet. By 1466 the Order, bankrupt and abandoned by its allies, sued for peace.

Second Peace of Thorn

The treaty, signed at Thorn on 19 October 1466, divided Prussia. Poland received Pomerelia (Gdańsk Pomerania), the Chełmno and Michałów lands, and the cities of Malbork, Elbląg, and Toruń, together with the bishopric of Warmia — territories that formed the autonomous province of Royal Prussia. The Teutonic Order retained eastern Prussia (later Ducal Prussia) but held it as a fief of the Polish king, and every Grand Master was obliged to swear allegiance to Poland.

Consequences

The war confirmed Poland's return to the Baltic coast, made it a major Baltic power, and left the Teutonic state permanently weakened — a path that led to its secularisation as the Duchy of Prussia in 1525. For the Prussian cities, especially Danzig, the war brought extensive privileges under the Polish Crown and a flourishing of Baltic trade.
