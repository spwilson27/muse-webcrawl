The Indo-Aryan languages (sometimes Indic languages) are a branch of the Indo-Iranian branch of the Indo-European language family. They are spoken by about 800 million people, chiefly in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, and the Maldives, and by large diaspora communities worldwide. The most widely spoken Indo-Aryan languages by native speakers are Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Punjabi, and Urdu.

History

The oldest attested Indo-Aryan language is Vedic Sanskrit, the language of the Rigveda, composed in the late Bronze Age in the Punjab region. Classical Sanskrit was codified by the grammarian Pāṇini. In the Middle Indo-Aryan period the vernaculars known as Prakrits flourished, and the canon of early Buddhism and Jainism was preserved in Pali and related languages. From roughly the 10th century CE, the modern (New Indo-Aryan) languages emerged from regional Apabhraṁśa varieties.

Classification and features

The branch comprises more than 200 languages. Scholars group them geographically and historically into zones — Northern (e.g. Nepali and the Pahari languages), Northwestern (Punjabi, Sindhi), Western (Hindi–Urdu, Gujarati, Rajasthani, Marathi), Central, Eastern (Bengali, Assamese, Odia), and Southern (Sinhala and Maldivian, an insular offshoot) — though classification remains debated. Typical shared features include retroflex consonants, split ergativity in several western languages, compound verbs, and a vast learned vocabulary borrowed from Sanskrit; eastern languages such as Bengali and Assamese have largely lost grammatical gender.
