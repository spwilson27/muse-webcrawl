MacPherson strut

The MacPherson strut is a type of automotive suspension system widely used for the front wheels of modern cars. It combines a shock absorber and a coil spring into a single structural unit that also serves as part of the steering linkage, eliminating the need for an upper control arm.

Design

The design was developed by Earle S. MacPherson of General Motors in the late 1940s and first used on the 1949 Ford Vedette. A telescopic damper forms the kingpin axis, attached at the top to the vehicle body through a bearing mount and at the bottom to the steering knuckle; a single lower control arm locates the wheel. Because the strut itself carries structural loads, the body structure around its top mount must be strong.

Advantages and drawbacks

The system is compact, light, and inexpensive to manufacture, and frees space in the engine compartment — reasons it dominates front-wheel-drive cars. Its disadvantages include limited camber control as the wheel moves, greater friction in the damper, and transmission of noise into the body, which is why many performance and luxury cars use double-wishbone or multi-link designs instead.
