Third Epistle of John

BookNew Testament; Catholic epistles
Chapters1 (15 verses in most editions)
AddresseeGaius
Main figuresThe Elder, Gaius, Diotrephes, Demetrius

The Third Epistle of John, usually called 3 John, is the shortest book in the New Testament by number of words and one of the seven Catholic (general) epistles. It is a personal letter from “the elder” to a man named Gaius, praising his hospitality to travelling Christians, warning against Diotrephes, who rejects the elder’s authority and refuses the brethren, and commending Demetrius. The letter gives a compact view of leadership, networks and conflict in early Christian communities.

Authorship and date

Ancient tradition associated the Johannine letters with John the Apostle or with another early leader sometimes distinguished as John the Elder. The writer of 2 and 3 John calls himself ho presbyteros, “the elder”, rather than naming himself. Style and vocabulary connect the letters to the Fourth Gospel and 1 John, but scholars debate whether the same person wrote all these works or whether they emerged from a Johannine circle. Dates proposed usually fall in the late first or early second century CE.

Contents

The letter opens with a greeting and a prayer for Gaius’s health and prosperity. The elder rejoices that Gaius “walks in the truth” and has shown love to strangers who went out for the sake of the Name, accepting nothing from unbelievers. Supporting such itinerants makes the settled believer a “fellow worker in the truth”.

Against this generosity stands Diotrephes, “who loves to be first”. He does not acknowledge the elder’s authority, spreads malicious talk, refuses to welcome the travelling brothers and expels those who wish to do so. The elder promises to come and confront the conduct. The letter closes by commending Demetrius, whose testimony is good from everyone and from the truth itself, and by expressing a preference for face-to-face speech over pen and ink. Peace is offered to the community, and the friends greet one another by name.

Themes

3 John turns hospitality into theology. In a world of house churches and itinerant missionaries, receiving a traveller meant food, lodging, protection and endorsement. Refusal was not mere rudeness; it could sever a community from a wider network of teaching and accountability. The conflict with Diotrephes also shows the tension between local autonomy and trans-local authority before later church offices hardened.

The letter’s ethical summary is stark: “do not imitate what is evil but imitate what is good.” Gaius and Demetrius embody truth enacted in welcome; Diotrephes embodies ambition and exclusion. Because it names individuals and concrete behaviours, 3 John is prized by social historians even though its brevity leaves the location and outcome unstated.

Canonicity

Like other short Catholic epistles, 3 John was received unevenly in some early lists, partly because of its private character and lack of explicit doctrine. By the fourth century it was widely accepted in the New Testament canon. Its preservation beside the more theological 1 John balances doctrine with practice: orthodoxy appears as fidelity shown in receiving strangers.
