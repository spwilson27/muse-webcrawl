Hydrogen cyanide

Hydrogen cyanide (HCN), historically called prussic acid, is a chemical compound with a linear H–C≡N structure. It is a colorless, extremely poisonous volatile liquid that boils slightly above room temperature, at 25.6 °C. It has a faint bitter-almond odor that a substantial fraction of people cannot detect because of a genetic trait.

Production

Hydrogen cyanide is produced on an industrial scale mainly by the Andrussow process, in which methane and ammonia react in the presence of oxygen over a platinum catalyst at about 1,200 °C. It also occurs naturally in small amounts in the pits of stone fruits such as apricots and cherries, bound in cyanogenic glycosides such as amygdalin.

Uses

HCN is a valuable precursor in chemical manufacturing: it is converted into adiponitrile for nylon, methyl methacrylate for acrylic plastics, sodium cyanide and potassium cyanide for gold mining and electroplating, and various chelating agents. Historically it was used as a fumigant and, infamously, as the basis of the Zyklon B pesticide used in the Holocaust.

Toxicity

Cyanide inhibits cytochrome c oxidase in the mitochondria, preventing cells from using oxygen and causing rapid asphyxiation at the cellular level. Antidotes include hydroxocobalamin and sodium thiosulfate. HCN is also of interest in prebiotic chemistry and astrochemistry: it polymerizes to form purines and pyrimidines and has been detected in interstellar clouds and comet comae.
