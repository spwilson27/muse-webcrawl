David Garrick (19 February 1717 – 20 January 1779) was an English actor, playwright, theatre manager and producer who influenced nearly every aspect of European theatrical practice throughout the 18th century. He was a pupil and friend of Samuel Johnson and is credited with introducing a more naturalistic style of acting and with reviving serious interest in Shakespeare on the English stage.

Early life

Garrick was born in Hereford, the grandson of a French Huguenot wine merchant who had fled to England. He was educated at Lichfield Grammar School, where he came under the influence of Samuel Johnson, and accompanied Johnson to London in 1737 to seek his fortune. After an unsuccessful attempt at the law and a stint as a wine merchant, he turned to the stage.

Acting career

Garrick's London debut as Richard III at Goodman's Fields Theatre in October 1741 was a sensation. Against the declamatory tradition of his predecessors he brought quicksilver changes of expression, conversational delivery and psychological detail; Johnson remarked that his acting was studied from the life. He excelled both in tragedy — Hamlet, Lear, Macbeth, Othello — and in comedy, and became the most celebrated actor in Europe, idolised in Paris and imitated everywhere.

Drury Lane management

In 1747 Garrick became joint patentee and manager of the Theatre Royal, Drury Lane, a post he held until his retirement in 1776. There he reformed nearly every department of the theatre: he banished spectators from the stage, modernised lighting and scenery, disciplined companies with regular rehearsal, and raised the status of the acting profession. His Shakespeare Jubilee at Stratford-upon-Avon in 1769, though rained out, fixed Stratford as the centre of Shakespeare tourism and further cemented the playwright's national status.

Garrick also wrote and adapted plays and farces, including The Clandestine Marriage (1766, with George Colman), while freely altering Shakespeare's texts for contemporary taste — his versions held the stage well into the next century.

Death and legacy

Garrick retired in 1776 and died three years later at his house in Adelphi Terrace, London. He was buried in Poets' Corner, Westminster Abbey, at the foot of Shakespeare's monument. His name survives in the Garrick Club, London theatres, and the phrase "the Garrick tradition" of natural, intelligent acting.
