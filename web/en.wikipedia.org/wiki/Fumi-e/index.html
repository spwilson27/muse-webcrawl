Fumi-e

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A bronze fumi-e plaque, worn smooth by years of being stepped upon.

Fumi-e (踏み絵, "stepping-on picture") were likenesses of Jesus or the Virgin Mary used by the Tokugawa shogunate in Japan to test whether suspected Christians would renounce their faith. Officials ordered people to step on the images; those who refused were identified as Christians and arrested.

History

After Christianity was banned in Japan in the early 17th century, the shogunate sought to root out the remaining Kakure Kirishitan ("hidden Christians"). The fumi-e ceremony (called e-fumi) began in Nagasaki in 1629 and was later extended. Every resident was required to tread on an image, usually a bronze plaque mounted on a wooden board, before officials; the ceremony was held annually in some districts. Those who hesitated or refused were interrogated, tortured, or executed — notably during the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–1638 and after.

The plaques were often engraved in Nagasaki and then reused year after year; many survive today, some with faces worn almost flat by feet. Similar tests were applied to suspected Christians at border posts and ports.

Aftermath

The practice ended with the end of the shogunate and the lifting of the ban on Christianity in the Meiji period. Surviving fumi-e are held in museums, including the Tokyo National Museum and the Nagasaki Museum of History and Culture, where they are displayed as evidence of religious persecution.

In literature

The fumi-e is a central motif in Shūsaku Endō's novel Silence (1966), in which the Jesuit missionary Rodrigues is forced to step on a fumi-e, and in its film adaptations by Masahiro Shinoda (1971) and Martin Scorsese (2016).
