Burning of Washington

The Burning of Washington was a British attack during the Chesapeake campaign of the War of 1812. On 24 August 1814, after defeating the American militia at the Battle of Bladensburg, a British force of some 4,500 men under Major-General Robert Ross occupied Washington, D.C., the young capital of the United States, and set fire to many public buildings, including the White House (then called the President's House), the Capitol, the Treasury and the War Office — the most famous episode of the war on American soil, and the only time since the Revolutionary War that a foreign power has captured and burnt the American capital.

The British commanders presented the burning as retaliation for the American destruction of York (the capital of Upper Canada, now Toronto) in 1813, including the burning of its parliament buildings, and for the later American burning of Port Dover. Private property in Washington was, by and large, respected; the destruction was confined to public buildings. A violent storm — the "storm that saved Washington", sometimes described as including a tornado — struck the city the following day and helped extinguish the fires.

Background

Britain's Chesapeake campaign, conducted by Admiral Sir George Cockburn's raiding squadrons from 1813, was designed to punish the American coast, destroy supplies and divert American troops from Canada. With Napoleon's abdication in April 1814, veteran reinforcements under Ross arrived, and in August 1814 an expedition sailed up the Patuxent River and landed at Benedict, Maryland, aiming at Washington itself. American resistance collapsed at Bladensburg on 24 August — a rout so swift that wits called it the "Bladensburg Races" — while President James Madison, who had watched the battle, fled the city with his government. Dolley Madison, remaining at the President's House until the last moment, famously directed the rescue of the Lansdowne portrait of George Washington and other valuables before escaping ahead of the British advance guard.

The burning

British troops entered the deserted capital on the evening of 24 August. Ross ordered the public buildings fired. The Capitol — its two wings housing the Senate and the House of Representatives, connected by a wooden walkway, and containing the young Library of Congress — was set alight, as were the President's House (whose furniture and fittings were burnt, after the officers had eaten the dinner prepared for the Madisons), the Treasury, and the buildings of the War and State departments. The Washington Navy Yard was burnt by its own commandant to prevent capture of its stores and ships. Notable acts of restraint included the sparing of the Patent Office, saved by its superintendent William Thornton, who argued its inventions were the property of mankind, and of the Marine Barracks. At Alexandria, across the Potomac, the town capitulated and was looted of shipping and stores.

On the afternoon of 25 August a tremendous thunderstorm — with torrential rain and, according to some accounts, a tornado that lifted cannon and killed soldiers of both armies — drenched the city and put out most of the fires, and the British, their work done, withdrew that night to their ships, having occupied the capital for about a day.

Aftermath

The humiliation of Washington was a political catastrophe for Madison's administration: Secretary of War Armstrong was forced to resign, and there was serious talk of removing the capital elsewhere. Instead Congress chose to rebuild on the same site; the Capitol was rebuilt (its ruined sandstone blackened and replaced in part with marble) and the President's House rebuilt under James Hoban — its smoke-stained walls were whitewashed and repainted, fixing the name "White House" in common usage. The same expedition that burnt Washington moved next against Baltimore, where the defence of Fort McHenry in September 1814 restored American pride; Ross was killed in the fighting before the city. Internationally, opinion was divided: the burning was cheered in Britain as revenge for York, but condemned by many in Europe as a barbarous act against a capital of no military value.
