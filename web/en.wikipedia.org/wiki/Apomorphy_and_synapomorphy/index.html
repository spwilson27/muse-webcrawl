Apomorphy and synapomorphy

In phylogenetics, an apomorphy is a derived character — a trait that is evolutionarily novel compared with an ancestral form. When an apomorphy is shared by two or more taxa, it is called a synapomorphy, and such shared derived characters provide evidence that the taxa form a clade (a monophyletic group) descended from a common ancestor in which the trait first evolved. The terminology was introduced by the German entomologist Willi Hennig, the founder of cladistics.

Definitions

An apomorphy that is unique to a single terminal taxon is an autapomorphy; it cannot link that taxon to others but can serve as a diagnostic feature. The opposite of an apomorphy is a plesiomorphy, an ancestral character; a plesiomorphy shared by several taxa is a symplesiomorphy. Symplesiomorphies do not indicate close relationship within the group under study, because the trait was inherited from a more distant ancestor — for example, the presence of five digits is a symplesiomorphy among many tetrapod groups.

Examples

Feathers are a synapomorphy of birds (and their close theropod relatives) relative to other reptiles, while the beak of any one bird species may be an autapomorphy of that species. Hair and mammary glands are synapomorphies of mammals. In cladistic analysis, synapomorphies are the primary evidence used to construct cladograms depicting evolutionary relationships.

Relativity of the terms

Whether a character counts as an apomorphy or a plesiomorphy depends on the level of analysis: a trait that is derived for a large clade becomes ancestral within it. Feathers, apomorphic for birds as a whole, are plesiomorphic when comparing groups within birds.
