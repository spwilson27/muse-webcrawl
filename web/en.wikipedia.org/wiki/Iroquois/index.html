The Iroquois (also known by their self-designation Haudenosaunee, "People of the Longhouse") are a confederacy of Native American and First Nations peoples in North America. The Iroquois Confederacy (Iroquois League) was originally formed by five nations — the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca — joined in 1722 by the Tuscarora to make six. United under the Great Law of Peace, the Confederacy became one of the most powerful Indigenous polities in northeastern North America during the 17th and 18th centuries.

The Great Law of Peace

According to oral tradition, the confederacy was founded by the Great Peacemaker (Deganawida) and his spokesman Hiawatha, who persuaded the warring nations to bury their weapons beneath the Tree of Peace at Onondaga. Scholars debate the date — proposals range from the 12th to the late 16th century. The Great Law of Peace (Kaianere'kó:wa) established a federal council of fifty sachems (roia:ne) nominated by clan mothers of matrilineal clans, with the Onondaga keeping the council fire and the wampum records. The system combined hereditary office, female authority over appointments, consensus decision-making and condolence ceremonies to heal grief and forestall blood feud.

History

The Iroquois homeland stretched across what is now upstate New York between the Hudson River and Lake Erie, in a region of palisaded longhouse villages supported by maize, bean and squash agriculture. During the 17th-century Beaver Wars, armed with Dutch and then English firearms, the Confederacy dispersed neighbouring peoples and dominated the fur trade of the interior. In the 18th century the Iroquois pursued a strategy of diplomatic balance between New France and the British colonies, expressed in the Covenant Chain alliance with Britain. The American Revolutionary War split the Confederacy: most nations sided with the British, the Oneida and Tuscarora largely with the Americans. Sullivan's punitive expedition of 1779 destroyed dozens of towns, and after the war many Haudenosaunee removed to Canada (notably the Grand River reserve in Ontario) or to reservations in New York, Wisconsin and Oklahoma.

Society and culture

Traditional society was organised in matrilineal clans named for animals (Wolf, Bear, Turtle and others) cutting across national lines. Women owned the longhouses and fields and controlled the distribution of food — leverage that could check war parties — while men hunted, traded, conducted diplomacy and made war. Oratory, wampum diplomacy and the lacrosse game (the "Creator's game") remain culturally central. The Handsome Lake religion (Gaiwiio), preached from 1799, combined traditional ceremony with a moral code and continues alongside Longhouse, Christian and other practice.

Modern nations and influence

Today the member nations exist as federally recognised tribes in the United States and First Nations in Canada, asserting sovereignty, reviving their languages and succeeding in land-claim litigation. The Hiawatha Belt and the confederacy's consensus federalism have often been cited — controversially — as an influence on the framers of the United States Constitution, a connection acknowledged in a 1988 US Senate resolution. The Haudenosaunee passport and the Iroquois Nationals lacrosse team have become prominent assertions of nationhood.
