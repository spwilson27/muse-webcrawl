Salmacis

In Greek mythology, Salmacis (Ancient Greek: Σαλμακίς) was an naiad nymph who lived in a fountain or spring in Caria, in southwestern Asia Minor. She is best known from the fourth book of Ovid's Metamorphoses, in which she falls in love with Hermaphroditus, the son of Hermes and Aphrodite.

Myth

Unlike other naiads, who followed Diana in hunting and chastity, Salmacis idled by her pool. When the young Hermaphroditus arrived at the spring and undressed to bathe, Salmacis was overcome with desire. She leapt into the water and clung to him, praying to the gods that they should never be parted. The gods granted her wish literally: their two bodies were merged into a single form, neither wholly male nor female but possessing characteristics of both sexes. Hermaphroditus then cursed the pool, so that any man who bathed in it would emerge softened and half-man like himself.

The fountain of Salmacis

The spring of Salmacis was located near Halicarnassus in Caria. In antiquity its waters had a reputation for effeminating those who drank from them, a belief mentioned by Strabo and Vitruvius, though Strabo himself suggested the ill repute of the water was unfounded and that luxury, not water, was to blame.

In culture

The story has been a recurring subject in art, inspiring sculptures and paintings by artists such as Bartholomeus Spranger and François-Joseph Navez. The myth also gives its name to the concept of a single body combining male and female attributes.
