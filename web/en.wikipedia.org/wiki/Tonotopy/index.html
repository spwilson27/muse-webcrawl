Tonotopy

Tonotopy is the spatial arrangement of where sounds of different frequency are processed in the brain. A tonotopic map is the representation of the auditory world in a neural structure, in which neurons are arranged in an orderly fashion according to the sound frequency to which they respond most strongly. Tonotopy is a general organizing principle of the auditory system, found at nearly every level from the cochlea to the auditory cortex.

Cochlear tonotopy

In the cochlea, sound vibrations of different frequencies cause maximal displacement of the basilar membrane at different positions along its length. High frequencies produce maximal displacement near the base of the cochlea, close to the oval window, while low frequencies peak near the apex. This mechanical frequency-to-place mapping arises because the basilar membrane is narrower and stiffer at the base and wider and more flexible at the apex. Hair cells at each position are most sensitive to a particular characteristic frequency, and the auditory nerve fibers innervating them preserve this ordering.

Central tonotopy

Tonotopic organization is maintained through the auditory pathway, including the cochlear nuclei, the superior olivary complex, the lateral lemniscus, the inferior colliculus, the medial geniculate body of the thalamus, and the primary auditory cortex. In the auditory cortex, frequency bands are arranged as stripes or gradients across the cortical surface. Tonotopic maps are established during development and can be modified by experience and by hearing loss, a form of neural plasticity.
