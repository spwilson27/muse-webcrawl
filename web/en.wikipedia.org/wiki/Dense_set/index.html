Dense set

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In topology and related areas of mathematics, a subset A of a topological space X is said to be dense in X if every point of X either belongs to A or is arbitrarily close to a member of A. Formally, A is dense in X if the closure of A is equal to X — that is, cl(A) = X. Equivalently, A intersects every non-empty open subset of X.

Examples

  • The rational numbers are dense in the real numbers: between any two reals there is a rational.
  • The irrational numbers are likewise dense in the reals.
  • By the Weierstrass approximation theorem, the set of polynomial functions is dense in the space of continuous functions on a closed interval (with the uniform topology).
  • A metric space M is dense in its completion.

Related notions

A topological space with a countable dense subset is called separable. A subset whose complement is dense is called nowhere dense in a stronger sense when its closure has empty interior; sets of the first category (meagre sets) are countable unions of nowhere dense sets. The Baire category theorem states that a complete metric space is of the second category in itself, a result with wide applications in functional analysis.

Density in metric spaces

In a metric space, A is dense in X if for every x in X and every ε > 0 there is a point of A within distance ε of x. This makes precise the idea that members of A can approximate every point of X arbitrarily well — the basis for defining real numbers as limits of rational sequences.
