Natural monopoly

A natural monopoly arises when long-run average costs fall over the whole relevant range of output.

A natural monopoly is a monopoly in an industry in which high infrastructural costs and other barriers to entry relative to the size of the market give the largest supplier an overwhelming advantage over potential competitors. Formally, an industry is a natural monopoly if a single firm can supply the entire market demand at a lower cost than any combination of two or more firms — a property known as subadditivity of the cost function. Under these conditions, competition is inefficient or impossible, and the industry tends towards a single supplier.

Causes

Natural monopolies typically arise where production requires a costly physical network — water mains, sewer systems, electricity grids, railways, or gas pipelines — that would be wasteful to duplicate. Very large fixed costs combined with low marginal costs cause average costs to decline across the entire range of market demand, so the first firm to build the network can always undercut a rival that must build its own. Control of a scarce natural resource can have a similar effect.

Regulation

Because an unregulated natural monopolist can restrict output and charge monopoly prices, governments typically respond in one of three ways: public ownership of the utility; private ownership subject to price regulation, such as rate-of-return regulation or price-cap (RPI-X) regulation; or restructuring to separate the naturally monopolistic network (wires, pipes, track) from potentially competitive services (generation, retail supply, train operation), allowing competition over the shared network. Regulation carries its own risks, including regulatory capture and weak incentives for cost efficiency.

Changing boundaries

Technological change can erode natural monopolies. Telecommunications were long treated as a natural monopoly, but microwave, fibre-optic, mobile, and internet technologies undermined that rationale and led to widespread deregulation from the 1980s onward. Conversely, economists debate whether some digital platforms exhibit natural-monopoly characteristics through network effects and economies of scale in data.
