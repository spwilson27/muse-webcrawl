Big Three (automobile manufacturers)

The Big Three is a term for the three largest automobile manufacturers in a given market. In the United States, the Big Three refers to General Motors, Ford Motor Company, and Chrysler (since 2021 part of Stellantis). Together they dominated the American automobile industry for most of the twentieth century and are closely associated with Detroit, Michigan.

[Image: Headquarters buildings of General Motors, Ford, and Chrysler in the Detroit area]

Formation and dominance

By the late 1920s, hundreds of early American automakers had consolidated into a handful of firms. GM, Ford, and Chrysler (founded 1925) commanded the overwhelming majority of U.S. sales through the post-war boom, shaping American car culture with annual model changes, dealer networks, and powerful influence in Washington. Their share peaked around the 1960s–70s at roughly four-fifths of the market.

Decline and restructuring

From the 1970s, competition from Japanese and European "transplant" factories, fuel-economy regulation, and quality problems eroded the Big Three's share to under half. The 2008 financial crisis pushed GM and Chrysler through federally funded bankruptcies in 2009; Ford avoided bankruptcy but mortgaged its assets. In the twenty-first century the three have pivoted toward trucks and SUVs and invested heavily in electric vehicles, competing with Tesla and foreign makers.

Other markets

Similar groupings exist elsewhere: Germany's "Big Three" are Volkswagen, Mercedes-Benz, and BMW; Japan's are Toyota, Honda, and Nissan; South Korea's industry is dominated by Hyundai and Kia.
