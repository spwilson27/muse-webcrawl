Wavenumber

Symbolk (angular wavenumber); ν̃ (spectroscopic wavenumber)
DefinitionSpatial frequency of a wave: number of wavelengths per unit distance
SI unitReciprocal metre (m−1); spectroscopy commonly uses cm−1
Relationsk = 2π/λ; ν̃ = 1/λ

In the physical sciences, the wavenumber (also wave number or repetency) is the spatial frequency of a wave, measured in cycles per unit distance (ordinary wavenumber) or radians per unit distance (angular wavenumber). It is analogous to temporal frequency, and waves are sometimes described as functions of both frequency and wavenumber through a dispersion relation.

Definitions

The spectroscopic wavenumber ν̃ is defined as the reciprocal of the wavelength λ: ν̃ = 1/λ. It is widely used in infrared and Raman spectroscopy, where spectral positions are quoted in reciprocal centimetres (cm−1); because photon energy is proportional to 1/λ, wavenumber is directly proportional to energy, making it a convenient scale for molecular vibrations.

The angular wavenumber k, used in wave mechanics and electromagnetism, includes a factor of 2π: k = 2π/λ = 2πν/c for a wave of frequency ν travelling at speed c. In vector form, the wave vector k points in the direction of propagation and has magnitude k. In quantum mechanics the de Broglie relation connects wavenumber to momentum: p = ħk.

Applications

Wavenumber appears throughout physics: in the wave equation and dispersion relations for light, sound and water waves; in crystallography (reciprocal lattice vectors); in atmospheric science, where planetary waves are counted by zonal wavenumber; and in spectroscopy, where absorption and emission features are charted against wavenumber. In a dispersive medium the phase velocity depends on wavenumber, and the derivative of angular frequency with respect to k gives the group velocity.
