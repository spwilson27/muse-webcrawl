In fluid dynamics, gravity waves are waves generated in a fluid medium or at the interface between two media when the force of gravity or buoyancy tries to restore equilibrium. An example of such an interface is that between the atmosphere and the ocean, which gives rise to wind waves. When a fluid element is displaced on an interface or internally in a region of differing density, gravity restores it toward equilibrium, and the resulting oscillation propagates as a wave.

Gravity waves on an air–sea interface of the ocean are called surface gravity waves — the ordinary wind waves and swells of the sea surface. In the Earth's atmosphere, gravity waves transfer energy and momentum from the troposphere to the middle and upper atmosphere, where their breaking deposits momentum that drives large-scale circulation. Lee waves, formed when stable air flows over mountains, are a familiar visible manifestation, often marked by stationary lenticular or wave clouds.

Internal gravity waves

Within a stably stratified fluid, displaced parcels experience buoyancy as a restoring force, and the resulting internal waves oscillate at frequencies below the Brunt–Väisälä (buoyancy) frequency. Internal waves in the ocean, with amplitudes of tens of metres, are generated by tides flowing over submarine topography and play a major role in ocean mixing.

Relation to gravitational waves

Gravity waves must not be confused with gravitational waves, which are ripples in the curvature of spacetime predicted by general relativity and first directly detected in 2015. Gravity waves are classical phenomena in fluids; the similar names are a historical coincidence.
