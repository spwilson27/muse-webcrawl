Contact print

A contact print is a photographic image produced from film or digital negatives, photographic paper, or photographic plates by placing the negative in direct contact with a photosensitive surface and exposing it to light. Because no enlarger or lens is used, the resulting positive image is exactly the same size as the negative. A sheet containing multiple contact prints from a roll of film is called a contact sheet.

Process

In a traditional darkroom, the photographer places strips of film emulsion-side down on photographic paper, holds them flat under glass (often in a contact printing frame), and exposes them briefly to light from an enlarger lamp or a bare bulb. The paper is then developed, stopped, fixed and washed like any print. Exposure and contrast are controlled by exposure time and paper grade or filtration.

Uses

Contact sheets were for decades the standard first step in a film photographer's workflow: they served as an index of the negatives, a proofing tool for selecting frames worth enlarging, and a compact archive record. Photographers famously marked selections directly on the sheet with a grease pencil. Because contact prints of large-format negatives (4×5 inches and above) are themselves of presentable size, many fine-art photographers — notably Edward Weston — made contact printing their primary method, valuing its sharpness and full tonal range, since the absence of an enlarging lens avoids optical losses.

In photojournalism and art

The contact sheet acquired an aesthetic and documentary status of its own, revealing the sequence of a shoot and the photographer's editing decisions; Magnum Photos' archive of contact sheets was exhibited and published as Magnum Contact Sheets. In the digital era, software-generated "contact sheets" or thumbnails replicate the function, while some film photographers continue to make darkroom contact prints as an archival and artistic practice.
