Thermostat

Thermostat
Function: regulates temperature by switching heating or cooling equipment on and off at a set point
Word origin: Greek thermē (heat) + statos (standing, stationary)
Sensor types: bimetallic strip, gas bellows, wax pellet, thermistor, digital sensor
Iconic design: the Honeywell Round (1953), designed by Henry Dreyfuss

A thermostat is a device that senses the temperature of a physical system and controls a heating or cooling appliance so as to maintain the temperature near a desired set point. Thermostats are found wherever temperature must be regulated: in buildings (central heating and air conditioning), in refrigerators, ovens, water heaters, incubators, greenhouses, and in the cooling systems of engines. The system need only be controlled roughly: a thermostat typically switches the equipment fully on or off (bang-bang control), with a small hysteresis band to prevent rapid cycling, though modulating designs also exist.

History

Temperature feedback control began in the seventeenth century with Cornelis Drebbel's mercury-based regulator for furnaces and incubators. In the 1830s Andrew Ure coined the word "thermostat" for a bimetallic device controlling steam heat. Warren S. Johnson patented an electric room thermostat in 1883 and founded a controls company; Albert Butz's 1885 damper-flapper patent and company were a foundation of Honeywell. The familiar round domestic thermostat — the Honeywell Round of 1953, designed by Henry Dreyfuss — became a design icon.

Sensor types

  • Bimetallic strips bend as two metals of different thermal expansion heat up, tilting a mercury switch or opening contacts.
  • Gas-filled bellows expand and contract with temperature, common in automotive and older HVAC controls.
  • Wax pellets that melt and expand drive the thermostat valves in car cooling systems and thermostatic radiator valves.
  • Electronic sensors — thermistors and integrated digital sensors — feed microprocessor controllers in modern programmable and smart thermostats.

Modern thermostats

Programmable thermostats let occupants schedule different set points by time of day and day of week, cutting energy use when buildings are empty or occupants asleep. Smart thermostats add learning algorithms, occupancy detection, remote control by phone, and integration with energy-demand programmes. In vehicles, the cooling-system thermostat is a purely mechanical valve that stays closed while the engine warms up, then opens to route coolant through the radiator.
