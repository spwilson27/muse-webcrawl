Tsuneko Okazaki (岡崎 恒子, Okazaki Tsuneko; born 7 June 1933) is a Japanese molecular biologist famous for discovering, together with her husband Reiji Okazaki, the short DNA segments now known as Okazaki fragments—the pieces in which the lagging strand of DNA is discontinuously synthesised during replication. She was one of the first Japanese women to build a major career in the life sciences.

Life and career

Born in Nagoya, Tsuneko studied biology at Nagoya University, graduating in the 1950s when women in Japanese science were rare. She and Reiji Okazaki (1930–1975) worked together at Nagoya and, during a period in the United States, in the laboratories of leading nucleic-acid researchers. Returning to Nagoya, the Okazakis tackled the puzzle of how DNA polymerase—which synthesises only in the 5′→3′ direction—could copy both antiparallel strands of the double helix.

Okazaki fragments

In 1968 the Okazakis and their colleagues published evidence that one strand (the lagging strand) is made as short pieces, each started by a primer and later joined by DNA ligase. These fragments—about 100–200 nucleotides in eukaryotes and 1,000–2,000 in bacteria—are called Okazaki fragments in the textbooks of every biology student. After Reiji's early death from leukaemia in 1975 (he had been exposed to radiation effects as a young man in Hiroshima), Tsuneko continued the research programme, becoming professor at Nagoya and later at Fujita Health University, and studied replication initiation and development in sea urchins and other systems.

Recognition

Okazaki received the L'Oréal-UNESCO Award for Women in Science in 2000 and was elected to the Japan Academy; she was named a Person of Cultural Merit and awarded the Order of Culture, among Japan's highest honours. She has also been a prominent advocate for women in science and for childcare support for researchers at Japanese universities.
