Sodium alginate

Sodium alginate
Chemical formula: (C6H7NaO6)n
Appearance: White to yellowish-brown powder
E number: E401
Source: Brown algae cell walls

Sodium alginate is the sodium salt of alginic acid, a naturally occurring anionic polysaccharide found in the cell walls of brown algae (Phaeophyceae), including giant kelp (Macrocystis pyrifera) and Laminaria species. It is a linear copolymer of β-D-mannuronic acid (M) and α-L-guluronic acid (G) residues linked 1→4. It is widely used as a thickener, stabilizer, gelling agent, and emulsifier in food, pharmaceutical, textile, and biotechnology industries. Its food additive code is E401.

Properties

Sodium alginate dissolves in water to form viscous solutions. In the presence of divalent cations such as calcium (Ca2+), it forms strong gels: the calcium ions cross-link guluronate blocks in the so-called "egg-box" model. Gel strength depends on the proportion and arrangement of G-blocks in the polymer. The compound is extracted commercially by treating seaweed with alkaline solutions, filtering, and precipitating the alginate.

Uses

Food industry

Sodium alginate is used to thicken ice cream, sauces, and dressings; to form restructured foods such as imitation crab and pimento fillings for olives; and in modernist cuisine for spherification, a technique popularized by Ferran Adrià at elBulli, in which alginate solutions dropped into a calcium bath form gel-skinned spheres of liquid.

Medical and scientific

Alginate dressings are used for wound care because they absorb exudate and maintain a moist environment. Alginate hydrogels are used for cell encapsulation, drug delivery, and dental impressions. In microbiology and biotechnology, alginate beads are used to immobilize cells and enzymes.

Industrial

It serves as a thickener in textile printing pastes, in paper coating, and in welding rod coatings.
