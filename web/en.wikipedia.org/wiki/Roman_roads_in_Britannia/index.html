Roman roads in Britannia

Roman roads in Britannia were the paved military and administrative highways built in the Roman province of Britain between the invasion of AD 43 and the end of Roman rule around AD 410. At their height the principal routes comprised roughly 3,200 km (2,000 miles) of metalled road, supplemented by a far larger network of secondary and local routes. Many survive in the modern British road network, and others are preserved as earthworks, hollow ways and straight alignments across the landscape.

Construction

Roads were typically built on a raised embankment (agger) with drainage ditches on either side. A foundation of large stones was covered with gravel or flint and finished with compacted metalling; in major towns and on important routes the surface could be paved with stone slabs. Roads were surveyed in straight segments between high points, using sighting instruments such as the groma. Milestones recording the emperor's name and the distance to the next town were erected along the routes.

Principal roads

  • Watling Street – from Dover (Dubris) through London (Londinium) and St Albans (Verulamium) to Wroxeter (Viroconium) and onward toward Chester and York.
  • Ermine Street – from London north through Lincoln (Lindum) to York (Eboracum).
  • The Fosse Way – from Exeter (Isca Dumnoniorum) through Bath (Aquae Sulis) and Cirencester (Corinium) to Lincoln.
  • Akeman Street – from St Albans through Alchester and Cirencester.
  • Dere Street – from York north through Hadrian's Wall to the Antonine Wall in Scotland.
  • Stane Street – from London to Chichester (Noviomagus).

Purpose and legacy

The network was built first for military supply and rapid troop movement, then served administration, taxation, the imperial post (cursus publicus) and commerce, with posting stations (mansiones) at regular intervals. After the Roman withdrawal, many roads remained in use through the Anglo-Saxon and medieval periods; several, including long sections of the A5 (Watling Street) and A1 (Ermine Street), still form part of the modern road system. The Antonine Itinerary and the Peutinger Table preserve lists of routes and stations.
