Siege of Tripolitsa

The siege of Tripolitsa (Greek: Άλωση της Τριπολιτσάς) was a decisive early battle of the Greek War of Independence, fought from the spring of 1821 until the storming of the city on 5 October 1821 (Old Style: 23 September). Tripolitsa (modern Tripoli, Arcadia) was the Ottoman administrative capital of the Morea (Peloponnese) and the seat of the Ottoman governor. Its capture by Greek revolutionary forces under Theodoros Kolokotronis gave the revolution control of the central Peloponnese — but the fall of the city was followed by a massacre of thousands of Muslim and Jewish inhabitants, one of the most notorious atrocities of the war.

Background

When the Greek uprising began in March 1821, some 30,000 people — Ottoman officials, soldiers, Muslim townspeople, and the city's Jewish community — were crowded into Tripolitsa. Kolokotronis, appointed commander in the Peloponnese, established fortified camps around the city and cut it off, while Greek forces elsewhere in the Morea reduced the outlying Ottoman strongholds.

The siege

The blockade lasted through the summer of 1821, punctuated by skirmishes and Ottoman sorties. A large Ottoman relief attempt through the Argolid failed after the Greek victory at the Battle of Doliana and pressure around the city tightened. Inside, famine and disease grew severe. Negotiations for surrender — conducted mainly with the city's Albanian troops, who eventually agreed to depart under safe conduct — dragged on into the autumn. Before terms could be completed for the rest of the garrison and population, Greek irregulars stormed the walls on 5 October 1821 after fires and confusion at the gates.

The massacre

Over the following days, Greek forces killed a large part of the Muslim and Jewish population — contemporary estimates vary widely, from several thousand to more than ten thousand — and plundered the city. Kolokotronis famously recalled that from the gate to the citadel his horse's hooves never touched the ground, a reference to the carpet of corpses. The atrocity, answering earlier Ottoman massacres of Greeks such as the Constantinople pogroms and the execution of Patriarch Gregory V, shocked European opinion even as philhellene sympathy for the Greek cause grew, and remains a grim emblem of the war's cycle of communal violence.
