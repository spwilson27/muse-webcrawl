Ramon Llull

Ramon Llull (Catalan: [rəˈmoɲ ˈʎuʎ]; c. 1232 – 1315/1316), also Latinised as Raimundus Lullus, was a Majorcan philosopher, theologian, mystic, logician and writer. A prolific author in Catalan, Arabic and Latin, he is regarded as a pioneer of computation theory for his logical "Art", a system of combining concepts that anticipated later ideas of mechanical reasoning and influenced figures such as Leibniz. He is also considered the first major writer in Catalan literature.

Life

Llull was born in Palma, Majorca, shortly after the island's conquest by James I of Aragon. He served as a courtier and seneschal at the Majorcan court and married Blanca Picany, with whom he had two children. Around 1263–1265 he underwent a religious conversion after a series of visions of the crucified Christ, abandoned court life, and devoted himself to converting Muslims to Christianity through rational argument rather than force. He learned Arabic, founded a school at Miramar to train missionaries, and travelled extensively through Europe and North Africa seeking support for his projects.

The Art

Llull's central project was his Ars ("Art"), set out in works such as the Ars Magna (1305–1308). The Art used rotating concentric paper discs (volvelles) inscribed with letters representing divine attributes and other basic concepts; by mechanically combining the letters, the user could generate and evaluate propositions about God and creation. Llull believed the Art provided a universal method of demonstrating the truths of Christianity to Jews and Muslims using "necessary reasons" shared by all three faiths. The system is an early landmark in the history of logic, combinatorics and artificial intelligence.

Writings and legacy

Llull wrote more than 250 works, including the novel Blanquerna (which contains the mystical Book of the Lover and the Beloved), the Book of the Gentile and the Three Wise Men, and the chivalric manual Book of the Order of Chivalry, which was widely translated. Tradition holds that he was stoned by a crowd in Béjaïa or Tunis on his final missionary journey around 1315 and died of his wounds. He was beatified by the Catholic Church in 1847, and "Lullism" remained an influential, if sometimes controversial, school of thought for centuries.
