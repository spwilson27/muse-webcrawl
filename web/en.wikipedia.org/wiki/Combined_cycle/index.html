A combined cycle power plant is an assembly of heat engines that work in tandem from the same source of heat, converting it into mechanical energy. The most common type cycles the engines in tandem: first a gas turbine (Brayton cycle) generates electricity, and its hot exhaust is then used to make steam to drive a steam turbine (Rankine cycle). The principle is that after completing its cycle, the working fluid of the first engine still has usable energy — exhaust gases at roughly 500–600 °C — which an ordinary simple-cycle plant would waste.

Operation

Fuel, usually natural gas, is burned in the gas turbine, which typically supplies about two-thirds of the plant's output. The exhaust passes through a heat recovery steam generator (HRSG), which may include supplementary firing, raising steam at multiple pressure levels to feed a steam turbine supplying the remaining output. Modern installations achieve thermal efficiencies above 60% in combined-cycle gas turbine (CCGT) service — the highest of any fossil-fuel technology — with record plants such as EDF's Bouchain (GE 9HA) exceeding 62%.

Variants and uses

Integrated gasification combined cycle (IGCC) plants gasify coal or other feedstocks to fuel the gas turbine, enabling pre-combustion carbon capture. Combined heat and power (cogeneration) plants extract steam for district heating or industry, raising total fuel utilisation further. Combined-cycle plants are widely used for mid-merit and increasingly flexible generation, balancing low emissions (about half the CO2 per kWh of a coal plant) against rapid start-up capability.
