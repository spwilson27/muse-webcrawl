The PDP-6, short for Programmed Data Processor model 6, was a computer developed by Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) during 1963 and first delivered in the summer of 1964. It was an expansion of DEC's existing 18-bit systems (the PDP-4 and PDP-5 lineage aside) into a full 36-bit mainframe-class machine, intended for large-scale time-sharing, batch processing and real-time applications.

Description

The PDP-6 used discrete germanium transistors on DEC's System Modules, magnetic core memory, and a 36-bit word with a symmetrical instruction format: 18-bit addresses allowed memory addressing that grew from 16K to 256K words. It included 16 fast general-purpose accumulators (initially implemented with flip-flops, later using the first 16 words of memory), index registers, hardware for floating-point arithmetic, and a sophisticated interrupt and memory-protection system designed for timesharing monitors. A distinctive feature was its electrostatic paper-tape and high-speed input/output equipment, including DECtape, plus support for teletype and graphics terminals that made it popular in early artificial-intelligence laboratories.

History and influence

Only 23 PDP-6 systems were built — the machine was expensive and difficult to manufacture, and DEC briefly lost money on the line. Customers included MIT's Project MAC and AI Lab, Stanford's AI laboratory and various universities and laboratories. Its cultural and technical significance far exceeded its sales: the PDP-6 architecture, timesharing monitors and software culture (including the TOPS-10 lineage and the hacker community around ITS) directly evolved into the highly successful PDP-10 (1966), which defined large-scale interactive computing for a generation. Instructions, addressing modes and even console-switch style carried over almost unchanged.

Software

PDP-6 installations ran DEC's timesharing monitor software and an influential body of programs: assemblers (MACRO), FORTRAN and LISP compilers and interpreters, the TECO text editor at MIT, and early artificial-intelligence programs. The machine is associated with the golden age of the MIT hacker culture that later produced the ITS operating system on the PDP-10.
