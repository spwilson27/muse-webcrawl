Budapest Ghetto

LocationDistrict VII, Budapest, Hungary
EstablishedDecember 1944
Liberated18 January 1945
Population~70,000 at its peak

The Budapest Ghetto was a Nazi ghetto set up in Budapest, Hungary, in the final months of the Second World War, where at least 70,000 Jews were forced to live in severely crowded and harsh conditions in the traditional Jewish quarter of District VII, around the Dohány Street Synagogue.

History

Following the German occupation of Hungary in March 1944 and the Arrow Cross coup of October 1944, Budapest's Jews were progressively stripped of rights, robbed, and marked with yellow stars. In late November 1944 the Arrow Cross government ordered the creation of a closed ghetto, sealed off in December. In addition, an "international ghetto" of houses under neutral diplomatic protection housed Jews with papers from diplomats such as Raoul Wallenberg and Carl Lutz.

Conditions and liberation

Inside the ghetto, food and medicine were desperately short, disease spread in unheated, overcrowded buildings, and thousands of unburied bodies accumulated by January 1945. Arrow Cross gangs repeatedly raided the ghetto and murdered residents on the banks of the Danube. The ghetto was liberated by Soviet forces on 18 January 1945; roughly 70,000 people had survived within it, an unusually high survival rate compared with provincial Hungarian Jewry, most of whom had been deported to Auschwitz in the summer of 1944.
