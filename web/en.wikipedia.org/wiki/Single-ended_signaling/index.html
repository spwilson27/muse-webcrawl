Single-ended signaling

Single-ended signaling (in American spelling; also written "single-ended signalling" in British usage) is the simplest and most common method of transmitting electrical signals over wires. One wire (the signal conductor) carries a varying voltage that represents the signal, while a second wire is connected to a reference voltage, usually ground. The receiver interprets the difference between the signal voltage and the shared ground reference.

Characteristics

Single-ended signaling is cheap and easy to implement — each signal needs only one conductor plus a common ground — and it was used for most early computer interfaces: RS-232 serial ports, parallel ports, the original SCSI bus, TTL and CMOS logic connections, and the analogue audio in consumer RCA cables. Its weakness is susceptibility to noise: any interference picked up by the signal wire, and any difference in ground potential between transmitter and receiver ("ground bounce" or ground loops), adds directly to the measured signal. For this reason single-ended links are limited in speed and distance compared with differential signaling.

Comparison with differential signaling

Differential signaling sends the signal on two conductors as a complementary pair and reads only the difference between them, so common-mode noise cancels. High-speed interfaces (USB, Ethernet, HDMI, modern serial buses) are differential. Single-ended signaling persists wherever cost and simplicity dominate and distances are short: inside circuit boards, in memory interfaces (where single-ended parallel buses with careful termination are still common), and in low-speed control and audio circuits.
