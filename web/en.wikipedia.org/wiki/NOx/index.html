NOx

In atmospheric chemistry, NOx is a generic term for the nitrogen oxides most relevant to air pollution: nitric oxide (NO) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2). These gases are produced by combustion at high temperatures and by lightning and soil microbes, and they play central roles in the formation of smog, acid rain and fine particulate matter.

Sources

At the high temperatures of internal-combustion engines and furnaces, atmospheric nitrogen and oxygen combine to form NO (thermal NOx); nitrogen contained in fuels adds a further fraction. Road transport, power generation, shipping and industrial boilers are the dominant anthropogenic sources. Most combustion NOx leaves the source as NO, which oxidises in air to NO2, the reddish-brown gas responsible for the colour of polluted skies.

Environmental and health effects

NO2 irritates the airways and is associated epidemiologically with asthma and reduced lung function. In sunlight, NOx reacts with volatile organic compounds to produce ground-level ozone, the principal component of photochemical smog. NOx also converts to nitric acid and nitrate particles, contributing to acid deposition and to eutrophication of waters; conversely, stratospheric NOx (largely from nitrous oxide, N2O) participates in ozone-layer chemistry.

Control

Regulators set ambient limits for NO2 — for example in the US National Ambient Air Quality Standards and EU air-quality directives. Control technologies include low-NOx burners, exhaust-gas recirculation, three-way catalytic converters on petrol vehicles, and selective catalytic reduction (SCR) using urea, fitted to diesel vehicles and power plants. The 2015 Volkswagen emissions scandal ("Dieselgate") revealed that many diesel cars emitted far more NOx in real driving than in laboratory tests, accelerating shifts toward electrification and real-driving emissions testing.
