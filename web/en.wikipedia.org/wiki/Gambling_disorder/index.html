Gambling disorder

Gambling disorder (also called pathological gambling, compulsive gambling or gambling addiction) is a behavioural addiction characterised by persistent and recurrent problematic gambling behaviour leading to clinically significant impairment or distress. In the DSM-5 it is classified among the substance-related and addictive disorders — the first behavioural addiction to be formally grouped with them — while the ICD-11 classifies it under disorders due to addictive behaviours.

Diagnosis

DSM-5 diagnosis requires at least four of nine criteria within a 12-month period, including needing to gamble with increasing amounts of money (tolerance), restlessness or irritability when trying to cut down (withdrawal-like symptoms), repeated unsuccessful efforts to control the behaviour, preoccupation with gambling, gambling when distressed, chasing losses, lying to conceal involvement, jeopardising relationships or employment, and relying on others for bailout money. Severity is graded mild (4–5 criteria), moderate (6–7) or severe (8–9).

Epidemiology and causes

Lifetime prevalence estimates in adults are roughly 0.4–1.0% for the full disorder, with higher rates of problem gambling. Onset often begins in adolescence or young adulthood in men and later in life in women, whose course may be more rapid ("telescoping"). Risk factors include impulsivity, genetic influences (twin studies suggest substantial heritability), comorbid substance use, mood and anxiety disorders, ADHD, and exposure to gambling environments. Neurobiological research implicates dysregulation of dopaminergic reward pathways, and gambling disorder can be induced in some patients by dopamine agonist treatment for Parkinson's disease.

Treatment

No medication is specifically approved for gambling disorder, though opioid antagonists such as naltrexone have shown some evidence of benefit. Psychological treatments — cognitive behavioural therapy, motivational interviewing and self-help programmes such as Gamblers Anonymous — are the mainstay. Financial counselling, self-exclusion schemes and blocking software are used as harm-reduction measures. The consequences of untreated disorder can include debt, bankruptcy, relationship breakdown and an elevated risk of suicide.
