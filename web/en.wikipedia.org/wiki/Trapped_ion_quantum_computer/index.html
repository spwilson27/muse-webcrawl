Trapped ion quantum computer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A trapped ion quantum computer is one proposed approach to a large-scale quantum computer. Ions, or charged atomic particles, can be confined and suspended in free space using electromagnetic fields. Qubits are stored in stable electronic states of each ion, and quantum information can be transferred through the collective quantized motion of the ions in a shared trap. Lasers are applied to induce coupling between the qubit states (for single-qubit operations) or between the internal qubit states and the external motional states (for entanglement operations).

History

The scheme was proposed in 1995 by Juan Ignacio Cirac and Peter Zoller, building on the ion-trapping expertise of groups at NIST (led by David Wineland) and elsewhere. The first entangling gate between two ions was demonstrated at NIST in 1995, and trapped ions have since been a leading platform for quantum information science, quantum simulation, and the world's most precise atomic clocks.

Operation

Common qubit ions include Ca+, Yb+, Ba+, Sr+, and Be+, held in Paul (radio-frequency) traps or Penning traps, often in linear chains. After laser cooling to near the motional ground state, individual lasers — or global beams with tightly focused addressing — rotate qubit states. Two-qubit gates (such as the Mølmer–Sørensen gate) couple ions through their shared vibrational modes, giving an effective all-to-all connectivity within a chain: any qubit can be entangled with any other without swap operations.

Advantages and challenges

Trapped ions offer exceptionally long coherence times (minutes or more for memory qubits), high-fidelity preparation and measurement, and among the highest reported gate fidelities — single-qubit gates above 99.99% and two-qubit gates around 99.9% in laboratory systems. Because all ions of a species are identical, manufacturing variability is minimal. The main challenges are speed (gates are slower than in solid-state platforms, typically microseconds to hundreds of microseconds) and scaling: long chains become hard to control, so architectures propose modular designs — many small traps linked by shuttling ions between zones (the QCCD architecture) or by photonic interconnects between modules. Commercial systems from companies such as IonQ and Quantinuum deploy tens of high-quality qubits and pursue error correction with these modules.

Requirements

Like other platforms, trapped ions are assessed against the DiVincenzo criteria: well-defined qubits, initialization, long coherence relative to gate time, a universal gate set, and qubit-specific measurement — all of which the platform has demonstrated in principle.
