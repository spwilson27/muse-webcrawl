Border reivers were raiders along the Anglo-Scottish border from the late 13th century to the beginning of the 17th century. They included both English and Scottish people, and they raided the entire border country without regard for their victims' nationality. Their heyday was in the last hundred years of their existence, during the Tudor era and the time of the Stewart kings in Scotland. The region's near-constant warfare between England and Scotland made farming precarious, and raiding — "reiving," from an old word meaning robbery — became a way of life for the riding surnames, or clans, of the Marches, such as the Armstrongs, Elliots, Nixons, Kerrs, and Grahams.

Reivers raided on horseback, typically on moonlit autumn and winter nights ("reiver's moon"), driving off cattle and sheep, taking household goods and prisoners for ransom, and extorting protection money known as "blackmail" — a word that originates with the borderers. March law, jointly administered by wardens of the East, Middle, and West Marches on both sides, attempted to regulate redress for raids through mechanisms such as the "hot trod," a legalised cross-border pursuit.

Way of life

The reivers were superb light horsemen, riding sturdy ponies called hobblers or hobbies (the origin of "hobby horse") and wearing padded jacks, steel bonnets, and later breastplates. Their weapons were the lance, sword, and the "lang spear," supplemented in the 16th century by pistols and the distinctive border lance. Loyalty ran first to family and surname, not to crown or nation; reivers served in both English and Scottish armies as mercenaries, yet were notorious for deciding battles by switching sides or standing aside — the Armstrongs and their allies were accused of indifference at the Battle of Solway Moss (1542).

Feuds between surnames could last for generations and ranged across the whole marches; the feud between the Johnstones and the Maxwells culminated in the Battle of Dryfe Sands (1593), the last great clan battle in the borders.

Architecture

The insecurity of the region produced distinctive defensive buildings: peel towers, square stone tower houses with walls up to two metres thick; bastle houses, two-storey farmhouses with byres below and living quarters above, reached by an external stair; and fortified strongholds such as Hermitage Castle, associated with the powerful border wardens. Carlisle and Berwick-upon-Tweed were the great English strongholds facing the Scottish marches.

March law and the wardens

Border law ("the Leges Marchiarum") was a body of customary law administered by the wardens of the marches, who held courts and "days of truce" at fixed meeting places to hear "bills" of complaint. Remedies included the hot trod, in which victims could legally pursue raiders across the border for up to six days with hound and horn. The system worked only when wardens were honest and strong; many wardens were themselves heads of reiving families.

Suppression

The Union of the Crowns in 1603 united England and Scotland under James VI and I, who set out to pacify the "Middle Shires," as he renamed the region. A joint commission enforced order ruthlessly: notorious reivers such as the Grahams of the Debatable Land were exiled to Ireland and the Low Countries, executions were carried out without lengthy process ("Jeddart justice" — hang first, try later), and disarmament and the demolition of strongholds followed. By the 1620s the reiving era was effectively over.

Legacy

The reivers left a lasting mark on the language and culture of the borders: words such as "blackmail" and "bereaved" descend from their activities, and the border ballads — collected by Walter Scott in his Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border (1802–03) — celebrate figures like Kinmont Willie, whose rescue from Carlisle Castle in 1596 became legendary. Annual common ridings in the Scottish border towns still commemorate the riding traditions of the period, and many reiver descendants later emigrated to Ulster and then to North America, some families claiming descent in the Scots-Irish population of Appalachia.
