Coastal sage scrub

Coastal sage scrub in Southern California.

Coastal sage scrub, also known as soft chaparral, is a low, soft-leaved scrub plant community of the California Floristic Province. It occurs along the coasts and foothills of Southern California and northern Baja California, Mexico, typically below 1,000 metres (3,300 ft) on dry, rocky slopes within reach of cooling marine air. It is one of the characteristic plant communities of the California chaparral and woodlands ecoregion.

Vegetation

The community is dominated by low, aromatic, drought-deciduous shrubs, typically half a metre to two metres tall. Characteristic species include California sagebrush (Artemisia californica), white sage and black sage (Salvia apiana and S. mellifera), California buckwheat (Eriogonum fasciculatum), laurel sumac, lemonade berry, prickly pear cactus, and coyote brush. Unlike the hard-leaved evergreen shrubs of chaparral, sage scrub plants often drop their leaves or fold them during the summer drought, giving the community a grey-green, soft appearance.

Fauna and conservation

Coastal sage scrub supports many specialized animals, including the coastal California gnatcatcher, listed as threatened under the U.S. Endangered Species Act, as well as the coastal cactus wren, orange-throated whiptail lizard, and San Diego horned lizard. Because the community occupies gentle coastal terrain prized for development, it has been reduced to a small fraction — often estimated at 10 to 15 percent — of its historical extent, making it one of the most endangered habitat types in the United States. Conservation planning in San Diego and Orange counties, through habitat conservation plans, aims to preserve connected reserves of the remaining scrub.
