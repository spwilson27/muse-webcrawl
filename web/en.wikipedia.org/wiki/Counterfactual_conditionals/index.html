Counterfactual conditional

Counterfactuals are often analysed using the notion of possible worlds.

Counterfactual conditionals (also subjunctive conditionals) are conditional sentences which discuss what would have been true under different circumstances, e.g. "If Peter believed in ghosts, he would be afraid to be here." While typical indicative conditionals like "If Peter is in the office, his computer is on" concern open possibilities, counterfactuals characteristically presuppose that their antecedent is false — hence the name — although this presupposition can be cancelled in some contexts.

Semantics

The most influential formal accounts are the possible-worlds analyses developed by Robert Stalnaker and David Lewis in the late 1960s and 1970s. On Lewis's account, a counterfactual "If A were the case, C would be the case" is true when, among the possible worlds most similar to the actual world in which A holds, C also holds. This similarity ordering explains why counterfactuals are not contrapositive and why strengthening the antecedent can fail: "If I had struck the match, it would have lit" does not imply "If I had struck the match and soaked it in water, it would have lit."

Logic and puzzles

Counterfactuals resist analysis as material implication, since a material conditional with a false antecedent is trivially true, whereas counterfactuals with false antecedents can be false. Philosophers including Nelson Goodman studied the problem of stating which generalisations and background facts may legitimately be "cotenable" with a counterfactual supposition. Counterfactuals play a central role in theories of causation, dispositions, laws of nature, decision theory and historical explanation, and their logic is studied through systems of conditional logic and variably strict conditional operators.

Counterfactual history

In historiography, counterfactual reasoning — asking what would have happened if some event had gone differently — is used to test causal claims about the past, though speculative "virtual history" remains controversial among historians.
