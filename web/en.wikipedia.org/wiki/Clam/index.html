Clam

Clam is a common name for several kinds of bivalve molluscs. The word is often applied only to those that are edible and live as infauna, spending most of their lives partially buried in sediment on the seafloor or in the beds of rivers and lakes. In a broader sense, "clam" can refer to most of the members of the class Bivalvia, although in casual usage it is generally restricted to burrowing species other than mussels, oysters, scallops, and their close relatives. Clams are invertebrates with a two-part hinged shell, a muscular foot for digging, and paired siphons through which they draw in and expel water while filter-feeding on plankton and organic particles.

Anatomy

A clam's shell consists of two valves joined by a ligament and closed by adductor muscles. Inside lie the mantle, gills (ctenidia) used both for respiration and for straining food from the water, the digestive system, and the foot. Most burrowing clams extend two siphons to the surface — an inhalant siphon that takes in water and food and an exhalant siphon that expels filtered water and wastes. Clams lack a head, eyes (in most species), and a radula. Their nervous system is simple, with three pairs of ganglia.

Life cycle

Most marine clams reproduce by broadcast spawning, releasing eggs and sperm into the water, where fertilisation produces free-swimming trochophore and veliger larvae. After a planktonic period the larvae settle, metamorphose, and burrow into the sediment. Some clams are extremely long-lived: an ocean quahog (Arctica islandica) nicknamed Ming was collected off Iceland in 2006 and aged at 507 years, making it the longest-lived non-colonial animal known.

Human uses

Clams are eaten around the world — steamed, in chowders and pasta dishes, or raw — and support major fisheries for species such as the hard clam or quahog (Mercenaria mercenaria), soft-shell clam, Pacific geoduck, and Manila clam. Quahog shells were traditionally used by Indigenous peoples of eastern North America to make wampum beads. Because they filter large volumes of water, clams are also valued for improving water quality, though the same filter-feeding can concentrate pollutants and algal toxins, making shellfish harvesting subject to public-health closures.

Notable types

  • Giant clam (Tridacna gigas), the largest living bivalve
  • Geoduck, a large burrowing clam of the Pacific Northwest
  • Razor clam, an elongated burrowing species
  • Freshwater pearl mussels and other unionids, though commonly called mussels
