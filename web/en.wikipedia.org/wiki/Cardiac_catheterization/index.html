Cardiac catheterization

Cardiac catheterization (heart cath) is the insertion of a catheter into a chamber or vessel of the heart, performed for both diagnostic and interventional purposes. It is one of the most common hospital procedures in the developed world. Its best-known application is coronary angiography — injecting contrast dye into the coronary arteries under X-ray to localise narrowings and blockages — and percutaneous coronary intervention (angioplasty and stenting) to treat them.

History

In 1929 the German surgeon Werner Forssmann, then a trainee, inserted a catheter into a vein in his own arm, advanced it into his right atrium, and confirmed its position by X-ray — an experiment for which he was disciplined at the time. In the 1940s André Cournand and Dickinson Richards developed the technique into a clinical tool at Bellevue Hospital; the three men shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Selective coronary angiography followed from Mason Sones's accidental but revealing injection in 1958, and Charles Dotter and Andreas Gruentzig extended the method to balloon angioplasty in the 1970s.

Procedure

Access is usually gained through the radial artery at the wrist (now preferred for lower bleeding risk) or the femoral vessels in the groin. Under fluoroscopic guidance, catheters are advanced to the heart. Left heart catheterization measures pressures in the left ventricle and aorta and permits coronary angiography and ventriculography; right heart catheterization — often with a balloon-tipped Swan–Ganz catheter — measures right-sided and pulmonary artery pressures, cardiac output and blood oxygen saturations, and is used to assess pulmonary hypertension, heart failure and congenital shunts.

Indications

  • Evaluation of chest pain and suspected or confirmed myocardial infarction (primary PCI for STEMI).
  • Assessment of valve disease, cardiomyopathy and congenital heart disease.
  • Measurement of pulmonary artery pressures and cardiac output.
  • Interventions: stenting, balloon valvuloplasty, transcatheter valve replacement (TAVR), septal defect closure, pericardiocentesis guidance.

Risks

Serious complications are uncommon (major events in roughly 1% or fewer of diagnostic cases) but include bleeding or vascular injury at the access site, arrhythmia, contrast-associated kidney injury and allergic reactions, stroke, heart attack, arterial dissection and, rarely, death. Radiation exposure is a further consideration for patients and staff.
