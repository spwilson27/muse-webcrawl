Reparations

Reparations are compensation measures owed by a party that has caused harm to another. The term is used in several contexts: war reparations paid by a defeated state after conflict; reparations to victims of human-rights abuses under transitional justice; and reparations claimed for historical injustices such as slavery and colonialism.

War reparations

After the First World War, the Treaty of Versailles required Germany to pay reparations, fixed in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks. The scale of the burden, and attempts to collect it, became a major grievance in German politics and a contributing factor to the instability of the Weimar Republic; the Dawes and Young Plans restructured the debt, and payments effectively ended in the early 1930s, with the final related debt settled in 2010.

After the Second World War, reparations took the form of dismantled industrial plant, labour, and territory, as well as negotiated agreements. West Germany's 1952 Luxembourg Agreement committed it to pay Israel and Jewish organisations in compensation for the Holocaust. After Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in 1990, the UN Compensation Commission processed claims and paid out over 52 billion US dollars to victims, completing its work in 2022.

Reparations for historical injustice

Governments have also made reparations to victimised groups within their own societies: the United States paid compensation to Japanese Americans interned during the Second World War under the Civil Liberties Act of 1988. Claims for reparations for slavery and colonialism — for example by Caribbean states and African nations — remain politically contested, involving arguments about moral responsibility, causation, and the passage of time.

Legal framework

In international law, the Permanent Court of International Justice held in the Chorzów Factory case (1928) that a breach of an engagement entails an obligation to make reparation that, as far as possible, wipes out the consequences of the illegal act. Reparation may take the forms of restitution, compensation, and satisfaction, principles reflected in the International Law Commission's Articles on State Responsibility (2001).
