History of the Jews in Scotland

The history of the Jews in Scotland dates to at least the seventeenth century, though claims of medieval Jewish presence remain unverified — Scotland is notable as the one European country with no record of state-sponsored Jewish expulsions or blood libel persecutions in the medieval period. Organised communities formed in Edinburgh (1816) and Glasgow (1823), growing with nineteenth-century immigration from Germany and Eastern Europe. Scottish Jewry peaked at perhaps 15,000–20,000 in the mid-twentieth century, concentrated in Glasgow, and has since declined to around 6,000 (2022 census), while producing figures such as the stained-glass artist and the world's first Jewish professor of medicine in Britain in Edinburgh.

Garnethill Synagogue (1879), the oldest synagogue in Scotland.

Early presence

Individual Jews appear in records from the late 1600s; David Brown, a Jewish merchant, applied to reside and trade in Edinburgh in 1691. The first recorded graduate was Herman Lyon, a dentist and chiropodist who graduated from the University of Edinburgh in 1795 — Edinburgh's medical school, uniquely open in its admissions, attracted many Jewish students barred from English universities. The Edinburgh Hebrew Congregation was founded in 1816 and Glasgow's in 1823.

Nineteenth-century growth

Migration from the German states and, after 1881, from the Russian Empire (many arriving via the port of Leith, some en route to America) built communities in Glasgow's Gorbals district, where Yiddish newspapers, theatres and friendly societies flourished. Glasgow's Garnethill Synagogue opened in 1879. Jews entered Scottish commerce — tobacco, jewellery, tailoring — and civic life; Scotland's first Jewish Lord Provost, Michael Simons, led Glasgow in 1987, following earlier pioneers in local government.

Twentieth century and decline

Refugees from Nazism arrived in the 1930s, including children on the Kindertransport; a farming hostel at Whittingehame House in East Lothian sheltered refugee boys. The community peaked around the 1950s and then shrank through emigration, aliyah, and movement to England. The Scottish Council of Jewish Communities (SCoJeC), founded in 1999, represents the community, which today centres on Glasgow's suburbs (Giffnock, Newton Mearns) with smaller congregations in Edinburgh, Aberdeen and Dundee.

Culture

Scotland developed a distinctive Jewish identity — a popular humorous claim holds that the country even gave rise to a "tartan" Judaism, symbolised by the Jewish tartan officially registered in 2008. Notable Scottish Jews include poet A. C. Jacobs, artist Hannah Frank, and physicist and broadcaster in earlier generations; the community archive is held by the Scottish Jewish Archives Centre in Glasgow.
