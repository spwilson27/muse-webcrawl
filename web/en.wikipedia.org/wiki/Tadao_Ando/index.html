Tadao Ando

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Church of the Light in Osaka: a concrete box split by a cross of pure light behind the altar]

Tadao Ando (born 13 September 1941) is a Japanese architect, the leading exponent of a minimalism in concrete, light and geometry that has made him — self-taught, a former professional boxer — one of the most revered living architects. He received the Pritzker Architecture Prize in 1995, donating the prize money to orphans of the Kobe earthquake of that year.

Born in Osaka to a working-class family, raised largely by his grandmother, Ando boxed professionally as a teenager, then educated himself: he learned carpentry and drafting, travelled in his twenties to study Le Corbusier, Kahn, Mies and Wright at first hand, and founded his practice in Osaka in 1969. His Row House in Sumiyoshi (Azuma House, 1976) — a concrete box that forces its residents to cross an open courtyard in all weather — announced his ascetic creed.

Concrete and light

Ando's signature — board-marked, silky 'Ando concrete', walls that slice light into crosses and wedges, water and wind admitted as building materials — runs from the Church of the Light (1989) and the Water Temple (1991) to the museums that made him global: the Modern Art Museum of Fort Worth (2002), the Chichu Art Museum on Naoshima (2004, buried in the island), the Punta della Dogana in Venice (2009) and the Bourse de Commerce in Paris (2021, a concrete cylinder inside the old exchange for François Pinault).

Standing

Ando's asceticism is Japan's great architectural export of the late twentieth century: Zen emptiness rendered in industrial material, a rebuke to both postmodern noise and commercial glass. Now in his eighties he continues to build worldwide, and his Naoshima works with Benesse have made an art island that is itself a pilgrimage.
