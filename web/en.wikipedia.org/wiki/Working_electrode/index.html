Working electrode

The working electrode is the electrode in an electrochemical cell at which the reaction of interest takes place. In a standard three-electrode cell it is used together with a reference electrode, whose stable potential the working electrode's potential is controlled against, and a counter (auxiliary) electrode, which completes the circuit and carries the current.

Materials and forms

Common working electrode materials include platinum, gold, glassy carbon and carbon paste, chosen for a wide usable potential window and chemical inertness. Mercury electrodes—such as the dropping mercury electrode and hanging mercury drop electrode—were historically important in polarography because of their reproducible, renewable surface and high overpotential for hydrogen evolution, though toxicity has reduced their use. Microelectrodes (ultramicroelectrodes) with dimensions of micrometres exploit radial diffusion for fast and steady-state measurements.

Techniques

The working electrode is the site of measurement in voltammetric techniques such as cyclic voltammetry, where its potential is swept and the resulting current reveals redox behaviour of analytes. Rotating disk and ring-disk electrodes impose controlled convection to study reaction kinetics.
