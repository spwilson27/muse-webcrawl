The Juche calendar, named after the Juche ideology, is the system of year numbering formerly used in the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (North Korea). The calendar takes the birth year of the state's founder, Kim Il Sung (1912), as its year one. It was formally adopted in 1997 and used, usually in conjunction with the Gregorian year, on official documents, newspapers, and public inscriptions for 27 years until North Korea quietly abandoned it in October 2024.

History

The calendar was introduced three years after Kim Il Sung's death, as part of the consolidation of the personality cult around the Kim family. Under rules adopted in 1997, the year of Kim Il Sung's birth — 15 April 1912, celebrated as the Day of the Sun — became Juche 1; years before 1912 were counted only in the Gregorian system. North Korean years thus began on 1 January as in the Gregorian calendar, but the numbering lagged by 1911: Gregorian 2024, for instance, was Juche 113. Dates were commonly printed in parallel, as "Juche 113 (2024)."

Usage

The Juche year appeared on state media mastheads such as the Rodong Sinmun, on coins and stamps, in official correspondence, and in the dating of monuments and documents. Everyday life generally used Gregorian month and day conventions; the era system affected only the year number. South Korea, by contrast, uses the Gregorian calendar (the historical Dangi system, counting from 2333 BC, fell out of official use in the 1960s).

Discontinuation

In October 2024, South Korean observers noted that the Rodong Sinmun and other state outlets had stopped printing the Juche year and begun using only the Gregorian year, and the change was subsequently visible across North Korean media and publications. Analysts interpreted the move as part of Kim Jong Un's effort to build his own authority and diminish reliance on his predecessors' cult symbolism.

Conversion

To convert a Juche year to a Gregorian year, add 1911: Juche 1 = 1912, Juche 100 = 2011, Juche 113 = 2024.
