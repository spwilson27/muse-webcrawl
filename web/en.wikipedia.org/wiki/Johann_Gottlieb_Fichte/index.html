Johann Gottlieb Fichte

Johann Gottlieb Fichte (19 May 1762 – 29 January 1814) was a German philosopher who became a founding figure of the philosophical movement known as German idealism, which developed from the theoretical and ethical writings of Immanuel Kant.

Life

Fichte was born in Rammenau, Saxony, the son of a ribbon weaver. After studying theology at Jena and Leipzig, he became a private tutor. His Attempt at a Critique of All Revelation (1792), published anonymously, was initially mistaken for a work of Kant and brought him instant fame. He succeeded Karl Leonhard Reinhold as professor at Jena in 1794.

Philosophy

Fichte's Wissenschaftslehre ("Doctrine of Science") sought to derive all knowledge from a single first principle: the self-positing activity of the I (the ego). The I posits itself, and in opposition to itself posits the not-I, the world of objects. This dialectical structure influenced Schelling and Hegel.

Later life

After the atheism controversy forced him from Jena in 1799, Fichte moved to Berlin, where he became the first rector of the newly founded University of Berlin in 1810. His Addresses to the German Nation (1808) became a foundational text of German nationalism. He died of typhus in 1814.
