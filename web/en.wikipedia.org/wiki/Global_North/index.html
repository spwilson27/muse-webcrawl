Global North

The Global North is a term used in the study of globalisation and international relations for the grouping of developed countries. In general, definitions of the Global North include the United States, Canada, Western Europe, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea, Israel and the developed countries of East Asia. These countries are characterised by high levels of economic development, industrialisation, technological infrastructure and political stability, and they dominate international trade and political institutions.

The Brandt Line

The distinction is often visualised through the Brandt Line, proposed by former German Chancellor Willy Brandt in the 1980 report of the Independent Commission on International Development Issues. The line encircled the world at approximately 30 degrees north latitude, separating the wealthy "North" from the poorer "South", although Australia and New Zealand, geographically in the south, were placed in the northern grouping. The terms Global North and Global South largely replaced the older Cold War terminology of the First World and Third World after the end of the Cold War.

Criticism

Scholars have criticised the North–South divide as an oversimplification. Economic growth in countries such as China, India and Brazil, and the emergence of wealthy states in the Gulf, blur the boundary, while poverty and inequality exist within wealthy northern states. The terms nonetheless remain widely used by the United Nations, development agencies and academics to describe broad patterns of global inequality in income, health, education and political influence.
