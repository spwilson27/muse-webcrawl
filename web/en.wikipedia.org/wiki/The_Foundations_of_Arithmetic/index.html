The Foundations of Arithmetic (German: Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik) is a book by the German philosopher and logician Gottlob Frege, published in 1884. It investigates the philosophical foundations of arithmetic and is a landmark in the development of analytic philosophy and the philosophy of mathematics. The English translation by J. L. Austin appeared in 1950 and helped establish Frege's reputation in the English-speaking world.

Contents

Frege begins by setting out three guiding principles: always separate the psychological from the logical; never ask for the meaning of a word in isolation, but only in the context of a proposition (the "context principle"); and never lose sight of the distinction between concept and object. He then criticises earlier accounts of number, including the views of Mill, Kant and various psychologistic and formalist theories, arguing that number is neither a property of external things nor a subjective idea.

The positive thesis of the book is logicism: arithmetical truths are analytic and can be derived from purely logical laws and definitions. Frege sketches a definition of number in terms of extensions of concepts — the number belonging to a concept F is the extension of the concept "equinumerous with F" — and argues, in the famous discussion of Julius Caesar, that identity statements involving numbers must be handled through explicit criteria of identity. He concludes with a sketch of how the natural numbers, including zero and one, and the notion of succession might be defined logically.

Influence

The Grundlagen laid the groundwork for Frege's later Grundgesetze der Arithmetik (1893/1903), in which the logicist programme was developed formally — a programme famously shaken by Russell's paradox. The book profoundly influenced Russell, Wittgenstein and the subsequent philosophy of language and mathematics.
