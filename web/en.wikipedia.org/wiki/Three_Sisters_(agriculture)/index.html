Three Sisters (agriculture)

The Three Sisters (Spanish: las tres hermanas) are the three main agricultural crops of various Indigenous peoples of North and Central America: squash, maize (corn) and climbing beans (typically tepary beans or common beans). In the technique known as companion planting or intercropping, the crops are planted together in the same mound, each contributing to the others' growth — a method developed over millennia in Mesoamerica and spread throughout the Americas.

How it works

  • Maize is planted first; its tall stalks provide a living trellis on which the beans climb.
  • Beans, planted a few weeks later, fix atmospheric nitrogen in their root nodules, enriching the soil for the nutrient-hungry maize and squash.
  • Squash, planted last, spreads along the ground; its large leaves shade the soil, conserving moisture and suppressing weeds, while its prickly stems deter pests and animals.

History and significance

Squash was domesticated in Mesoamerica around 8,000–10,000 years ago, maize from teosinte by about 7,000 years ago, and beans followed; the three were combined into a single system by roughly a thousand years ago in many regions. Among the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois), the crops held spiritual significance as the "Three Sisters", sustaining the people physically and culturally. Nutritionally the combination is complementary: maize lacks certain amino acids (lysine and tryptophan) that beans supply, while beans lack others provided by maize, and squash adds vitamins and edible seeds.

Modern use

The Three Sisters remains a textbook example of companion planting and agroecology, and is widely used in permaculture and home gardening as a low-input polyculture.
