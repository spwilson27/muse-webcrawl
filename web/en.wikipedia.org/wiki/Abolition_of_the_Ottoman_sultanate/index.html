The abolition of the Ottoman sultanate by the Grand National Assembly of Turkey on 1 November 1922 ended the Ottoman Empire, which had lasted for more than six centuries, and terminated the reign of the last sultan, Mehmed VI.

Background

After the Ottoman defeat in the First World War, the empire was partitioned under the Treaty of Sèvres (1920), and Istanbul was occupied by the Allies. The sultan's government in Istanbul coexisted uneasily with the nationalist government established by Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk) in Ankara in 1920, which claimed to represent the sovereignty of the Turkish nation. The Turkish victory in the War of Independence, sealed by the Armistice of Mudanya in October 1922, destroyed the sultanate's remaining authority.

Abolition

When the Allies invited both the Istanbul and Ankara governments to the forthcoming Lausanne peace conference, the Grand National Assembly responded by passing a law on 1 November 1922 declaring that the government based in Istanbul had ceased to exist as of 16 March 1920 (the date of the Allied occupation of the city) and separating the religious office of caliph from the political office of sultan, which was abolished. Mehmed VI, realising his position was untenable, left Istanbul aboard the British battleship HMS Malaya on 17 November 1922. His cousin Abdülmecid II was elected caliph — without temporal power — by the Assembly.

Aftermath

The Treaty of Lausanne (1923) recognised the Ankara government internationally; the Republic of Turkey was proclaimed on 29 October 1923 with Mustafa Kemal as president. The caliphate itself was abolished on 3 March 1924, and the Ottoman dynasty was exiled.
