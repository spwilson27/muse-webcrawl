Light infantry

Light infantry refers to infantry soldiers equipped and organised for mobility, speed and flexibility rather than for fighting in the dense formations of line infantry. Historically they fought as skirmishers — in open order ahead of the main line, harassing the enemy with aimed fire — and were used for reconnaissance, screening, raids and fighting in broken terrain.

History

Ancient armies employed light troops such as Greek peltasts and Roman velites. The modern role emerged in the 18th century: Austrian Grenzer and Pandurs, Prussian and French chasseurs, and British light companies demonstrated the value of aimed skirmish fire, especially in the wooded terrain of North America. During the Napoleonic Wars every major army fielded light infantry; Britain's 95th Rifles, armed with the Baker rifle and dressed in green, became famous. Frederick the Great and later reformers integrated light companies into line battalions.

In the 19th and 20th centuries, as all infantry adopted open-order tactics, the distinction narrowed, but elite light regiments persisted, and the two World Wars saw dedicated light formations for mountain, jungle and airborne operations.

Modern usage

Today "light infantry" denotes forces that operate without heavy vehicles or armour — deployable by air and able to fight on foot in difficult terrain. Examples include airborne and air assault units, mountain troops, and formations such as the U.S. Army's 10th Mountain Division and light infantry brigades in many other armies. Their trade-off is strategic and tactical mobility for reduced protection and organic firepower.
