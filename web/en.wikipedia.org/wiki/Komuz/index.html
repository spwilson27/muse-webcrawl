Komuz

The komuz, national instrument of Kyrgyzstan

The komuz (Kyrgyz: комуз) is a traditional Kyrgyz three-stringed, fretless long-necked lute and the national instrument of Kyrgyzstan. It is carved — traditionally from a single piece of apricot or juniper wood — has an elongated pear-shaped body, and is plucked and strummed with the right hand while the left hand stops the strings along the neck. The komuz is one of the most recognisable symbols of Kyrgyz culture: it appears on the 1-som banknote of Kyrgyzstan.

Construction and playing

The classic komuz is hollowed from one block of wood, closed with a thin soundboard, and fitted with three strings historically of sheep gut, today usually of nylon or fishing line. The middle string is the highest in pitch. Because the neck has no frets, the player slides along the string. Playing styles range from simple strumming to virtuosic techniques in which the instrument is flipped, balanced, and played behind the head or back — displays that accompany story-telling and contests between musicians. Tunings vary with the melody and region.

Role in Kyrgyz culture

The komuz accompanies the recitation of the Epic of Manas and the performances of the akyn, improvising poet-musicians who sing of heroes, history, and current events. Kyrgyz legend attributes the instrument's invention to the hunter Kambar, who is said to have fashioned the first komuz after hearing the wind hum through strings of dried entrails stretched between branches. Distinct named melodies (küü) encode stories: pieces imitate galloping horses, cuckoos, laments, and historical battles, and a master komuzchu preserves dozens of them. Since independence in 1991 the komuz has been vigorously promoted, with massed komuz ensembles — sometimes thousands of players — performing at national celebrations.
