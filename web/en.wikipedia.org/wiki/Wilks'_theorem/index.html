Wilks's theorem, published by Samuel S. Wilks in 1938, is a fundamental result in mathematical statistics concerning the likelihood-ratio test. It states that, under the null hypothesis and standard regularity conditions, twice the negative logarithm of the likelihood ratio converges in distribution to a chi-squared distribution as the sample size grows.

−2 ln Λ ⟶ χ²ν in distribution,

where Λ is the likelihood ratio comparing the maximised likelihood under the null hypothesis with the maximised likelihood under the full (alternative) model, and ν is the difference in the number of free parameters between the two models (the degrees of freedom).

Statement and conditions

Consider a parametric model with parameter θ, and a null hypothesis that restricts θ to a lower-dimensional subspace Θ₀ ⊂ Θ. The theorem requires, among other regularity conditions, that the true parameter lie in the interior of the parameter space, that the model be identifiable, and that the log-likelihood be sufficiently smooth. Wilks's original 1938 paper, "The large-sample distribution of the likelihood ratio for testing composite hypotheses", appeared in The Annals of Mathematical Statistics.

Applications

The result underpins the construction of likelihood-ratio tests and profile-likelihood confidence intervals throughout applied statistics, and is heavily used in fields such as particle physics, where the test statistic for discovery significance relies on its asymptotic chi-squared behaviour. Extensions address cases where parameters lie on boundaries of the parameter space, where the limiting distribution becomes a mixture of chi-squared distributions (as in Chernoff's theorem).
