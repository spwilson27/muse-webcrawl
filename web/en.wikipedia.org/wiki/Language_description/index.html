Language description

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Overview
Field:Descriptive linguistics
Main products:Reference grammars, dictionaries, text collections

Language description is the work of objectively documenting and analysing how a language is actually used by its speakers. It is the central activity of descriptive linguistics, and contrasts with linguistic prescription, which seeks to impose rules of correct usage. A full description typically covers the sound system (phonetics and phonology), word structure (morphology), sentence structure (syntax), meaning (semantics) and patterns of use (pragmatics and discourse), usually presented in a reference grammar accompanied by a dictionary and a collection of glossed texts.

Methods

Descriptive work is based on data: recordings of natural speech and elicited material gathered with native-speaker consultants, transcribed — often in the International Phonetic Alphabet — and interlinearised so that each word and morpheme can be examined. Fieldworkers test hypotheses against speakers' judgements and corpus evidence. The Leipzig Glossing Rules provide a widely used standard for interlinear morpheme-by-morpheme glosses, and digital archives increasingly preserve the recordings underlying a description.

Significance

Most of the world's roughly 7,000 languages have never been adequately described, and many are endangered; language description and documentation are therefore urgent priorities, supplying the materials on which language maintenance and revitalisation depend. Descriptive grammars also underpin linguistic typology and theory: generalisations about possible human languages rest on the accumulated descriptions of individual languages, from classical grammars of Sanskrit, Greek and Latin to modern fieldwork-based reference grammars such as those of the Mouton Grammar Library series.
