Periodic paralysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Periodic paralysis is a group of rare genetic diseases that lead to weakness or paralysis from common triggers such as cold or a high-carbohydrate meal. These are channelopathies — disorders of ion channels in skeletal muscle — and are usually inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. Attacks last from minutes to hours or days, and between attacks muscle function is typically normal, although a fixed myopathy may develop later in life.

Types

Hypokalemic periodic paralysis is the most common form; attacks are associated with low serum potassium, which shifts into muscle cells, and are triggered by strenuous exercise followed by rest, stress, or carbohydrate-rich meals. Most cases are caused by mutations in the calcium channel gene CACNA1S, with a minority in the sodium channel gene SCN4A.

Hyperkalemic periodic paralysis, caused by SCN4A mutations, produces attacks associated with high or rising potassium, often triggered by rest after exercise, cold, or fasting, and usually shorter and accompanied by myotonia.

Thyrotoxic periodic paralysis is an acquired, hypokalemia-associated form occurring in hyperthyroid patients, disproportionately men of Asian or Latin American ancestry. Andersen–Tawil syndrome combines periodic paralysis with ventricular arrhythmias and characteristic physical features, and is caused by mutations in the potassium channel gene KCNJ2.

Treatment

Management includes avoiding triggers, potassium supplementation or restriction as appropriate, and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors such as acetazolamide or dichlorphenamide. Diagnosis combines clinical history, serum potassium measured during an attack, provocative exercise testing, and genetic testing.
