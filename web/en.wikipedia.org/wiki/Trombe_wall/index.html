Trombe wall

Trombe wall
TypePassive solar building design
Named forFélix Trombe, French engineer
ComponentsThick masonry wall, exterior glazing, air gap, vents
PurposeHeating (and, with venting, cooling) interior spaces using stored solar heat

A Trombe wall is a passive solar heating system consisting of a thick, dark-coloured masonry wall placed just inside a layer of glazing on the sun-facing side of a building. Sunlight passing through the glass is absorbed by the wall, which stores the heat and slowly releases it into the interior, typically with a time lag of several hours so that warmth arrives in the evening.

Operation

Air in the gap between glazing and wall is heated, expands and rises; vents at the top and bottom of the wall can be opened to circulate this warm air into the room by convection during the day, or closed so the wall heats the space only by radiation and conduction at night. In summer, external vents can exhaust the hot air, drawing cooler air through the building.

History

The concept was explored by Edward Morse in the 19th century and popularised by French engineer Félix Trombe and architect Jacques Michel, whose solar houses in the Pyrenees in the 1960s gave the design its name. It became a staple of the passive solar movement of the 1970s.
