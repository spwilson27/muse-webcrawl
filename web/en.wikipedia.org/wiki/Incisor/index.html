Incisor

Incisors (from Latin incidere, "to cut") are the front teeth present in most mammals. They are located in the premaxilla above and the mandible below, and their flat, chisel-shaped edges are adapted for cutting and biting off pieces of food. Humans have eight incisors in total — two central and two lateral incisors in each jaw.

The eight human incisors occupy the front of the upper and lower jaws.

Human incisors

The upper central incisors are the widest front teeth and among the most prominent in the smile; the upper laterals are smaller, while the lower incisors are the smallest teeth in the mouth. Primary (baby) incisors erupt at about six to twelve months of age, and the permanent incisors replace them at about six to eight years. Each incisor typically has a single root. Incisors help with biting food, speech articulation, and supporting the lips.

In other animals

Incisors are highly specialised in many groups. Rodents have a single pair of large, continuously growing incisors in each jaw, kept sharp by gnawing and worn into a chisel edge because enamel covers only the front surface. Lagomorphs (rabbits and hares) have a second small pair, the "peg teeth", behind the upper incisors. Elephants' tusks are greatly enlarged upper incisors composed of ivory (dentine) that grow throughout life. Ruminants lack upper incisors entirely, pressing their lower incisors against a hard dental pad. In horses, the age of an animal can be estimated from the wear patterns of its incisors.
