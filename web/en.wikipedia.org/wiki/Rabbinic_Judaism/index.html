Rabbinic Judaism

Image placeholder: a traditional page of the Babylonian Talmud, the central text of Rabbinic Judaism.

Rabbinic Judaism (Hebrew: יהדות רבנית, Yahadut Rabanit), also called Rabbinism, has been the mainstream form of Judaism since the 6th century CE, after the codification of the Babylonian Talmud. It grew out of the Pharisaic school of Second Temple Judaism and is based on the belief that at Mount Sinai, Moses received both the Written Torah (the Pentateuch) and the Oral Torah, traditions transmitted orally and eventually codified as the Mishnah and Talmud.

Origins

Following the destruction of the Second Temple by the Romans in 70 CE, Judaism could no longer centre on temple sacrifice and priesthood. Under figures such as Yochanan ben Zakai at Yavneh, the rabbinic sages reorganised Jewish life around Torah study, prayer and the commandments. Around 200 CE Judah ha-Nasi compiled the Mishnah, the first major written redaction of the Oral Torah; commentaries and discussions over the following centuries produced the Jerusalem and Babylonian Talmuds.

Beliefs and practice

Rabbinic Judaism teaches that God gave Moses both the written and oral law, and that the oral law provides the authoritative interpretation of scripture, applied through halakha (Jewish law). Its institutions — the synagogue, the rabbi, the study house — replaced the temple cult. Nearly all modern Jewish denominations, including Orthodox, Conservative and Reform Judaism, descend from the rabbinic tradition; Karaite Judaism, which rejects the Oral Torah, is its principal historical alternative.

Influence

The rabbinic corpus — Mishnah, Talmud, Midrash and later codes such as Maimonides' Mishneh Torah and the Shulchan Aruch — set the framework for Jewish law, ethics and communal life throughout the diaspora to the present day.
