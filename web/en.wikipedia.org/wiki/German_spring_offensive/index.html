German spring offensive

Date: 21 March – 18 July 1918
Location: Northern France and Flanders
Also known as: Kaiserschlacht ("Kaiser's Battle"); Ludendorff offensives
Result: German tactical gains; strategic failure

The German spring offensive, also known as the Kaiserschlacht ("Kaiser's Battle") or the Ludendorff offensives, was a series of German attacks along the Western Front in 1918 during World War I. Launched on 21 March 1918 with Operation Michael, it was Germany's last major attempt to win the war before American forces arrived in strength.

Background

Russia's withdrawal from the war after the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk freed some 50 German divisions for the West. General Erich Ludendorff planned a sequence of offensives using infiltration tactics by specially trained stormtroopers (Sturmtruppen), short, intense hurricane bombardments directed by Colonel Georg Bruchmüller, and bypassing strongpoints to disrupt Allied rear areas.

The offensives

Operation Michael (21 March – 5 April) struck the British Fifth Army on the Somme and advanced up to 65 kilometres, the largest territorial gain on the Western Front since 1914. Subsequent operations — Georgette in Flanders (April), Blücher-Yorck on the Aisne (May), Gneisenau (June), and the final Marne attack (July) — made further salients but at heavy cost. Germany suffered roughly 680,000–800,000 casualties, disproportionately among its irreplaceable elite assault troops, while outrunning its supply lines and artillery.

Aftermath

The offensives failed to achieve a decisive breakthrough. Exhausted German forces, weakened by influenza, hunger from the Allied blockade, and declining morale, faced the Allied Hundred Days Offensive from August 1918, which drove Germany to seek the armistice of 11 November 1918.
