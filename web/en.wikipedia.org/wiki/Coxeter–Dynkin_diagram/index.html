Coxeter–Dynkin diagram

In geometry, a Coxeter–Dynkin diagram (or Coxeter diagram, Coxeter graph) is a graph with numerically labelled edges (called branches) representing the angles between pairs of fundamental mirrors in a kaleidoscopic construction, and thus encodes a Coxeter group. The diagrams are named for H. S. M. Coxeter, who used them systematically to classify reflection groups and regular polytopes, and for Eugene Dynkin, who independently introduced the closely related Dynkin diagrams used in the theory of Lie algebras.

Description

Each node of a Coxeter–Dynkin diagram represents a fundamental mirror (a hyperplane of reflection), and each branch joining two nodes represents the dihedral angle between the corresponding mirrors. A branch labelled p indicates an angle of π − π/p equivalently that the product of the two reflections has order p. By convention, branches labelled 3 are drawn unlabelled, and nodes joined by no branch correspond to perpendicular mirrors whose reflections commute. Rings or marks placed on nodes (as in Coxeter diagrams for uniform polytopes) can further specify how a polytope is generated from the reflection group by the Wythoff construction.

Classification

The connected Coxeter–Dynkin diagrams whose associated bilinear form is positive definite correspond exactly to the finite Coxeter groups. These fall into the familiar families An, Bn, Dn, and the exceptional cases E6, E7, E8, F4, G2, together with the non-crystallographic groups H3, H4, and the dihedral groups I2(p). Positive semi-definite diagrams classify the affine Coxeter groups, which describe the symmetries of periodic tilings.

Applications

Coxeter–Dynkin diagrams organise a large part of the classification theory of symmetric structures: they enumerate the finite reflection groups, the regular and uniform polytopes in each dimension, the root systems and simple Lie algebras (where the Dynkin variant is standard), and the symmetry groups of honeycombs. The diagrams also occur in singularity theory, in the ADE classification of simply laced structures, and in string theory and gauge theory.
