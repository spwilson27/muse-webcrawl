Astigmatism (optical systems)

An optical system with astigmatism is one where rays that propagate in two perpendicular planes have different foci. If an optical system with astigmatism is used to form an image of a cross, the vertical and horizontal lines will be in sharp focus at two different distances. The term comes from the Greek α- (a-, "without") and στίγμα (stigma, "a mark, spot, puncture").

Forms of astigmatism

Third-order astigmatism

In the analysis of third-order aberrations, astigmatism occurs for off-axis points: a point source away from the optical axis is imaged not as a point but as two short line segments, the tangential focus and the sagittal focus, lying at different distances along the axis. The blur between them takes the form of an ellipse, and at the position of the "circle of least confusion" the image is a small round disc. Astigmatism is one of the five Seidel aberrations described in 1857 by Philipp Ludwig von Seidel.

Astigmatism in systems with rotational symmetry

Even a perfectly rotationally symmetric optical system exhibits astigmatism for objects off the optical axis, because the light strikes the lens surfaces obliquely. It increases roughly with the square of the angle of the object point off-axis, and is corrected in well-designed photographic lenses by combining elements of different powers and glasses.

Astigmatism due to imperfect surfaces

Astigmatism can also be caused by optical elements that are not rotationally symmetric, such as a lens surface that is more strongly curved in one meridian than another (a toric surface). In the human eye, this kind of astigmatism is a common refractive error, corrected by cylindrical lenses in spectacles or by toric contact lenses.

History

The phenomenon was noticed by Isaac Newton in his optical experiments, and was investigated by Thomas Young and by George Biddell Airy, who in 1825 described correcting his own astigmatic eye with a cylindrical lens — the first such correction.
