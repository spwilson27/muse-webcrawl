Blood–brain barrier

The neurovascular unit forming the blood–brain barrier

The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is a highly selective semipermeable border of endothelial cells that regulates the transfer of solutes and chemicals between the circulatory system and the central nervous system, thus protecting the brain from harmful or unwanted substances in the blood. The blood–brain barrier is formed by endothelial cells of the capillary wall, astrocyte end-feet ensheathing the capillary, and pericytes embedded in the capillary basement membrane.

Function

The barrier allows the passage of some molecules by passive diffusion, as well as the selective and active transport of various nutrients, ions, organic anions, and macromolecules such as glucose and amino acids that are crucial to neural function. Tight junctions between endothelial cells restrict the passage of large or hydrophilic molecules, while efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein pump many foreign substances back into the blood.

History

The concept emerged from experiments by Paul Ehrlich in the 1880s, who found that dyes injected into the bloodstream stained every organ except the brain. His student Edwin Goldmann showed that dye injected into the cerebrospinal fluid stained the brain but nothing else, demonstrating a barrier between blood and brain. The structure was visualised with electron microscopy in the 1960s.

Clinical significance

The barrier protects the brain from toxins and pathogens, but it also frustrates drug delivery: most large-molecule pharmaceuticals and the great majority of small-molecule drugs cannot cross it. This is a major obstacle in treating brain tumours, infections, and neurodegenerative diseases. Conversely, breakdown of the barrier occurs in stroke, meningitis, multiple sclerosis, and Alzheimer's disease, contributing to pathology.
