Transatlantic telegraph cables were undersea cables running under the Atlantic Ocean for telegraph communications. Telegraphy was a much earlier form of communication that sent messages along wires by electrical means; the transatlantic cable first connected North America and Europe in 1858, and a durable, commercially successful link followed in 1866, reducing communication time between the continents from about ten days by ship to a matter of minutes.

Early attempts

The American entrepreneur Cyrus West Field organised the Atlantic Telegraph Company in 1856 to lay a cable from Newfoundland to Ireland. Attempts in 1857 and June 1858 ended in cable breaks. A cable completed on 5 August 1858 carried a celebratory exchange between Queen Victoria and President James Buchanan, but the chief electrician Edward Whitehouse's use of excessive voltage destroyed its insulation within weeks, and the American Civil War delayed renewal of the project.

The 1865–66 cables

Using Isambard Kingdom Brunel's enormous ship SS Great Eastern, the only vessel able to carry the whole cable, a new attempt in 1865 failed 600 miles from Newfoundland when the cable snapped and was lost. In July 1866 a fresh cable was successfully landed at Heart's Content, Newfoundland, on 27 July; the expedition then recovered and completed the 1865 cable as well, giving two working lines. William Thomson (Lord Kelvin), whose mirror galvanometer made faint signals readable, was a key scientific adviser.

Later development

Dozens more cables followed on northern and southern routes. Transmission speed rose from a few words per minute to hundreds with automatic senders and, later, multiplexing. Telegraph cables gradually gave way in the 20th century to transatlantic telephone cables (beginning with TAT-1 in 1956) and then fibre-optic cables, but they remained in service into the 1960s.
