Aristippus of Cyrene (Greek: Ἀρίστιππος ὁ Κυρηναῖος; c. 435 – c. 356 BC) was a Greek philosopher and a pupil of Socrates. He founded the Cyrenaic school of philosophy, which taught that pleasure — especially immediate, bodily pleasure — is the highest good and that it is wise to adapt oneself to any circumstances in pursuit of it.

Aristippus of Cyrene

Life

Born in Cyrene in North Africa, Aristippus traveled to Athens to study with Socrates. Unlike other Socratics, he charged fees for his teaching and lived luxuriously at the courts of tyrants, including Dionysius I of Syracuse, where anecdotes collected by Diogenes Laërtius show him enduring humiliations with wit and equanimity. A famous saying attributed to him — "I possess, I am not possessed" — expressed his ideal of enjoying pleasures without being ruled by them. His daughter Arete was his student and successor in the school.

Philosophy

Aristippus held that knowledge is limited to our own immediate sensations or "affections" (pathe): we can know that we are "sweetened" or "whitened," but not the nature of the external things that cause these states. Ethically, the Cyrenaics divided motions of the soul into pleasure (a smooth motion) and pain (a rough motion), judging the present pleasure the goal of life; past pleasures are gone and future ones uncertain. Later Cyrenaics such as Anniceris and Theodorus modified these doctrines.

Influence

The Cyrenaic school anticipated aspects of Epicureanism, though Epicurus criticized its emphasis on bodily and momentary pleasures. Aristippus's writings are lost, and knowledge of his thought comes mainly from Diogenes Laërtius, Aristocles, and later doxographers.
