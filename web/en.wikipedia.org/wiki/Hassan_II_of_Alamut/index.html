Ḥasan ʿAlā Dhikrihi's-Salām (1138 or 1126 – 1166), known as Hassan II of Alamut, was the fourth ruler of the Nizari Ismaili state centred on Alamut Castle in Persia, reigning from 1162 until his assassination in 1166. He is best known for proclaiming the doctrine of qiyāma — the spiritual resurrection — in 1164, a dramatic turning point in Nizari Ismaili history.

Background

Hassan was the son of Muhammad ibn Buzurg-Umid, the third lord of Alamut, but Nizari tradition held him to be in reality a descendant of the Fatimid imam Nizar ibn al-Mustansir, secretly brought to Alamut as an infant — a claim central to his authority as the designated Imam of the Nizaris. He was noted for his deep engagement with Ismaili theology and philosophy and, reportedly, for habits such as wine-drinking that scandalised the more austere members of the community at Alamut.

The proclamation of the Qiyama

On 17 Ramadan 559 AH (8 August 1164), Hassan assembled the Nizaris of Alamut and publicly declared the advent of the qiyāma: the resurrection and the end of the era of the sharia's outward observance. A sermon (khutba) was read in the name of the hidden Imam, and Hassan announced himself as the Imam's deputy (khalīfa) and proof (ḥujja). In its esoteric interpretation, the qiyama meant the unveiling of spiritual truths (ḥaqāʾiq) previously concealed behind literal law: paradise and resurrection were understood as spiritual realities. Similar proclamations followed in the Nizari communities of Syria and elsewhere.

Late in 1165 Hassan began to make clear that he was himself the Imam — a position later Nizari tradition fully accepted, tracing his descent to Nizar. The abrogation of ritual law attracted hostile attention from Sunni chroniclers, who portrayed Alamut as a place of impiety.

Death and succession

Hassan II was stabbed to death in January 1166 at Lambsar Castle by his brother-in-law, Hasan ibn Namawar. He was succeeded by his son, Nur al-Din Muhammad II, who consolidated and elaborated the doctrine of the qiyama over a long reign.
