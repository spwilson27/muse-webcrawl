Spermidine is a polyamine compound (C₇H₁₉N₃) found in ribosomes and living tissues and having various metabolic functions in organisms. It was originally isolated from semen, as was its close relative spermine. Spermidine is a precursor to spermine, is derived from putrescine, and is essential for cell growth and proliferation: it serves as the donor of the aminobutyl group in the synthesis of hypusine, a unique modified amino acid required for the activity of the translation factor eIF5A.

Biological roles

Polyamines bind to DNA and RNA, modulating chromatin structure, ion channels, and gene expression. Spermidine in particular is noted for inducing autophagy, the cellular recycling pathway: feeding spermidine extends the lifespan of yeast, flies, worms, and mice in laboratory studies, and dietary intake has been associated in observational studies with lower mortality and better cognitive outcomes in humans. Spermidine levels decline with age in many tissues.

Sources

Spermidine occurs in many foods, notably wheat germ, aged cheese, mushrooms, soy products and other legumes, and grapefruit. It is also produced by the gut microbiota. Wheat-germ-derived spermidine is sold as a dietary supplement, though health claims regarding longevity remain under investigation and are not established by large randomized trials.

Chemistry and synthesis

In cells, ornithine is decarboxylated to putrescine, which receives an aminopropyl group from decarboxylated S-adenosylmethionine to form spermidine (via spermidine synthase); a second aminopropyl addition yields spermine. Spermidine is a hygroscopic, alkaline liquid with three amino groups that are substantially protonated at physiological pH, giving it a polycationic character that underlies its binding to nucleic acids.
