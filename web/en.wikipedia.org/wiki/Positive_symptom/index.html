Positive symptom

Positive symptoms are psychotic symptoms that are not typically experienced by healthy people, but appear in people suffering from a range of illnesses, most prominently schizophrenia. They are the presence of pathological experiences, as opposed to the absence of normal functions seen in negative symptoms. The term derives from the notion of something being added to normal behaviour rather than subtracted from it.

Types

Positive symptoms include:

  • Hallucinations — perceptions in the absence of a stimulus, most commonly auditory (hearing voices), but also visual, tactile, olfactory or gustatory.
  • Delusions — fixed false beliefs not amenable to reason and not shared by the person's culture, such as persecutory delusions, delusions of reference, grandiose delusions and thought insertion or withdrawal.
  • Disorganised thinking and speech — formal thought disorder, including loose associations, derailment, tangentiality and word salad.
  • Disorganised or catatonic behaviour — including agitation, bizarre movements and catatonia.

Causes and treatment

Positive symptoms are strongly associated with dysregulation of dopaminergic neurotransmission, particularly hyperactivity of dopamine D2 receptor signalling in subcortical structures — the basis of the dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia. They typically respond better to antipsychotic medication, which blocks dopamine receptors, than negative symptoms do. Positive symptoms occur in episodes ("psychotic episodes") and may remit between them, whereas negative symptoms tend to be more persistent.

Rating scales such as the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) and the Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SAPS) quantify positive symptoms for clinical assessment and research.
