Lehi is a prophet in the Book of Mormon, the founding patriarch of the Nephite and Lamanite nations described in that scripture of the Latter Day Saint movement. According to the narrative, Lehi lived in Jerusalem around 600 BC, during the reign of King Zedekiah, and was commanded by God in a vision to leave the city with his family before its destruction by Babylon. He led his family through the Arabian wilderness to the sea, where his son Nephi built a ship that carried them across the ocean to the Americas, the "promised land."

Narrative in the Book of Mormon

Lehi's story is told principally in the First and Second Books of Nephi. He is introduced as a descendant of Joseph of Egypt through the tribe of Manasseh. While praying, he receives a vision of God enthroned, surrounded by angels, and reads from a prophetic book foretelling the destruction of Jerusalem. When he preaches repentance to the people of Jerusalem, they mock him and seek his life.

Commanded to flee, Lehi takes his wife Sariah and his sons Laman, Lemuel, Sam, and Nephi into the wilderness, leaving behind his gold and silver. From a camp in the valley of Lemuel, he sends his sons back to Jerusalem twice: first to obtain the brass plates containing the scriptures from Laban, and later to persuade the family of Ishmael to join them. During the journey, Lehi receives the vision of the tree of life, one of the most frequently cited passages in Latter Day Saint teaching, in which a tree bearing white fruit symbolizes the love of God, and an iron rod leads to it along a strait and narrow path.

Lehi also finds the Liahona, a brass ball with spindles that directs the family's travels according to their faith. After years in the wilderness, including a sojourn at a place called Bountiful on the coast, Lehi's family sails to the Americas. Shortly after their arrival, the elderly Lehi gathers his sons, blesses them, delivers a notable discourse on agency, opposition, and the Atonement (recorded in 2 Nephi 2), and then dies.

Family and descendants

After Lehi's death, his family divides: the followers of his righteous son Nephi become the Nephites, while the followers of his elder sons Laman and Lemuel become the Lamanites. This division drives the central conflict of the Book of Mormon for the next thousand years of its narrative. Lehi's younger sons Jacob and Joseph, both born in the wilderness, become important religious teachers in the record.

Archaeological and scholarly discussion

Some Latter Day Saint scholars have pointed to sites in the Arabian Peninsula, such as a possible altar inscription reading "NHM" near the Book of Mormon's place-name Nahom and the fertile coastal region of Khor Kharfot as a candidate for Bountiful, as consistent with Lehi's route. Mainstream archaeologists and historians do not regard the Book of Mormon narrative as a historical account, and the historicity of Lehi remains a matter of religious belief rather than scholarly consensus.
