The Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME), often called "the Merc", is an American financial and commodity derivative exchange based in Chicago. It is part of CME Group, the world's largest derivatives marketplace, which also owns the Chicago Board of Trade (CBOT), the New York Mercantile Exchange (NYMEX), and COMEX.

History

The exchange began in 1898 as the Chicago Butter and Egg Board, a spin-off of the Chicago Board of Trade, and was renamed the Chicago Mercantile Exchange in 1919. It originally traded futures on agricultural products such as frozen pork bellies and live cattle. Under chairman Leo Melamed, the CME pioneered financial futures, launching currency futures through its International Monetary Market (IMM) division in 1972, shortly after the collapse of the Bretton Woods system.

Trading

Trading takes place both on the exchange's Globex electronic platform, launched in 1992, and historically by open outcry in trading pits. Landmark products include Eurodollar futures, S&P 500 index futures (introduced in 1982), and Bitcoin futures (launched in December 2017). The CME's clearing house acts as counterparty to every trade, guaranteeing performance.
