Ephemeris time

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ephemeris time (ET) is a time standard that was used by astronomers from 1952 to 1984 as the independent variable in the equations of celestial mechanics. It was introduced because time based on the Earth's rotation (Universal Time) is irregular: the rotation rate varies seasonally and secularly, so a uniform time scale was needed for accurate ephemerides — tables of the positions of astronomical objects.

Definition and measurement

Ephemeris time was defined through the orbital motion of the Earth around the Sun, with the ephemeris second defined as a fraction of the tropical year 1900. In practice ET could only be determined after the fact, chiefly from observations of the Moon's motion (largely by the method of lunar occultations and photographic observations with the Markowitz Moon camera), because the Moon's known orbit acts as a clock hand against the uniform scale.

Successors

In 1976 the IAU adopted relativistic time scales to replace ET; from 1984, ephemeris time was superseded for most purposes by Terrestrial Dynamical Time (TDT, later Terrestrial Time, TT) and Barycentric Dynamical Time (TDB). The difference between ET and Universal Time, known as ΔT, amounted to roughly 60 seconds by the 1980s, reflecting the accumulated slowing of Earth's rotation.
