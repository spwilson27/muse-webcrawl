Sant'Agnese in Agone

LocationPiazza Navona, Rome, Italy
DenominationRoman Catholic
ArchitectsGirolamo Rainaldi, Francesco Borromini, others
StyleBaroque

Sant'Agnese in Agone is a 17th-century Baroque church in Rome, Italy. It faces onto the Piazza Navona, one of the city's most celebrated public squares, in the place where the early Christian Saint Agnes was martyred in the ancient Stadium of Domitian. The church's name derives not from "agony" but from the Greek agones (games), referring to the stadium, corrupted in medieval Latin to agone.

The facade of Sant'Agnese in Agone, Piazza Navona

History

Construction began in 1652 under Pope Innocent X Pamphilj, whose family palace adjoined the site and who intended the church as a family chapel. Girolamo Rainaldi and his son Carlo produced the first design; in 1653 they were replaced by Francesco Borromini, who gave the church its distinctive concave facade, central dome, and twin bell towers. After Borromini's dismissal in 1657, work continued under others, and the church was consecrated in 1672.

Interior

The Greek-cross interior is richly decorated with marble, gilded stucco, and frescoes, including a dome fresco of the Glory of Paradise by Ciro Ferri and his pupil Sebastiano Corbellini. The church preserves the relic of Saint Agnes's skull in a chapel, and a marble relief of her martyrdom. Popular Roman legend holds that Bernini's river god statues on the Fountain of the Four Rivers opposite gesture in horror at Borromini's facade — a charming story, though the fountain predates the church's facade, making it chronologically impossible.
