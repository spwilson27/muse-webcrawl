Adaptive immune system

The adaptive immune system, also known as the acquired immune system, is a subsystem of the immune system composed of specialised cells and processes that eliminate pathogens or prevent their growth. It is distinguished by its ability to recognise specific antigens and to mount a stronger, faster response on re-exposure — immunological memory. The adaptive system is found in all vertebrates and works alongside the older innate immune system.

Components

The principal cells are lymphocytes: B cells, which mature in the bone marrow and produce antibodies (humoral immunity), and T cells, which mature in the thymus and kill infected cells or coordinate the response (cell-mediated immunity). Each lymphocyte bears receptors of a single specificity, generated by somatic recombination of gene segments (V(D)J recombination), producing a repertoire capable of recognising an enormous diversity of antigens.

Activation and memory

When an antigen-presenting cell displays a foreign antigen on MHC molecules, matching T cells are activated and stimulate B cells to proliferate (clonal expansion) and differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells and long-lived memory cells. A first ("primary") response is slow, taking days to weeks; on later encounters the memory cells drive a rapid, powerful "secondary" response. This is the principle exploited by vaccination. The system must also maintain tolerance to the body's own tissues; failures of tolerance produce autoimmune disease, while deficiencies produce immunodeficiency.
