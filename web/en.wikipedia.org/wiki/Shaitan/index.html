Shaitan

Shaitan (Arabic: شيطان, shayṭān, plural shayāṭīn) is an Arabic term for evil spirits in Islamic tradition, cognate with the Hebrew Satan. In the Quran, the word is used both generally, for devils who incite humans and jinn to sin, and specifically as a name-like designation for Iblis, the chief devil, who was cast down for refusing to bow before Adam.

In the Quran

The Quran mentions shayṭān dozens of times. Iblis, a being created from fire and counted among the jinn (18:50), refused God's command to prostrate before Adam, was expelled, and was granted respite until the Day of Judgement to lead humans astray. Shaitans are said to whisper (waswasa) into the hearts of people, beautify evil deeds, and inspire false revelation to "satans of men and jinn" (6:112), yet to have no coercive authority over the faithful (15:42; 16:99). At the judgement, Satan will disown his followers (14:22).

In theology and tradition

Islamic tradition elaborates the shaitans' activities: they are chained during Ramadan according to hadith; the stoning of the pillars at Mina during Hajj (the ramy al-jamarāt) ritually repels Satan, commemorating Abraham's rejection of his temptations. Folk Islam across the Middle East and South Asia includes protective practices against shaitanic whispers, such as reciting the taʿawwudh ("I seek refuge in God from the accursed Satan") and the Throne Verse.

Related concepts

The term is related to Shaytan in pre-Islamic Arabian belief in desert spirits, to the Jewish-Christian Satan (with whom Iblis is often equated in comparative discussion), and to the jinn more broadly. In modern usage, "Shaitan" has also entered political rhetoric — most famously in the Iranian revolutionary epithet "Great Satan" for the United States.
