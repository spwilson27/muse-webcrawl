Spectrum auction

A spectrum auction is a process whereby a government uses an auction system to sell the rights to transmit signals over specific bands of the electromagnetic spectrum and to assign scarce spectrum resources. Depending on the specific auction format used, a spectrum auction can last from a single day to several months from the opening bid to the final payment. Spectrum auctions are a step toward market-based spectrum management and privatization of public airwaves, and are a way for governments to allocate scarce resources.

History

Economists such as Ronald Coase had advocated market allocation of spectrum since the 1950s, but licences were long assigned by administrative hearings or lotteries. New Zealand and the United States pioneered auctions in the early 1990s; the U.S. Federal Communications Commission received auction authority in 1993 and held its first auctions in 1994 using the simultaneous multiple-round format designed by economists Paul Milgrom, Robert Wilson and Preston McAfee — work later recognized by the 2020 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences. The UK's 2000 3G auction raised £22.5 billion, far beyond forecasts, and became a landmark for auction design.

Design issues

Regulators must decide licence size, duration, reserve prices, eligibility and activity rules, and whether to use simultaneous ascending, sealed-bid, clock, or combinatorial formats. Goals may include efficient assignment to the highest-value users, promoting competition (through spectrum caps or set-asides for new entrants), covering populations, and raising revenue. Critics note that very high prices can burden operators and slow network rollout; auctions also became tools of industrial policy, as in incentive auctions that repurpose broadcast television spectrum for mobile broadband (the U.S. 2016–2017 broadcast incentive auction).
