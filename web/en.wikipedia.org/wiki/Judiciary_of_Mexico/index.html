Judiciary of Mexico

The judiciary of Mexico is the branch of the Mexican federal government charged with interpreting and applying the law. Judicial power of the federation is vested, under the Constitution of 1917, in the Supreme Court of Justice of the Nation, the Electoral Tribunal, circuit courts (collegiate and unitary), and district courts, while each of Mexico's 32 states maintains its own judiciary.

Federal judiciary

The Supreme Court (Suprema Corte de Justicia de la Nación) is Mexico's highest court, composed of eleven justices (ministros). It hears constitutional controversies, actions of unconstitutionality, and amparo proceedings of exceptional importance. Below it, collegiate circuit courts (tribunales colegiados de circuito), sitting in panels of three magistrates, and unitary circuit courts (tribunales unitarios) hear appeals and amparo cases, while district courts (juzgados de distrito) serve as federal trial courts. A distinctive instrument of Mexican law is the juicio de amparo, a suit by which individuals challenge acts of authority that violate their constitutional rights; it originated in the 19th century and has influenced constitutional remedies throughout Latin America.

Judicial council and administration

The Federal Judicial Council (Consejo de la Judicatura Federal), created by the 1994 judicial reform, administered the federal courts below the Supreme Court, overseeing appointments, careers, and discipline of judges and magistrates. The 1994 reforms also strengthened judicial independence, fixed justices' terms at fifteen years, and expanded the Supreme Court's constitutional-review powers.

2024 judicial reform

In September 2024, a controversial constitutional reform promoted by President Andrés Manuel López Obrador was enacted, providing that federal judges—including Supreme Court justices, magistrates, and district judges—would be chosen by popular election, reducing the Supreme Court to nine justices, shortening terms, and replacing the Judicial Council with new administrative and disciplinary bodies. The first judicial elections under the new system were held in June 2025. The reform drew strong criticism from opposition parties, legal scholars, and foreign governments concerned about judicial independence, and strong support from the governing coalition, which argued it would democratize a corrupt and unaccountable judiciary.
