Urban park

A metropolitan park provides open green space within the city.

An urban park or metropolitan park is a park set aside within a city or town for recreation, greenery, and public amenity. Urban parks range from small "pocket parks" on single lots to vast landscapes such as New York's Central Park. They may include lawns, trees, gardens, water features, playgrounds, sports fields, paths, and cultural facilities, and are typically owned and maintained by a municipality.

History

Public parks emerged in the 19th century as a response to industrial urbanization: Birkenhead Park in England (1847), designed by Joseph Paxton, pioneered the publicly funded park and directly inspired Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux's Central Park (from 1858). Reformers argued parks were the "lungs of the city", providing health, moral uplift, and democratic social mixing. The 20th century added playground movements, parkways, and national-park-style urban reserves; the 21st has seen linear parks on reclaimed infrastructure such as New York's High Line.

Benefits and design

Studies associate urban parks with improved physical and mental health, reduced air temperature and pollution, stormwater absorption, biodiversity habitat, and higher nearby property values. Design balances active recreation with quiet landscape, ecological function with maintenance cost. Access is unequally distributed in many cities — "park poverty" correlates with low-income districts — making equitable provision a focus of contemporary planning.

Types

Common categories include neighborhood parks, community parks, regional parks, sports complexes, botanical gardens, waterfront parks, and greenways connecting them into networks.
