Dangling modifier

Type: Grammatical ambiguity
Related concepts: Misplaced modifier, squinting modifier
Field: Grammar, usage

A dangling modifier (also called a dangling participle) is a word or phrase — typically a participial phrase — that modifies a word not clearly or logically present in the sentence. Because the subject the modifier describes is missing, the sentence often reads as though the modifier attaches to the wrong noun, producing unintended, sometimes comic meanings.

Examples

In "Walking down the street, the trees were beautiful," the phrase "walking down the street" has no one to attach to — the grammatical subject is "the trees", which absurdly appear to be walking. A corrected version supplies the missing subject: "Walking down the street, I found the trees beautiful."

Usage debate

Traditional grammarians and style guides class dangling modifiers as errors to be revised. Descriptive linguists note that many loosely attached participles ("speaking generally", "judging by appearances") are idiomatic and unambiguous in context, and that celebrated writers have used dangling constructions for centuries. Nevertheless, in formal writing, recasting the sentence is usually advised where any real ambiguity or absurdity results.
