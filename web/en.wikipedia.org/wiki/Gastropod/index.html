Gastropods, commonly known as snails and slugs, belong to the class Gastropoda, the largest class of molluscs. They are found in marine, freshwater and terrestrial habitats on every continent, and range from microscopic sea snails to the giant African land snail and large marine conchs.

Anatomy

The gastropod body consists of a muscular foot used for locomotion, a head with tentacles and eyes, and a visceral mass that in most species is coiled and enclosed in a single, usually spiralled shell. A distinctive developmental process called torsion rotates the visceral mass and mantle cavity, so that the anus and gills come to lie near the head. Gastropods feed with a radula, a ribbon of teeth. Respiration is by gills in most aquatic forms and by a vascularised mantle cavity acting as a lung in land snails and slugs.

Diversity and ecology

With tens of thousands of described species, gastropods occupy roles as grazers, predators, filter feeders, parasites and scavengers. Some, such as cone snails, produce potent venoms; others, such as apple snails and the giant African snail, are invasive pests. Many species are hermaphroditic.

Evolution

Gastropods appear in the fossil record in the late Cambrian and diversified greatly in the Ordovician. Land gastropods evolved multiple times. Modern classification divides the class into clades including Patellogastropoda, Vetigastropoda, Neritimorpha, Caenogastropoda and Heterobranchia, the last containing the land snails, slugs and sea slugs.
