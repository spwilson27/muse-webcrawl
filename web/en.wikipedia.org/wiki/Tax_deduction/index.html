Tax deduction

A tax deduction is an expense that a taxpayer can subtract from gross income to arrive at taxable income, thereby reducing the amount of tax owed. Because a deduction lowers the income to which marginal rates apply, the money value of a deduction equals the expense multiplied by the taxpayer's marginal tax rate — so deductions are worth more to higher-rate taxpayers. This distinguishes them from tax credits, which reduce the tax bill directly by a fixed amount.

Forms

Systems differ by country. In the United States, individuals may take the standard deduction — a fixed amount that roughly doubled under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 — or itemise deductions such as mortgage interest, state and local taxes (capped at $10,000 since 2018), charitable donations and large medical expenses. "Above-the-line" deductions (for example contributions to traditional retirement accounts and health savings accounts, educator expenses and student loan interest) are taken before adjusted gross income. Businesses deduct their ordinary and necessary expenses — wages, rent, materials — and recover capital costs through depreciation or amortisation deductions.

Rationale and criticism

Some deductions exist to measure income correctly: a business cannot be taxed fairly without netting its costs. Others are deliberate subsidies delivered through the tax code ("tax expenditures"), encouraging home ownership, charity, saving or health insurance. Such deductions are criticised for being "upside down" — most valuable to those who need subsidy least — and for complicating the code, which is why many tax reform proposals pair lower rates with narrower deduction bases.
