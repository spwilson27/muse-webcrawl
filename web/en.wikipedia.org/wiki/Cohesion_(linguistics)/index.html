Cohesion (linguistics)

Cohesive ties—reference, conjunction, lexical links—bind sentences into a text.

Cohesion in linguistics is the grammatical and lexical linking that holds a text together and gives it meaning. It is a surface property of discourse: a text is cohesive when its elements are connected by explicit linguistic devices, whereas coherence concerns the underlying sense that makes the text hang together for a reader.

Halliday and Hasan's framework

The most influential account is that of M. A. K. Halliday and Ruqaiya Hasan in Cohesion in English (1976), which identifies five categories of cohesive tie:

  • Reference — pronouns and determiners pointing to other items (e.g. "she", "this");
  • Substitution — replacing a word or clause with another ("do so", "one");
  • Ellipsis — omitting material recoverable from context;
  • Conjunction — linking expressions such as "and", "but", "therefore";
  • Lexical cohesion — repetition, synonymy, collocation and other vocabulary links.

Cohesion and coherence

Later discourse analysts noted that cohesion is neither necessary nor sufficient for coherence: a text full of cohesive ties can still be meaningless, and short, highly coherent exchanges may contain few overt ties. Nevertheless the framework remains standard in applied linguistics, language teaching and stylistics for describing how texts are constructed.
