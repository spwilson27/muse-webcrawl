Claude Chappe

Claude Chappe (25 December 1763 – 23 January 1805) was a French inventor who in 1792 demonstrated a practical semaphore system that eventually spanned all of France. His system consisted of a series of towers, each with a pair of articulated wooden arms mounted on a crossbeam, whose positions were set by operators using pulleys and copied by the next tower through a telescope. It was the first practical telecommunications system of the industrial age, and the network, administered as the French state telegraph, eventually comprised 556 stations covering some 4,800 kilometres (3,000 miles).

Life and invention

Chappe was born at Brûlon in the Sarthe, one of five brothers, and was educated for the church before losing his benefice in the French Revolution. At their family property, the Chappe brothers experimented with systems for transmitting messages over distance, first with synchronised clocks and numbered panels and then with pivoting arms. Claude first called his device the tachygraphe ("fast writer"), then settled on télégraphe ("far writer") — the origin of the word "telegraph".

In 1792 Chappe demonstrated the system to the Legislative Assembly, and in 1793 he was appointed ingénieur-télégraphe and authorised to build a line from Paris to Lille, near the front in the war with Austria. On 15 August 1794 the line carried its first celebrated news: the recapture of Le Quesnoy from the Austrians, reported in Paris less than an hour after the event. Napoleon made extensive use of the network, which was extended to Strasbourg, Lyon, Milan, Venice, and Brest. Messages were encoded in codebooks of thousands of numbered phrases, known only to senior officials; station operators handled only numbers, and could signal in all but foggy weather.

Death and legacy

Worn down by ill health, depression, and disputes with rivals who claimed to have invented the system, Chappe took his own life in Paris in January 1805, drowning himself in a well at the telegraph administration's headquarters. His brothers, notably Ignace Chappe, continued to run the network, which remained in French government service until the 1850s, when electric telegraphy replaced it. Similar semaphore systems were adopted in Sweden, Britain (as shutter and arm telegraphs), and elsewhere. Chappe is commemorated by a statue in Paris and by the survival of a few towers, including the restored tower at Saverne.
