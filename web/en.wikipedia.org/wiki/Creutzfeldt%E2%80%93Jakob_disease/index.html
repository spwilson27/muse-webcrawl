Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease
TypePrion disease
SymptomsRapid dementia, myoclonus, ataxia
PrognosisInvariably fatal

Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD) is a rare, rapidly progressive and invariably fatal neurodegenerative disorder caused by prions — misfolded forms of the prion protein that induce further misfolding. It is the most common human prion disease, occurring in about one to two people per million per year.

Forms

Most cases (about 85%) are sporadic, arising without known cause. Others are familial, due to mutations in the PRNP gene, or iatrogenic, transmitted through contaminated medical procedures. Variant CJD (vCJD), identified in the United Kingdom in 1996, is linked to eating beef from cattle with bovine spongiform encephalopathy ("mad cow disease").

Course and diagnosis

The disease typically begins in people around 60 with memory loss, personality change and coordination problems, progressing to severe dementia, involuntary jerking (myoclonus) and death usually within a year of onset. MRI changes, EEG patterns and cerebrospinal fluid tests such as RT-QuIC support the diagnosis, which can only be confirmed definitively by brain tissue examination. There is no cure.
