Conjugation (organic chemistry)

1,3-Butadiene, the simplest conjugated diene: alternating double and single bonds allow π-electron delocalisation.

In chemistry, a conjugated system is a system of connected p-orbitals with delocalised electrons in a molecule, which in general lowers the overall energy of the molecule and increases its stability. It is conventionally represented as alternating single and multiple bonds. Lone pairs, radicals and charges can all participate in conjugation, provided the atom bearing them has a p-orbital aligned with the rest of the system.

Structure and energetics

In a conjugated system such as 1,3-butadiene, the four carbon atoms are all sp2-hybridised and their unhybridised p-orbitals overlap across the whole carbon skeleton. The π electrons are therefore not confined between pairs of atoms but are delocalised over the molecule, which shows up experimentally as intermediate bond lengths: the central C–C bond of butadiene (≈146 pm) is shorter than a typical single bond (≈154 pm), and the molecule gains extra thermodynamic stability (a lower heat of hydrogenation than two isolated double bonds would predict).

Consequences

  • Reactivity: conjugated dienes undergo 1,4-addition as well as 1,2-addition, and are far more reactive in the Diels–Alder reaction than isolated alkenes. Conjugation stabilises allylic and benzylic carbocations, radicals and carbanions.
  • Spectroscopy: conjugation narrows the HOMO–LUMO gap, shifting ultraviolet–visible absorption to longer wavelengths. Extended conjugation produces colour: β-carotene (11 conjugated double bonds) is orange, and most organic dyes and pigments are conjugated systems.
  • Conductivity: conjugated polymers such as polyacetylene conduct electricity when doped, a discovery recognised by the 2000 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
  • Aromaticity: cyclic, planar, fully conjugated systems with 4n+2 π electrons are aromatic and unusually stable; benzene is the archetype.

Types

Cross-conjugation occurs when two π-systems share a single bond but are not conjugated with each other (as in benzophenone or dendralenes). Hyperconjugation is the related, weaker overlap of σ-bonds with adjacent p-orbitals. Conjugation may also involve carbonyl groups (α,β-unsaturated carbonyls, or enones), nitriles, and heteroatoms bearing lone pairs, as in amides and enols.
