Shale oil

Shale oil is an unconventional oil produced from oil shale rock fragments by pyrolysis, hydrogenation, or thermal dissolution. These processes convert the organic matter within the rock (kerogen) into synthetic oil and gas. The resulting oil can be used immediately as a fuel oil or upgraded to meet refinery feedstock specifications by adding hydrogen and removing impurities such as sulfur and nitrogen.

The term "shale oil" is also used for crude oil produced from shales of low-permeability, "tight" formations — so-called tight oil, such as that of the Bakken and Eagle Ford formations in the United States, extracted by horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing. Strictly, however, "oil shale" refers to immature rock rich in kerogen that has not yet been converted to oil by geological heating, and this article concerns that resource.

History

Oil shale has been used since ancient times — Mesopotamians used its oil for roads and waterproofing — and modern shale oil extraction began in France in 1837 and in Scotland in the 1850s, where James Young's industry distilled oil from local shales and coal. Shale oil industries later operated in Estonia, China, Brazil, Germany, South Africa and Australia. The industry collapsed in many countries after the discovery of abundant cheap conventional petroleum in the Middle East in the 20th century, but Estonia still generates most of its electricity from oil shale, and China and Brazil maintain significant industries.

Extraction and processing

Commercial production usually mines the shale by surface or underground methods, crushes it, and heats it in retorts to about 450–500 °C in the absence of oxygen, decomposing kerogen to oil vapour, gas, and char. In-situ methods heat the shale underground over months or years — for example, Shell's in-situ conversion process used electric heaters within a freeze wall — but none has yet achieved large-scale commercial deployment. The raw shale oil differs from conventional crude: it contains more olefins, oxygen and nitrogen compounds, and requires substantial upgrading.

Reserves and environment

World oil shale resources are enormous — the Green River Formation of Colorado, Utah and Wyoming alone contains an estimated resource comparable to the world's proven conventional reserves — but recovery is energy-intensive, produces spent shale waste and mining scars, demands large quantities of water, and has a higher carbon footprint than conventional oil production. These factors, plus cost, have kept production limited: global shale oil output from oil shale has remained in the tens of thousands of barrels per day.
