Gibbs phase rule

The Gibbs phase rule is a principle of thermodynamics that relates the number of degrees of freedom of a system in equilibrium to the number of components and the number of phases present. It was derived by the American physicist Josiah Willard Gibbs in his landmark paper On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances, published between 1875 and 1878.

Statement

The rule is commonly written as:

F = C − P + 2

where F is the number of degrees of freedom (the number of intensive variables, such as temperature and pressure, that can be changed independently without disturbing the equilibrium), C is the number of chemically independent components, and P is the number of phases in the system. The constant 2 accounts for temperature and pressure as the external intensive variables.

Examples

For a pure substance such as water (C = 1), a single phase (P = 1) gives F = 2: both temperature and pressure may be varied independently within the single-phase region of the phase diagram. Where two phases coexist, such as liquid water and ice along the melting curve, F = 1, so fixing the pressure determines the temperature. At the triple point (P = 3), F = 0: the state is invariant, occurring at one unique combination of temperature and pressure (273.16 K and 611.657 Pa for water).

Derivation

The rule follows from counting variables and constraints. A system with C components and P phases is described by P(C − 1) composition variables plus temperature and pressure. Equilibrium requires the chemical potential of each component to be equal in all phases, imposing C(P − 1) constraints. Subtracting constraints from variables yields F = P(C − 1) + 2 − C(P − 1) = C − P + 2.

Variants

When additional external fields are relevant (electric or magnetic fields, gravity), the constant is adjusted. For systems at fixed pressure — common in condensed-matter and materials work — the rule is often written in the reduced form F = C − P + 1. The phase rule underlies the interpretation of phase diagrams in chemistry, metallurgy and geology, and is closely related to Gibbs–Duhem thermodynamics.
