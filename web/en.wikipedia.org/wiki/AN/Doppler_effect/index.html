Doppler effect

The Doppler effect (also called Doppler shift) is the change in the frequency of a wave in relation to an observer who is moving relative to the source of the wave. It is named after the physicist Christian Doppler, who proposed it in 1842.

Explanation

When a source moves toward an observer, each successive wave crest is emitted from a position closer to the observer, so the waves are compressed and the observed frequency increases — a siren's pitch rises as an ambulance approaches. When the source recedes, the waves are stretched and the frequency decreases. The effect applies to sound, light and other waves; for light it appears as blueshift for approaching objects and redshift for receding ones.

Applications

The Doppler effect underlies Doppler radar and weather radar, police speed guns, medical ultrasound imaging of blood flow, and astronomical measurements of radial velocity that reveal the motion of stars and galaxies and the expansion of the universe.
