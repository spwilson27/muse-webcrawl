Also calledSecond Germanic consonant shift
Datec. AD 500 – 800 (in stages)
AreaSouthern/central German speech area
EffectVoiceless stops → fricatives/affricates; þ, ð → d; etc.

The High German consonant shift (German: zweite Lautverschiebung) is a chain of sound changes that affected the southern and central German dialects in the early Middle Ages and distinguishes High German from Low German, Dutch and English. It is the second great Germanic shift, after the First (Grimm's law) that defined Germanic itself.

The changes

Proto-Germanic voiceless stops became affricates or long fricatives depending on position: compare English pepper with German Pfeffer, water with Wasser, make (Dutch maken) with German machen. The dental fricatives also changed: English th-words appear with d in German (three – drei, thou – du). Geminate and post-vocalic positions show the fullest effects.

Geography

The shift spread from the Alpine south northward, weakening by degrees, so that each change has its own northern limit — producing the criss-crossing bundle of isoglosses called the Rhenish fan. Two lines are famous: the Benrath line (maken/machen), taken as the Low/High German boundary, and the Uerdingen line (ik/ich). Standard German rests on shifted (East Central and southern) varieties, so the shift shapes every comparison between German and English vocabulary today.
