European Convention on Human Rights

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR), formally the Convention for the Protection of Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms, is an international convention to protect human rights and political freedoms in Europe. Drafted in 1950 by the then newly formed Council of Europe, it entered into force on 3 September 1953. All Council of Europe member states are party to the Convention, and new members are expected to ratify it at the earliest opportunity (Russia ceased to be a party in 2022 following its expulsion after the invasion of Ukraine, the Court retaining jurisdiction over earlier acts).

Rights and machinery

The Convention guarantees, among others, the right to life, freedom from torture and slavery, liberty and security, fair trial, privacy, freedom of thought, conscience and religion, expression, assembly and association, the right to marry, prohibition of discrimination in the enjoyment of Convention rights, and (by later protocols) property, education, free elections, abolition of the death penalty in peacetime and (Protocol No. 13) in all circumstances. Its enforcement machinery — the European Court of Human Rights at Strasbourg, receiving individual applications after domestic remedies are exhausted — is the most developed international rights adjudication in the world, generating doctrines (margin of appreciation, positive obligations, the Convention as a "living instrument") studied globally. Reform debates (caseload, subsidiarity, the Brighton and Copenhagen declarations, national pushback in the UK and elsewhere) chronicle its central political problem: a court whose subjects are its own creators.
