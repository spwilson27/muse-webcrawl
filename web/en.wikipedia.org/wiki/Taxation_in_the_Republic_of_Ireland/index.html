Taxation in the Republic of Ireland


Taxes in Ireland are administered by the Revenue Commissioners

Taxation in Ireland is administered by the Revenue Commissioners (often simply "Revenue"). The principal taxes are income tax, corporation tax, value-added tax (VAT), excise duties, and social insurance contributions (PRSI), supplemented by the Universal Social Charge (USC), capital gains tax, capital acquisitions tax, stamp duties, and local property tax.

Income tax

Individuals are taxed at a standard rate of 20% up to a cut-off point and 40% above it, with the threshold varying by marital status and family circumstances. Tax credits — including the personal and employee credits — reduce the tax payable. Employees pay through the PAYE system, under which employers deduct tax, PRSI, and USC at source. The Universal Social Charge, introduced in 2011 during the financial crisis, applies at graduated rates on gross income above a low threshold.

Corporation tax

Ireland is internationally known for its low corporation tax rate of 12.5% on trading income, in place since 2003, which has been central to its strategy of attracting foreign direct investment, particularly from US technology and pharmaceutical firms. A higher rate of 25% applies to certain non-trading (passive) income. Under the OECD's global minimum tax agreement (Pillar Two), large multinational groups with revenues above €750 million became subject to a 15% effective rate in Ireland from 2024.

VAT and excise

The standard VAT rate is 23%, with reduced rates of 13.5% and 9% for certain goods and services such as hospitality, construction, and domestic fuel, a 4.8% rate for livestock, and zero-rating for most food, children's clothing, and books. Excise duties apply to alcohol, tobacco, fuel, and vehicles (including vehicle registration tax).

Other taxes

  • Capital gains tax: generally 33% on gains from disposal of assets.
  • Capital acquisitions tax: 33% on gifts and inheritances above tax-free thresholds based on the relationship to the disponer.
  • Local property tax: an annual self-assessed charge on residential property, introduced in 2013.
  • Stamp duty: 1–2% on residential property transfers and 7.5% on most commercial property.
  • DIRT: deposit interest retention tax withheld on savings interest.

History

The modern Irish tax system descends from the British system inherited at independence in 1922. Corporation tax policy evolved from export-profits relief in the 1950s through a 10% rate for manufacturing to the uniform 12.5% rate. Ireland's tax strategy has attracted sustained international attention and criticism, including the European Commission's 2016 state-aid decision concerning Apple, which Ireland and Apple contested; in 2024 the European Court of Justice ruled in the Commission's favour, requiring Ireland to recover €13 billion in escrowed funds.
