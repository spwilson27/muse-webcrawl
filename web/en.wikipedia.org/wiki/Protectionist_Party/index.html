The Protectionist Party, also known as the Protectionist and Liberal Association, was an Australian political party, organised at the federal level from Federation in 1901 until its merger with the Anti-Socialist Party in the "Fusion" of 1909, which created the Commonwealth Liberal Party. It advocated protective tariffs to develop Australian manufacturing, against the free-trade policy of its main rival, the Free Trade Party. Under Edmund Barton and then Alfred Deakin, Protectionists formed the first Commonwealth government and, despite never winning a parliamentary majority in their own right, supplied the prime minister for most of the first decade of the Federation, governing with the support or tolerance of the young Australian Labor Party.

Origins

Protectionism had been the dominant fiscal creed of the colony of Victoria since the 1860s, where tariffs defended Melbourne's manufacturing industries and were championed by David Syme's newspaper The Age and by the politician George Turner. New South Wales, by contrast, was the stronghold of free trade. A Protectionist association was formed in New South Wales in 1887, and protectionist candidates contested colonial elections through the 1890s. At the first federal election in March 1901, the Protectionists—standing on a platform of a protective tariff, a White Australia policy, and conciliation and arbitration—won 31 of the 75 seats in the House of Representatives, more than any other group, and Edmund Barton became Australia's first prime minister.

In government

The Barton government (1901–1903) passed much of the Federation's foundational legislation, including the Immigration Restriction Act 1901, a cornerstone of the White Australia policy, and the first, moderately protective, customs tariff in 1902. Barton resigned in 1903 to become a founding justice of the High Court, and Deakin succeeded him. The Protectionists, however, lacked a majority and depended on Labor support; the resulting instability produced the "three elevens" period of 1903–1904, when three parties of near-equal size—Protectionists, Free Traders and Labor—each took office in turn.

Deakin returned to power in 1905 with Labor support and governed until 1908, the party's most productive period. Its legislative record included the 1907 "New Protection", which linked tariff protection to fair and reasonable wages for workers (the scheme underpinning the Harvester judgment on the basic wage, although the High Court later struck down the excise mechanism), the beginnings of an Australian navy, old-age pensions at the federal level, and the transfer of British New Guinea (Papua) to Australian administration. Deakin's Protectionists increasingly defined themselves as a liberal, reformist "middle" party—"three elevens" cricketing rhetoric aside—opposed both to laissez-faire conservatism and to Labor socialism, and Deakin spoke of his creed as "liberal protectionism".

The Fusion

As Labor grew stronger and the tariff question faded—both major non-Labor parties having accepted a protective settlement—the logic of anti-socialist unity prevailed. In 1909 Deakin agreed to merge the Protectionists with Joseph Cook's Anti-Socialist Party (the former Free Traders) in the "Fusion", forming the Commonwealth Liberal Party. Many old Protectionists saw the merger as a betrayal of liberal principles, and some, such as William Lyne, refused to join; but the Fusion held and Deakin formed his third government, which lost heavily to Andrew Fisher's Labor at the 1910 election. The merger established the enduring pattern of a single broad anti-Labor party in Australian politics, ancestral to the later United Australia Party and the modern Liberal Party of Australia.
