Disability-adjusted life year

The disability-adjusted life year (DALY) is a measure of overall disease burden, expressed as the number of years lost due to ill-health, disability, or early death. It was developed in the 1990s as a way of comparing the overall health and life expectancy of different countries, principally through the Global Burden of Disease study led by the World Bank and the World Health Organization.

Calculation

The DALY combines two components:

  • Years of life lost (YLL) due to premature mortality — the number of deaths multiplied by the standard life expectancy at the age at which death occurs.
  • Years lived with disability (YLD) — the number of incident cases of a condition multiplied by its average duration and by a disability weight between 0 (perfect health) and 1 (death), reflecting the severity of the condition.

Thus DALY = YLL + YLD. One DALY can be thought of as one lost year of "healthy" life. The sum of DALYs across a population measures the gap between current health status and an ideal situation in which everyone lives to an advanced age free of disease and disability.

Uses

DALYs are widely used in public health to rank diseases and injuries by their total burden, to compare the cost-effectiveness of interventions, and to guide resource allocation. The Global Burden of Disease project, now coordinated by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, publishes DALY estimates for hundreds of diseases and risk factors by country, age, and sex.

Criticism

The measure has been criticized over the choice of disability weights, the use of age-weighting and time discounting in early versions, and the ethical implications of valuing life-years. A related measure is the quality-adjusted life year (QALY), which instead counts years of healthy life gained.
