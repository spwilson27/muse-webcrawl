Trochee

Trochee
TypeMetrical foot
PatternStressed syllable followed by an unstressed syllable (— ∪)
EtymologyGreek trokhaios, "running"
OppositeIamb (∪ —)

A trochee is a metrical foot in poetry consisting of a stressed syllable followed by an unstressed syllable, the opposite of an iamb. The adjective is trochaic. The word-pattern of most two-syllable English words—garden, poet, happy—is naturally trochaic, which is why verse in trochaic metre has a falling, songlike rhythm.

Examples

William Blake's "The Tyger" is in trochaic tetrameter: "Tyger, Tyger, burning bright". Shakespeare uses a trochaic chant for the witches in Macbeth ("Double, double, toil and trouble"), setting them apart from the iambic pentameter of the other characters. Longfellow's The Song of Hiawatha is sustained trochaic tetrameter throughout.

History

The trochee was common in Greek and Latin lyric and dramatic verse. In English, trochaic metres are less frequent than iambic ones for long poems but are widespread in songs, nursery rhymes and incantatory verse.
