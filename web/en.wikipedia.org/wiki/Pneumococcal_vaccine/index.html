TypePolysaccharide and conjugate vaccines
TargetStreptococcus pneumoniae
ExamplesPPSV23 (Pneumovax 23); PCV13 (Prevnar 13); PCV15; PCV20; PCV21

Pneumococcal vaccines are vaccines against the bacterium Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus), a leading cause of pneumonia, meningitis, bacteraemia, otitis media, and sinusitis, especially in young children, older adults, and immunocompromised people. Because the bacterium has more than 90 serotypes distinguished by their polysaccharide capsules, vaccines target the serotypes responsible for most invasive disease.

Types

Polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23)

The 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine, licensed in 1983, contains purified capsular polysaccharides from 23 serotypes. Pure polysaccharides are poorly immunogenic in children under two and produce no immune memory, so PPSV23 is used mainly in adults and at-risk groups.

Conjugate vaccines (PCV)

Conjugate vaccines link the polysaccharides to a carrier protein (such as CRM197, a diphtheria toxoid variant), converting the response into a T-cell-dependent one that works in infants and creates memory. The first, 7-valent PCV7 (Prevnar), was licensed in 2000 for routine infant immunization in the United States; it was succeeded by PCV13 (2010), PCV15 (Vaxneuvance, 2021), PCV20 (Apexxnar/Prevnar 20, 2021), and a 21-valent vaccine designed for adults (2024).

Impact

Routine childhood conjugate vaccination dramatically reduced invasive pneumococcal disease in children and, through herd effects (reduced carriage and transmission), in unvaccinated adults as well. A phenomenon called serotype replacement—non-vaccine serotypes becoming more common—has partly offset gains and motivated higher-valency vaccines. The World Health Organization recommends pneumococcal conjugate vaccines in all national childhood immunization programmes.

Recommendations

Schedules vary by country. In the United States, conjugate vaccine is given in infancy (typically at 2, 4, 6, and 12–15 months), and vaccination is recommended for adults at an age threshold lowered from 65 to 50 years in 2024, and for younger adults and children with conditions such as asplenia, immunodeficiency, chronic heart or lung disease, diabetes, or cochlear implants.
