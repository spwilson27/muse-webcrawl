Aqua Virgo

Built19 BC, by Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa
Lengthc. 20.9 km (13 mi)
RouteSalone springs to the Campus Martius, Rome

The Aqua Virgo is one of the eleven Roman aqueducts that supplied the city of ancient Rome. Completed in 19 BC by Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa, Augustus' general and son-in-law, it supplied water to the Baths of Agrippa on the Campus Martius and, remarkably, still functions today: after Renaissance restoration it feeds several famous fountains of Rome, including the Trevi Fountain.

Route

The aqueduct collected water from springs near the eighth milestone of the Via Collatina, east of Rome, and ran about 21 kilometres almost entirely underground — a low gradient design that protected it from damage in wartime. According to Frontinus, it delivered some 100,000 cubic metres of water per day to distribution tanks in the Campus Martius, supplying baths, fountains, and private users.

Name and later history

Tradition held that a young girl (virgo) pointed thirsty soldiers to the springs, giving the aqueduct its name; a small shrine painting commemorated the story at the source. Damaged after the fall of Rome, the channel was restored under Pope Adrian I in the 8th century and more thoroughly in the Renaissance, when as the "Acqua Vergine" it was brought to new monumental fountains. It remains part of Rome's working water supply. See also Roman aqueducts and Trevi Fountain.
