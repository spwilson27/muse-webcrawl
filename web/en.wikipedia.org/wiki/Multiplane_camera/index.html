The multiplane camera is a motion-picture camera system used in traditional hand-drawn animation to create a convincing illusion of depth. Instead of photographing a single flat drawing, the artwork is divided among several transparent panes of glass stacked at different distances below a vertically mounted camera; background layers move more slowly than foreground layers as the camera or the artwork shifts, reproducing the parallax effect of a real camera moving through space.

History

Precursors were built by several studios in the early 1930s: Lotte Reiniger used a related layered rig for silhouette films, Ub Iwerks built a multiplane camera from Chevrolet parts after leaving Disney, and the Fleischer Studios' "setback" process achieved similar depth with miniature sets. The definitive version was developed at Walt Disney Studios under technician William Garity and debuted in the Silly Symphony The Old Mill (1937), winning acclaim for its moody, dimensional storm sequence; weeks later it was used in Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs. Disney's camera stood some 3.7 metres tall, accommodated up to seven layers, and allowed the camera itself to track and zoom between planes.

Legacy

The multiplane camera defined the lush look of the golden age of American animation and was used at Disney into the era of The Little Mermaid (1989), after which computer systems — including Disney's own CAPS — reproduced and extended the effect digitally. The parallax principle survives in modern 2D animation software and in the "2.5D" techniques of digital compositing.
