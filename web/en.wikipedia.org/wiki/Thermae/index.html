Thermae
Latin: thermae (from Greek θέρμη, "heat")
Type: Roman public bathing complex
Principal rooms: caldarium (hot), tepidarium (warm), frigidarium (cold), apodyterium (changing room), palaestra (exercise yard)
Famous examples: Baths of Caracalla, Baths of Diocletian, Baths of Trajan (Rome); Roman Baths at Bath, England

In ancient Rome, thermae (singular sometimes therma) were large public bathing complexes, among the most characteristic institutions of Roman urban life. The word derives from the Greek for "hot (springs)". The grand imperial thermae combined bathing with exercise grounds, gardens, libraries, lecture halls, and shops, functioning as combined leisure centres, sports clubs, and social hubs for the whole population.

The bathing routine

A typical visit began in the apodyterium, where bathers undressed and left their clothes. Exercise in the palaestra — ball games, wrestling, weightlifting — worked up a sweat, after which the bather passed through a sequence of heated rooms. The tepidarium provided moderate warmth; the caldarium, the hottest room, often contained a hot plunge bath and was heated to high temperatures. Sweat and grime were scraped off with a curved metal strigil after oiling the skin. The routine ended with a bracing plunge in the cold pools of the frigidarium, frequently a monumental vaulted hall, and sometimes an open-air swimming pool (natatio).

Heating and engineering

The thermae depended on the hypocaust system: furnaces (praefurnia) stoked by slaves forced hot air beneath raised floors supported on brick pillars and up through hollow tiles in the walls, heating rooms and pools. Enormous quantities of water were delivered by aqueduct; the great imperial complexes consumed a significant share of Rome's supply.

History and examples

Smaller commercial baths (balneae) existed from the Republic, but the grand thermae began with the Baths of Agrippa (c. 25 BC). Successive emperors outbuilt their predecessors: the Baths of Titus, Trajan, Caracalla (212–216 AD, accommodating perhaps 1,600 bathers at once), and Diocletian (c. 298–306 AD, the largest of all, for some 3,000 bathers). Entry cost little or nothing, and the baths were open to virtually all free inhabitants, though men and women bathed at separate times or in separate suites.

The institution declined with the empire in the West after the aqueducts were cut during the Gothic sieges of the sixth century, though public bathing on the Roman model persisted for centuries in the Byzantine world and influenced the Islamic hammam. Immense ruins of the great thermae still dominate parts of Rome; the frigidarium of Diocletian's baths became the church of Santa Maria degli Angeli.
