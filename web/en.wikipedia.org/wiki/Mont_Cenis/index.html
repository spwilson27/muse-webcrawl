Mont Cenis (Italian: Moncenisio) is a massif in the Cottian Alps and a high mountain pass at an elevation of 2,085 metres (6,841 ft) between France and Italy. The pass, on the border between the French department of Savoie and the Italian region of Piedmont, has for centuries been one of the principal routes across the western Alps, linking the Maurienne valley and the town of Lanslebourg-Mont-Cenis with Susa in the Susa Valley.

History

The route over Mont Cenis was used from antiquity, and tradition holds that Hannibal may have crossed the Alps here in 218 BC — one of several candidate passes. Charlemagne crossed at Mont Cenis in 773 on his campaign against the Lombards. The road was greatly improved under Napoleon, who had a carriage road built between 1803 and 1810, along with a hospice that had existed in various forms since the Middle Ages to shelter travellers. Constantine's crossing in 312 is also traditionally located here.

In 1860, under the Treaty of Turin, Savoy was ceded by the Kingdom of Sardinia to France, and Mont Cenis became the border pass it remains today. The Mont Cenis Pass Railway, a Fell mountain railway, operated briefly from 1868 until the opening of the Fréjus (Mont Cenis) Rail Tunnel in 1871 — a tunnel bored beneath the nearby Col de Fréjus, roughly 27 km to the southwest of the pass itself, despite carrying the Mont Cenis name.

Geography

A large artificial lake, the Lac du Mont-Cenis, lies in a broad basin just south of the pass on the Italian side; its dam feeds a hydroelectric scheme shared by France and Italy. The area is popular in summer for hiking and in winter for cross-country skiing, and the pass road is a notable climb in cycling, having featured in both the Tour de France and the Giro d'Italia.
