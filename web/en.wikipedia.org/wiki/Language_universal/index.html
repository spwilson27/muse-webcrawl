Language universal
FieldLinguistic typology, generative linguistics
Key figuresJoseph Greenberg, Noam Chomsky

A language universal is a pattern or property that occurs systematically across the world's natural languages and is held to be true for all of them. Universals are studied in linguistic typology and are often proposed as evidence about the underlying nature of the human language faculty. They may be absolute (applying to every known language without exception) or statistical (implicational tendencies found in the great majority of languages).

Typological universals

Joseph Greenberg's pioneering 1963 paper formulated 45 universals, many of them implicational statements about word order: for example, languages with verb–subject–object order are prepositional, and if a language has dominant subject–object–verb order it will almost always have postpositions. Later large-scale databases, notably the World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS), have allowed universals to be tested against hundreds of languages.

Proposed near-universal features include the distinction between nouns and verbs, vowel and consonant inventories with at least a basic set of sounds, the use of personal pronouns, and the ability to negate and to question.

Generative approaches

In the generative tradition associated with Noam Chomsky, universals are attributed to an innate "universal grammar" that specifies the form any human language can take, such as structure dependence and recursion. This nativist interpretation contrasts with functional and usage-based approaches, which explain recurrent patterns through general cognition, communication pressures, and historical descent.

Criticism

Some linguists, notably in a widely discussed 2009 paper by Evans and Levinson, argue that absolute universals are few and that linguistic diversity has been underestimated, challenging the idea of a rich innate universal grammar.
