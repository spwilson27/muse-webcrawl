Plutonium-240

Plutonium-240 (240Pu) is an isotope of plutonium formed when plutonium-239 captures a neutron in a nuclear reactor. It has a half-life of about 6,561 years and decays by alpha emission to uranium-236. Its most consequential property is a relatively high rate of spontaneous fission, which makes it an undesirable contaminant in plutonium intended for nuclear weapons and a key determinant of the classification of plutonium grades.

Formation

In a reactor fuelled with uranium, 239Pu is bred by neutron capture in 238U followed by two beta decays. As the fuel remains in the reactor ("burnup"), some 239Pu nuclei absorb another neutron instead of fissioning, producing 240Pu; further captures produce 241Pu and 242Pu. The fraction of 240Pu therefore increases with burnup: weapons-grade plutonium, produced with short irradiation, contains less than about 7% 240Pu, whereas reactor-grade plutonium from spent power-reactor fuel typically contains 24% or more.

Spontaneous fission and weapons design

240Pu undergoes spontaneous fission at a rate of roughly a thousand fissions per second per gram, emitting a background of neutrons. In a gun-type or implosion weapon, these neutrons can initiate the chain reaction before the core reaches its intended degree of compression, causing "pre-initiation" and a reduced, unpredictable yield — a "fizzle". It was the discovery of this property in reactor-produced plutonium in 1944, by Emilio Segrè's group at Los Alamos, that forced the abandonment of the gun-type plutonium weapon design (Thin Man) in favour of the far more difficult implosion design used in the Trinity device and the Fat Man bomb. Even in implosion weapons, high 240Pu content degrades yield and reliability, which is why weapons programmes require low-burnup production plutonium.

Use in reactors

Although largely parasitic in thermal reactors, 240Pu is not inert fuel: a fraction of its nuclei fission with fast neutrons, and capture converts it to fissile 241Pu, so it contributes to energy production during long irradiations and in MOX fuel. In spent fuel, 240Pu also contributes significantly to decay heat and to the long-term radiotoxicity that reprocessing and waste-management strategies must address. Its presence is also central to nuclear safeguards: the 240Pu/239Pu ratio, measurable by mass spectrometry in even microscopic environmental samples, is a forensic signature revealing whether plutonium came from a weapons programme or from civil power generation, and it is used in attributing fallout from atmospheric nuclear tests.
