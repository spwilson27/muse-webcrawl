Prescription and description

In linguistics, prescription and description are two contrasting approaches to language rules. Linguistic prescription (or prescriptivism) is the establishment of rules defining preferred or correct usage of a language — rules about grammar, spelling, pronunciation, and vocabulary that may be enforced by teachers, editors, academies, or dictionaries. Linguistic description (or descriptivism), by contrast, is the non-judgmental analysis of actual language use: describing the regularities speakers in fact follow, regardless of whether those usages are judged "correct".

Descriptive linguistics

Modern academic linguistics, founded in the work of scholars such as Ferdinand de Saussure and Leonard Bloomfield, is overwhelmingly descriptive: its object is the grammar speakers actually have, including the systematic rules of stigmatized dialects. Descriptivists point out that forms condemned as "errors" — split infinitives, double negatives, sentence-final prepositions in English — are often ancient, regular, and communicatively effective, and that language change is inevitable. A dictionary made on descriptive principles records pronunciations and usages as found, with labels (e.g. "informal", "nonstandard") rather than prohibitions.

Prescriptive linguistics

Prescription has a long history: the grammar of Pāṇini for Sanskrit, the Académie Française (founded 1635), Samuel Johnson's Dictionary (1755), and the many English usage guides from Robert Lowth to Fowler's Modern English Usage and Strunk & White. Prescriptivists argue that shared standards aid clear communication, education, and access to formal registers, and that some codified norm is unavoidable in schooling, law, and publishing. Standard languages — such as Standard English or Hochdeutsch — are the product of both description and deliberate codification.

Debate

The tension between the two approaches is a recurring theme in public discussion of language. Critics of prescriptivism charge that its rules often encode class, regional, or ethnic prejudice and lag behind genuine usage; critics of pure descriptivism respond that dismissing standards altogether abandons writers and learners who need guidance. Most working linguists distinguish between describing language scientifically and the practical, register-specific recommendations of editors and style guides, regarding the two as serving different purposes.
