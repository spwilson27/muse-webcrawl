Ba'athism (from Arabic baʿth, "renaissance" or "resurrection") is an Arab nationalist ideology that promotes the development and creation of a unified Arab state through the leadership of a vanguard party over a socialist government. It was founded by Syrian intellectuals Michel Aflaq, a Christian, and Salah al-Din al-Bitar, a Sunni Muslim, in Damascus in the 1940s, and is summarised by the slogan "Unity, Freedom, Socialism".

History

The Arab Ba'ath Party was formally founded at a congress in Damascus in April 1947 and merged in 1952 with Akram al-Hawrani's Arab Socialist Party to form the Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party. The party came to power through coups in Iraq (1963, and again from 1968) and Syria (1963). A bitter 1966 split divided the movement into rival Syrian- and Iraqi-dominated parties, each claiming legitimacy; Syria's branch, after Hafez al-Assad's 1970 "Corrective Movement", and Iraq's under Saddam Hussein became vehicles for durable authoritarian rule.

Ideology and rule

Ba'athism advocates a single Arab nation, secular government, state-led socialist economics and opposition to Western imperialism. In practice, Ba'athist regimes in Syria and Iraq combined genuine development and secular modernisation with pervasive security services, personality cults and severe repression. Iraq's Ba'ath Party was dismantled after the 2003 US-led invasion under "de-Ba'athification", widely criticised for fuelling insurgency. Syria's Ba'ath-led government under Bashar al-Assad fell in December 2024, ending more than six decades of Ba'athist rule there.
