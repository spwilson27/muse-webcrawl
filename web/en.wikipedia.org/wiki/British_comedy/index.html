British comedy

British comedy is known internationally for its distinctive traditions of satire, irony, wordplay, surrealism and self-deprecation. Rooted in medieval farce, music hall and the works of writers from Shakespeare and Dickens to P. G. Wodehouse, British humour developed through radio programmes such as The Goon Show into the television sketch comedies of Monty Python's Flying Circus and situation comedies such as Fawlty Towers, Blackadder, Only Fools and Horses and The Office.

History

Victorian and Edwardian music hall produced stars such as Dan Leno and later the variety comedians who moved into film and radio. BBC radio comedy of the 1940s and 1950s — It's That Man Again, Hancock's Half Hour and The Goon Show — reshaped popular humour. The 1960s "satire boom", centred on the revue Beyond the Fringe, Private Eye magazine and television's That Was the Week That Was, mocked the establishment and fed directly into Monty Python.

Forms and themes

British comedy encompasses the sitcom (from Steptoe and Son and Dad's Army to Peep Show and Fleabag), sketch shows (Not the Nine O'Clock News, The Fast Show, French and Saunders), panel games (Have I Got News for You, QI), stand-up (from Max Miller to the alternative comedy generation of the 1980s, including Rik Mayall, Ben Elton and later performers such as Victoria Wood, Eddie Izzard and Ricky Gervais), and comedy film, from the Ealing comedies through the Carry On series to the films of Edgar Wright. Recurring themes include class, embarrassment, understatement, the absurd, and a fascination with failure — the comic anti-hero exemplified by Basil Fawlty and David Brent.

Influence

British comedy series are widely exported and remade abroad; The Office, created by Ricky Gervais and Stephen Merchant, was adapted in numerous countries, and Monty Python's influence on world comedy is pervasive. Edinburgh Festival Fringe, the largest arts festival in the world, remains the principal showcase for new British comic talent.
