The Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) is a refundable tax credit in the United States for low- to moderate-income working individuals and families, particularly those with children. Because it is refundable, the credit can reduce a filer's tax liability below zero and generate a payment. The EITC is one of the largest anti-poverty programmes in the United States, lifting millions of people — roughly half of them children — above the poverty line each year.

Structure

The credit rises with earned income at a phase-in rate (which varies with the number of qualifying children), reaches a maximum, plateaus, and then phases out as income increases further. Credit amounts are much larger for families with children: for tax year 2024 the maximum ranged from about $632 for a worker with no qualifying children to about $7,830 for a family with three or more children. Workers without children qualify only for a small credit and must generally be between 25 and 65 years old. Many states offer their own supplementary EITCs calculated as a percentage of the federal credit.

History

The EITC was enacted in the Tax Reduction Act of 1975, championed by Senator Russell Long as a "work bonus" alternative to welfare expansion. It was made permanent in 1978 and substantially expanded in 1986, 1990 and 1993 under Presidents Reagan, George H. W. Bush and Clinton; the 1993 expansion under Clinton made it a centrepiece of welfare-to-work policy. The American Rescue Plan of 2021 temporarily tripled the credit for childless workers.

Effects and criticism

Economists widely find that the EITC encourages labour-force participation, especially among single mothers, and reduces poverty. Criticisms focus on its complexity, which produces high rates of erroneous claims (the IRS has estimated improper payment rates of roughly a quarter to a third of claims), its marriage penalties in some income ranges, and the fact that eligible workers who do not file taxes miss the benefit.
