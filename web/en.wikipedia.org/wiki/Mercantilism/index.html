Mercantilism

[Image: a 17th-century Dutch East India Company ship loading cargo at Amsterdam]

Mercantilism is a body of economic doctrine and policy dominant in Europe from the 16th to the 18th centuries, holding that national wealth and power are best served by accumulating precious metals through a favourable balance of trade — exporting more than is imported — and by state intervention to that end.

Doctrine and decline

Mercantilist policies included tariffs and quotas on manufactured imports, subsidies and monopolies for exporters (the chartered East India Companies), Navigation Acts reserving colonial trade to national ships, and colonies valued as sources of raw materials and captive markets. Writers such as Thomas Mun defended the system as statecraft in a zero-sum world. David Hume's specie-flow mechanism (1752) showed bullion accumulation self-correcting through prices, and Adam Smith devoted Book IV of The Wealth of Nations (1776) to demolishing the 'mercantile system' in favour of Free trade and consumption as wealth's true end. The label itself came later; historians caution that 'mercantilism' covers varied national practices. Its instincts recur in modern export-led strategies and 'neo-mercantilist' critiques of persistent surplus countries.
