Programme music

Programme music (American English: program music) is a type of instrumental art music that attempts to musically render an extra-musical narrative. The narrative may be supplied by a poem, story, painting or scene described in a programme (program notes) given to the audience, or indicated by a descriptive title. It stands in contrast to absolute music, which is intended to be appreciated without reference to anything outside the music itself.

History

Descriptive elements appear in music from the Renaissance and Baroque eras — for example Vivaldi's The Four Seasons, accompanied by explanatory sonnets — but programme music became a central aesthetic question in the 19th century. Beethoven's Pastoral Symphony (1808), subtitled "more the expression of feeling than painting," is an early landmark. Hector Berlioz's Symphonie fantastique (1830) provided audiences with a detailed written programme describing an opium dream.

The symphonic poem

Franz Liszt coined the term symphonic poem for his one-movement orchestral works on literary subjects, and the form was taken up by composers including Bedřich Smetana (Má vlast), Camille Saint-Saëns, and above all Richard Strauss, whose tone poems such as Don Juan, Till Eulenspiegel and Also sprach Zarathustra are among the most virtuosic examples. In Russia, the nationalist composers of The Five produced programmatic works such as Mussorgsky's Night on Bald Mountain and Pictures at an Exhibition, and Rimsky-Korsakov's Scheherazade.

Debate

The concept provoked the so-called "War of the Romantics": critics such as Eduard Hanslick championed absolute music (epitomized by Brahms), while the New German School of Liszt and Wagner defended programmatic and dramatic content. In the 20th century, programme music's legacy continued in film scores and in works such as Paul Hindemith's and Sergei Prokofiev's Peter and the Wolf (1936), a narrated orchestral fairy tale.
