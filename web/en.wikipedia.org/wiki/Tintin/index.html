Tintin is the title character and protagonist of The Adventures of Tintin, the comics series by Belgian cartoonist Hergé (Georges Remi, 1907–1983). Tintin is a young Belgian reporter — instantly recognisable by his quiff of hair and plus-four trousers — who, with his fox terrier Snowy (Milou in French), travels the world investigating mysteries and thwarting villains. The series is one of the most famous works of the Franco-Belgian bande dessinée tradition, with more than 200 million copies sold and translations into over 70 languages.

The Adventures of Tintin

Tintin debuted on 10 January 1929 in Le Petit Vingtième, the children's supplement of the Belgian newspaper Le Vingtième Siècle, with Tintin in the Land of the Soviets. Hergé developed his celebrated ligne claire ("clear line") style: uniform line weight, meticulous researched backgrounds, and flat colours. From 1946 the stories appeared in the weekly Tintin magazine and in colour albums published by Casterman. The 24 albums include The Secret of the Unicorn and Red Rackham's Treasure, The Seven Crystal Balls and Prisoners of the Sun, the moon diptych Destination Moon and Explorers on the Moon (1953–54, which anticipated the Apollo landings with remarkable technical care), The Calculus Affair, Tintin in Tibet (1960 — often considered Hergé's masterpiece) and The Castafiore Emerald. The final album, Tintin and the Picaros, appeared in 1976; Tintin and Alph-Art was left unfinished at Hergé's death in 1983.

Characters

  • Snowy (Milou) — Tintin's loyal wire fox terrier, whose thought balloons comment wryly on the action in early albums.
  • Captain Archibald Haddock — the bearded, whisky-loving sea captain of the Karaboudjan, introduced in The Crab with the Golden Claws (1941), famous for his inventive curses ("Billions of blue blistering barnacles!").
  • Professor Cuthbert Calculus (Tryphon Tournesol) — the hard-of-hearing inventor of the moon rocket and the shark-proof submarine.
  • Thomson and Thompson (Dupond et Dupont) — the identical bowler-hatted detectives.
  • Bianca Castafiore — the "Milanese Nightingale", opera diva.

Legacy and controversies

Tintin shaped generations of European readers and cartoonists; Hergé's studio system and documentary rigour influenced the whole bande dessinée field. The Musée Hergé opened in Louvain-la-Neuve in 2009, and Steven Spielberg's motion-capture film The Adventures of Tintin appeared in 2011. Early albums have attracted sustained criticism: Tintin in the Congo (1931) reflects colonial and racist attitudes of its era (Hergé later revised and regretted aspects of it), and Tintin in the Land of the Soviets is crude anti-communist propaganda — both now published with contextual warnings. Nevertheless the mature albums are widely regarded as among the greatest achievements in comics.
