Trigeneration

Trigeneration, also called combined cooling, heat and power (CCHP), is the simultaneous production of electricity, useful heat, and cooling from a single fuel source. It extends cogeneration (CHP) by using part of the recovered heat to drive an absorption chiller, which produces chilled water for air conditioning or process cooling.

How it works

A prime mover — commonly a gas engine, gas turbine, or microturbine — generates electricity. Waste heat from exhaust gases and cooling circuits is recovered in heat exchangers. In heating season the heat serves buildings directly; in cooling season it powers a lithium-bromide absorption chiller. Overall fuel utilisation can reach 80–90 percent for well-matched loads, compared with roughly 50 percent for electricity alone from a conventional plant.

Applications

Trigeneration suits sites with simultaneous, year-round demands for power, heat, and cooling: hospitals, airports, universities, data centres, hotels, shopping centres, and district energy schemes. Benefits include lower energy costs and emissions and improved resilience, since the plant can island from the grid. Drawbacks include high capital cost, complexity, and poor economics where cooling demand is seasonal or heat cannot be used.
