Magnetophon

Magnetophon was the brand or model name of the first practical magnetic tape recorders, developed in Germany in the 1930s by AEG in collaboration with IG Farben (BASF). The name became a generic term for tape recorders in several European languages.

Development

The Magnetophon built on Fritz Pfleumer's 1928 patent for magnetic tape — a paper (later plastic) strip coated with iron oxide powder. The first public demonstration came at the 1935 Berlin Radio Fair with the Magnetophon K1, using BASF tape on 6.5 mm reels. Early machines suffered from poor sound quality until the serendipitous discovery, around 1940, that adding a high-frequency AC bias signal dramatically improved fidelity — a breakthrough associated with engineers Walter Weber and Hans Joachim von Braunmühl.

Wartime and postwar influence

German radio used Magnetophons extensively during the Second World War to broadcast time-shifted programming of startlingly lifelike quality, puzzling Allied monitors. After the war, American audio engineer Jack Mullin shipped two captured machines and reels of tape back to the United States; demonstrated to Bing Crosby, the technology became the basis of Ampex's Model 200 and the modern recording industry. Crosby invested in Ampex and used tape to prerecord his radio shows, making him one of the first American performers to master the medium.

Legacy

The Magnetophon established the reel-to-reel architecture, standard tape speeds, and editing by physically cutting and splicing tape — techniques that defined studio practice for half a century.
