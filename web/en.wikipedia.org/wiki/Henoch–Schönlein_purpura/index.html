Henoch–Schönlein purpura

SpecialtyRheumatology, paediatrics
Also known asIgA vasculitis
Usual onsetChildhood (ages 3–15)
Named afterEduard Heinrich Henoch; Johann Lukas Schönlein

Henoch–Schönlein purpura (HSP), now usually termed IgA vasculitis, is a disease of the skin and other organs arising from inflammation of small blood vessels with deposition of immunoglobulin A (IgA). It most often affects children and is the commonest systemic vasculitis of childhood. The classic tetrad is a palpable purpuric rash (especially on the legs and buttocks), joint pain, abdominal pain and kidney involvement.

Signs and symptoms

The rash begins as urticaria-like spots that evolve into palpable purpura — raised purple-red lesions that do not blanch — typically over the lower limbs and buttocks. Arthritis or arthralgia affects mainly the knees and ankles. Gastrointestinal symptoms include colicky abdominal pain, vomiting and occasionally bleeding or intussusception. Renal involvement (IgA vasculitis nephritis) ranges from microscopic haematuria and mild proteinuria to, rarely, nephrotic syndrome or progressive kidney failure; it is more frequent and more severe in older children and adults.

Cause and diagnosis

The disease often follows an upper respiratory tract infection. Abnormally glycosylated IgA1 forms immune complexes that deposit in small vessels, triggering inflammation. Diagnosis is usually clinical, supported by skin or kidney biopsy showing IgA deposition in vessel walls. Platelet counts are normal, distinguishing the purpura from thrombocytopenic causes of bleeding.

Treatment and prognosis

Most cases resolve spontaneously within a few weeks with supportive care, rest and analgesia. Corticosteroids may relieve joint and abdominal symptoms but do not clearly prevent kidney disease; patients with significant renal involvement require longer follow-up and sometimes immunosuppression. The long-term prognosis is generally good, though a minority develop chronic kidney disease, and recurrence occurs in about a third of children. The condition takes its name from the German physicians Eduard Heinrich Henoch and his teacher Johann Lukas Schönlein, who described its features in the 19th century.
