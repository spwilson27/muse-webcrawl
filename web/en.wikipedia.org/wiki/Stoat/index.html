The stoat (Mustela erminea), also known as the Eurasian ermine or ermine, is a mustelid native to Eurasia and the northern portions of North America. Because of its wide circumpolar distribution, it is listed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. The name ermine is used for the species in its pure white winter coat, and for the fur itself, historically prized and used in ceremonial robes.

The stoat was introduced into New Zealand in the late 19th century to control rabbits and hares, but is now a major threat to the country's native bird populations. It is similar in appearance to the least weasel, but is larger, and its tail always has a black tip, even in the white winter phase.

Description

The stoat has a long, slender body typically 17–32 cm in length, with a tail of 7.5–12.5 cm. Males are substantially larger than females. In summer the coat is sandy brown above and cream below; in winter, in northern parts of its range, the coat turns entirely white except for the black tail tip. Stoats are skillful climbers, swimmers and diggers, and feed mainly on rodents, rabbits, birds, eggs and insects. They are capable of killing prey much larger than themselves, such as adult rabbits.

Behaviour and reproduction

Stoats are largely solitary and territorial. Females produce a single litter of kits each year; mating occurs in summer, but implantation is delayed for around nine to ten months, so the young are born the following spring — a phenomenon known as embryonic diapause. Litters typically number six to twelve kits, which are born blind and helpless in a nest lined with fur.

Distribution and habitat

The stoat has a circumpolar distribution throughout North America, Europe and Asia, inhabiting tundra, woodland, farmland, marshland and mountains up to about 3,000 m. It is particularly associated with habitats supporting abundant small mammals.
