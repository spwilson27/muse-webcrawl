Rotational molding

Rotational molding, also called rotomolding or rotacasting, is a manufacturing process for producing hollow plastic parts. A measured charge of powdered (or liquid) polymer is placed inside a hollow mold, which is heated in an oven while rotating slowly about two perpendicular axes. The softened plastic coats the inside of the mold evenly; the mold is then cooled, still rotating, and opened to release the finished part.

Process

The process has four stages: loading the mold with resin powder (most often polyethylene); heating, in which the mold rotates biaxially in an oven at around 300 °C until the powder melts and adheres to the walls; cooling, by air or water spray while rotation continues so the part holds its shape; and unloading, followed by trimming and any secondary assembly. Cycle times are long compared with injection molding — often 20 minutes or more — but the tooling is inexpensive because the molds need not withstand high pressure.

Advantages and limitations

Rotomolding produces seamless, stress-free parts with uniform wall thickness and strong corners. Very large hollow objects can be made in one piece — tanks of tens of thousands of litres — and molds can incorporate colour changes, foam-filled double walls, and molded-in inserts or graphics. Limitations include slow cycle times, a restricted range of suitable materials (polyethylene accounts for the great majority of use), and looser dimensional tolerances than pressure-based processes.

Applications

Typical products include water and chemical storage tanks, kayaks and canoes, playground equipment, road barriers and traffic cones, fuel tanks, cool boxes, military cases, and the bodies of many ride-on toys. The process was first documented in Britain in the nineteenth century for hollow articles, and commercialised for plastics in the 1950s, initially for PVC plastisol dolls' heads.
