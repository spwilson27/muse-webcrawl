Drug tolerance
FieldPharmacology
DefinitionReduced response to a drug after repeated use

Tolerance shifts the dose–response curve to the right: a larger dose is needed for the same effect.

Drug tolerance is a pharmacological concept describing subjects' reduced reaction to a drug following its repeated use. Increasing its dosage may re-amplify the drug's effects; however, this may accelerate tolerance, further reducing the drug's effects. Drug tolerance is indicative of drug use but is not necessarily associated with drug dependence or addiction. The process of tolerance development is reversible (e.g., through a drug holiday) and can involve both physiological factors and psychological factors.

One may also develop drug tolerance to side effects, in which case tolerance is a desirable characteristic. A medical intervention that has an objective to increase tolerance (e.g., allergen immunotherapy or drug desensitisation) is called drug desensitisation.

Types

Pharmacokinetic tolerance

Pharmacokinetic (metabolic) tolerance occurs when the body becomes more efficient at eliminating a drug — for instance through induction of metabolising enzymes such as the cytochrome P450 system — so a lower concentration reaches the site of action. Barbiturates and alcohol are classic examples of drugs that induce their own metabolism.

Pharmacodynamic tolerance

Pharmacodynamic tolerance arises from adaptive changes at the cellular level: downregulation or internalisation of receptors, reduced receptor sensitivity, or compensatory changes in signalling pathways. Opioid tolerance is largely of this kind.

Behavioural and conditioned tolerance

Behavioural tolerance refers to learned compensation for a drug's effects. Conditioned tolerance, studied by Shepard Siegel, develops when drug-predictive cues trigger compensatory responses; taking the drug in a novel environment can therefore increase the risk of overdose.

Tachyphylaxis

Tachyphylaxis is a sudden, short-term onset of tolerance, arising after very few doses — typically due to depletion of a neurotransmitter or mediator — as seen with indirectly acting sympathomimetics such as ephedrine.

Cross-tolerance

Cross-tolerance occurs when tolerance to one drug confers tolerance to another, usually one with a similar mechanism of action — for example, between alcohol and benzodiazepines.
