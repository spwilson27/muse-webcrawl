Podolia

Podolia (Ukrainian: Поділля, Podillia; Polish: Podole; Russian: Подолье, Podolye) is a historic region in east-central Europe, in the west-central and southwestern parts of modern Ukraine and the northeastern part of Moldova (Transnistria). Its name means "the lowland" or "land along the valley" (po dolu, along the low ground). The region lies on the Podolian Upland between the Dniester and Southern Bug rivers, and its best-known cities are Kamianets-Podilskyi, Vinnytsia, and Khmelnytskyi.

Geography

Podolia occupies a fertile plateau of black earth (chernozem) dissected by deep river valleys, notably the canyon of the Dniester and the gorge of the Smotrych at Kamianets. The region's rich soil made it a historic breadbasket and also a coveted frontier corridor between Poland-Lithuania, the Ottoman Empire, the Crimean Khanate, and Russia.

History

In the Middle Ages the territory passed from Kievan Rus' to the Principality of Galicia-Volhynia and then, in the fourteenth century, to the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and the Kingdom of Poland. Podolia formed a palatinate (voivodeship) of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, defended by great border fortresses such as Kamianets-Podilskyi and Bar against Tatar raids. From 1672 to 1699 western Podolia was an Ottoman eyalet, before returning to Poland at the Treaty of Karlowitz.

In the Second Partition of Poland (1793) most of Podolia passed to the Russian Empire, where it became the Podolia Governorate; the western fringe went to Austria. The region saw heavy fighting in the Polish-Soviet war and the Russian Civil War, and in 1922 it became part of the Ukrainian SSR. During the Second World War, northern Transnistria and Podolia were occupied by Romania and Germany; Jewish communities that had lived in Podolian towns for centuries — the region is closely associated with the origins of Hasidism, including the Baal Shem Tov at Medzhybizh — were destroyed in the Holocaust.

Culture

Podolia is noted for its embroidered textiles, painted Easter eggs (pysanky), folk architecture, and the celebrated Podolian wedding and harvest customs. Its cultural landscape reflects centuries of interaction among Ukrainians, Poles, Jews, Armenians, and other peoples in the borderland towns of the region.
