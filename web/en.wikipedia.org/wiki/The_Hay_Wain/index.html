The Hay Wain — originally titled Landscape: Noon — is a painting by John Constable, finished in 1821, which depicts a rural scene on the River Stour between the English counties of Suffolk and Essex. It hangs in the National Gallery in London and is regarded as "Constable's most famous image" and one of the greatest British paintings.

Description

The oil-on-canvas work depicts a hay cart (a "wain") crossing a shallow ford, drawn by three horses, beside Flatford Mill and the cottage of the tenant farmer Willy Lott on the Suffolk bank. A dog watches from the bank, a man fishes nearby, and figures load hay in the meadows beyond. The scene is near Constable's birthplace at East Bergholt, in the landscape now marketed as "Constable Country". Constable prepared the composition with a full-size oil sketch, now in the Victoria and Albert Museum.

History

One of Constable's series of large "six-footer" canvases, the painting was exhibited at the Royal Academy in 1821 but failed to find a buyer in England. At the Paris Salon of 1824, however, it caused a sensation: it was admired by Eugène Delacroix, who is said to have reworked the background of his Massacre at Scio in response, and was awarded a gold medal by Charles X. Sold to the dealer John Arrowsmith and later owned by Henry Vaughan, it was presented to the National Gallery in 1886.

Legacy

The painting is an icon of the English landscape tradition and among the most reproduced of British artworks. The cottage, mill and river crossing survive much as painted, at Flatford, maintained by the National Trust. In 2005 a poll by BBC Radio 4's Today programme voted The Hay Wain the second most popular painting in a British gallery, after Turner's The Fighting Temeraire. In 2013 a protester glued a photograph to the painting's protective glass at the National Gallery, causing no lasting damage.
