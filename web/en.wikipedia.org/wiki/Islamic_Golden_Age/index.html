Islamic Golden Age

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: An astrolabe of brass, of the kind perfected by Islamic astronomers]

The Islamic Golden Age was a period of scientific, economic and cultural flourishing in the Islamic world, traditionally dated from the 8th century to the 13th or 14th century. Centred on Baghdad under the Abbasids and extending from al-Andalus to Central Asia, it saw major advances in mathematics (algebra, trigonometry, algorithms — all words of Arabic origin), astronomy, optics, medicine, chemistry, geography and philosophy, and the preservation and extension of Greek, Persian and Indian knowledge through a vast translation movement. Its scholars — al-Khwarizmi, Ibn al-Haytham, al-Razi, Ibn Sina, Averroes and many others — shaped learning in three continents.

Translation and science

Caliphs and patrons funded the translation of Greek science and philosophy into Arabic, chiefly in 9th-century Baghdad. On that foundation Islamic scholars innovated: al-Khwarizmi's algebra and the adoption of Indian numerals (including zero) transformed mathematics; Ibn al-Haytham's Book of Optics founded experimental optics; observatories from Maragha to Samarkand refined astronomical tables; Ibn Sina's Canon of Medicine remained a standard textbook in Europe into the 17th century. Paper-making from China, universities and hospitals (bimaristans), and agricultural diffusion of crops such as citrus, sugar and cotton underpinned the era.

End and legacy

There is no agreed end-date: candidates include the Mongol sack of Baghdad (1258), the completion of the Christian Reconquista in Spain (1492), or a long decline as economic and political centres shifted. Modern historians question the simple 'golden age/decline' arc, noting continued creativity in Ottoman, Safavid and Mughal lands. The era's translations into Latin from the 12th century fed directly into Europe's own scientific awakening.
