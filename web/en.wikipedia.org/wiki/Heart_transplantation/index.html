Heart transplantation

Heart transplantation is a surgical transplant procedure performed on patients with end-stage heart failure or severe coronary artery disease when other medical or surgical treatments have proven insufficient. The patient's diseased heart is removed (orthotopic procedure) and replaced with a healthy heart from a deceased organ donor. It remains the gold-standard treatment for advanced heart failure refractory to other therapy.

History

After years of animal research by surgeons including Norman Shumway at Stanford, the first human-to-human heart transplant was performed on 3 December 1967 by Christiaan Barnard at Groote Schuur Hospital, Cape Town, South Africa; recipient Louis Washkansky survived 18 days. Early results were poor, but the introduction of ciclosporin immunosuppression in the early 1980s transformed survival and made transplantation routine at centres worldwide.

Procedure and aftercare

Donor hearts are matched by blood group and size and transported cold (increasingly also by normothermic machine perfusion). The recipient's heart is excised on cardiopulmonary bypass and the donor organ implanted, usually by the bicaval technique. Recipients require lifelong immunosuppression (typically a calcineurin inhibitor, an antiproliferative agent and tapering steroids) and surveillance biopsies or gene-expression tests for rejection. Complications include acute rejection, infection, cardiac allograft vasculopathy (the leading late cause of graft loss), malignancy and drug side-effects.

Outcomes

Contemporary median survival exceeds 12–14 years in adults, with one-year survival around 90%. Donor shortage is the main limit on activity — roughly 5,000–6,000 heart transplants are performed globally each year — motivating mechanical circulatory support (LVADs) as bridge or destination therapy, expanded donor criteria, donation after circulatory death, and research into xenotransplantation: in January 2022 a genetically modified pig heart was transplanted into David Bennett, who survived two months.
