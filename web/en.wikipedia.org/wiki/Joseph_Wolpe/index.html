Joseph Wolpe

Joseph Wolpe (20 April 1915 - 4 December 1997) was a South African psychiatrist and a founding figure of behavior therapy. He developed systematic desensitization, a widely used treatment for phobias and anxiety, based on the principle of reciprocal inhibition.

Life and career

Wolpe studied medicine at the University of the Witwatersrand in Johannesburg. Serving as an army psychiatrist during the Second World War, he treated soldiers with "war neurosis" and became dissatisfied with the results of psychoanalytic methods. In the late 1940s he conducted experiments with cats in which neurotic anxiety induced by shock was eliminated through feeding in progressively more similar environments, drawing on the concept of reciprocal inhibition.

Systematic desensitization

Wolpe reasoned that if a response incompatible with anxiety - such as deep muscle relaxation - could be made to occur in the presence of anxiety-provoking stimuli, the anxiety would weaken. His method has three steps: training in relaxation, constructing a hierarchy of feared situations from least to most disturbing, and gradually pairing relaxation with imagined (or real) exposure to each item in the hierarchy. His 1958 book Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition launched the technique and helped found modern behavior therapy.

Later work

In 1965 Wolpe emigrated to the United States, working at the University of Virginia and then at Temple University in Philadelphia, where he founded a behavior therapy unit and the journal Behaviour Research and Therapy. He also developed assertiveness training and the Subjective Units of Disturbance (SUD) scale. He spent his final years in Los Angeles and died in 1997.
