Pancreatic islets

The pancreatic islets, also called islets of Langerhans, are the regions of the pancreas that contain its endocrine (hormone-producing) cells, discovered in 1869 by German pathological anatomist Paul Langerhans. The pancreatic islets constitute roughly 1–2% of the mass of the pancreas, but receive a disproportionately large share of its blood flow. There are about one million islets in a healthy adult human pancreas, each on average about 0.2 mm in diameter, separated from the surrounding exocrine tissue by a thin fibrous capsule.

Cell types

Islets contain several types of hormone-secreting cells:

In rodent islets, beta cells form a central core surrounded by a mantle of other cells; human islets have a more intermingled architecture. The hormones act in a paracrine fashion within the islet as well as entering the circulation: insulin lowers blood glucose, glucagon raises it, and somatostatin restrains both.

Function

The islets are the body's glucose sensor and regulator. Beta cells release insulin in response to rising blood glucose (and incretin signals from the gut), while alpha cells secrete glucagon when glucose falls. Their coordinated activity keeps blood sugar within a narrow range; blood flows through the islets before draining via the portal vein to the liver, the principal target of insulin and glucagon.

Clinical significance

Autoimmune destruction of beta cells causes type 1 diabetes, while beta-cell dysfunction combined with insulin resistance underlies type 2 diabetes. Tumours of islet cells (pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours, such as insulinomas and glucagonomas) cause syndromes of hormone excess. Islet transplantation — infusing isolated donor islets into the portal vein, pioneered clinically with the Edmonton protocol (2000) — can restore insulin independence in some people with type 1 diabetes, and stem-cell-derived islet products have become an active area of therapy.
