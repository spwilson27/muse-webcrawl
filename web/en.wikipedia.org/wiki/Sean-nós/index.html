Sean-nós

Sean-nós singing, the unaccompanied old style
Meaning"Old style" (Irish: sean nós)
Applies toSinging, dancing, and other Irish traditional arts
RegionsConnemara, Donegal, Munster Gaeltacht areas

Sean-nós (Irish for "old style", pronounced [ʃan̪ˠ n̪ˠoːsˠ]) is an Irish term for the highly ornamented traditional arts performed in the old style, most often applied to unaccompanied traditional Irish singing and to traditional solo step dancing. Sean-nós singing is sung solo, usually without instrumental accompaniment, in a free rhythm, with elaborate melodic ornamentation such as melisma, mordents and long glottal effects, and is traditionally performed in the Irish language, though English-language songs are also sung in the style.

The repertoire includes love songs, laments, historical and political ballads, lullabies and humorous songs, many of them centuries old. Distinct regional styles are recognised, most notably those of Connemara, Donegal and Munster, differing in their use of ornament and nasality. Singers such as Seosamh Ó hÉanaí (Joe Heaney) of Carna became celebrated exponents, and the style is a centrepiece of competitions at the Oireachtas na Gaeilge and of festivals such as the Sean-Nós Festival in Connemara.

Characteristics

Sean-nós singing places the singer's personal interpretation at its centre: the tempo is dictated by the words rather than a fixed metre, verses may end with a characteristic descending cadential formula, and the audience traditionally responds with exclamations of appreciation. Listeners customarily sit close to the singer, sometimes holding the singer's hand or resting a hand on their shoulder, following the older custom of communal listening in the céilí house. Sean-nós dance, by contrast with the formalised competition stepdancing of the dancing schools, is improvisational, low to the ground, with a relaxed upper body and close, percussive footwork.

History

The style preserves singing practices that predate the spread of printed music and accompanied song in Ireland, transmitted orally in Gaeltacht communities. Recorded from the early 20th century, it gained wider recognition through the Gaelic revival, RTÉ broadcasts, and the work of collectors and singers who carried it to international folk audiences. It remains a living tradition taught at workshops and summer schools throughout Ireland.
