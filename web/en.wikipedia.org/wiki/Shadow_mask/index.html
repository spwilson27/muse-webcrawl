Shadow mask

Shadow mask
Phosphor dots seen through a shadow mask
TypeComponent of colour cathode-ray tube displays
InventedLate 1940s–early 1950s (RCA)

A shadow mask is a metal plate with tiny holes punched through it, placed just behind the screen of a colour cathode-ray tube (CRT) television or computer monitor. The mask ensures that each of the tube's three electron beams strikes only phosphor dots of its own colour — red, green, or blue — so that a correct colour image is formed.

Operation

Three electron guns, angled slightly differently, fire beams through each hole in the mask. Because of the geometry, the beam from the "red" gun can pass through a hole only onto the red phosphor dot behind it, and likewise for green and blue. The spacing of the holes, measured as dot pitch, largely determines the display's resolution; finer pitch gives sharper images. Masks were typically made of Invar, a low-expansion alloy, to prevent distortion ("doming") as the mask heats.

History and alternatives

The shadow mask was developed by RCA and became the standard technology for colour television from the 1950s until CRTs were displaced by flat panels in the 2000s. Sony's Trinitron tubes used an alternative aperture grille of vertical wires and a phosphor pattern of stripes, offering brighter images. Other variants, such as slot masks, combined features of both approaches.
