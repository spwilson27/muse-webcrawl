Sheepdog trial

A sheepdog trial (also called a herding trial or dog trial) is a competitive dog sport in which herding dogs, most often Border Collies, move sheep around a field, through fences, gates and into enclosures under the direction of their handlers. The sport tests the working abilities that shepherds rely on daily: distance control, stock sense and precision.

The classic course

In a standard trial the elements are the outrun, in which the dog runs in a wide arc to approach the sheep from behind without startling them; the lift, the initial contact; the fetch, bringing the sheep straight to the handler; the drive, pushing them away through gates on a triangular course; shedding, separating specified sheep from the flock; and the pen, guiding the sheep into a small enclosure. Some finals add the "international shed" and a double lift. Points are deducted for faults such as crossing the course, excessive commands, bites or missed gates, and runs are timed.

Commands and history

Handlers direct dogs mainly by whistle and by traditional voice commands — "come-bye" (clockwise), "away" (counter-clockwise), "lie down", "steady" and "that'll do". The first recorded sheepdog trial took place at Bala, Wales, in 1873. The International Sheep Dog Society, founded in 1906, organises the prestigious International and World trials; the sport is also popular in North America, Australia and New Zealand, where huntaway dogs compete in distinct events.
