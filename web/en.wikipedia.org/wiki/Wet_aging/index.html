Wet aging

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Wet aging is a method of aging beef in which the meat is vacuum-sealed in plastic shortly after slaughter and allowed to age in refrigeration in its own juices. It is the dominant method of aging used in the United States and much of the world today.

Process

Primal cuts are sealed in vacuum bags within days of processing and refrigerated for typically seven to twenty-eight days. During this time, naturally occurring enzymes break down muscle fibres, making the meat more tender. Because the meat never loses moisture to the air, there is almost no weight loss, and no hard outer crust must be trimmed away as with dry aging.

Development and flavour

Wet aging became widespread from the 1960s and 1970s with improvements in vacuum packaging and refrigerated distribution, which let meat age in transit rather than requiring butchers to hold it on site. The process produces a somewhat different flavour from dry-aged beef: typically described as more metallic, tangy or "bloody", and lacking the nutty, funky concentration that evaporation and oxidation give dry-aged meat. Most steak sold in supermarkets is wet aged.
