Tropical cyclone naming

Tropical cyclones are named by official warning centres to provide ease of communication between forecasters and the general public, especially when two or more storms are active simultaneously. Names are drawn from predetermined lists maintained under the oversight of the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), with separate lists for each ocean basin.

History

Before formal naming conventions, storms were identified by latitude and longitude, by saints' days in the Spanish colonial Caribbean, or by memorable events. The Australian meteorologist Clement Wragge pioneered personal naming in the 1890s, using letters, mythological figures and eventually the names of politicians he disliked. During the Second World War, American forecasters in the Pacific informally named storms after women, a practice adopted officially in the 1950s. Male names were added to Atlantic lists in 1979 after criticism of sexism, and lists have since alternated male and female names.

Modern practice

In the Atlantic and eastern North Pacific, six rotating lists of 21 names are reused on a six-year cycle (letters Q, U, X, Y and Z are skipped in the Atlantic). Cyclones are named when they reach tropical storm strength, with winds of at least 39 mph (63 km/h). The western North Pacific uses names contributed by 14 nations and territories, many of them words for flowers, animals or local terms rather than personal names. Other basins, including the North Indian Ocean and the Southern Hemisphere regions, maintain their own lists through WMO regional bodies.

Retirement

Names of storms that cause exceptional death or destruction are retired by the WMO at the request of affected member states and are never reused for the same basin. Retired Atlantic names include Katrina (2005), Sandy (2012), Irma and Maria (2017), and Ian (2022). If a list is exhausted, as happened in 2005 and 2020, supplemental names are now drawn from a standby list rather than the Greek alphabet, which was discontinued because of confusion and retirement difficulties.
