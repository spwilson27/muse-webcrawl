Human geography is the branch of geography that studies the spatial organisation of human activity and the relationships between people, place, and environment. Together with physical geography, it forms the two main branches of the discipline. Human geographers examine how cultures, economies, political systems, and settlements are distributed across the Earth's surface and how they change over time.

The distribution of human population is a core subject of human geography.

Branches

Major subfields include population geography (migration, demography, settlement), urban geography (the internal structure and growth of cities), economic geography (industry, agriculture, trade, development), political geography (states, borders, geopolitics), cultural geography (language, religion, landscape and identity), and health and transport geography.

History

Nineteenth-century geographers such as Friedrich Ratzel developed theories of environmental determinism, while the French school around Paul Vidal de la Blache emphasised possibilism — the idea that environments set limits but cultures choose among possibilities. Carl Sauer and the Berkeley School stressed cultural landscapes. The mid-twentieth-century "quantitative revolution" brought statistical models and spatial science, followed in the 1970s by humanistic, Marxist, feminist, and postmodern critiques associated with figures such as Yi-Fu Tuan, David Harvey, and Doreen Massey.

Approaches

Contemporary human geography uses methods ranging from GIS and spatial statistics to ethnography and archival research. Core concepts include place, scale, region, diffusion, and globalisation. The field overlaps extensively with sociology, anthropology, economics, and urban planning.
