Benchmark (surveying)

TypeSurvey marker / control point
ProvidesKnown elevation (vertical control), sometimes position
Datum (US)NAVD 88 (vertical), NAD 83 (horizontal)
Maintained by (US)National Geodetic Survey

In surveying and geodesy, a benchmark (or bench mark) is a permanent marker at a point of precisely known elevation (and sometimes horizontal position) above a reference datum, used as a starting point for levelling and height determination in construction, mapping and engineering. Benchmarks are typically brass or aluminium disks set into concrete, bedrock or buildings, stamped with identifying information, or — in older British practice — the broad-arrow "cut mark" chiselled into stonework beneath a horizontal line.

Use

A surveyor sets up a level instrument, takes a backsight reading on a graduated staff held on the benchmark, and thereby establishes the height of instrument; all other points surveyed in the run are then determined relative to the benchmark's known elevation. National networks of benchmarks, connected by precise levelling, define a country's vertical datum: in the United States the North American Vertical Datum of 1988 (NAVD 88), and in Britain heights above mean sea level at Newlyn (Ordnance Datum Newlyn), historically recorded by the Ordnance Survey on benchmarks across the country.

Types and terminology

Strictly, "benchmark" refers to vertical control points, while horizontal control points are triangulation stations; however the term is widely used for survey marks generally. Temporary benchmarks are convenient stable objects (a nail, a hydrant, a chiselled cross) assigned an assumed or surveyed elevation for the duration of a project. With satellite geodesy, GNSS observations now provide elevations tied to geoid models, and many physical benchmarks are falling out of use, though they remain important for local construction and flood-risk work. Geocachers and hobbyists hunt surviving benchmarks as a pastime ("benchmarking").
