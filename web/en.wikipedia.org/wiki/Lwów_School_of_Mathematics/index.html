Lwów School of Mathematics

The Lwów School of Mathematics (Polish: Lwowska szkoła matematyczna) was a group of Polish mathematicians who worked in interwar Lwów (now Lviv, Ukraine), centred on the University of Lwów and Lwów Polytechnic. Flourishing in the 1920s and 1930s, it was famed for pioneering work in functional analysis and for its informal working style: long sessions in cafés, above all the Scottish Café (Kawiarnia Szkocka), where problems and solutions were written on marble tables and later in the famous Scottish Book.

Members

The school's central figures were Stefan Banach and Hugo Steinhaus, co-founders of the journal Studia Mathematica (1929). Other members included Stanisław Mazur, Stanisław Ulam, Juliusz Schauder, Władysław Orlicz, Karol Borsuk, Antoni Łomnicki, Stefan Kaczmarz, and Herman Auerbach; Kazimierz Kuratowski and others from the Warsaw school were frequent collaborators.

Contributions

The school created modern functional analysis: Banach spaces, the Banach–Steinhaus theorem, the Hahn–Banach theorem, the Banach fixed-point theorem, the Banach–Tarski paradox (with Alfred Tarski), and fundamental results in operator theory and measure theory. Problems posed in the Scottish Book — some with prizes of wine, coffee, or even a live goose — stimulated research for decades; one problem was solved by Per Enflo in 1972, who collected the goose from Mazur on Polish television.

Destruction

The Second World War destroyed the school. Lwów was occupied by the USSR in 1939 and by Nazi Germany in 1941, when many professors were murdered in the massacre of Lwów professors; others, including Schauder and Auerbach, perished in the Holocaust. Ulam and some others had emigrated to the United States, where Ulam contributed to the Manhattan Project and the Monte Carlo method. After 1945 Lwów became part of Soviet Ukraine and most surviving Polish academics were resettled to Wrocław.
