Carolingian Empire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Map of the Carolingian Empire at Charlemagne's death in 814]

The Carolingian Empire (800–887) was the Frankish realm ruled by the Carolingian dynasty at its greatest extent, when Charlemagne and his immediate successors governed most of western and central Europe — modern France, Germany, the Low Countries, Italy, Austria and parts of Spain and the Balkans. Created by Charlemagne's conquests and his imperial coronation in 800, it revived the idea of a Christian Roman empire in the West. After the death of Louis the Pious (840), civil war among his sons led to the Treaty of Verdun (843), partitioning the empire into West, Middle and East Francia — the ancestors of France, the Low Countries/Italy, and Germany.

Rise

The Carolingians rose as mayors of the palace under the Merovingian kings; Pepin the Short deposed the last Merovingian in 751 with papal blessing. Charlemagne doubled the kingdom's size. The empire's administration — counts, marches on the frontiers, missi dominici, capitularies, and a church harnessed to royal government — held together a realm of perhaps 10–20 million people.

Partition and dissolution

Louis the Pious' attempts to settle the succession provoked repeated rebellion by his sons. Verdun (843) was not meant to be permanent, but further partitions (Prüm 855, Meerssen 870) hardened the divisions. Viking, Magyar and Saracen raids, and the growing independence of regional aristocrats, emptied the imperial title of power; the last Carolingian emperor, Charles the Fat, was deposed in 887, and the imperial idea passed in 962 to the Ottonian kings of Germany.
