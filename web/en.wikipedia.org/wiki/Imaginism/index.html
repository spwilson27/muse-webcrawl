Imaginism

Imaginism was a poetic flow inside Russian avant-garde that was founded in 1918 after the Revolution by a group of poets including Anatoly Marienhof, Vadim Shershenevich, and Sergei Yesenin. They drew inspiration from English and American Imagism. Its name derives from imagisme, the French and English movement led by Ezra Pound.

History

The movement's founding declaration was signed in Moscow in January 1919 by Yesenin, Marienhof, Shershenevich and Rurik Ivnev. The Imaginists proclaimed the supremacy of the image (Russian: obraz) over meaning and idea in poetry, holding that a poem should be a "catalogue of images" linked verbally rather than logically. They published manifestos, ran the Hotel for Travellers in the Beautiful and other bohemian meeting places, and staged scandalous publicity stunts that made them notorious in early Soviet literary life.

Other members and associates included Alexander Kusikov and Ivan Gruzinov. Yesenin, by far the most famous of the group, drifted away from the movement by the mid-1920s. After Yesenin's death in 1925, increasing official criticism of the group's "decadence" led to its decline; the Imaginists formally disbanded in 1927.

Style

Imaginist poetry favoured chains of striking, often deliberately shocking metaphors, free rhythms, and a mixture of elevated and street language. Marienhof's theoretical writings, such as Buyan-Ostrov (1920), set out the doctrine that the image, not the word's dictionary meaning, is the fundamental unit of poetry.
