Hoarding disorder is a mental disorder characterised by a persistent difficulty discarding or parting with possessions, regardless of their actual value, together with excessive accumulation that congests living areas and significantly compromises their intended use. It was recognised as a distinct diagnosis in the DSM-5 in 2013, having previously been considered mainly a symptom or subtype of obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD); it also appears in ICD-11.

Symptoms and diagnosis

The DSM-5 criteria require persistent difficulty discarding possessions; a perceived need to save items and distress at the thought of discarding them; accumulation that clutters living areas (unless others intervene); and clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational or other functioning—including maintaining a safe environment. The specifiers "with excessive acquisition" and graded levels of insight (good, poor, or absent/delusional) may be added. The difficulty is not better explained by another medical or mental condition, such as brain injury, dementia or the avolition of schizophrenia.

Causes and course

Hoarding behaviour often begins in the teens, worsens gradually across the lifespan, and commonly becomes a serious problem by the mid-30s or later. Twin studies suggest a substantial heritable component (around 50 percent), and the disorder is associated with indecisiveness, perfectionism, deficits in information-processing and categorisation, and strong emotional attachment to objects. Traumatic loss and deprivation are frequently reported, though causal pathways are uncertain. Animal hoarding—in which large numbers of animals are kept without adequate care—is regarded as a special manifestation.

Treatment

Standard OCD medication (SSRIs) has limited benefit for hoarding when it occurs without OCD symptoms. The best-supported treatment is cognitive behavioural therapy specifically adapted for hoarding, developed by Steketee and Frost, which combines motivational interviewing, skills training in organising and decision-making, and graded exposure to discarding and non-acquiring. Forced cleanouts without treatment typically cause severe distress and rapid re-accumulation, and clinicians and housing authorities increasingly emphasise harm reduction—restoring safety and sanitation—alongside therapy.

In popular culture

Extreme cases have long attracted attention, notably the Collyer brothers of Harlem, found dead in 1947 amid more than a hundred tons of accumulated material. Television series such as Hoarders brought the disorder to wide attention but have been criticised for sensationalism.
