Italian Libya (Italian: Libia Italiana) was a colony of the Kingdom of Italy in North Africa, formed in 1934 from the colonies of Tripolitania and Cyrenaica, which Italy had seized from the Ottoman Empire in the Italo-Turkish War of 1911–12. Fezzan was incorporated from 1912 onward.

Conquest and resistance

Italian forces occupied the coastal cities in 1911–12, but resistance by Libyan fighters — most famously the Sanusi order in Cyrenaica under Omar Mukhtar — continued for two decades. The Fascist regime's brutal "pacification" of the late 1920s and early 1930s, involving concentration camps, mass deportation and the hanging of Mukhtar in 1931, brought the colony under full control at enormous human cost.

Administration and settlement

Under Governor Italo Balbo (from 1934) the colony was unified as Libya, divided into four provinces, and developed with roads (notably the coastal Via Balbia), new agricultural villages and the organised settlement of tens of thousands of Italian colonists — some 110,000 by 1939. In 1939 the four coastal provinces were formally incorporated into metropolitan Italy as its "Fourth Shore".

End of Italian rule

Libya was a major battleground of the North Africa campaign in the Second World War; Axis forces finally surrendered in Tunisia in May 1943 and Libya passed under British and French military administration. Italy renounced its claims in the 1947 Paris Peace Treaty, and Libya became independent as a kingdom in 1951.
