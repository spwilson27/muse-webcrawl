Justification (typesetting)

In typesetting, justification (also called full justification) is the alignment of text so that it is flush with both the left and right margins, producing straight edges on both sides of the column. This is achieved by adjusting the spacing between words (and, in some systems, between letters) so that each line of a paragraph, except typically the last, extends to the full measure of the column.

History

Justified text has been the norm in Western printing since the age of movable type: Gutenberg's Bible was set with justified margins, imitating the even edges of fine manuscripts. In hot-metal and hand composition, compositors adjusted spaces by hand, and in digital typesetting the task is performed by line-breaking algorithms such as the Knuth–Plass algorithm used in TeX, which evaluates entire paragraphs to distribute space evenly and minimise hyphenation.

Advantages and problems

Justification gives pages a tidy, formal appearance and is standard in books, newspapers, and academic journals. Its chief drawback in narrow columns is uneven word spacing, which can create conspicuous gaps called "rivers" of white space running through the text. Skilled typesetters mitigate this with careful hyphenation, letterspacing adjustments, and hanging punctuation. On the web, justified text has been criticised for poorer readability, especially for readers with dyslexia, because the variable spacing disrupts the eye's rhythm; most web style guides therefore recommend left-aligned ("ragged right") text.

Related alignments

The alternatives to full justification are flush left (ragged right), flush right (ragged left), and centred text. In CSS, justification is requested with text-align: justify, with additional control available through properties such as text-justify and hyphens.
