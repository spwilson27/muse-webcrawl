Harold Wilson

James Harold Wilson, Baron Wilson of Rievaulx (11 March 1916 – 24 May 1995), was a British statesman and Labour Party politician who twice served as Prime Minister of the United Kingdom, from October 1964 to June 1970 and again from March 1974 to April 1976. He was the Leader of the Labour Party from 1963 until his resignation in 1976, and remains the only Labour leader to have formed administrations after four general elections.

Prime Minister: 1964–1970; 1974–1976

Born: 11 March 1916, Huddersfield, England

Died: 24 May 1995 (aged 79), London

Party: Labour

Education: Jesus College, Oxford

Early life and career

Wilson was born in Huddersfield, Yorkshire, and won a scholarship to Jesus College, Oxford, where he gained a first-class degree in philosophy, politics and economics and became a lecturer in economic history. During the Second World War he worked as a civil servant and statistician. Elected MP for Ormskirk in 1945, he entered Clement Attlee's government and, as President of the Board of Trade at 31, was the youngest cabinet minister of the 20th century at that time. He resigned in 1951 alongside Aneurin Bevan over the imposition of NHS prescription charges to fund Korean War rearmament.

Prime ministership

Wilson became Labour leader after Hugh Gaitskell's sudden death in 1963, campaigning on the "white heat" of a technological revolution. His first government (1964–1970) carried out significant social reforms: capital punishment was abolished, homosexuality partially decriminalised, abortion law liberalised, and divorce reformed. His government founded the Open University, a project Wilson regarded as among his finest achievements. It struggled, however, with balance-of-payments crises, devalued the pound in 1967, and failed to reform trade union law with the proposals in In Place of Strife. Wilson kept Britain out of combat involvement in the Vietnam War despite American pressure.

Unexpectedly defeated by Edward Heath in 1970, Wilson returned to power in 1974, forming a minority and then a slim-majority government amid the oil crisis and miners' strikes. He renegotiated Britain's membership of the European Economic Community and held the 1975 referendum that confirmed it. He also oversaw early counter-inflation "social contract" policies with the unions. Wilson resigned suddenly in March 1976, citing exhaustion; he was succeeded by James Callaghan.

Later life

Wilson was created a life peer as Baron Wilson of Rievaulx in 1983. His later years were affected by Alzheimer's disease, and he died in 1995. His legacy is debated: praised for social reform and party management, criticised for economic drift and short-term tactics.
