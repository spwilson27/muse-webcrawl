French Renaissance literature

French Renaissance literature is the body of writing produced in France during the 16th century, when humanism, the printing press, and contact with Italy transformed French letters. Writers sought to enrich the French language so it could rival Latin and Italian as a vehicle for poetry, philosophy, and prose fiction.

Poetry

The dominant poetic movement was La Pléiade, a group of seven poets led by Pierre de Ronsard and Joachim du Bellay. Du Bellay's manifesto, La Défense et illustration de la langue française (1549), argued that French could equal the classical languages if enriched by imitation and invention. Ronsard — the "prince of poets" — produced odes, hymns, the epic fragment La Franciade, and the sonnet sequences Les Amours. Earlier in the century Clément Marot had modernized court poetry, and the Lyon school, including Louise Labé and Pernette du Guillet, produced notable lyric verse.

Prose

François Rabelais's comic chronicles of the giants Gargantua and Pantagruel (1532–1564) mixed erudition, satire, and exuberant vernacular prose. Michel de Montaigne essentially invented the essay with his Essais (1580–1595), an introspective exploration of the self whose motto was "Que sçay-je?" ("What do I know?"). Marguerite de Navarre's Heptaméron (published 1558–59) modelled a French collection of tales on Boccaccio's Decameron.

Theatre and other writing

Humanist tragedy and comedy, influenced by Seneca and Italian models, was written by Étienne Jodelle, Robert Garnier, and others. The period also saw major works of political and religious writing amid the French Wars of Religion, including Étienne de La Boétie's Discourse on Voluntary Servitude, Agrippa d'Aubigné's epic poem Les Tragiques, and the memoirs and pamphlets of the confessional conflict.
