Gemara

LanguagesJewish Babylonian Aramaic; Jewish Palestinian Aramaic
Periodc. 3rd–6th centuries CE
Companion textMishnah
Together with Mishnah formsThe Talmud

The Gemara (from Aramaic גמרא, gamar, "to study" or "learning by tradition") is the component of the Talmud comprising rabbinical analysis of and commentary on the Mishnah, the earlier compilation of Jewish oral law. In the printed Talmud, the Mishnah text appears at the centre of the page, surrounded by the Gemara and later commentaries such as those of Rashi and the Tosafists.

Content and style

The Gemara records the discussions of the Amoraim, the rabbis of the generations after the Mishnah's redaction (c. 200 CE). Its discourse moves between legal analysis (halakha), scriptural exegesis, parables, and narrative traditions (aggadah), frequently staging arguments between named sages in a dialectical style that tests definitions, raises contradictions, and resolves them — or leaves the matter standing (teiku).

The two Gemaras

Two versions were produced. The Jerusalem Gemara, compiled in the academies of Roman Palestine (mainly Tiberias and Caesarea) around the 4th–5th centuries, is written in Jewish Palestinian Aramaic and, together with the Mishnah, forms the Jerusalem Talmud. The Babylonian Gemara, produced in the academies of Sura, Pumbedita, and Nehardea in Sasanian Mesopotamia and redacted around the 5th–6th centuries, is written in Jewish Babylonian Aramaic; it is longer, more thoroughly argued, and historically became the authoritative version, forming the Babylonian Talmud.

Study

Gemara study (iyun) remains the centrepiece of traditional yeshiva education, and large-scale communal study cycles such as Daf Yomi, in which a folio page is studied each day, carry participants through the entire Babylonian Talmud in about seven and a half years.
