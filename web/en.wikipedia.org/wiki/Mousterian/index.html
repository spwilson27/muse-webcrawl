Mousterian

Mousterian
PeriodMiddle Paleolithic, c. 160,000 – 40,000 years ago
RegionEurope, North Africa, Near East, Central Asia

The Mousterian (or Mode III) is an archaeological industry of stone tools, predominantly made of flint, associated chiefly with the Neanderthals during the Middle Paleolithic. It is named after the type site of Le Moustier in the Dordogne region of southwestern France, excavated in the 19th century. The Mousterian lasted roughly from 160,000 to 40,000 years ago, ending as anatomically modern humans spread through Europe and the Upper Paleolithic began.

Technology

Mousterian toolkits were made using the Levallois technique, in which a stone core was carefully shaped so that a flake of predetermined size and shape could be struck from it. Typical tools include side scrapers, denticulates, hand axes (in some variants) and retouched points, some of which were probably hafted as spear tips. François Bordes classified the Mousterian of southwestern France into several variants — including the Typical, Denticulate, Ferrassie, Quina and Mousterian of Acheulean Tradition — and interpreted them as the toolkits of distinct cultural groups, an interpretation later debated by scholars who attributed the variation to tool use and resharpening instead.

Behaviour

Mousterian sites document sophisticated Neanderthal behaviour: systematic hunting of large game, controlled use of fire in hearths, hafting of tools with birch-tar adhesives, use of ochre pigments, and — at sites such as Shanidar Cave and La Chapelle-aux-Saints — deliberate burial of the dead. Neanderthal-associated Mousterian industries extended across Europe, North Africa and the Near East; in the Levant, early modern humans at sites such as Skhul and Qafzeh also produced Mousterian tools, complicating any simple equation of the industry with a single species.
