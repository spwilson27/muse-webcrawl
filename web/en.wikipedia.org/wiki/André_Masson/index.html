Died: 28 October 1987, Paris, France

André Masson (4 January 1896 – 28 October 1987) was a French artist associated with Surrealism and abstract expressionism. Severely wounded in the First World War, an experience that marked his art permanently, he became a central figure of the Parisian avant-garde in the 1920s.

Surrealism and automatism

Masson was a pioneer of automatic drawing, letting the pen move without conscious control to tap the unconscious mind — a technique at the heart of André Breton's surrealism. In 1927 he began his celebrated sand paintings, pouring glue onto canvas and covering it with coloured sand. His work explored mythology, eroticism, violence, and metamorphosis, as in the Massacres series and Gradiva (1938–39). Although Breton expelled him from the surrealist group in the 1940s, Masson's influence continued to grow.

Exile and later work

Fleeing the German occupation, Masson lived in the United States from 1941 to 1945, where his gestural, biomorphic canvases influenced the young New York painters who would form abstract expressionism, notably Jackson Pollock and Arshile Gorky. Returning to France, he painted landscapes around Aix-en-Provence and designed the ceiling of the Odéon Theatre in Paris (1965).
