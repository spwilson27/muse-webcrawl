A roundabout is a type of circular road junction in which traffic flows continuously in one direction around a central island, and entering traffic yields to traffic already on the roundabout. The modern roundabout, developed in the United Kingdom in the 1960s, differs from older traffic circles and rotaries by its yield-at-entry rule, smaller diameter and deflected entry paths that enforce low speeds.

Operation and safety

Because vehicles enter at shallow angles and travel in the same direction, roundabouts eliminate the most dangerous crossing conflicts of a conventional crossroads. Studies consistently find substantial reductions in fatal and serious-injury crashes compared with signalised junctions, though minor collisions can rise and multi-lane roundabouts can be harder for cyclists and pedestrians. Roundabouts also reduce delay and emissions when traffic is moderate, since vehicles need not stop for signals.

History and spread

Circular junctions date to the early 20th century (Columbus Circle, New York, opened 1905; the first British gyratory systems followed). The modern form spread after Britain adopted the offside priority (yield-on-entry) rule nationally in 1966; Frank Blackmore's innovations included the mini-roundabout. France built roundabouts at great scale from the 1980s, and since the 1990s the United States, Australia and many other countries have constructed them in growing numbers, with Carmel, Indiana famously adopting them city-wide.

Variants

Mini-roundabouts use a flush or slightly raised painted circle passable to large vehicles. Turbo roundabouts, developed in the Netherlands, channel traffic into spiralling lanes to reduce weaving. Large or complex junctions may be signalised or use hamburger (through-about) layouts. Swindon's "Magic Roundabout" (1972), a ring of five mini-roundabouts, is a celebrated British example.
