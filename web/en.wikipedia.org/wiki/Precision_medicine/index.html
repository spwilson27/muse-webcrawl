Precision medicine

Precision medicine
FocusTreatment tailored to genetics, environment and lifestyle
Key toolsGenomic sequencing, biomarkers, pharmacogenomics
Lead exampleTargeted cancer therapy

Precision medicine is a medical model that proposes the customisation of healthcare, with treatments and practices tailored to subgroups of patients — or to the individual — based on genetic, biomarker, phenotypic or psychosocial characteristics, rather than a one-size-fits-all approach. It is closely related to the older term personalised medicine, though "precision" is preferred because care is usually precise for a group sharing a molecular profile rather than unique to one person.

Approach

The field grew from advances in sequencing that made a human genome affordable to read, large biobanks linking DNA to health records, and medical tests that classify disease molecularly. A cancer, for example, may be typed by its driver mutations and matched to a targeted drug: HER2-positive breast cancer to trastuzumab, EGFR-mutant lung cancer to tyrosine kinase inhibitors, BCR-ABL leukaemia to imatinib. Pharmacogenomics adjusts drug choice and dose by metabolic genotype, as with DPYD testing before fluoropyrimidine chemotherapy or HLA-B*57:01 screening before abacavir.

Initiatives

The United States launched a Precision Medicine Initiative in 2015, including the All of Us research programme aiming to enrol a million participants; other countries have built national genomics programmes, such as Genomics England's 100,000 Genomes Project. Risk stratification tools such as age-adjusted interpretation of prostate-specific antigen illustrate the same principle in screening.

Criticisms and challenges

Critics note that most diseases are multifactorial, that benefits so far concentrate in oncology and rare genetic disease (such as haemochromatosis, long diagnosed genetically), and that precision medicine risks diverting resources from public-health measures with larger population effects. Data privacy, incidental findings, cost and unequal access to sequencing remain practical obstacles.
