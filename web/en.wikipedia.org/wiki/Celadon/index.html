Celadon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Celadon is a term for pottery denoting both the ware and its distinctive pale green glaze, produced in China and later throughout East Asia. The term derives, by popular tradition, from the shepherd Celadon in the French pastoral romance L'Astrée (1607–1627), whose grey-green garments echoed the colour of the glaze. Celadon wares are typically stoneware or porcelain fired in a reduction atmosphere, the green tint coming from iron oxide in the glaze.

History

Proto-celadon glazes appeared in China during the Shang and Zhou dynasties, and mature celadon developed in the Yue kilns of Zhejiang by the Eastern Han dynasty. Song dynasty kilns at Longquan produced the most celebrated celadons, prized at court and exported widely across Asia and to the Middle East and East Africa.

Korean celadon

During the Goryeo dynasty (918–1392), Korean potters developed their own celebrated celadon tradition, famous for the sanggam inlay technique, in which designs were carved into the body, filled with white or black slip, and covered with the translucent green glaze. Goryeo celadon is regarded as among the finest ceramics ever produced.

Production

The glaze contains small amounts of iron oxide; fired in a reducing (oxygen-poor) kiln atmosphere at temperatures around 1,200–1,300 °C, the iron yields colours ranging from pale blue-green to deep olive. Slight variations in firing produce prized effects such as crackled "golden wire and iron thread" glazes of Ge ware.
