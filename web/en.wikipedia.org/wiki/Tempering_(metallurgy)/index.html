Tempering (metallurgy)

Tempering is a process of heat treating, used to increase the toughness of iron-based alloys, most commonly steel. Tempering is performed after quenching (hardening): the metal, which quenching has left extremely hard but brittle and highly stressed, is reheated to a temperature below its lower critical point and held there, then cooled. The result is a deliberate trade of some hardness for greatly improved toughness and ductility, along with relief of internal stresses.

Process

Freshly quenched steel consists largely of martensite, a supersaturated, tetragonal form of iron-carbon that is hard and brittle. During tempering, carbon atoms diffuse out of the martensite and precipitate as fine carbides; the martensite itself gradually decomposes towards a mixture of ferrite and cementite sometimes called tempered martensite. The higher the tempering temperature and the longer the hold, the more the hardness falls and the toughness rises. Typical practice ranges from around 150–200 °C, which scarcely softens the steel and is used for cutting edges, up to 600 °C or more for structural parts where toughness dominates. Alloying elements such as chromium, molybdenum, and vanadium retard softening and can produce "secondary hardening" at higher tempering temperatures.

Tempering colours

Before pyrometers, smiths judged temperature from the colours of the thin oxide film that forms on polished steel as it heats: pale straw (about 220 °C), progressing through dark straw, brown, purple, and blue (about 300 °C), to grey. Each trade chose its colour — straw for razors and lathe tools, purple for axes, blue for springs and saw blades — and the colour terms survive in workshop language today.

Stages and embrittlement

Tempering proceeds in recognisable stages: precipitation of transition carbides, decomposition of retained austenite, and finally formation and coarsening of cementite. Certain temperature ranges must be avoided for susceptible alloy steels because of temper embrittlement, a loss of impact toughness caused by impurity segregation (phosphorus, antimony, tin) to grain boundaries during slow cooling through roughly 375–575 °C; molybdenum additions and fast cooling after tempering mitigate it.

Related techniques

Differential tempering hardens one part of a workpiece more than another — for example, a sword blade's edge versus its spine — historically achieved by selective reheating. Austempering and martempering are alternative interrupted-quench treatments that achieve similar toughness by transforming the steel isothermally. The word "temper" is also used loosely for the condition of a metal generally, including in non-ferrous alloys and in glass, where tempering instead means strengthening by thermal prestress.
