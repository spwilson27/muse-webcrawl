Ionic radius

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Comparison of atomic and ionic radii

The ionic radius is the radius of a monatomic ion in an ionic crystal structure. Although neither atoms nor ions have sharp boundaries, the ionic radius is treated as though ions were hard spheres whose sizes are additive: the internuclear distance between a cation and anion in an ionic compound can be partitioned into the sum of their ionic radii.

Trends

Ionic radius increases down a group of the periodic table (more electron shells) and, for isoelectronic species, decreases with increasing nuclear charge. Cations are smaller than their parent atoms because they lose electrons (often an entire shell), while anions are larger because added electrons increase electron–electron repulsion. Ionic radii also increase with coordination number.

Determination

Classic tabulations were compiled by Linus Pauling and, more comprehensively, by R. D. Shannon (1976), whose "effective ionic radii" depend on coordination number and spin state and are widely used in crystallography and materials science. Ionic radii typically range from about 30 pm for small highly charged cations to over 200 pm for large anions such as iodide.
