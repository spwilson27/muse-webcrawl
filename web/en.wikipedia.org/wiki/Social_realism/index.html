Social realism

Social realism is the term used for work produced by artists and writers that features working-class and poor people, depicting their lives and conditions realistically and often critically, drawing attention to social and racial injustice and economic hardship. Although the practice has roots in 19th-century European realism — Courbet, Millet, Daumier — the term is used most specifically of art and literature of the interwar period, especially in the United States during the Great Depression, and of related movements in Mexico, Britain and elsewhere. It is distinct from socialist realism, the officially mandated doctrine of the Soviet Union and other communist states.

History

In the United States, social realism grew out of the urban scenes of the Ashcan School and was given urgency by the crash of 1929. Painters such as Ben Shahn, Reginald Marsh, Isabel Bishop and the Soyer brothers depicted breadlines, tenements, strikes and subway crowds; photographers of the Farm Security Administration, including Dorothea Lange and Walker Evans, documented rural poverty in images that became icons of the era. Federal art programmes under the New Deal, including the Works Progress Administration, employed thousands of artists on murals and easel works with social themes. In Mexico, the muralists Rivera, Orozco and Siqueiros combined social realism with revolutionary politics on a monumental scale.

In literature and theatre, social realism encompassed proletarian novels, John Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath (1939), Clifford Odets's plays, and later British "kitchen sink realism" of the 1950s–60s. In cinema its descendant is often traced through Italian neorealism.

Distinction from socialist realism

Unlike socialist realism, social realism was not an official doctrine, prescribed no single party line, and frequently criticised governments and employers; its practitioners ranged across the democratic left. The two movements overlapped in subject matter and sometimes in personnel, but conflating them obscures the independence — and frequent dissent — of social realist artists.
