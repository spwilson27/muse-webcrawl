Western Desert

The Western Desert of Australia is the arid region covering much of the interior of Western Australia and adjoining parts of the Northern Territory and South Australia. Geographically it comprises the Great Sandy Desert, the Gibson Desert and the Great Victoria Desert. In anthropology, the term also denotes the Western Desert cultural bloc — the network of Aboriginal peoples who speak closely related Wati languages and share religious and social traditions across this vast region.

Peoples and languages

Peoples of the Western Desert include the Martu, Pintupi, Pitjantjatjara, Yankunytjatjara, Ngaanyatjarra and Luritja. Their languages belong to the Wati subgroup of the Pama–Nyungan family and many speak several as a matter of course. Despite the harsh environment — rainfall is low and erratic, and permanent water is scarce — Aboriginal occupation extends back tens of thousands of years, sustained by detailed knowledge of soaks, rockholes and seasonal foods.

Contact history

European explorers such as Ernest Giles and David Carnegie crossed the region in the 1870s–1890s. Some Western Desert groups remained in isolation from settler society well into the 20th century; the last first contacts occurred as late as 1984, when a group of Pintupi (the "Pintupi Nine") came in from the desert near Lake Mackay. The region became known worldwide through the Western Desert art movement, which began at Papunya in 1971 and produced leading artists such as Clifford Possum Tjapaltjarri.
