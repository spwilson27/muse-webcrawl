ProcessDifferential erosion of resistant rock

A monadnock (also called an inselberg, German for "island mountain") is an isolated hill, ridge, or small mountain that rises abruptly from a gently sloping or virtually level surrounding plain. In the United States the term derives from Mount Monadnock in southwestern New Hampshire, whose name comes from an Abenaki word meaning "mountain that stands alone."

Formation

Monadnocks form where a body of unusually hard or erosion-resistant rock — such as quartzite, granite, or other intrusive igneous rock — survives while surrounding softer rock is worn down by weathering and erosion. They may result from an igneous intrusion exposed by erosion, or from a volcanic plug. In the cycle of erosion described by William Morris Davis, the monadnock is the residual elevation left on a nearly featureless erosion surface (peneplain) in the final, old-age stage of landscape development.

Examples

Famous examples include Mount Monadnock (3,165 ft) itself, one of the most-climbed mountains in the world; Stone Mountain and Panola Mountain in Georgia; Sugarloaf Mountain in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil; Pilot Mountain in North Carolina; and the inselbergs of Africa and Australia, including the bornhardts of the savanna landscapes. Uluru is sometimes described as a monadnock-like inselberg.
