Michigan Avenue is a north–south street in Chicago, Illinois, one of the city's most famous thoroughfares. It runs from the Chicago River through downtown to the South Side, and its northern stretch — flanked by luxury shops, hotels and landmark skyscrapers — is known as the Magnificent Mile. The avenue forms the eastern boundary of the Loop for much of its downtown course.

History

Michigan Avenue developed as a residential boulevard along the lakefront in the nineteenth century. The Great Chicago Fire of 1871 destroyed much of the surrounding district, but the Chicago Water Tower (1869), near the northern end, survived and became a symbol of the city's rebirth. The 1909 Plan of Chicago by Daniel Burnham and Edward Bennett proposed widening the avenue and bridging it across the Chicago River; the double-deck Michigan Avenue Bridge (now the DuSable Bridge) opened in 1920, linking the avenue to Pine Street on the north bank and triggering the commercial boom that created the Magnificent Mile.

The Magnificent Mile

The stretch of North Michigan Avenue between the Chicago River and Oak Street is Chicago's premier shopping district, lined with department stores, luxury boutiques, high-rise residences and hotels. Landmark buildings include the Wrigley Building (1924), the neo-Gothic Tribune Tower (1925), the Palmolive Building, the John Hancock Center and Water Tower Place. The district is a major centre of the annual Magnificent Mile Lights Festival, which opens the Christmas shopping season.

South Michigan Avenue

South of the river, Michigan Avenue fronts Grant Park and the Art Institute of Chicago, passing the Chicago Cultural Center and a wall of historic early skyscrapers and former automobile showrooms known as Motor Row. Further south it traverses the South Side neighbourhoods, including Bronzeville, before ending near 124th Street.
