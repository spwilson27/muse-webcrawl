Leningrad Affair

The Leningrad Affair was a series of fabricated criminal cases in the Soviet Union in the late 1940s and early 1950s that destroyed the political leadership of Leningrad and a number of senior officials in Moscow associated with it. It was the last great purge of Stalin's rule and is generally seen as part of the power struggle among Stalin's lieutenants, particularly between the faction of Georgy Malenkov and Lavrentiy Beria and the rising "Leningraders" associated with Andrei Zhdanov.

Background

During the Siege of Leningrad, the city's party organisation under Zhdanov had gained prestige and relative autonomy. After the war, Leningraders such as Alexei Kuznetsov were promoted to senior posts in Moscow, and Nikolai Voznesensky headed Gosplan. Zhdanov's death in August 1948 removed their protector, and their rivals moved against them, using as a pretext irregularities in party voting at a Leningrad conference and the unauthorised All-Union Wholesale Fair held in the city.

Arrests and trial

In 1949 Kuznetsov, Voznesensky, Pyotr Popkov (the Leningrad party chief), and others were arrested on charges of treason and anti-party activity. After secret proceedings, the principal defendants were tried in Leningrad on 29–30 September 1950, sentenced to death, and shot immediately after the verdict. In total, more than two hundred people were sentenced in connected cases, and around two thousand Leningrad officials and their relatives were exiled or dismissed.

Aftermath

The victims were rehabilitated after Stalin's death: in 1954 the Military Collegium of the USSR Supreme Court annulled the verdicts, recognising the cases had been fabricated, in part by Minister of State Security Viktor Abakumov, who was himself executed. The affair is regarded as a warning by Stalin to any regional or generational network that might develop into an alternative power base.
