Midwifery

Midwifery is the health profession concerned with pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period, including the care of the newborn and women's reproductive health across the lifespan. A practitioner is a midwife. The International Confederation of Midwives defines the midwife as a person educated to proficiency in the essential competencies for basic midwifery practice and licensed or registered to practise.

Scope of practice

Midwives provide antenatal care, attend births in hospitals, birth centres and homes, and provide postnatal care for mother and baby. In many countries midwives are the primary carers for low-risk pregnancies, referring women to obstetricians when complications arise. Midwifery-led care is associated in research with lower rates of intervention for low-risk women and high satisfaction.

History

Midwifery is an ancient occupation, attested in Egyptian papyri and in the works of Hippocrates and Soranus. For centuries childbirth was attended almost exclusively by women. From the 17th century male practitioners ("man-midwives") entered the field with instruments such as obstetric forceps. The 20th century saw birth move into hospitals and midwifery become a regulated, university-educated profession in many countries, though regulation and autonomy vary widely.

Education

Routes include direct-entry midwifery degrees and post-nursing qualifications. In the United States, certified nurse-midwives (CNMs) and certified midwives (CMs) are nationally credentialed, while certified professional midwives (CPMs) specialise in out-of-hospital birth; state licensure differs.
