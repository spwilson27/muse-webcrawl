ʻOumuamua (formally 1I/2017 U1) is the first known interstellar object detected passing through the Solar System. It was discovered on 19 October 2017 by Canadian physicist Robert Weryk using the Pan-STARRS telescope at Haleakalā Observatory, Hawaii, some 40 days after it passed its closest point to the Sun. Its name, chosen by the discovery team in consultation with Hawaiian language experts, means "scout" or "messenger from the distant past reaching out to us" in Hawaiian.

Observations

ʻOumuamua travelled on a strongly hyperbolic trajectory with an eccentricity of about 1.2, far too extreme for an object bound to the Sun, proving it originated outside the Solar System. It showed no visible coma or tail, yet it exhibited a small non-gravitational acceleration as it departed — behaviour typical of comet outgassing but without detectable gas or dust. Its brightness varied dramatically with a period of about 8 hours, implying an unusual shape that tumbles through space; estimates variously describe an elongated, cigar- or pancake-like body a few hundred metres across with a reddish, tholin-coated surface.

Hypotheses

Explanations for the anomalous acceleration and shape have included a hydrogen or nitrogen iceberg fragment of an exoplanetary body, a fractal dust aggregate, and — in a widely publicised and controversial suggestion — an artificial light sail of extraterrestrial origin. Most astronomers favour natural explanations, and subsequent studies have argued that outgassing of volatile ices can account for the observations without invoking exotic compositions.

Significance

The detection of ʻOumuamua, followed by the interstellar comet 2I/Borisov in 2019, demonstrated that interstellar objects pass through the inner Solar System regularly and led the International Astronomical Union to create the "I" designation for interstellar objects.
