Ambrosian chant

Ambrosian chant is the liturgical plainchant repertory of the Ambrosian rite of the Roman Catholic Church, associated with the Archdiocese of Milan and named after Saint Ambrose, its bishop in the 4th century. It is the only surviving regional chant tradition of Western Europe besides Gregorian chant to remain in continuous use, having resisted replacement even when other local rites were suppressed under Charlemagne.

History

Tradition credits Ambrose with introducing antiphonal hymn singing to the West during the siege of his basilica in 386. Although the surviving repertory was shaped much later, Milan's fierce attachment to its own rite preserved the chant through the Carolingian standardisation that eliminated Gallican and Old Roman variants elsewhere. The chant was codified under Cardinal Charles Borromeo in the 16th century and revised again after the Second Vatican Council.

Characteristics

Ambrosian chant differs from Gregorian in its chant genres (including ingressa, psalmellus, confractorium, and transitorium), its responsory forms, and melodic features such as distinctive repeated-note figures. Its liturgical calendar and structure of the Mass and Office also diverge from the Roman rite; the Ambrosian Lent, for example, begins on the Monday after Ash Wednesday's Roman equivalent, and Milan famously keeps carnival until the Saturday before Lent (carnevale ambrosiano).

Manuscripts

The earliest complete notated sources date from the 12th century, notably the antiphoner known as British Library Add MS 34209. Modern editions were prepared in the early 20th century under the monks of Solesmes and others.
