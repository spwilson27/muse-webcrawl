Platform Sutra

The Platform Sutra of the Sixth Patriarch (Chinese: 六祖壇經, Liùzǔ Tánjīng) is a Chan Buddhist scripture composed in China between the 8th and 13th centuries. It is attributed to Huineng (638–713), the Sixth Patriarch of Chan (Zen), and purports to record a sermon he delivered from a platform at Dafan Temple in Shaozhou, together with his autobiography. It is the only Chinese Buddhist work traditionally designated a "sutra" (經) despite not being presented as the word of the Buddha — a mark of its extraordinary status in East Asian Buddhism.

Contents

The text opens with Huineng's life story: an illiterate woodcutter from the south, awakened on hearing the Diamond Sutra, who journeys to the monastery of the Fifth Patriarch, Hongren. In the famous verse contest, the head monk Shenxiu writes that the mind is like a mirror that must be polished constantly to keep it free of dust; Huineng replies:

Bodhi originally has no tree,
The mirror has no stand.
The Buddha-nature is always clear and pure;
Where is there room for dust?

Hongren secretly transmits the robe and bowl of the patriarchate to Huineng. The sermon that follows teaches sudden enlightenment, the identity of meditation (ding) and wisdom (hui), "no-thought" (wunian), and the practice of repentance and refuge "in one's own nature". It rejects gradual cultivation in favour of seeing one's original nature directly.

Versions and scholarship

The earliest surviving version was found among the Dunhuang manuscripts (c. 780s–860s) and is much shorter than the later, expanded editions of the Song and Yuan dynasties that became standard. Modern scholars regard the text as a layered composition produced by Huineng's followers, probably associated with the school of his disciple Shenhui, who campaigned to have Huineng recognised as Sixth Patriarch and to establish "sudden" teaching over the "gradual" Northern School of Shenxiu.

Influence

The Platform Sutra became one of the foundational texts of Chan and Zen, shaping the Southern School's doctrine of sudden awakening and the later use of koans. Its teaching that laypeople and even an illiterate "barbarian" from the south could attain Buddhahood gave it enduring popular appeal, and its verses remain widely quoted.
