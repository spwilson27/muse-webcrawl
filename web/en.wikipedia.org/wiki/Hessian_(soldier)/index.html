Hessian (soldier)

Hessians were German soldiers who served as auxiliaries to the British Army during the American Revolutionary War. The term derives from the Landgraviate of Hesse-Kassel, whose rulers had a long-established practice of hiring out regiments of their army to foreign powers; about 30,000 German troops, the majority of them from Hesse, fought in North America between 1776 and 1783.

Background

The small German states of Hesse-Kassel, Hesse-Hanau, Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel, Hanover, Waldeck, Anhalt-Zerbst, and Ansbach-Bayreuth all supplied troops to Britain under subsidy treaties. Hesse-Kassel, a militarised state whose army was one of the largest in Europe relative to its population, contributed the largest contingent — roughly 17,000 men — hence the American habit of calling all the German auxiliaries "Hessians". The treaties paid the German princes a levy for each soldier supplied and extra compensation for casualties, an arrangement denounced by the American revolutionaries; the Declaration of Independence listed among its grievances against George III the transporting of "large Armies of foreign Mercenaries".

Service in America

The first Hessians landed on Staten Island in August 1776 and fought in the New York campaign, at White Plains, and at Fort Washington, where they played a prominent role. Hessian regiments served throughout the war, including at Brandywine, Germantown, Newport, Charleston, and Yorktown. Their most famous reverse came on the night of 25–26 December 1776, when George Washington crossed the Delaware River and surprised a Hessian brigade at Trenton, capturing some 900 men of Colonel Johann Rall's force.

Reputation and prisoners

American propaganda painted the Hessians as brutal mercenaries, and Washington Irving's short story "The Legend of Sleepy Hollow" later fixed the figure of the headless Hessian trooper in popular culture. In reality the troops were mostly professional soldiers of ordinary background rather than hired adventurers. Congress offered German deserters grants of land, and of some 30,000 who served, about 5,000 remained in the United States and Canada after the war instead of returning home.

Legacy

The word "Hessian" became a general American term for a foreign mercenary, and units recruited from the former German auxiliaries' homelands are still remembered in place names and monuments across the eastern United States. The practice of subsidy treaties was widely criticised in Europe, and Friedrich Schiller and other writers treated the sale of soldiers as a symbol of petty princely despotism.
