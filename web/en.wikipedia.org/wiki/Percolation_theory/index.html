Percolation theory

Percolation theory
FieldStatistical physics, mathematics, materials science
IntroducedBroadbent & Hammersley (1957)
Key conceptPercolation threshold pc

In statistical physics and mathematics, percolation theory describes the behaviour of a network when nodes or links are added. When the number of connections reaches a critical value, a giant connected component emerges and the system undergoes a phase transition — in physical terms, a fluid can suddenly "percolate" through a porous material, a conductor can span a random network, or a disease can spread through a population. The theory was introduced by Simon Broadbent and John Hammersley in 1957, motivated by the design of gas masks for coal miners.

Models

In site percolation, each site of a lattice is occupied independently with probability p; in bond percolation, each edge is open with probability p. Clusters are groups of neighbouring occupied sites or connected open bonds. The central quantity is the percolation threshold pc: below it, all clusters are finite; above it, an infinite (spanning) cluster exists with probability one. For bond percolation on the square lattice, pc = 1/2 exactly, a result established by Harry Kesten in 1980; for site percolation on the square lattice, pc ≈ 0.5927 is known only numerically.

Critical behaviour and universality

Near pc, quantities such as the mean cluster size and the correlation length diverge as power laws with critical exponents that depend only on the dimension of the system, not on microscopic details — an example of universality in statistical mechanics. In two dimensions many exponents are known exactly through conformal field theory and Schramm–Loewner evolution, work connected to the Fields Medals of Wendelin Werner (2006) and Stanislav Smirnov (2010), the latter for proving conformal invariance of percolation on the triangular lattice.

Applications

  • Flow of liquids through porous media, oil recovery and groundwater hydrology
  • Conductivity of random resistor networks and composite materials
  • Forest-fire and epidemic spread models
  • Gelation and polymer network formation
  • Network robustness and the resilience of the Internet
