Townsend Plan

The Townsend Plan, formally the Old-Age Revolving Pensions proposal, was a popular old-age pension scheme advanced in the United States during the Great Depression by Dr. Francis E. Townsend, a retired physician from Long Beach, California. First published in a letter to a local newspaper in 1933 and promoted from 1934, the plan proposed that the federal government pay every American citizen aged 60 or older a pension of $200 per month, on the conditions that the recipient retire from work and spend the entire sum within the month. It was to be financed by a 2 percent national tax on business transactions.

The movement

The plan ignited a mass grassroots movement. Thousands of "Townsend Clubs" formed across the country, claiming millions of members, and supporters flooded Congress with petitions bearing millions of signatures. The movement gave elderly Americans — among the groups hardest hit by the Depression — an organized political voice for the first time.

Criticism and response

Economists and the Roosevelt administration attacked the plan as fiscally impossible: the pensions would have consumed roughly half of national income, and the transaction tax was widely regarded as inadequate and regressive. Nevertheless, the pressure generated by the Townsend movement, together with other pension agitation such as Huey Long's Share Our Wealth campaign, pushed President Franklin D. Roosevelt to act. The administration's answer was the Social Security Act of 1935, which established a more modest, contributory old-age insurance system.

Legacy

Although the Townsend Plan itself was never enacted, bills embodying it were introduced in Congress repeatedly into the 1940s, and Townsend continued campaigning until his death in 1960. Historians credit the movement with demonstrating the political power of organized senior citizens and with helping to create the climate in which Social Security became law.
