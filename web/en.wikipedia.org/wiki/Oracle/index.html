Oracle

An oracle is a person or agency considered to provide wise counsel or prophetic predictions — divine revelations of the future — in ancient cultures. Oracles were consulted by individuals and states before important decisions such as wars, colonisation and legislation.

Greece

The most prestigious Greek oracle was the Pythia, priestess of Apollo at Delphi, whose often cryptic utterances were interpreted by temple priests and influenced Greek affairs for centuries — from the founding of colonies to the Persian Wars. Other famous shrines included Zeus's oracle at Dodona, where priests interpreted the rustling of oak leaves, and the oracle of Apollo at Didyma in Asia Minor.

Elsewhere in the ancient world

Egypt consulted oracles of Amun, notably at Siwa Oasis, which Alexander the Great visited in 331 BC. Rome used the Sibylline Books and augury. In China, "oracle bones" of the Shang dynasty — inscribed ox scapulae and turtle shells used in divination — preserve the earliest substantial corpus of Chinese writing.

Ambiguity and interpretation

Oracle responses were proverbially ambiguous: Croesus of Lydia was told that if he attacked Persia he would "destroy a great empire" — his own. This double-edged style protected the shrines' reputations and left priests considerable interpretive latitude. The practice declined with the spread of Christianity, and Delphi's last recorded responses date to the late 4th century AD.

Modern usage

By extension, "oracle" came to mean any authoritative source of knowledge, and the word is used in computing (e.g. oracle machines in computability theory, blockchain oracles) and finance.
