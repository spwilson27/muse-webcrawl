Quantum evolution

Quantum evolution is a hypothesis in evolutionary biology proposed by George Gaylord Simpson in his Tempo and Mode in Evolution (1944). Simpson used the term — unrelated to quantum mechanics — for relatively rapid evolutionary transitions between adaptive zones, in which a population passes through an unstable "inadaptive phase" before emerging into a new ecological niche and radiating.

Concept

Simpson distinguished three tempos of evolution: bradytely (very slow), horotely (typical rates) and tachytely (rapid). Quantum evolution was his mechanism for tachytelic change at the level of higher taxa: small, isolated populations, subject to drift and strong selection, cross an adaptive valley quickly — too quickly to leave much fossil record — and then diversify into a new zone (for example, the shift from terrestrial ancestors to the bats, or early diversification of mammals). He linked the idea to Sewall Wright's shifting balance theory.

Relationship to later theory

The concept anticipates elements of punctuated equilibrium (Eldredge and Gould, 1972), though the two differ: punctuated equilibrium concerns stasis and speciation in the fossil record generally, whereas Simpson's quantum evolution specifically invoked adaptation to new zones and genetic drift in small populations. Simpson later moderated the idea, and the term is now mainly of historical interest, though "quantum speciation" is still occasionally used for rapid, chromosomally driven speciation in plants.
