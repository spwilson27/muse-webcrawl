History of the textile industry

The history of the textile industry covers the production of cloth from ancient hand spinning and weaving, through the mechanisation that ignited the Industrial Revolution in Britain, to the globalised synthetic-fibre industry of today.

Pre-industrial textiles

Humans have spun and woven fibres for over 20,000 years. Linen in Egypt, wool in Mesopotamia, silk in China (from c. 2700 BCE) and cotton in the Indus Valley and Mesoamerica supported major trades; Indian calicoes and muslins dominated world cotton markets into the 18th century. Medieval Europe organised production through guilds and the putting-out system, with centres such as Florence and Flanders.

Mechanisation and the factory system

A cascade of British inventions mechanised cotton: Kay's flying shuttle (1733), Hargreaves's spinning jenny (1764), Arkwright's water frame (1769), Crompton's mule (1779) and Cartwright's power loom (1785). Steam power concentrated production in Lancashire mills; "King Cotton" drew raw fibre from slave-worked plantations in the American South and later Egypt and India, while displaced handloom weavers and Luddites resisted. Britain's factory model spread to New England, continental Europe and Japan.

Science, synthetics and globalisation

Aniline dyes (Perkin's mauveine, 1856), the sewing machine and ready-to-wear clothing industrialised garment-making. The 20th century added synthetic fibres — rayon, nylon (1935), polyester — and petrochemical textiles. Production migrated from the old mill towns to East and South Asia; Bangladesh, China and Vietnam became garment export giants, amid recurring concerns over wages, safety (the 2013 Rana Plaza collapse) and environmental impact.

Recent trends

Fast fashion, automation, technical textiles and circular-economy recycling now define the industry's frontier, as production of cloth — humanity's second-oldest manufacture — exceeds 100 million tonnes of fibre a year.
