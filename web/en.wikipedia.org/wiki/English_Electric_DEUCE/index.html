English Electric DEUCE

Also known asDigital Electronic Universal Computing Engine
DeveloperEnglish Electric
Release date1955
PredecessorPilot ACE
MemoryMercury delay lines; magnetic drum

The DEUCE (Digital Electronic Universal Computing Engine) was one of the earliest commercial British computers, built by English Electric and first delivered in 1955. It was derived from the Pilot ACE, the scaled-down version of Alan Turing's ACE (Automatic Computing Engine) design developed at the National Physical Laboratory.

Design

The DEUCE used thermionic valves (vacuum tubes) and mercury delay lines for its main store, giving it a working memory of 384 words of 32 bits, supplemented by a magnetic drum store of 8,192 words. Input and output were by punched cards, using equipment derived from standard tabulating machinery. The machine operated with a clock period derived from the delay-line circulation time, and its instruction format was closely modelled on that of the Pilot ACE.

Software

Programming the DEUCE was demanding, but a library of subroutines and a simple assembly scheme (known as "alphacode") eased the task. The General Interpretation Programme (GIP) allowed users to describe calculations in a compact notation. The machine was used for scientific and engineering computation, including aircraft design work at English Electric's own establishments, crystallography, and civil engineering.

Production and use

Around 33 DEUCE machines were sold, making it a notable commercial success for its era. Installations included universities, government laboratories and industrial firms in Britain and abroad. The DEUCE remained in service into the late 1960s at some sites. A DEUCE at the University of New South Wales in Australia, known as UTECOM, was among the first computers in that country.
