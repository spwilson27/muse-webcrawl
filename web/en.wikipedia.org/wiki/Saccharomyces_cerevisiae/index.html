Saccharomyces cerevisiae is a species of yeast that has been instrumental in winemaking, baking and brewing since ancient times, and is one of the most intensively studied eukaryotic model organisms in molecular and cell biology. It is a single-celled fungus that reproduces both asexually by budding and sexually through the formation of ascospores, and it ferments sugars to ethanol and carbon dioxide even in the presence of oxygen (the Crabtree effect).

Uses

Selected strains of S. cerevisiae are sold as baker's yeast for leavening bread and as brewer's yeast for top-fermenting ales and wine production. It is also used industrially for bioethanol production and as a nutritional supplement, and engineered strains produce pharmaceuticals and other compounds.

As a model organism

The yeast's short generation time, ease of genetic manipulation and conserved cell biology have made it central to research on the cell cycle, meiosis, DNA repair, ageing and protein folding. Its genome — about 12 million base pairs on 16 chromosomes — was the first eukaryotic genome to be fully sequenced, in 1996. Work in S. cerevisiae contributed to several Nobel Prizes, including studies of the cell cycle, telomeres and vesicle trafficking.

Life cycle

Cells exist in haploid and diploid forms. Haploid cells of two mating types, a and α, can mate to form diploids, which under starvation conditions undergo sporulation to produce four haploid ascospores. Individual strains are often heterothallic or homothallic depending on their HO gene status.
