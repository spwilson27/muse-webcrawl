Mammoth

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A woolly mammoth with long curved tusks crossing a snowy steppe]
A woolly mammoth with long curved tusks crossing a snowy steppe

A mammoth is any species of the extinct elephantid genus Mammuthus, which lived from the Pliocene (about 5 million years ago) until its extinction in the Holocene, with a last relict population on Wrangel Island surviving until about 4,000 years ago. The best known is the Woolly mammoth (M. primigenius), adapted to the cold mammoth steppe with a thick coat, small ears and huge curved tusks; mammoths were hunted by early humans and feature in cave art across Eurasia.

Evolution and species

Mammoths evolved in Africa from Primelephas-like ancestors, spread through Eurasia and crossed into North America about 1.5 million years ago. Species include the southern mammoth, the steppe mammoth (among the largest elephants ever, over 4 m at the shoulder), the Columbian mammoth of North America, the woolly mammoth of the northern tundra-steppe, and island dwarfs on Crete, Sardinia and California's Channel Islands.

Extinction

Mainland mammoths vanished between 14,000 and 10,000 years ago as the climate warmed and shrubland replaced the steppe, with human hunting contributing by degrees still debated. Wrangel Island's isolated mammoths persisted another 6,000 years — long enough to be contemporaries of the Egyptian pyramids — before dying out around 2000 BC, their genomes showing accumulated harmful mutations ('genomic meltdown').

Frozen remains and de-extinction

Siberian permafrost yields spectacularly preserved carcasses — the calf Lyuba (2007) among them — complete with flesh, stomach contents and blood. Mammoth DNA has been fully sequenced, and ventures such as Colossal Biosciences aim to engineer mammoth-like cold-resistant elephants ('de-extinction'), a project raising profound ecological and ethical debate. Tusks from the permafrost still supply a legal ivory trade in Russia and China.
