Neuromuscular blocking agent

Neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) are a class of drugs that block neuromuscular transmission at the neuromuscular junction, causing paralysis of the affected skeletal muscles. This is accomplished via their action on the postjunctional nicotinic acetylcholine receptors. They are used in anesthesia to facilitate endotracheal intubation and to provide skeletal muscle relaxation during surgery or mechanical ventilation.

NMBAs act at nicotinic receptors of the motor end plate.

Classification

NMBAs fall into two classes:

  • Depolarizing agents, exemplified by succinylcholine (suxamethonium), which activate the receptor, causing initial fasciculations followed by a block that cannot be reversed by acetylcholinesterase inhibitors.
  • Non-depolarizing agents, such as rocuronium, vecuronium, atracurium, cisatracurium, and pancuronium, which competitively antagonize the receptor without activating it; their effects can be reversed by acetylcholinesterase inhibitors such as neostigmine, or, for rocuronium and vecuronium, by the encapsulating agent sugammadex.

History

Curare, the South American arrow poison, was the first known agent of this class; its active alkaloid tubocurarine was introduced into clinical anesthesia in 1942 by Harold Griffith and Enid Johnson in Montreal. Gallamine, succinylcholine, and later the modern aminosteroid and benzylisoquinolinium agents followed.

Monitoring and adverse effects

Depth of blockade is monitored with a peripheral nerve stimulator using the train-of-four technique. Adverse effects include prolonged paralysis, residual weakness in recovery, anaphylaxis, and, with succinylcholine, hyperkalemia and triggering of malignant hyperthermia in susceptible patients.
