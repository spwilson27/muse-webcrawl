Waterflooding

Waterflooding is a method of secondary oil recovery in which water is injected into an oil reservoir through injection wells to maintain reservoir pressure and sweep oil toward production wells. It is the most widely used enhanced recovery technique in the petroleum industry, often roughly doubling the fraction of oil recovered compared with primary depletion alone.

How it works

As an oil field is produced, reservoir pressure falls and production declines. In a waterflood, water — usually treated seawater or produced water — is pumped into the reservoir, commonly in patterns such as five-spot arrangements of injectors around producers. The injected water displaces oil through the pore spaces of the rock, maintaining pressure and forming an oil bank that moves toward the producing wells.

Design and challenges

Engineers study reservoir heterogeneity, mobility ratios and sweep efficiency to design flood patterns; unfavourable mobility causes water to finger through oil and break through early at producers. Produced water must be separated, treated and often reinjected. Waterflooding has been used since the early twentieth century and underlies the productivity of many giant fields; chemical and thermal methods build on it for further recovery.
