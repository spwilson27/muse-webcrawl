Ink

Ink is a gel, sol, or solution that contains at least one colourant, such as a dye or pigment, and is used to colour a surface to produce an image, text or design. Ink is used for drawing or writing with a pen, brush, reed pen or quill, and in printing processes of every kind.

History

Ancient Egyptians and Chinese independently developed inks from carbon black (soot) bound with gum or glue. In China, ink sticks — ground against an inkstone with water — became fundamental to calligraphy and painting. In medieval Europe, iron gall ink, made from tannin and iron salts, became the standard writing ink for over a millennium; it darkens as it oxidises but can corrode paper. India ink, lampblack-based and waterproof once dry, spread from Asia to artists worldwide.

Types

Modern inks include fountain pen inks (dye-based or pigmented), ballpoint inks (viscous oil-based pastes), gel inks, printing inks formulated for offset, flexography and gravure, and inkjet inks (dye or pigment in water-based carriers). Ink formulations balance colourants, vehicles, solvents and additives controlling drying, viscosity and permanence. Tattoo inks and security inks (fluorescent, magnetic, thermochromic) serve specialised purposes.

Composition

Thicker inks in paste form are used extensively in letterpress and lithographic printing; thinner inks rely on capillary action and rapid absorption or evaporation. Colourants may be soluble dyes or insoluble pigments, the latter generally more lightfast.
