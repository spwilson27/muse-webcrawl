Coriolis force

In physics, the Coriolis force is an inertial (or fictitious) force that acts on objects in motion relative to a rotating reference frame. In a frame rotating clockwise, the force acts to the left of the direction of motion; in one rotating counter-clockwise (as the Earth does viewed from above the North Pole), to the right. The effect was described by French engineer-mathematician Gustave-Gaspard Coriolis in 1835.

Mathematically, the Coriolis acceleration is −2Ω × v, where Ω is the angular velocity vector of the rotating frame and v is the velocity of the object relative to that frame.

Earth applications

Because the Earth rotates, the Coriolis effect deflects moving air and water: to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. It is essential to meteorology and oceanography: it shapes trade winds, drives the rotation of cyclones (counter-clockwise in the northern hemisphere, clockwise in the southern), and underlies geostrophic balance in large-scale winds and currents. The relevant measure is the Coriolis parameter f = 2Ω sin φ, where φ is latitude; the effect vanishes at the equator and is strongest at the poles.

Contrary to popular belief, the Coriolis effect is far too weak to determine the direction of rotation of water draining from a bathtub or sink, which is dominated by local geometry and residual motion.

Other applications

The effect matters in ballistics and long-range artillery, in the precession of the Foucault pendulum, and in rotating machinery. A related phenomenon, the Eötvös effect, changes the apparent weight of objects moving east or west.
