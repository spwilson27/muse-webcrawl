Tubocurarine

Tubocurarine (d-tubocurarine) is a toxic benzylisoquinoline alkaloid and the principal active component of tube curare, the arrow poison prepared by Indigenous peoples of South America from plants such as Chondrodendron tomentosum. It is a non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent.

Mechanism

Tubocurarine acts as a competitive antagonist at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction, preventing acetylcholine from triggering muscle contraction and thereby producing flaccid paralysis of skeletal muscle. Because it is a quaternary ammonium compound, it is poorly absorbed from the gut — which is why hunted animals poisoned with curare could be eaten safely.

Medical use

Introduced into anaesthesia in 1942, tubocurarine was the first widely used muscle relaxant in surgery, allowing lighter anaesthesia with controlled ventilation. It has largely been superseded by agents such as atracurium, vecuronium and rocuronium with fewer side effects; tubocurarine can provoke histamine release and ganglion blockade, causing hypotension.
