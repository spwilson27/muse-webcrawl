EraPre-Socratic philosophy
Main ideasLogos; unity of opposites; universal flux ("everything flows")

Heraclitus of Ephesus (c. 535 – c. 475 BC) was an ancient Greek pre-Socratic philosopher from the city of Ephesus in Ionia, on the coast of Asia Minor. He is famous for his doctrine that "everything flows" (panta rhei) and for the unity of opposites, and he regarded fire as the fundamental element and ordering principle of the cosmos. His cryptic, aphoristic style earned him the epithet "the Obscure"; tradition also calls him the "weeping philosopher", in contrast to Democritus, the "laughing philosopher".

Doctrine

Heraclitus held that the world is governed by the logos — a rational order or account that most people fail to comprehend. Change is fundamental and constant: in his best-known image, one cannot step into the same river twice, for new waters are always flowing. Opposites are interdependent and unified — day and night, up and down, war and peace are aspects of a single process — and strife (polemos) is "the father of all things", the tension through which the cosmos maintains its balance. Only fragments of his book survive, quoted by later authors, yet they have influenced Plato, the Stoics, Hegel, Nietzsche, and Heidegger.
