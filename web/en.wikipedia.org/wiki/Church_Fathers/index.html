The Church Fathers, also called the Fathers of the Church or patristic authors, are the ancient and influential Christian theologians and writers who established the intellectual and doctrinal foundations of Christianity in the centuries after the Apostles. Their writings — letters, sermons, commentaries and treatises in Greek, Latin, Syriac and other languages — shaped doctrines such as the Trinity and Christology and remain authoritative, to varying degrees, for Catholic, Orthodox and many Protestant churches.

Groups and periods

The earliest are the Apostolic Fathers (c. late 1st–mid 2nd century), including Clement of Rome, Ignatius of Antioch and Polycarp, whose works stand close to the New Testament era. The Apologists, such as Justin Martyr, defended Christianity to pagan audiences. The Greek Fathers of the East — Athanasius, the Cappadocians (Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nyssa, Gregory of Nazianzus), John Chrysostom and the Alexandrians Clement and Origen — developed Trinitarian theology through the great councils. The Latin Fathers of the West include Tertullian, Cyprian, Ambrose, Jerome (translator of the Vulgate Bible) and above all Augustine of Hippo. The ascetic Desert Fathers of Egypt founded Christian monasticism.

Legacy

The period is conventionally closed in the West with Gregory the Great or Isidore of Seville, and in the East with John of Damascus (8th century). Catholic tradition further honours some Fathers as Doctors of the Church. The scholarly study of their writings is called patristics.
