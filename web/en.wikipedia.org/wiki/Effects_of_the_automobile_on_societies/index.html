Effects of the automobile on societies

The effects of the automobile on societies have been profound since mass motorisation began in the early twentieth century. The car reshaped settlement patterns, economies, the environment and everyday life, becoming one of the defining technologies of modernity.

Urban form and mobility

Cars enabled suburbanisation and low-density development, decentralised employment and retail (shopping malls, drive-through services), and vast road infrastructure such as the US Interstate Highway System and German autobahns. Land devoted to roads and parking consumes a substantial share of many cities. Car-dependent development has been criticised for "induced demand", congestion, and disadvantages for those who cannot drive — children, the elderly and the poor.

Safety and health

Road traffic crashes kill about 1.19 million people annually worldwide and are the leading cause of death for young people, though per-distance fatality rates have fallen greatly with seat belts, airbags, safer road design and drink-driving enforcement. Air pollution from vehicles contributes to respiratory disease, and car-centred lifestyles are associated with reduced physical activity and obesity.

Environment and economy

Road transport is a major source of greenhouse gas emissions and oil demand, motivating emissions standards, catalytic converters, and the shift to electric vehicles. The automobile industry became a pillar of manufacturing employment in the United States, Germany, Japan, South Korea and China, and the car acquired deep cultural significance as a symbol of freedom, status and individual identity.

Alternatives and change

Public transport, cycling infrastructure, walkable-city movements (such as the 15-minute city), congestion charging, car-sharing and remote work all respond to the car's costs, and many cities now seek to reduce car dominance.
