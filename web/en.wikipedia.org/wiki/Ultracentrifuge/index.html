Ultracentrifuge

An ultracentrifuge is a centrifuge optimised for spinning a rotor at very high speeds, capable of generating accelerations as high as 1,000,000 g (about 9,800 km/s²). There are two main classes: the preparative and analytical ultracentrifuges. Both are essential tools in molecular biology, biochemistry and polymer science for separating and analysing macromolecules such as proteins, nucleic acids and viruses.

History

The ultracentrifuge was invented in the 1920s by the Swedish chemist Theodor Svedberg, who used it to determine the molecular weights of proteins and demonstrated that proteins are discrete molecules rather than colloids, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1926. The unit of sedimentation coefficient, the svedberg (S), is named after him. Edward Greydon Pickels developed the vacuum ultracentrifuge in the 1930s, and commercial instruments from Spinco (later Beckman) made the technique widely available after the Second World War.

Preparative ultracentrifugation

Preparative ultracentrifuges are used to pellet particles such as viruses, organelles and membrane fractions, or to separate molecules in density gradients, for example caesium chloride gradients used historically to separate plasmid DNA and to demonstrate the semi-conservative replication of DNA in the Meselson–Stahl experiment. Rotors operate in a vacuum chamber to eliminate air friction and heating, and the chamber is armoured because a rotor failing at full speed releases enormous energy.

Analytical ultracentrifugation

Analytical ultracentrifuges incorporate optical systems (ultraviolet absorption or refractive-index based Schlieren and interference optics) that observe the sample while it spins. Two fundamental experiments are sedimentation velocity, which yields information about molecular mass, shape and interactions, and sedimentation equilibrium, which gives absolute molecular weights independent of shape. The method experienced a renaissance from the 1990s with improved instruments and computer analysis.
