Mithridates VI Eupator (c. 135–63 BC) was the king of Pontus in northern Anatolia from about 120 BC until his death. He is remembered as one of the Roman Republic's most formidable and determined opponents, fighting three wars against Rome — the Mithridatic Wars — over several decades, against generals including Sulla, Lucullus, and Pompey.

Early life

Mithridates claimed descent from both Persian royalty and the successors of Alexander the Great. Legend held that, fearing poison after his father's assassination, he immunised himself by taking small doses of poisons and antidotes — hence the term mithridatism and the universal antidote called mithridate. He was famed for his learning and was said to speak the languages of all the peoples he ruled, more than twenty nations.

Wars with Rome

Having expanded Pontus around the Black Sea, Mithridates invaded Roman Asia in 88 BC and ordered the massacre of tens of thousands of Roman and Italian residents — the Asiatic Vespers. Defeated by Sulla in the First Mithridatic War, he fought again in the 80s and launched a third war in 73 BC. After initial successes he was driven from his kingdom by Lucullus and then Pompey, fleeing to the Bosporan Kingdom. In 63 BC, betrayed by his son Pharnaces and facing capture, he attempted suicide; when poison failed, he had a bodyguard kill him.

Legacy

Mithridates became a legendary figure in European culture, the subject of works by Racine and an opera by Mozart, and his story exemplified the archetype of the brilliant but doomed enemy of Rome.
