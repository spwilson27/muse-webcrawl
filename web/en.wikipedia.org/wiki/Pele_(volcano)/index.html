Pele (volcano)

Location: Io (moon of Jupiter)
Type: Volcano with plume ("Pele-type")
Coordinates: approx. 18.7°S, 255.3°W

Pele is an active volcano on the surface of Io, a moon of Jupiter. Located on Io's trailing hemisphere at about 18.7°S, 255.3°W, it is famous for its enormous volcanic plume and the huge red ring of sulfur deposits surrounding it — the largest such ring in the Solar System, some 1,400 km across. It is named after Pele, the Hawaiian goddess of volcanoes and fire.

Discovery and observations

Pele's plume was the first active volcanic plume ever observed beyond Earth: it was discovered in March 1979 in images from the Voyager 1 spacecraft, when navigation engineer Linda Morabito noticed a crescent-shaped plume rising some 300 km above Io's limb. Analysis of earlier images showed the plume had been active since at least that encounter. Voyager 2, passing four months later, found the plume had subsided. The Galileo spacecraft imaged Pele repeatedly in the 1990s, and the New Horizons flyby in 2007 caught a fresh plume rising about 320 km.

Characteristics

Pele is the archetype of the "Pele-type" plume: a tall, umbrella-shaped plume driven by sulfur and sulfur dioxide gas escaping from a very hot silicate lava lake in the volcano's caldera (patera). Thermal measurements indicate eruption temperatures among the highest known on Io, in some analyses exceeding 1,600 K, suggesting ultramafic or superheated basaltic lava. The plume deposits red-orange short-chain sulfur (S3 and S4) in a vast ellipse around the volcano; over time the red material fades to yellow as it converts to stable cyclo-octasulfur (S8). The size and brightness of the ring vary as the plume waxes and wanes.

Significance

Pele demonstrates that Io's volcanism is driven by silicate magmatism like Earth's, in contrast to the lower-temperature sulfur volcanism also seen on the moon. Its persistent activity makes it one of the most closely monitored volcanoes in the Solar System.
