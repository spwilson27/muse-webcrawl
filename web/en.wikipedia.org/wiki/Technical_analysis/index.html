Technical analysis

Technical analysis
FieldFinance, securities trading
InputsPrice and volume history
Key conceptsTrend, support and resistance, chart patterns, indicators
FoundersCharles Dow, Richard Schabacker, Robert Edwards, John Magee
ContrastFundamental analysis

Technical analysis is a method of evaluating securities by analysing statistics generated by market activity, such as past prices and volume. Unlike fundamental analysis, which attempts to estimate a security's intrinsic value from financial statements and economic data, technical analysis holds that past trading activity already reflects all relevant information and that prices move in identifiable trends and patterns.

Principles

The discipline rests on three assumptions commonly traced to the Dow Theory of Charles H. Dow, founder of The Wall Street Journal and co-creator of the Dow Jones Industrial Average: (1) market action discounts everything; (2) prices move in trends; and (3) history tends to repeat itself, because market participants react similarly to similar stimuli.

Tools and techniques

Analysts study price charts — line, bar and Japanese candlestick charts — for patterns such as head and shoulders, double tops and bottoms, triangles and flags. Quantitative indicators include moving averages, the relative strength index (RSI), MACD, Bollinger Bands and Fibonacci retracement levels. Volume, breadth and sentiment measures supplement price data. Support and resistance levels mark price zones where buying or selling pressure has historically emerged.

Criticism and evidence

Technical analysis conflicts with the weak form of the efficient-market hypothesis, which holds that past prices cannot predict future ones. Academic studies are mixed: some find modest predictive value in simple rules such as moving-average crossovers, while others attribute apparent profits to data snooping and transaction costs. Surveys nevertheless show the method is widely used by traders, especially in foreign exchange and short-horizon trading.
