Posterior cruciate ligament

The posterior cruciate ligament links the femur and tibia within the knee joint

LocationKnee joint
FromMedial femoral condyle
ToPosterior intercondylar area of tibia
FunctionPrevents posterior displacement of the tibia

The posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) is one of the four main ligaments of the human knee and, along with the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL), one of the two cruciate ligaments that cross within the joint. It runs from the lateral surface of the medial femoral condyle to the posterior intercondylar area of the tibia. The PCL is the strongest ligament of the knee, roughly twice as strong as the ACL, and its primary function is to prevent the tibia from displacing backwards relative to the femur, particularly when the knee is flexed.

Structure

The ligament consists mainly of two functional bundles — a larger anterolateral bundle that tightens in flexion and a smaller posteromedial bundle that tightens in extension — which together stabilise the knee through its range of motion. It also acts as a secondary restraint to rotation and side-to-side stress.

Injury

PCL injuries are considerably less common than ACL tears. They typically result from a direct blow to the front of the flexed knee — classically a dashboard injury in a car crash — or from falling onto a bent knee, or from severe hyperextension. Clinicians test for PCL damage with the posterior drawer test and the posterior sag sign. Many isolated PCL sprains are managed without surgery, through physiotherapy focused on strengthening the quadriceps, which pulls the tibia forward. Surgery to reconstruct the ligament is generally reserved for combined ligament injuries, persistent instability, or injuries involving a bony avulsion.
