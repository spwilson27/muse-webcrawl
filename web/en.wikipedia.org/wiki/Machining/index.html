Machining is a manufacturing process in which a desired shape is obtained by removing material from a larger piece of stock, most commonly by cutting it away with powered machine tools. It is a family of subtractive processes — including turning, milling, drilling, boring, reaming, broaching and grinding — used to produce parts to precise dimensions and fine surface finishes.

Overview

In nearly all machining, a cutting tool moves relative to the workpiece and shears material away as chips or swarf. The three principal machining parameters are cutting speed, feed rate and depth of cut; together they determine the material removal rate, tool life, surface finish and forces involved. Cutting fluids are often applied to cool the cut, lubricate the interface and flush away chips.

Principal operations

  • Turning — the workpiece rotates against a stationary single-point tool on a lathe, producing cylindrical surfaces.
  • Milling — a rotating multi-point cutter moves across a stationary workpiece on a milling machine, producing flat, contoured or slotted surfaces.
  • Drilling — a rotating drill bit creates or enlarges holes; related operations include boring, reaming, tapping and counterboring.
  • Grinding — an abrasive wheel removes small amounts of material to achieve very tight tolerances and smooth finishes.
  • Other processes — planing, shaping, broaching, sawing, and non-traditional methods such as electrical discharge machining (EDM) and water-jet cutting.

History and CNC

Precision machining grew out of 18th- and 19th-century clockmaking and the development of machine tools during the Industrial Revolution; the screw-cutting lathe of Henry Maudslay and the milling machines of the American armories made interchangeable parts practical. In the mid-20th century numerical control, and later computer numerical control (CNC), automated tool movement from programmed instructions, enabling the rapid, repeatable production of complex geometry. Modern machining centres combine milling, drilling and turning with automatic tool changers, and CAD/CAM software generates toolpaths directly from digital models.

Economics

Machining is generally economical for prototypes and low-to-medium volumes, and remains essential wherever tight tolerances — commonly to a few micrometres — are required, as in aerospace, medical devices and precision instruments.
