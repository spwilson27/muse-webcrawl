The strange quark or s quark (from its symbol, s) is the third lightest of all quarks, a type of elementary particle. Strange quarks are found in subatomic particles called hadrons. Examples of hadrons containing strange quarks include kaons (K), strange D mesons (Ds), Sigma baryons (Σ), and other strange particles.

According to the IUPAP, the symbol s should be used, but the symbol "strange" is also used. The strange quark was postulated in 1964 by Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig (alongside the up and down quarks) to explain the Eightfold Way classification scheme of hadrons.

History

In the early days of particle physics (first half of the 20th century), hadrons such as protons, neutrons and pions were thought to be elementary particles. However, new hadrons were discovered; the "particle zoo" grew from a few particles in the early 1930s to dozens in the 1950s. Some particles had unexpectedly long lifetimes; these were called "strange particles". Murray Gell-Mann and Abraham Pais proposed that the discrepancy was due to a new property, later called strangeness, conserved by the strong interaction but not by the weak interaction. The quark model of 1964 explained strangeness as the presence of a new flavour of quark.

Properties

The strange quark has an electric charge of −1/3 e and a bare mass of about 95 MeV/c², making it much heavier than the up and down quarks but far lighter than the charm, bottom, and top quarks. It belongs to the second generation of matter particles. Particles containing strange quarks can decay via the weak interaction into lighter quarks, changing strangeness.
