Aridoamerica
RegionNorthern Mexico and the southwestern United States

Aridoamerica is a cultural and ecological region in pre-Columbian North America, encompassing the arid and semi-arid lands of what is today northern Mexico and parts of the southwestern United States. The term was proposed by anthropologist Paul Kirchhoff in 1954 to complement his earlier concept of Mesoamerica, the civilization area to the south where intensive agriculture supported dense populations and complex states.

Chihuahuan Desert landscape typical of Aridoamerica

Environment and cultures

Aridoamerica's deserts, scrublands, and mountains — including the Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts and the Baja California peninsula — could not support large-scale rainfall agriculture. Its peoples were therefore predominantly hunter-gatherers and small-scale cultivators, living in bands and small villages and relying on desert plants such as mesquite, agave, prickly pear, and piñon. Spanish colonizers lumped many of these groups together as "Chichimeca."

Distribution

The region covered, among other areas, most of Chihuahua, Coahuila, Nuevo León, Tamaulipas, Durango, Zacatecas, San Luis Potosí, Sonora, Sinaloa, and Baja California in Mexico, and parts of Arizona, New Mexico, and Texas in the United States. Within it, the subregion known as Oasisamerica — centered on the river valleys of the Southwest — developed irrigation agriculture and pueblo settlements influenced by trade with Mesoamerica.

Trade and contact

Despite the cultural contrast drawn by Kirchhoff, Aridoamerica was connected to Mesoamerica by trade routes carrying turquoise, shells, copper, macaws, and ballcourts northward. The major trading center of Paquimé (Casas Grandes) in Chihuahua linked the two worlds. Scholars have debated the usefulness of the Aridoamerica concept, noting the diversity of the cultures it groups together.
