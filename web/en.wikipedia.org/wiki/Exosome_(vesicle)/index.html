Exosome (vesicle)

Exosomes are a type of extracellular vesicle (EV) that are released from cells upon fusion of the multivesicular body (MVB) with the cell surface. They range in size from roughly 30 to 150 nanometres and carry a cargo of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids — including microRNAs — from their cell of origin.

Exosomes are among the smallest extracellular vesicles released by cells.

Biogenesis

Exosomes begin as intraluminal vesicles formed by inward budding of the endosomal membrane, creating multivesicular bodies. When an MVB fuses with the plasma membrane instead of a lysosome, these vesicles are released into the extracellular space as exosomes. The ESCRT machinery and ceramide-dependent pathways help sort cargo into the budding vesicles.

Functions

Once dismissed as a means of disposing of cellular waste, exosomes are now understood to mediate intercellular communication: they can transfer functional proteins and RNA between cells, modulate immune responses, and contribute to processes such as antigen presentation, tissue repair, and — in disease — tumour progression and the spread of pathological proteins.

Clinical applications

Because exosomes circulate in blood, urine, saliva and other fluids, they are investigated as biomarkers for "liquid biopsy" diagnostics, especially in cancer. Their natural targeting and low immunogenicity also make them candidates as drug-delivery vehicles.
