Campaign finance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Campaign finance — also called election finance or political finance — refers to the funds raised and spent to influence political campaigns and policy outcomes, and to the laws regulating them. Money in politics is among the oldest democratic controversies: funding can equalise competition (enabling challengers to be heard) or corrupt it (buying access, influence or outcomes), and every democracy regulates it differently.

Models

Instruments include donation caps and bans (corporate, foreign, anonymous), spending limits, disclosure regimes, public funding (subsidies, matching funds, tax credits, free broadcast time) and enforcement commissions. The United States has the world's most expensive elections and the most speech-protective doctrine: Buckley v. Valeo (1976) equated spending limits with speech restrictions, and Citizens United v. FEC (2010) freed independent corporate and union spending, producing Super PACs and "dark money" nonprofits — developments other democracies cite when defending stricter regimes. Many European systems cap spending, restrict paid broadcast advertising and fund parties publicly; Canada combines low donation limits with (until 2015) per-vote subsidies.

Debates

Empirical work finds money matters more for access and agenda-setting than for buying votes outright; reforms cycle through scandal-driven waves (Watergate produced the 1974 US amendments). Cryptocurrency donations, online micro-donations, influencer campaigning and foreign-interference risks are current regulatory frontiers.
