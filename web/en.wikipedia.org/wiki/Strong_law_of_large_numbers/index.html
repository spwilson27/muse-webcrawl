Strong law of large numbers

Sample means converge almost surely to the expected value

The strong law of large numbers (SLLN) is a theorem in probability theory stating that the sample average of a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables converges almost surely to their common expected value, provided the expected value of the absolute value is finite.

Statement

Let X1, X2, ... be i.i.d. random variables with E|X1| < ∞ and E[X1] = μ. Then the averages X̄n = (X1 + ... + Xn)/n satisfy P(lim X̄n = μ) = 1. "Almost sure" convergence means the set of outcomes for which convergence fails has probability zero.

History and proof

The first strong law was proved by Émile Borel in 1909 for fair coin tosses (Bernoulli trials). Francesco Paolo Cantelli extended the result in 1917, and Andrey Kolmogorov proved the definitive theorem in 1930–1933, showing that the condition E|X1| < ∞ is both sufficient and necessary. Kolmogorov's proof uses truncation, maximal inequalities, and the Borel–Cantelli lemma; modern treatments often use martingale methods, deriving the SLLN from the martingale convergence theorem via reversed martingales.

Comparison with the weak law

The weak law of large numbers asserts only convergence in probability—the probability that the average deviates from μ by more than any fixed ε tends to zero. The strong law is strictly stronger: almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability. The distinction matters because almost sure convergence guarantees that a single long-run sequence (for example, one simulation or one polling history) will eventually track the expectation, which underlies the validity of Monte Carlo methods.
