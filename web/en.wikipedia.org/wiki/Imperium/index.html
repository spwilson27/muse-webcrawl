Imperium

Imperium is a Latin word that, in ancient Rome, meant the authority to command — the supreme executive power held by kings and later by senior magistrates of the Roman Republic and Empire. A holder of imperium could command armies, enforce the law, and exercise jurisdiction, and the concept became one of the foundations of Roman constitutional law.

The fasces, carried by lictors before a magistrate, symbolised the power of imperium.

Origins and meaning

The word derives from the verb imperare, "to command". In the earliest period of Rome, imperium belonged to the kings (reges). After the expulsion of the kings in 509 BC, it passed to the two annually elected consuls, and could also be held by praetors, dictators, and certain other officials. The power was conferred by a special law, the lex curiata de imperio, passed by the assembly of the curiae.

Scope of the power

Imperium embraced military command (imperium militiae) outside the sacred boundary of the city, the pomerium, and civil authority (imperium domi) within it. In the field, a commander holding imperium had power of life and death over his soldiers; within the city, the citizen's right of appeal (provocatio) limited this power. The insignia of imperium included the lictors bearing the fasces, the curule chair, and the purple-bordered toga (toga praetexta).

Imperium maius and the Empire

Under the late Republic, extraordinary commands such as those granted to Pompey and Caesar carried imperium maius — "greater imperium" — which outranked that of ordinary provincial governors. Augustus gathered these powers into his own hands: from 23 BC he held proconsular imperium over the imperial provinces and imperium maius over the rest, making the emperor the supreme commander of all Roman forces. The word imperium also came, by extension, to denote the territory ruled — the origin of the English word "empire".
