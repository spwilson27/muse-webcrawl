Despotate of Epirus

The Despotate of Epirus was one of the Greek successor states of the Byzantine Empire established after the Fourth Crusade in 1204, when Constantinople fell to the Latin crusaders. Founded by Michael I Komnenos Doukas, it centred on the region of Epirus in north-western Greece, with its capital at Arta.

History

Michael I and his half-brother Theodore expanded Epirote territory dramatically. Theodore captured Thessalonica in 1224 and was crowned emperor, creating the short-lived Empire of Thessalonica, which rivalled the Empire of Nicaea for the Byzantine inheritance. Epirote ambitions were checked by the Bulgarian victory at Klokotnitsa in 1230.

After the Byzantine restoration of Constantinople in 1261, Epirus survived as a semi-independent state, oscillating between Byzantine overlordship, Angevin and Serbian influence, and periods of effective independence under the Orsini and Tocco families, who ruled from Ioannina and Arta.

Ottoman conquest

The Ottomans took Ioannina in 1430 and Arta in 1449; the last Epirote territory, Vonitsa, fell in 1479, ending the Despotate.

Legacy

The Despotate is remembered for its churches in Arta, such as the Paregoretissa, and as one of the principal Greek states preserving Byzantine culture during the Latin occupation of Constantinople.
