Motor protein

Motor protein
ClassMolecular motors
Energy sourceATP hydrolysis (usually)
TracksActin filaments, microtubules
Major familiesMyosins, kinesins, dyneins
FunctionsTransport, muscle contraction, cell division, motility

Motor proteins are a class of molecular motors that convert chemical energy, typically from the hydrolysis of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), into mechanical work, moving along cytoskeletal filaments within cells. They power intracellular transport of vesicles and organelles, muscle contraction, the beating of cilia and flagella, chromosome segregation in mitosis, and many other forms of biological motion. Motor proteins generally move unidirectionally along their filament tracks, with catalytic "head" domains that bind the filament and hydrolyse ATP, coupled to a conformational change that produces a step.

Classes

The three best-studied superfamilies are the myosins, which move along actin filaments and power muscle contraction (myosin II) and membrane transport (myosins I and V); the kinesins, which mostly walk toward the plus end of microtubules, hauling cargo and organising the mitotic spindle; and the dyneins, large complexes that move toward the microtubule minus end — cytoplasmic dynein drives much organelle positioning and retrograde axonal transport, while axonemal dyneins slide microtubules in cilia and flagella to produce beating. Plants rely chiefly on kinesins and myosins, lacking cytoplasmic dynein.

Mechanism

Motors such as kinesin-1 advance in ~8 nm steps, one per ATP hydrolysed, with the two heads alternating in a "hand-over-hand" fashion so the motor stays attached to the track over long runs (processivity). Myosin V takes ~36 nm steps along actin, matching the filament's helical pitch. Optical-tweezer experiments have measured the forces these motors generate, typically a few piconewtons, and revealed how load and nucleotide state regulate stepping.

Cellular roles and disease

Motor-driven transport is essential in neurons, whose axons can be a metre long; defects in kinesin and dynein/dynactin components are linked to neurodegenerative disease and neurodevelopmental disorders, and myosin mutations cause cardiomyopathies and deafness. Intracellular pathogens, including viruses such as herpesviruses, hijack host motors to reach the nucleus. Polymerases and helicases moving along nucleic acids are sometimes classed as motor proteins as well.
