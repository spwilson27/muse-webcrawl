Pre-exposure prophylaxis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pre-exposure prophylaxis, commonly abbreviated as PrEP, is the use of medications to prevent the spread of disease in people who have not yet been exposed to a disease-causing agent, usually a virus. In common usage the term refers principally to HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis: the use of antiretroviral drugs by HIV-negative people to reduce the risk of acquiring HIV.

HIV PrEP

The most widely used regimen is a daily oral combination of tenofovir disoproxil fumarate and emtricitabine, approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2012. A second oral combination, tenofovir alafenamide with emtricitabine, followed in 2019. Long-acting injectable cabotegravir, given every two months, was approved in 2021, and long-acting lenacapavir injections have since been introduced in some settings. When taken as prescribed, PrEP reduces the risk of acquiring HIV through sex by about 99 percent.

Evidence

Major trials — including iPrEx (2010), Partners PrEP, and TDF2 — demonstrated substantial reductions in HIV incidence among adherent users, and PrEP use has been associated with declining HIV diagnoses in cities with high uptake. Effectiveness depends strongly on adherence; drug-level studies show protection rises with the number of weekly doses taken.

Use and monitoring

Guidelines recommend PrEP for people at substantial risk of HIV acquisition, with HIV testing before initiation and at regular intervals, along with screening for other sexually transmitted infections and monitoring of kidney function for tenofovir-based regimens. The World Health Organization has recommended offering oral PrEP as an additional prevention choice since 2015.

Other uses of the term

Prophylaxis before exposure is also used in other contexts, for example rabies vaccination for people at occupational risk; treatment given after a possible exposure is termed post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP).
