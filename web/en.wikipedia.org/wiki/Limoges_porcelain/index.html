Limoges porcelain

Limoges porcelain is hard-paste porcelain produced by factories in and around the city of Limoges in southwestern France. It is renowned for its whiteness, translucency, and finely painted decoration, and the name is protected as a geographical indication for porcelain fired in the Limoges region.

History

The industry arose after the discovery in 1768 of rich deposits of kaolin — the essential ingredient of hard-paste porcelain — at Saint-Yrieix-la-Perche near Limoges. A first manufactory was established under royal patronage in 1771 and was later acquired by the crown and associated with the Sèvres manufactory. After the French Revolution, numerous private factories were founded, and during the 19th century Limoges became one of the world's great porcelain centres, supplying white ware blanks ("whiteware") that were decorated in Paris studios and abroad.

Manufacture and makers

Important makers include Haviland, Bernardaud, and Raynaud. Limoges firms became especially prominent in the American market from the 1860s, when the Haviland family of New York established production in Limoges. Factories produced tableware, decorative boxes, and enamelled pieces; the region had also been famous since the Middle Ages for champlevé enamel (Limoges enamel), a distinct craft.
