Air navigation is the process of planning, recording and controlling the movement of an aircraft from one place to another. It combines the science of determining the aircraft's position and direction with the practical tasks of route planning, fuel calculation and compliance with air traffic procedures.

Methods

The classical techniques are pilotage (navigating by reference to visible landmarks) and dead reckoning (computing position from heading, airspeed, wind and elapsed time). The basic dead-reckoning calculation uses the wind triangle, historically worked with a circular slide rule known to pilots as the "whiz wheel".

Radio and satellite navigation

From the mid-20th century, ground-based radio aids became standard: the non-directional beacon (NDB), the VHF omnidirectional range (VOR), distance measuring equipment (DME) and the instrument landing system (ILS) for approaches. Modern airliners navigate primarily by satellite navigation (GNSS, principally GPS) combined with inertial reference systems, all managed through the flight management system, which follows a programmed route of waypoints along published airways.

Rules and planning

Flights operate under visual flight rules (VFR) or instrument flight rules (IFR). Navigation planning must account for wind, weather, airspace restrictions, terrain clearance, performance and fuel reserves, and IFR flights file a flight plan with air traffic control.
