Immunophenotyping

Immunophenotyping is the process of identifying cells based on the types of antigens or markers on the surface of the cell. This process is widely used in basic science research and in laboratory diagnostics, particularly for the diagnosis and classification of leukaemias and lymphomas. It involves labelling cells with antibodies conjugated to fluorescent dyes and analysing them by flow cytometry or by microscopy of tissue sections (immunohistochemistry).

Method

White blood cells (or cells from bone marrow, lymph node, or other tissue) are incubated with a panel of monoclonal antibodies, each bound to a different fluorochrome and each specific for a particular cell-surface or intracellular molecule. As cells pass one by one through a laser beam, the fluorescence emitted reveals which markers each cell expresses and at what intensity. Analysis software displays the results as scatter plots and histograms that a trained analyst interprets.

Markers are conventionally referred to by their cluster of differentiation (CD) numbers—for example CD3 for T cells, CD19 and CD20 for B cells, CD34 for haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, and CD45 for all leukocytes.

Applications

  • Diagnosis of haematological malignancies: distinguishing acute myeloid leukaemia from acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, classifying B-cell and T-cell lymphomas, and identifying chronic lymphocytic leukaemia by its characteristic marker profile.
  • Measurable residual disease: detecting very small numbers of residual malignant cells after treatment, which guides prognosis and therapy.
  • Immunodeficiency monitoring: counting CD4 T cells in HIV infection is a form of immunophenotyping that historically guided antiretroviral treatment.
  • Paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria: diagnosis by demonstrating loss of GPI-anchored proteins such as CD55 and CD59 on blood cells.
  • Stem cell enumeration: counting CD34-positive cells in grafts for haematopoietic stem cell transplantation.
