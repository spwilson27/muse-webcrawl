Citrinin

Citrinin is a mycotoxin originally isolated from Penicillium citrinum in 1931. It is produced by several species of the genera Penicillium, Aspergillus and Monascus, and has been found as a contaminant of cereals, grains and other stored plant products, as well as in red yeast rice products fermented with Monascus. Citrinin is a polyketide with the molecular formula C13H14O5.

Chemical structure of citrinin.

Properties

Citrinin forms lemon-yellow crystals and is only sparingly soluble in water but soluble in dilute alkali and many organic solvents. It shows fluorescence under ultraviolet light, a property used in its detection. It decomposes at high temperatures, and its stability in food processing has been studied because of its presence in fermented products.

Toxicity

Citrinin acts mainly as a nephrotoxin in all animal species tested, although the potency varies, and it has also been reported to be hepatotoxic at higher doses. It is suspected to have contributed to Balkan endemic nephropathy, a kidney disease associated with contaminated food in parts of southeastern Europe, in combination with other mycotoxins such as ochratoxin A. Citrinin can occur alongside ochratoxin A in grains because some fungi produce both toxins. Regulatory bodies including the European Food Safety Authority have assessed dietary exposure; the European Union has set maximum levels for citrinin in food supplements based on red yeast rice.

Occurrence

Citrinin has been detected in wheat, barley, oats, rye, maize and other stored commodities, particularly when improperly dried or stored. It was one of the compounds associated with "yellow rice" toxins studied in Japan after the Second World War.
