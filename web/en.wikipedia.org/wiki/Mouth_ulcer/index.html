A mouth ulcer is an open sore on the mucous membrane lining the mouth. Mouth ulcers are common and often painful, making eating, drinking, and speaking uncomfortable. Most are harmless and heal by themselves within one to two weeks, but persistent ulcers can occasionally indicate systemic disease or, rarely, oral cancer.

Types and causes

The most common type is the aphthous ulcer (canker sore), which recurs in episodes in a condition called recurrent aphthous stomatitis. Aphthous ulcers are round or oval, with a white or yellow centre and a red border. Their cause is not fully understood; local trauma, stress, hormonal changes, and certain foods are recognised triggers.

Other causes of oral ulceration include mechanical trauma (such as biting the cheek or sharp teeth), thermal or chemical burns, viral infections such as herpes simplex, bacterial and fungal infections, nutritional deficiencies (iron, vitamin B12, folate), coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and Behçet's disease. A non-healing ulcer lasting more than about three weeks warrants professional evaluation to exclude malignancy.

Treatment

Treatment is usually aimed at relieving pain and speeding healing: protective gels, antiseptic or anti-inflammatory mouthwashes, topical corticosteroids, and avoiding spicy or acidic foods. Underlying causes, such as a sharp tooth or a vitamin deficiency, should be addressed where identified.
