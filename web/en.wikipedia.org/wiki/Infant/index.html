Infant

An infant is a very young child, from birth to about one year of age. A baby in the first 28 days of life is called a newborn or neonate. The word "infant" comes from the Latin infans, "unable to speak." After infancy comes the toddler stage, when the child begins to walk.

Development

Infancy is a period of extraordinarily rapid growth and development. A newborn typically weighs about 3.2–3.4 kilograms and doubles its birth weight within roughly five months. During the first year infants develop motor skills in a predictable sequence — lifting the head, rolling over, sitting, crawling, and often walking by around twelve months — and progress from reflexive crying to babbling and, usually by the end of the first year, first words. Vision, hearing, and social responsiveness mature quickly; infants form attachments to caregivers and by about eight months may show separation anxiety and stranger wariness.

Feeding and care

Health authorities such as the World Health Organization recommend exclusive breastfeeding for about the first six months of life, followed by continued breastfeeding alongside complementary foods. Formula feeding is the principal alternative. Infants require frequent feeding, sleep of up to 16–17 hours a day in the newborn period, and protection from infection, as their immune systems are still maturing. Safe-sleep guidance — placing infants on their backs on a firm, clear surface — has greatly reduced deaths from sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS).

Health

Regular check-ups monitor growth and development, and vaccination schedules in most countries begin in the first months of life. Global infant mortality — deaths before the first birthday per 1,000 live births — has fallen dramatically over the past century thanks to sanitation, nutrition, antibiotics, and immunisation, though large disparities remain between countries.
