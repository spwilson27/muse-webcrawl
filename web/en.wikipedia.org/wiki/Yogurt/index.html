Yogurt (also spelled yoghurt or yoghourt) is a food produced by bacterial fermentation of milk. The bacteria used, known as yogurt cultures, ferment lactose, the sugar in milk, producing lactic acid, which gives yogurt its characteristic tangy flavor and gel-like texture.

History

Fermented milk products have existed for thousands of years, probably arising wherever milk animals were herded in Central Asia and the Balkans. The word comes from Turkish yoğurt. Yogurt reached Western Europe and North America in the early 20th century; Isaac Carasso industrialized production in Barcelona in 1919, founding the company that became Danone. In the 1900s, Nobel laureate Élie Metchnikoff popularized the idea that yogurt contributed to the longevity of Bulgarian peasants, launching scientific interest in probiotics.

Production

Milk is heated, cooled to about 43 °C, inoculated with Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus, and incubated for several hours. Greek yogurt is strained to remove whey, producing a thicker, higher-protein product. Yogurt is used in cooking across many cuisines: as raita in India, tzatziki in Greece, marinades and sauces in the Middle East, and drinks such as lassi and ayran.

Nutrition and health

Yogurt is rich in protein, calcium, B vitamins, and phosphorus. Live-culture yogurt is easier for many lactose-intolerant people to digest than milk, since bacterial lactase breaks down some lactose. Sweetened commercial yogurts may contain substantial added sugar, sometimes sweetened with sugar substitutes in "light" varieties. Frozen yogurt is a popular dessert related to sorbet and ice cream.
