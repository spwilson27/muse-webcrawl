Foreshortening

Foreshortening is the visual effect or optical illusion that causes an object or distance to appear shorter than it actually is because it is angled toward the viewer. In art, it is a technique used in perspective to create the illusion of depth and to make objects recede into the picture plane, giving the impression that a limb or body extends toward the viewer.

In art

Foreshortening is particularly important in depictions of the human figure, since apparent shortening of limbs when viewed at an angle gives a composition dynamism and realism. The technique developed rapidly during the Italian Renaissance as artists systematized linear perspective. Paolo Uccello experimented extensively with foreshortened figures and objects. Perhaps the most celebrated example is Andrea Mantegna's The Lamentation over the Dead Christ (c. 1480), in which Christ's body is dramatically foreshortened, viewed from the feet. Michelangelo's figures on the Sistine Chapel ceiling and Caravaggio's paintings also rely heavily on the effect.

In other fields

In cartography and astronomy, apparent foreshortening occurs when surface features near the limb of a sphere appear compressed — for example, sunspots near the edge of the solar disk, or lunar craters near the Moon's limb. In photography, telephoto lenses are popularly said to compress perspective, though the compression results from viewing distance rather than focal length itself.
