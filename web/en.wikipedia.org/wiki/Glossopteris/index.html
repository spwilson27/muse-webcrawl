Glossopteris

Glossopteris is a genus of extinct seed ferns (pteridosperms) that dominated the flora of the southern supercontinent Gondwana during the Permian period, roughly 300 to 250 million years ago. Named from the Greek glossa (tongue) and pteris (fern), in reference to the tongue-shaped leaves, the plant is one of the most important pieces of fossil evidence for the theory of continental drift and plate tectonics.

Description

The leaves of Glossopteris are simple, lanceolate to tongue-shaped, with a prominent central midrib and a dense network of fine, reticulate (net-like) secondary veins — a pattern unlike that of any living fern. Leaves ranged from a few centimetres to over 30 cm in length. The plant is reconstructed as a woody shrub or tree, possibly reaching several metres in height, with stems assigned to the form genus Vertebraria, whose wood shows distinctive wedge-shaped segments and clear growth rings indicating strongly seasonal climates.

Reproductive structures were borne on or near the leaves. Pollen-producing organs and ovule-bearing structures (such as Lidgettonia) show that Glossopteris reproduced by seeds rather than spores, placing it among the seed ferns.

Distribution and significance

Fossils of Glossopteris have been found in South America, Africa, Antarctica, India, Australia and Madagascar — landmasses that today are separated by wide oceans. Because the plant's heavy seeds could not have crossed thousands of kilometres of open ocean, the matching distributions were explained by Alfred Wegener and later geologists as evidence that these continents were once joined in the supercontinent Gondwana. Fossils recovered from Antarctica by the Scott expedition of 1911–12, found near the Beardmore Glacier, became a celebrated part of this story.

Ecology

The Glossopteris flora grew in high southern latitudes under cool temperate conditions with extreme seasonal variation in daylight. Growth rings and the deciduous habit inferred for the plant suggest it shed its leaves during the dark polar winters. The flora disappeared at the end-Permian mass extinction, the largest extinction event in Earth's history.
