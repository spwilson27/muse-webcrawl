Guthrie test

The Guthrie test (also called the Guthrie bacterial inhibition assay) is a medical test performed on newborn infants to detect phenylketonuria (PKU), an inborn error of metabolism that causes intellectual disability if untreated. Developed by American microbiologist Robert Guthrie in 1961, it became the model for universal newborn screening programmes worldwide.

Procedure

A few drops of blood are taken by heel prick from the baby, usually at five to eight days of age, and blotted onto a filter-paper card (the "Guthrie card"). A disc punched from the dried blood spot is placed on an agar plate seeded with Bacillus subtilis spores and a substance that inhibits bacterial growth unless phenylalanine is present. If the blood contains excess phenylalanine, the inhibition is overcome and bacterial growth around the disc indicates a positive result, prompting confirmatory testing.

History

Before Guthrie's assay, PKU testing relied on a ferric chloride urine test in older infants, by which time brain damage had begun. Guthrie, whose own son and niece were affected by developmental disorders, designed a cheap, mass-producible test that could be run in central laboratories on thousands of samples. Pilot screening began in Buffalo, New York, in 1961–62; the test's adoption in Massachusetts in 1962–63 made that state the first to mandate newborn PKU screening.

Legacy

The Guthrie card is still the standard means of collecting newborn blood spots, though tandem mass spectrometry has largely replaced the bacterial assay itself and expanded screening to dozens of metabolic, endocrine, and genetic disorders, including congenital hypothyroidism and cystic fibrosis.
