Deianira

Deianira, or Deianeira (Ancient Greek: Δηϊάνειρα, "destroyer of men" or "man-destroyer"), was a Calydonian princess in Greek mythology, the daughter of King Oeneus and Althaea, and the sister of Meleager. She is best known as the second wife of the hero Heracles, whose death she unwittingly caused.

Mythology

According to the myth, Heracles wrestled the river god Achelous — breaking off one of his horns — for Deianira's hand, in fulfilment of a promise made to her brother Meleager's shade in the underworld. After the marriage, the couple left Calydon when Heracles accidentally killed a servant. At the river Evenus, the centaur Nessus offered to carry Deianira across, then attempted to assault her; Heracles shot him with an arrow poisoned with the blood of the Hydra. As he died, Nessus told Deianira that his blood, smeared on a garment, would act as a charm to keep her husband's love.

Years later, fearing that Heracles favoured the captive princess Iole, Deianira anointed a tunic (the "shirt of Nessus") with the centaur's blood and sent it to him. The poison caused Heracles unbearable agony; he built his own funeral pyre on Mount Oeta and was taken up to Olympus and deified. On learning what she had done, Deianira killed herself, by hanging or by the sword in different accounts.

In literature

Deianira is the central figure of Sophocles' tragedy Women of Trachis (the Trachiniae), which portrays her sympathetically as anxious and loving rather than malicious. She also appears in Book 9 of Ovid's Metamorphoses, in Apollodorus's Library, and in later treatments including Handel's oratorio Hercules.
