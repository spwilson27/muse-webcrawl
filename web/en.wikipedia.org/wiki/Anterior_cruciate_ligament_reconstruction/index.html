Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction

Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACL reconstruction) is a surgical procedure in which a torn anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) in the knee is replaced with a graft, usually a tendon taken from the patient (autograft) or a donor (allograft). The ACL is one of the four main ligaments of the knee and is essential for stabilizing the joint, particularly during pivoting and cutting movements common in sports such as football, basketball, and skiing.

Background

ACL tears are among the most common knee injuries, with an estimated 150,000 to 200,000 cases per year in the United States alone. They most often occur during non-contact deceleration, sudden changes of direction, or awkward landings from jumps. Because the ACL has limited capacity to heal on its own once completely torn, surgical reconstruction is the standard treatment for young, active patients who wish to return to pivoting sports.

Procedure

Reconstruction is usually performed arthroscopically. The surgeon removes the remnants of the torn ligament and drills tunnels in the tibia and femur through which the graft is passed and fixed with screws or other fixation devices. Common autograft choices include the patellar tendon, hamstring tendons (semitendinosus and gracilis), and the quadriceps tendon.

Rehabilitation

Recovery typically takes six to twelve months. Rehabilitation progresses through stages emphasizing restoration of range of motion, quadriceps strength, neuromuscular control, and finally sport-specific agility training before return to play. Modern criteria-based return-to-sport testing aims to reduce the risk of re-injury, which remains substantial, particularly in adolescents.

Outcomes and risks

Most patients regain knee stability and return to activity, but risks include graft failure, infection, stiffness (arthrofibrosis), and an increased long-term risk of osteoarthritis. See also knee anatomy resources and related orthopedic topics.
