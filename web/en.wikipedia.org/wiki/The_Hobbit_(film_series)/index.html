The Hobbit is a trilogy of epic fantasy adventure films directed by Peter Jackson, based on the 1937 novel The Hobbit by J. R. R. Tolkien. The films serve as a prequel to Jackson's The Lord of the Rings trilogy (2001–2003). The series consists of An Unexpected Journey (2012), The Desolation of Smaug (2013), and The Battle of the Five Armies (2014).

The screenplay was written by Jackson, Fran Walsh, Philippa Boyens, and Guillermo del Toro, who was originally attached to direct before departing the project in 2010. The films were shot back-to-back in New Zealand, with principal photography beginning in March 2011 and concluding in July 2012.

Plot overview

The trilogy follows the hobbit Bilbo Baggins, who is swept into a quest by the wizard Gandalf the Grey and a company of thirteen dwarves led by Thorin Oakenshield to reclaim the Lonely Mountain (Erebor) and its treasure from the dragon Smaug. Along the way Bilbo encounters Gollum and acquires a magic ring, and the company battles trolls, goblins, orcs, wargs, giant spiders, and wood-elves. Subplots drawn from Tolkien's appendices follow Gandalf's investigation of a rising evil at Dol Guldur and the growing conflict over the Lonely Mountain that culminates in the Battle of the Five Armies.

Production

Development began in the mid-2000s, delayed by legal disputes over distribution rights and by MGM's financial difficulties. Guillermo del Toro was hired to direct in 2008 and worked on the project for nearly two years before leaving in May 2010 because of ongoing delays; Jackson then took over as director. The project was originally conceived as two films, but in July 2012 Jackson announced the story would be expanded into a trilogy.

The first film was notably shot at a frame rate of 48 frames per second, twice the industry standard of 24, a choice that drew mixed reactions from critics and audiences. The films made extensive use of CGI and digital environments alongside practical sets built in Wellington, New Zealand.

Reception

The trilogy was a major commercial success, grossing nearly $3 billion worldwide against a combined production budget of roughly $700–745 million, making it one of the most expensive film productions ever undertaken. Critical reception was generally positive but less enthusiastic than for The Lord of the Rings trilogy; reviewers praised the performances, production design, and Howard Shore's score while criticising the trilogy's length and pacing. The films received numerous award nominations, including three Academy Award nominations for An Unexpected Journey.
