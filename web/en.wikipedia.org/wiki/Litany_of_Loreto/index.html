The Litany of Loreto, also called the Litany of the Blessed Virgin Mary, is a Marian litany in the Roman Catholic Church, originally prayed at the Shrine of the Holy House (Santa Casa) in Loreto, Italy. It is one of only a handful of litanies approved for public prayer in the Catholic Church — along with the Litany of the Saints, the Litany of the Sacred Heart, and a few others — and is among the best known of all Marian devotions.

The litany consists of a series of invocations of Mary under her many titles, each answered by the response ora pro nobis ("pray for us"). It opens with the Kyrie eleison and invocations of the Holy Trinity, and closes with invocations of Christ as the Lamb of God.

History

Litanies in honour of the Virgin Mary multiplied in the late Middle Ages. The Loreto form developed at the shrine of the Holy House, where pilgrims had prayed it from at least the early 16th century; an early printed version appears in a 1558 collection of prayers. In 1587 Pope Sixtus V formally approved the Loreto text, and in 1601 Clement VIII restricted public use of Marian litanies to this approved form, suppressing the many variants then in circulation.

Over the centuries successive popes have added invocations: "Help of Christians" (Auxilium Christianorum) is associated with the victory at Lepanto in 1571; "Queen of Peace" was added by Benedict XV during the First World War; "Mother of the Church" by Paul VI; and in 2020 Pope Francis added "Mother of Mercy", "Mother of Hope", and "Solace of Migrants".

Structure and invocations

After the opening Kyrie and the invocations of the Trinity, the litany addresses Mary in grouped titles:

Holy Mary, pray for us.
Holy Mother of God,
Holy Virgin of virgins,
Mother of Christ,
Mother of the Church,
Mother of divine grace…
Mirror of justice,
Seat of wisdom,
Cause of our joy,
Tower of David,
Tower of ivory,
House of gold,
Ark of the covenant,
Gate of heaven,
Morning star,
Health of the sick,
Refuge of sinners,
Queen of angels,
Queen of patriarchs,
Queen of prophets,
Queen of apostles,
Queen of martyrs,
Queen of all saints,
Queen of peace, pray for us.

The litany concludes with the threefold Agnus Dei ("Lamb of God, who takes away the sins of the world…") and a closing prayer.

Musical settings

The Litany of Loreto has been set to music by many composers, including Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, who composed two settings of the Litaniae Lauretanae (K. 109 and K. 195) in Salzburg in the 1770s.
