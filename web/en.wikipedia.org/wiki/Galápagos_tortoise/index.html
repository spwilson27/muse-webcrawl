Galápagos tortoise

Galápagos tortoise
Scientific name: Chelonoidis niger complex
Family: Testudinidae
Habitat: Galápagos Islands, Ecuador
Conservation status: Vulnerable to Critically Endangered (varies by taxon)

The Galápagos tortoise or Galápagos giant tortoise (Chelonoidis niger, with a complex of subspecies and species) is a very large species of tortoise in the family Testudinidae. It is endemic to the Galápagos Islands off the coast of Ecuador. The tortoise is among the longest-living vertebrates, with a lifespan of over 100 years in the wild and up to around 177 years in captivity.

Description

Modern Galápagos tortoises can weigh up to 417 kilograms (919 lb). Shell shapes vary between populations: tortoises from humid highland islands have large, domed shells, while those from arid lowland islands have smaller, "saddleback" shells that allow greater upward reach to browse tall vegetation such as cactus.

Evolution and Darwin

The variation in shell shape between islands was noted by Charles Darwin during the voyage of the Beagle in 1835 and contributed, along with the mockingbirds and finches, to his thinking on evolution by natural selection.

Conservation

Populations were devastated by whalers and fur sealers, who collected tortoises as a source of fresh meat that could survive for months aboard ship, and by introduced species such as goats, rats and pigs. Captive breeding and repatriation programmes, including the work associated with the famous tortoise Lonesome George (died 2012), have restored several populations.
