Cyclohexane conformation

The chair conformation of cyclohexane, showing axial (vertical) and equatorial substituent positions.

A cyclohexane conformation is any of the three-dimensional shapes adopted by a molecule of cyclohexane (C6H12). Of these, the chair conformation is by far the most stable and the most important: at any instant the great majority of cyclohexane molecules are in a chair form. In the chair, all carbon–carbon bond angles are close to the ideal tetrahedral value of 109.5°, and all neighbouring C–H bonds are staggered, so both angle strain and torsional strain are essentially absent.

The chair form

In a chair, the twelve hydrogen atoms divide into two sets of six. The axial hydrogens point straight up or straight down, parallel to the principal axis of the ring, alternating in direction around the ring. The equatorial hydrogens lie roughly in the "equator" of the ring, splayed outward. The two chair forms of cyclohexane interconvert rapidly at room temperature by a process called ring flipping (ring inversion), passing through higher-energy half-chair, twist-boat and boat conformations. On flipping, every axial substituent becomes equatorial and vice versa, while "up" substituents remain up and "down" remain down.

Substituted cyclohexanes

Relative energies (approx.)
Chair: 0 kJ/mol (reference)
Twist-boat: ~23 kJ/mol
Boat: ~27 kJ/mol
Half-chair: ~45 kJ/mol
Interconversion barrier: ~45 kJ/mol

For a substituted cyclohexane, the two chair conformers are usually of unequal energy. Bulky substituents suffer steric crowding in the axial position — so-called 1,3-diaxial interactions — and prefer the equatorial position; the energy cost of placing a group axial is quantified by its A-value. A tert-butyl group, with an A-value of about 20 kJ/mol, effectively locks the ring in the conformer that places it equatorial. Conformational analysis, developed by Derek Barton and Odd Hassel (who shared the 1969 Nobel Prize in Chemistry), uses these principles to predict the stereochemistry and reactivity of cyclic molecules, and is fundamental to understanding the shapes of sugars, steroids and many other natural products.

Other conformations

The boat conformation has two carbons bent to the same side; it suffers from torsional strain and a steric clash between its two "flagpole" hydrogens, and is a transition structure rather than a stable form. The twist-boat relieves some of this strain and is a genuine intermediate on the interconversion pathway, lying between the chair and the boat.
