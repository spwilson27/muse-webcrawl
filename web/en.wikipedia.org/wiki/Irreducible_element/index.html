Irreducible element

In abstract algebra, a non-zero non-unit element in an integral domain is said to be irreducible if it is not a product of two non-units. A weaker concept is that of a prime element: a non-zero element p such that whenever p divides a product ab, p divides a or p divides b.

Relations between notions

Every prime element is irreducible in an integral domain, but the converse is not true in general. The two notions coincide in unique factorisation domains (UFDs), and more generally in GCD domains. An element that is neither prime nor a product in the required sense illustrates the failure of unique factorisation: in the ring Z[√−5], for example, 6 = 2·3 = (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5) gives two genuinely different factorisations into irreducibles.

Examples

In the integers, irreducible elements are exactly the prime numbers (up to sign). In a polynomial ring over a field, irreducible elements are the irreducible polynomials. In the Gaussian integers, the rational prime 5 is not irreducible, since 5 = (2 + i)(2 − i).
