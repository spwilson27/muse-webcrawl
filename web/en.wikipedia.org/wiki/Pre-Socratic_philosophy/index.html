Pre-Socratic philosophy

Pre-Socratic philosophy, also known as Early Greek Philosophy, is ancient Greek philosophy before Socrates and, by extension, includes contemporaries of Socrates whose thought was not influenced by him. In German, the term Vorsokratiker was used. It was popularized by Hermann Diels, who in 1903 published Fragmente der Vorsokratiker (The Fragments of the Pre-Socratics), collecting the surviving quotations and testimony about these thinkers, whose own writings are almost entirely lost.

The pre-Socratics are traditionally divided into several schools: the Milesian school, the Pythagoreans, the Eleatics, the Heracliteans, the pluralists, and the atomists. Their central concern was cosmology and ontology: the origin and structure of the universe, and the question of what everything is ultimately made of — the archē or "beginning/principle". In posing rational, naturalistic explanations in place of myth, they are often credited with founding Western philosophy and science.

Origins and terminology

Greek philosophy is generally held to begin in the early 6th century BC in Miletus, a prosperous Greek city on the coast of Ionia (in present-day Turkey), with Thales of Miletus. The label "pre-Socratic" is a modern scholarly convenience rather than an ancient category: it groups thinkers separated by more than a century and by geography, from Ionia to Elea and Abdera. Thinkers such as Democritus were in fact contemporaries of Socrates, but are classed as pre-Socratic because their thought developed independently of Socratic influence.

The Milesians

Thales proposed water as the archē of all things. His successor Anaximander posited the apeiron — the "indefinite" or "boundless" — as the origin from which opposites such as hot and cold emerge, and produced an early theory of the evolution of animals and one of the first maps of the world. Anaximenes chose air as the first principle, explaining change through rarefaction and condensation.

Pythagoras and Xenophanes

Pythagoras of Samos founded a religious-philosophical community in Croton, in southern Italy, teaching that number is the essence of reality and that the soul transmigrates. Xenophanes of Colophon criticized the anthropomorphism of Homeric religion, arguing that there is "one god, greatest among gods and men, in no way similar to mortals either in body or in thought".

Heraclitus and Parmenides

Heraclitus of Ephesus taught that all things are in flux (panta rhei), that the world is governed by the logos, and that opposites are unified in a process of strife, with fire as the fundamental element. Parmenides of Elea argued in his poem On Nature that being is one, eternal, indivisible, and unchanging, and that change and plurality are illusions — a doctrine defended by his student Zeno of Elea in his famous paradoxes. The Eleatic challenge shaped all subsequent Greek metaphysics.

The pluralists and atomists

LATER thinkers attempted to reconcile Parmenides' arguments with the evident reality of change. Empedocles proposed four eternal "roots" — earth, air, fire, and water — mixed and separated by the forces of Love and Strife. Anaxagoras posited an infinite mixture of "seeds" ordered by a cosmic Mind (nous). Leucippus and Democritus developed atomism: reality consists of indivisible atoms moving in the void, and all qualitative differences arise from differences in atomic shape, arrangement, and position.

The sophists

The sophists, itinerant teachers of rhetoric and virtue such as Protagoras and Gorgias, are sometimes included among the pre-Socratics. Protagoras' dictum that "man is the measure of all things" marks a turn from cosmology toward epistemology and ethics.

Legacy

Aristotle treated the pre-Socratics as his predecessors in the search for causes, and his accounts in the Metaphysics remain a major source for their thought, though shaped by his own system. Their surviving fragments — preserved in quotations by later authors such as Simplicius — are standardly cited by the Diels–Kranz numbering system (e.g. DK 22B1 for a fragment of Heraclitus).
