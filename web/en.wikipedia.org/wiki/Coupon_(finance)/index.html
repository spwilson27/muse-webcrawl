Coupon (finance)

In finance, a coupon is the periodic interest payment made by the issuer of a bond to its holder during the life of the bond. The coupon rate is the annual interest rate paid, expressed as a percentage of the bond's face (par) value.

Terminology

The term derives from bearer bonds, which were issued with physical coupons attached. On each payment date the holder would clip off a coupon and present it to the issuer or its agent to receive the interest due. Although modern bonds are almost entirely electronic ("book-entry"), the terminology survives: a bond's stated interest payment is still called its coupon, and a bond sold without accrued interest adjustments is said to trade with its coupon terms unchanged.

Coupon rate and yield

The coupon rate is fixed at issuance for a standard fixed-rate bond and does not change with market prices; what changes is the bond's yield, which adjusts as the market price moves above or below par. A bond whose coupon rate is higher than its yield trades at a premium, and one whose coupon is lower trades at a discount.

Zero-coupon bonds

A zero-coupon bond pays no periodic interest. Instead it is sold at a deep discount to face value and redeemed at par at maturity, with the investor's return consisting entirely of the price appreciation. United States Treasury STRIPS are a well-known example.
