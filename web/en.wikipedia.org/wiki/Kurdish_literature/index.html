Kurdish literature (Kurdish: وێژەی کوردی, Wêjeya Kurdî) is literature written in the Kurdish languages, principally the Kurmanji and Sorani dialects. Its history spans a rich oral tradition — maintained by dengbêj singers who memorised and performed epics and ballads — and a written tradition of classical poetry that flourished from the 15th century in the Kurdish principalities.

Classical period

The first named Kurdish poets include Ali Hariri (11th century) and, in the classical age, Melayê Cizîrî (1570–1640), whose divan of philosophical Sufi verse remains foundational, and Feqiyê Teyran (1590–1660), author of narrative poems including The Story of Sheikh Sanan. The masterpiece of classical Kurdish literature is Mem û Zîn (1692) by Ahmad Khani (1650–1707), a tragic romance of Mem and Zin that is also read as an early statement of Kurdish national consciousness. In the 19th century, Nalî (1797–1877) pioneered classical poetry in Sorani, while Haji Qadir Koyi championed vernacular, politically engaged verse.

Modern literature

The 20th century brought journalism and print: the first Kurdish newspaper, Kurdistan, appeared in Cairo in 1898, and the journal Hawar (1932), founded in Damascus by Celadet Alî Bedirxan, established the Latin-based Kurmanji alphabet. In Soviet Armenia, Kurdish authors such as Erebê Şemo (author of the novel Şivanê Kurmanca, 1935) wrote in a third literary centre. Within Turkey, publishing in Kurdish was long restricted, pushing writers into exile in Europe; within Iraq, Sorani literature developed in Sulaymaniyah and Erbil. Novelists such as Mehmed Uzun (in exile in Sweden) and Bachtyar Ali (Sorani; I Stared at the Night of the City) achieved international readership in translation.

Themes and oral tradition

Recurring themes include love, exile, the mountains as refuge, and national identity. The oral tradition remains vital: dengbêj performers preserve long epic songs (kilam) of heroes, battles, and tragedies, and were recognised by the Kurdish regional government in Iraq and cultural bodies as heritage bearers.
