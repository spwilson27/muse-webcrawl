Crito

Author: Plato

Language: Ancient Greek

Subject: Ethics, political philosophy

Setting: Socrates' prison cell, Athens, 399 BC

Series: Socratic dialogues

Crito (Greek: Κρίτων) is a dialogue that was written by the ancient Greek philosopher Plato. It depicts a conversation between Socrates and his wealthy friend Crito. They discuss the nature of justice and injustice, and Crito offers to help Socrates escape from prison after he has been sentenced to death. This dialogue, probably written in Plato's early period, is usually grouped with the Euthyphro, Apology, and Phaedo as a tetralogy covering the trial and death of Socrates.

Summary

Crito arrives at Socrates' cell before dawn and urges him to escape: the execution is imminent, the jailers can be bribed, friends in Thessaly will shelter him, and public opinion will condemn his friends for failing to save him. Socrates insists that the decision must rest not on popular opinion but on reasoned argument, and that one must never do injustice, even in return for injustice suffered.

Socrates then delivers the famous speech of the personified Laws of Athens, which argue that by living in the city for seventy years, accepting its benefits and raising his children there, he has entered into an implicit agreement to obey its laws — or to persuade the city where it is wrong. To break the law by escaping would be to undermine the legal order itself. Socrates concludes that he must stay and accept the sentence, and Crito has nothing more to say.

Themes

The dialogue is a foundational text of political philosophy, raising enduring questions about the obligation to obey the law, civil disobedience, and the social contract. Socrates' refusal anticipates later contractarian arguments: consent inferred from continued residence grounds a duty of obedience even to unjust verdicts, provided the citizen was free to leave or to argue against them. Commentators debate whether Socrates' position is consistent with his defiance of unjust orders elsewhere in the dialogues, such as his refusal to arrest Leon of Salamis under the Thirty Tyrants.
