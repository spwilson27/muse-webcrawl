The Uffington White Horse is a prehistoric hill figure, 110 metres (360 ft) long, cut into the steep chalk slope of White Horse Hill near Uffington in Oxfordshire, England. Formed from deep trenches filled with crushed white chalk, the highly stylised figure is the oldest of the many white horse hill figures in Britain.

Dating and purpose

Excavations in the 1990s, using optically stimulated luminescence dating of the trench fill, dated the figure to the late Bronze Age or early Iron Age, roughly between 1380 and 550 BC. Its purpose is unknown; suggestions include a tribal emblem, a religious symbol connected with a horse goddess, and a marker visible to travellers on the ancient Ridgeway track that passes below. Unlike most chalk figures, which are naturalistic, the Uffington horse is composed of elegant, flowing lines with a distinctively "beaked" head.

Surroundings and folklore

Nearby landmarks include Uffington Castle, an Iron Age hillfort on the summit; Dragon Hill, a small flat-topped mound where legend says Saint George slew the dragon; and the Manger, a dry glacial valley. The horse has been maintained for centuries by periodic "scouring" — cleaning and rechalking — traditionally accompanied by local fairs and games. It is protected as a scheduled ancient monument and cared for by the National Trust.
