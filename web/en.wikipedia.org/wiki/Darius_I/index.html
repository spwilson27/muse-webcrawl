Darius I (Old Persian: Dārayavaʰuš; c. 550 – 486 BC), commonly known as Darius the Great, was the third King of Kings of the Achaemenid Empire, reigning from 522 BC until his death in 486 BC. He ruled at the empire's territorial peak, when it spanned some 5.5 million square kilometres across Western Asia, Egypt, the Balkans and parts of the Indus Valley, making it the largest empire yet seen. Darius is remembered as a great administrator and builder as well as a military commander.

Accession

After the death of Cambyses II in 522 BC, the throne was seized by a man Darius identified (in his Behistun Inscription) as the magus Gaumata, impersonating Cambyses' brother Bardiya. Darius, a member of a collateral Achaemenid line, killed the usurper with six co-conspirators and was proclaimed king. He then spent over a year suppressing revolts across the empire, events he recorded in the trilingual Behistun Inscription — a key source for deciphering cuneiform.

Administration and building

Darius reorganised the empire into satrapies with fixed tribute, standardised laws and currency (introducing the gold daric), and promoted Aramaic as an administrative lingua franca. He built the Royal Road from Susa to Sardis with its relay stations, completed a canal linking the Nile to the Red Sea, and undertook vast building programmes at Susa and at Persepolis, the ceremonial capital he founded. Zoroastrian tradition remembers him as a devout follower of Ahura Mazda.

Campaigns

Darius consolidated rule in Egypt, annexed Thrace and Macedonia, campaigned against the Scythians north of the Danube (c. 513 BC), and extended the empire into the Indus Valley. His intervention in the Ionian Revolt led to the first Persian invasions of Greece: after victories at Lade and the capture of Eretria, the Persian expeditionary force was defeated by the Athenians at the Battle of Marathon in 490 BC. Darius was preparing a larger expedition when he died in 486 BC and was succeeded by his son Xerxes I.

Legacy

Herodotus devotes much of his Histories to Darius's reign. To the Greeks, Darius organised the empire that confronted them; in Iranian memory he is one of the foundational Achaemenid kings, and he also appears in the Hebrew Bible (in Ezra, Haggai and Zechariah) in connection with the rebuilding of the Temple in Jerusalem.
