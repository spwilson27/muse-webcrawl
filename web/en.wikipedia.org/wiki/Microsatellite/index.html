Microsatellite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Microsatellite
Also calledShort tandem repeat (STR); simple sequence repeat (SSR)
Unit length1–6 base pairs per repeat
FieldMolecular biology; genetics

A microsatellite is a tract of repetitive DNA in which certain motifs of one to six base pairs are repeated, typically 5–50 times in succession. Also known as short tandem repeats (STRs) or simple sequence repeats (SSRs), microsatellites occur at thousands of locations across eukaryotic genomes and are notable for their high mutation rates, which make repeat numbers vary between individuals.

Mechanism of variation

Most microsatellite length variation arises from slipped-strand mispairing (replication slippage), in which the DNA polymerase transiently dissociates and the nascent strand realigns out of register within the repeat, adding or deleting a unit. Mutation rates can reach 10−2 to 10−6 per locus per generation — orders of magnitude higher than ordinary point mutations.

Applications

Because they are highly polymorphic and inherited in a Mendelian, codominant fashion, microsatellites became the workhorse markers of genetics from the late 1980s: they underpin DNA profiling in forensic science and paternity testing, linkage mapping of disease genes, population genetics, conservation biology and studies of relatedness and parentage in wild animals. In the 2010s, SNP genotyping and whole-genome sequencing progressively displaced microsatellites for many large-scale uses, but STR profiling remains the forensic standard.

Disease associations

Expansion of trinucleotide microsatellites within or near genes causes a class of disorders called trinucleotide repeat diseases, including Huntington's disease, fragile X syndrome, myotonic dystrophy and several spinocerebellar ataxias; longer repeats generally cause earlier and more severe disease (anticipation). Defective DNA mismatch repair also produces microsatellite instability (MSI), a marker of hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (Lynch syndrome) and a predictor of response to immunotherapy in several cancers.
