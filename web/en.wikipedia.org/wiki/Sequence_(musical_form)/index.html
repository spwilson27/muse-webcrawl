In music, a sequence is the restatement of a motif or longer melodic (and/or harmonic) passage at a higher or lower pitch in the same voice. It is a common device in Western tonal music, particularly in the Baroque and Classical periods, used to build momentum, extend a phrase, and carry a modulation from one key to another.

Types

An ascending sequence repeats the pattern at a higher pitch, often raising the tension, while a descending sequence repeats it lower. When the repetition preserves the exact intervals of the original, adjusting only where the key requires, the sequence may be described as real (exactly transposed) or tonal (adjusted to stay within the key). A repetition that alternates between keys is a modulating sequence.

The best-known harmonic sequence is the descending fifths progression, in which the bass falls by a fifth (or rises by a fourth) at each step, a pattern heard throughout Baroque music and memorably in Pachelbel's Canon (which uses a descending-step variant).

Usage

Sequences appear frequently in development sections of sonata-form movements, in fugue episodes — where they link entries of the subject — and in the sequential passagework of concertos by composers such as Vivaldi and Handel. Theorists since the eighteenth century have warned against overlong or mechanical sequences, but the technique remains a fundamental tool of musical construction.
