Mechanical pencil

A typical click-advance mechanical pencil

A mechanical pencil, also called a propelling pencil or clutch pencil depending on the mechanism, is a pencil with a replaceable and mechanically extendable solid pigment core called a "lead" (traditionally graphite). The lead is not bonded to the outer casing and can be extended as its point is worn away, so the pencil never needs sharpening and maintains a constant line width in use.

Description

Mechanical pencils consist of a barrel, an internal mechanism, and one or more thin graphite leads, commonly 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, or 0.9 mm in diameter; larger clutch pencils hold leads of 2 mm or more. Mechanisms include ratchet-based click advance (a button, usually at the end or side, pushes the lead forward in fixed increments), twist advance, and shaker mechanisms, in which shaking the pencil advances the lead. A chuck grips the lead and releases it momentarily as the mechanism advances it.

History

The earliest surviving example of a mechanical pencil was found in the wreck of HMS Pandora, which sank in 1791. In 1822 Sampson Mordan and John Isaac Hawkins patented the first refillable propelling pencil in Britain. Charles Keeran's Eversharp pencil, patented in the United States in the early 20th century, popularised the type. In Japan, Tokuji Hayakawa's "Ever-Ready Sharp Pencil" of 1915 gave the Sharp Corporation its name.

Uses

Because of their consistent line width, mechanical pencils are favoured for technical drawing, drafting, and precise writing. Leads are available in the same hardness grades as wooden pencils, from 9H to 9B. Many models include a small replaceable eraser under the end cap.
