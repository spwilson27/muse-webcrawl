The MGM-31A Pershing was a two-stage, solid-fuel medium-range ballistic missile built by Martin Marietta to replace the PGM-11 Redstone as the United States Army's primary theatre-level weapon. Named after General John J. Pershing, the missile entered service in 1963 and was deployed with US Army units in the United States and Europe, and with the West German Air Force. It carried the W50 thermonuclear warhead with selectable yields.

Development and service

The Pershing was designed for mobility: the missile, its transporter-erector-launcher, and support equipment were mounted on tracked (later wheeled) vehicles, allowing rapid deployment and launch from unprepared sites. Development began in 1958, the first test flight took place in 1960, and operational deployment began in 1964. Pershing units served in West Germany as part of NATO's nuclear deterrent, with missiles held at quick reaction alert.

Pershing IA and Pershing II

The improved Pershing IA, fielded from 1969, featured faster launch equipment and improved electronics, and remained in service until replaced by the Pershing II beginning in 1983. The Pershing II had a longer range (about 1,770 km), a manoeuvring re-entry vehicle with radar area guidance giving greatly improved accuracy, and the W85 warhead. Its deployment in Western Europe, alongside ground-launched cruise missiles, was a central element of the NATO Double-Track Decision and a major point of contention in the Cold War politics of the early 1980s.

Elimination under the INF Treaty

The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty of 1987 between the United States and the Soviet Union required the elimination of all Pershing II and remaining Pershing IA missiles. Destruction took place by static firing and crushing at facilities including Longhorn Army Ammunition Plant in Texas, and the last missiles were eliminated by May 1991. Surviving inert examples are displayed in museums, including one at the National Air and Space Museum.
