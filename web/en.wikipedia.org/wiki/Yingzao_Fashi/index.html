Yingzao Fashi

Author: Li Jie
Published: 1103 (presented 1100), Song dynasty
Language: Classical Chinese
Subject: Architecture and construction standards

The Yingzao Fashi (Chinese: 營造法式; "Treatise on Architectural Methods") is a technical manual on architecture and craftsmanship compiled by the Song dynasty official Li Jie and promulgated by Emperor Huizong in 1103. It is the oldest complete Chinese architectural treatise to survive and a foundational source for the study of traditional Chinese timber construction.

Contents

The work comprises 34 chapters covering terminology, foundations, stonework, carpentry, joinery, carving, tile work, painting and decoration, together with specifications for labour and materials. Its most famous contribution is the cai-fen modular system, which graded structural timber members into eight standard sizes; the proportions of every component of a building — from bracket sets (dougong) to roof beams — were derived from the module of its grade, allowing standardised prefabrication and cost control across state building projects.

Purpose and influence

Li Jie, a director of the Directorate of Construction, revised an earlier 1091 manual after surveying existing practice and earlier texts; the treatise was intended in large part as a fiscal instrument, setting quotas that curbed waste and corruption in government construction. After falling into obscurity, the text was rediscovered in 1919 by the scholar Zhu Qiqian, whose discovery spurred the founding of the Society for Research in Chinese Architecture and the pioneering fieldwork of Liang Sicheng, who produced an annotated edition of the treatise.

Surviving copies

No Song printing survives complete; the text is known from Ming and Qing dynasty manuscript copies, including a hand-copied edition preserved from the Siku Quanshu project. The extant illustrations of brackets, joinery, and decorative patterns remain an essential reference for conservation of historic Chinese buildings.
