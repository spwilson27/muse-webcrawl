Uncertainty

Uncertainty is a state of incomplete or limited knowledge in which it is impossible to describe exactly the existing state of affairs, a future outcome, or more than one possible outcome. It arises in prediction, measurement, and everyday decisions. In his 1921 work Risk, Uncertainty and Profit, the economist Frank Knight drew the influential distinction between risk, where outcomes and their probabilities are known or measurable, and uncertainty proper, where they are not.

Forecast "fan charts", such as those published by central banks, visualise uncertainty growing with the forecast horizon.

Types and sources

Uncertainty may be aleatory, reflecting inherent variability or randomness, or epistemic, reflecting lack of knowledge that could in principle be reduced by more information. In measurement science, "uncertainty" is quantified as a parameter characterising the dispersion of values attributable to a measurand, estimated through error analysis and expressed, for example, as a standard deviation or confidence interval.

Uncertainty in science and decision-making

Scientific practice quantifies and communicates uncertainty through statistics, error bars, and probabilistic models such as weather-ensemble forecasts and climate projections. In economics, finance, and policy, uncertainty influences investment and consumption ("uncertainty shocks"), motivates insurance and hedging, and is studied through decision theory, probability, and information theory. Philosophically, uncertainty underlies questions about induction, probability interpretations, and the limits of knowledge; in quantum mechanics, Heisenberg's uncertainty principle places a fundamental limit on the simultaneous precision of certain pairs of physical quantities.
