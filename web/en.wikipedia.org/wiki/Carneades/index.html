Carneades (Greek: Καρνεάδης, Karneadēs; c. 214 – 129/8 BC) was a Greek philosopher and perhaps the most famous head of the Platonic Academy in its sceptical phase. Born in Cyrene, he moved to Athens and succeeded Hegesinus as scholarch around 155 BC.

The embassy to Rome

In 155 BC Carneades was sent with the Stoic Diogenes of Babylon and the Peripatetic Critolaus on an embassy to Rome. He dazzled Roman audiences by arguing eloquently for justice one day and demolishing his own argument the next — a display that scandalized conservatives like Cato the Elder, who urged the Senate to send the philosophers home.

Philosophy

Carneades wrote nothing; his views survive through his pupil Clitomachus. He argued that nothing can be known with certainty — against the Stoic criterion of the "cognitive impression" — but proposed that impressions may be probable (pithanon), and graded probability by whether an impression is untested, tested, or thoroughly examined, giving scepticism a practical guide to action. He also attacked Stoic theology and the doctrine of fate.

Problem of Carneades

His thought experiment of two planks — in which a shipwrecked sailor may push another off a plank that can hold only one — became a classic problem in the ethics and law of self-defence and necessity.
