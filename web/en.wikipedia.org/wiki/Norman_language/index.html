Norman language

[Image: Distribution of Norman]

Norman, also called Norman French or Norman langue d'oïl (Norman: Normaund), is a Romance language belonging to the langues d'oïl family, spoken in Normandy in northern France and historically in the Channel Islands. Its insular varieties include Jèrriais (Jersey), Guernésiais (Guernsey), and Sercquiais (Sark).

History

Norman developed from the Gallo-Romance spoken in Neustria after Norse settlers arrived in the 9th and 10th centuries; Old Norse contributed vocabulary concerning the sea and everyday life. After the Norman Conquest of 1066, the Anglo-Norman dialect became the language of the English court, law, and administration for centuries and deeply influenced the English lexicon.

Features

Norman retains features such as the retention of Latin /k/ before /a/ where central French palatalised it (compare Norman cat with French chat), aligning it with Picard. Dialects vary between the Cotentin peninsula, the Pays de Caux, and southern Normandy along the Joret line.

Status

The language is classified as endangered; speaker numbers in mainland Normandy are small and mostly elderly, though revival efforts include teaching in the Channel Islands, where Jèrriais and Guernésiais have official recognition and appear on signage and in schools. Writers in Norman include the Jersey poet Robert Le Roy and the Côtis-Capel of the Cotentin.
