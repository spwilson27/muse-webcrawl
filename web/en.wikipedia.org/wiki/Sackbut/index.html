The sackbut is a type of trombone used during the Renaissance and Baroque periods. It differs from the modern trombone in its narrower bore, smaller and less flared bell, and softer, more vocal tone, which made it well suited to blending with voices and other wind instruments in church and court ensembles.

A sackbut, the Renaissance predecessor of the trombone.

History

The instrument developed in the 15th century from the slide trumpet, with important early centres of manufacture in Nuremberg and the Low Countries. The English word "sackbut" comes from the French saqueboute ("pull-push"), describing the operation of the slide. Sackbuts were played in alta cappella wind bands, in cathedral music alongside cornetts, and in early opera and oratorio — famously in Monteverdi's L'Orfeo (1607) and the works of Giovanni Gabrieli and Heinrich Schütz.

Construction and sound

Sackbuts were made in a family of sizes from alto to contrabass, usually in C, D or B-flat pitch worlds corresponding to their parts. Their thin-walled bells, often with a decorative garland, produce a mellow, covered sound quite distinct from the modern orchestral trombone. With the revival of historically informed performance from the mid-20th century, replicas and surviving originals returned to regular use in early music ensembles.
