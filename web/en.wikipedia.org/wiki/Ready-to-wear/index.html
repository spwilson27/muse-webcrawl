Ready-to-wear (RTW) — also called prêt-à-porter from the French — is the term for garments made in standard sizes and sold in finished condition, rather than being sewn to order for a particular customer. It is the dominant form of clothing retail worldwide, and marks the industrialisation of fashion: clothes designed for a market, produced in factories in size runs, and bought off the rack.

History

Off-the-peg clothing first appeared at scale in the early 19th century, initially for military uniforms and for menswear (such as sailors' "slops" and workmen's clothing) where fit was forgiving. Standard sizes were developed from data collected during the American Civil War, when mass-produced uniforms generated the first large anthropometric datasets; sewing machines, paper patterns and cutting factories then made ready-made suits and dresses reliable enough for the middle-class market. Mail-order catalogues from firms such as Sears carried ready-to-wear to rural customers, and by the early 20th century most everyday clothing in industrialised countries was bought ready-made, while bespoke tailoring and dressmaking retreated upmarket.

Paris designers, whose prestige rested on haute couture — unique garments made to order — at first resisted ready-to-wear. In the 1960s this changed decisively: Yves Saint Laurent opened his Rive Gauche boutique in 1966 selling designer prêt-à-porter, and the distinction between couture, luxury ready-to-wear and mass-market clothing settled into the structure the industry still uses. Milan, New York and later London and Tokyo built their fashion weeks around ready-to-wear collections shown to buyers months ahead of the retail season.

The industry today

Ready-to-wear ranges from luxury designer labels to high-street chains and fast fashion, whose vertically integrated retailers (Inditex/Zara, H&M and others) compress design-to-store cycles to a few weeks. The trade-offs are heavily debated: standard sizing fits bodies imperfectly (and women's sizing remains notoriously inconsistent), while mass production has driven low prices at the cost of low wages in garment-exporting countries, short garment lifespans and a large waste stream of discarded clothing. Alternatives promoted within the model include better size ranges, rental and resale platforms, and made-to-order production enabled by automated cutting and digital printing.
