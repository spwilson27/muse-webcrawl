Oliver Cromwell (25 April 1599 – 3 September 1658) was an English statesman, politician, and soldier, widely regarded as one of the most important statesmen in English history. He came to prominence during the Wars of the Three Kingdoms (1639–1653), initially as one of the commanders of the New Model Army, and later as the Lord Protector of the Commonwealth — effectively ruling England, Scotland, and Ireland from 1653 until his death.

Early life

Cromwell was born in Huntingdon into the lesser gentry, a descendant of Thomas Cromwell's family. After attending Sidney Sussex College, Cambridge, he farmed in Huntingdon and St Ives and underwent a religious conversion to Puritanism. He sat in the Parliaments of 1628 and 1640, where he became known as an outspoken critic of royal policy and an advocate of religious reform.

Civil War

When war broke out between Charles I and Parliament in 1642, Cromwell raised a troop of cavalry — the "Ironsides" — and rose rapidly: his cavalry played the decisive role at Marston Moor (1644), and as second-in-command of the New Model Army he helped crush the Royalists at Naseby (1645). In the war's political aftermath he supported the trial of the king, signing Charles I's death warrant in January 1649. As commander-in-chief he then subdued Ireland (1649–50) in a campaign remembered for the massacres at Drogheda and Wexford — a principal reason he remains a reviled figure in Ireland — and defeated the Scots at Dunbar (1650) and Worcester (1651).

Lord Protector

Frustrated with the Rump Parliament, Cromwell dissolved it by force in 1653 and, after the failure of the nominated Barebone's Parliament, became Lord Protector under the Instrument of Government, Britain's first written constitution. His rule combined religious toleration for most Protestants, legal and administrative reform, an assertive foreign policy (war with Spain, the seizure of Jamaica), and the readmission of Jews to England — but also periods of military rule by the Major-Generals. In 1657 Parliament offered him the crown; he refused it.

Death and reputation

Cromwell died in 1658, probably of malarial fever complicated by other illness, and was buried in Westminster Abbey. His son Richard proved unable to hold the Protectorate, and the monarchy was restored in 1660; Cromwell's body was exhumed, hanged at Tyburn, and beheaded. His reputation has been contested ever since: hailed as a hero of liberty and a founder of parliamentary government, condemned as a regicide, military dictator, and — in Ireland — an agent of conquest and massacre. He regularly ranks among the greatest Britons in historical polls.
