Osip Emilyevich Mandelstam (14 January 1891 – 27 December 1938) was a Russian and Soviet poet. He was one of the foremost members of the Acmeist school of poetry, alongside Anna Akhmatova and Nikolay Gumilev. He was arrested by Joseph Stalin's government during the repressions of the 1930s and sent into internal exile with his wife, Nadezhda. Given a reprieve after the intervention of Nikolai Bukharin and others, he was rearrested in 1938 and died in a transit camp near Vladivostok.

Life and career

Mandelstam was born in Warsaw (then part of the Russian Empire) to a wealthy Polish-Jewish leather merchant family and grew up in St. Petersburg. He studied at the Sorbonne and the University of Heidelberg before enrolling at the University of Saint Petersburg, which he never completed. His first collection, Stone (Kamen, 1913), and the Acmeist manifesto "The Morning of Acmeism" established him as a leading voice of the movement, which prized clarity, craft, and the material world against Symbolist mysticism. Tristia (1922), steeped in classical allusion, followed. In the 1920s he supported himself with children's books, translations, and essays such as The Noise of Time (1925), while growing increasingly marginal to Soviet literary life.

Persecution and death

In November 1933 Mandelstam composed the "Stalin Epigram", a savage satire of Stalin ("the Kremlin mountaineer"), and read it to a small circle. He was arrested in May 1934 and exiled to Cherdyn and then Voronezh, where he wrote the Voronezh Notebooks. In May 1938 he was arrested again and sentenced to five years in labour camps; he died at the Vtoraya Rechka transit camp near Vladivostok on 27 December 1938, of illness and starvation. His wife Nadezhda memorised his work to preserve it, an act recounted in her memoirs Hope Against Hope and Hope Abandoned. Mandelstam was posthumously rehabilitated, and he is now regarded as one of the greatest Russian poets of the 20th century.
