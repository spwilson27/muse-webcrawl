The Brumalia was an ancient Roman festival in honour of Saturn, Ops, and Bacchus, held at the approach of the winter solstice. The name derives from bruma, "winter" (or the shortest day), itself a contraction of brevima, "shortest". In the later empire the festival began on 24 November and lasted for roughly a month, a season of feasting, drinking and exchanging good wishes, during which the greeting was Vives annos! — "May you live for years!"

Byzantine continuation

The Brumalia survived in the Byzantine Empire for many centuries despite repeated condemnation by the Church; the Council in Trullo (692) forbade clergy and laity from celebrating it. In the Byzantine court it developed distinctive customs, including banquets at which the emperor entertained guests and the distribution of gifts, with each day of the festival associated with a letter of the Greek alphabet. Elements of the Brumalia's season — midwinter feasting and well-wishing — are often discussed alongside the Saturnalia and Kalends in accounts of the background of Christmas customs.
