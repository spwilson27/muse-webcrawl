Low-power television

Low-power television (LPTV) is a class of television broadcasting in the United States licensed by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) at substantially lower transmitter power than full-service stations, giving them a limited coverage area. The service was authorised in 1982 to bring local and specialised programming to small communities and niche audiences. LPTV stations are a "secondary" service: they must not cause interference to full-power stations and must accept interference from them.

Characteristics

LPTV stations typically serve a radius of a few miles to a few tens of miles. Because they are inexpensive to build and operate, they have been used for community and religious broadcasting, foreign-language and ethnic programming, home shopping, and as affiliates of smaller networks. Call signs for LPTV stations historically consisted of the letter W or K, the channel number, and a two-letter suffix (for example, W23AB), though digital stations may receive conventional four-letter calls.

Class A and translators

The Community Broadcasters Protection Act of 1999 created the Class A service, giving qualifying LPTV stations that broadcast locally oriented programming certain protections, including a measure of protection from displacement by full-power stations, in exchange for meeting additional obligations. Television translator stations, a related secondary service, merely rebroadcast another station's signal to reach areas blocked by terrain or distance.

Digital transition

Full-power American television stations switched to digital broadcasting in 2009, but LPTV and translator stations were allowed to continue in analog for much longer. After several extensions, the FCC required all remaining analog LPTV and translator stations to cease operations by July 13, 2021, completing the digital transition for the low-power service. The spectrum incentive auction and subsequent "repack" of television channels displaced many LPTV stations to new channels.
