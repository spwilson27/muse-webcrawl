Transpiration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transpiration is the process of water movement through a plant and its evaporation from aerial parts, such as leaves, stems and flowers. It is a passive process largely driven by evaporation from the stomata — the tiny pores on leaf surfaces — and is essential for transporting minerals from roots to shoots and for cooling the plant.

Mechanism

According to the cohesion–tension theory, evaporation of water from leaf mesophyll cells lowers the water potential in the leaf, generating tension that pulls continuous columns of water up through the xylem from the roots, held together by cohesion between water molecules. More than 90% of the water taken up by roots is typically lost to transpiration. Guard cells regulate the aperture of the stomata, balancing water loss against the uptake of carbon dioxide needed for photosynthesis; the hormone abscisic acid triggers stomatal closure under drought stress.

Factors affecting transpiration

Transpiration rate increases with light, temperature, wind and low atmospheric humidity, and depends on plant features such as leaf area, cuticle thickness and stomatal density. Desert plants (xerophytes) reduce loss with small or sunken stomata, thick cuticles and CAM photosynthesis, opening stomata only at night.

Significance

Transpiration is a major component of the global water cycle: moisture released by forests contributes to cloud formation and regional rainfall, a process sometimes called a "biotic pump". A single large tree can transpire several hundred litres of water on a hot day.
