Convertibility plan

The Convertibility plan (Spanish: Plan de Convertibilidad) was a plan by the Argentine Currency Board that pegged the Argentine peso to the U.S. dollar at a rate of one to one. Established by the Convertibility Law of March 1991 under President Carlos Menem and Economy Minister Domingo Cavallo, it was intended to combat the hyperinflation that had devastated the Argentine economy in 1989 and 1990.

Background

Argentina had suffered chronic inflation for decades, culminating in hyperinflation of roughly 3,000 percent in 1989. Earlier stabilization attempts, including the Austral Plan, had collapsed. The Convertibility Law required the central bank to maintain foreign-currency reserves backing the monetary base, effectively creating a currency board arrangement and ending the practice of financing government deficits by printing money.

Effects

The plan succeeded rapidly in ending hyperinflation: inflation fell to single digits within a few years, and the economy grew strongly during the early 1990s, attracting large inflows of foreign capital. However, the fixed peg left Argentina unable to devalue in response to external shocks, such as the Mexican peso crisis of 1994, the Asian financial crisis of 1997, and the Brazilian devaluation of 1999. Unemployment rose and the economy entered recession in 1998.

Collapse

Following four years of recession, mounting debt and capital flight, the government imposed the corralito freeze on bank deposits in December 2001. Amid riots and the resignation of President Fernando de la Rúa, Argentina defaulted on its debt and abandoned convertibility in January 2002, after which the peso depreciated sharply.
