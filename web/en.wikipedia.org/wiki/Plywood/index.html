Plywood

Plywood is a composite material manufactured from thin layers, or "plies," of wood veneer glued together with adjacent layers having their wood grain rotated up to 90 degrees to one another (cross-lamination). It is an engineered wood from the family of manufactured boards that also includes medium-density fibreboard (MDF) and particle board (chipboard).

Properties

Cross-lamination gives plywood good dimensional stability, resistance to splitting, and roughly equal strength along both dimensions of the panel—advantages over solid wood, which is strong along the grain but weak across it and prone to expansion and contraction with moisture changes. Sheets almost always have an odd number of plies so that the grain of the two faces runs the same way and the panel stays balanced and flat. Typical thicknesses range from about 3 mm to 30 mm, and standard sheets are often 4 × 8 feet (1220 × 2440 mm).

Manufacture

Logs are softened (often by steaming), then peeled on a rotary lathe into continuous veneer sheets, which are dried, graded, and cut. Core veneers are laid up with grain alternating at right angles, spread with adhesive—commonly urea-formaldehyde for interior grades and phenol-formaldehyde for exterior and marine grades—and pressed under heat and pressure. Higher-quality panels use hardwood face veneers such as birch (Baltic birch), okoumé, or decorative species; softwood plywood, typically Douglas fir or spruce, is used in construction.

Types and uses

  • Structural/construction plywood – sheathing, flooring, roofing, and formwork for concrete.
  • Marine plywood – made with waterproof glue and durable face veneers, for boatbuilding.
  • Aircraft plywood – thin, high-strength birch panels, historically used in aircraft such as the de Havilland Mosquito.
  • Flexible plywood – thin panels that can be bent for curved furniture and mouldings.
  • Decorative/cabinet plywood – furniture, cabinetry, and interior panelling.

History

Laminated wood was known in ancient Egypt and China, but modern plywood awaited the rotary veneer lathe and industrial adhesives of the nineteenth century. Emmanuel Nobel (father of Alfred Nobel) developed veneer-lathe technology, and plywood production industrialized in the early twentieth century, notably in the Pacific Northwest of the United States, expanding greatly with the invention of waterproof synthetic-resin glues in the 1930s.
