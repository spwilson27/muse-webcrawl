Hypostatic union

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hypostatic union (from the Greek ὑπόστασις, hypóstasis, "subsistence/person") is a term in Christian theology describing the union of Christ's divine and human natures in the single person (hypostasis) of Jesus Christ. According to the doctrine, Jesus is simultaneously fully God and fully man — one person with two complete natures, united "without confusion, change, division, or separation."

History of the doctrine

The doctrine was defined at the Council of Chalcedon in 451 AD, following controversies with positions later condemned as heresies: Nestorianism, which was accused of dividing Christ into two persons; Monophysitism, associated with Eutyches, which held that Christ had only one nature; and Apollinarianism, which denied Christ a complete human mind. The Chalcedonian Definition affirmed "one and the same Christ, Son, Lord, Only-begotten, acknowledged in two natures unconfusedly, unchangeably, indivisibly, inseparably."

Most Christian traditions — Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox (with differences of formulation), and most Protestant churches — accept some form of the doctrine, though the wording of Chalcedon contributed to the schism with the Oriental Orthodox churches, who prefer the miaphysite formula of Cyril of Alexandria, "one incarnate nature of God the Word." Later councils, including Constantinople II (553) and Constantinople III (680–681), clarified that Christ possesses both a divine and a human will (dyothelitism).
