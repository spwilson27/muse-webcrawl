Semi-automatic pistol

A semi-automatic pistol is a type of repeating single-chamber handgun (pistol) that automatically cycles its action to reload after each shot, but requires the shooter to release and pull the trigger again for each successive shot. This contrasts with fully automatic pistols (machine pistols), which continue firing while the trigger is held, and with revolvers, which use a rotating cylinder with multiple chambers.

Operation

Most semi-automatic pistols use the energy of the fired cartridge — through recoil operation (blowback, short recoil, or delayed blowback) or, rarely, gas operation — to eject the spent casing, cock the hammer or striker, and chamber a fresh round from a magazine, usually housed in the grip. The first shot may be fired in double-action or single-action mode depending on the design.

History

The first commercially successful semi-automatic pistol was the Borchardt C-93 of 1893, designed by Hugo Borchardt, whose toggle-lock mechanism inspired the later Luger pistol. The Mauser C96 (1896), the Browning-designed FN M1900, and John Browning's Colt M1911 followed. The M1911, a short-recoil, single-action pistol chambered in .45 ACP, served as the standard sidearm of the United States military for most of the 20th century. The Walther PP (1929) popularized double-action/single-action operation, and the Walther P38 introduced it to military service. The Beretta 92 and the polymer-framed, striker-fired Glock 17 (1982) exemplify modern service pistols.

Action types

  • Single-action (SA): the trigger performs the single function of releasing the hammer or striker.
  • Double-action only (DAO): every trigger pull both cocks and releases the hammer or striker.
  • Double-action/single-action (DA/SA): the first pull is double-action; subsequent shots are single-action as the slide cocks the hammer.
  • Striker-fired: uses a spring-loaded firing pin (striker) instead of a hammer, common in modern polymer pistols.

Terminology

In common usage "automatic pistol" often refers to a semi-automatic pistol, because historically the self-loading mechanism was the "automatic" part. In technical usage, "automatic" denotes fully automatic fire. A detachable box magazine is frequently (incorrectly) called a "clip".
