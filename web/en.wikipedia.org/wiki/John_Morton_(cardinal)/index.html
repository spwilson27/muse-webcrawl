John Morton (cardinal)

John Morton (c. 1420–1500) was Archbishop of Canterbury and Lord Chancellor of England under Henry VII, and the supposed source of the tax dilemma "Morton's Fork".

John Morton (c. 1420 – 15 September 1500) was an English Catholic churchman, lawyer and statesman. He was Archbishop of Canterbury from 1486 until his death and Lord Chancellor of England from 1487; Pope Alexander VI made him a cardinal in 1493. A survivor of the Wars of the Roses who switched from Lancaster to Edward IV and then helped Henry Tudor take the throne, Morton was Henry VII's chief minister.

Career

Educated at Balliol College, Oxford, Morton became a leading civil and canon lawyer. As a Lancastrian he followed Margaret of Anjou into exile, but after 1471 made his peace with Edward IV, who employed him on diplomatic missions and made him Bishop of Ely in 1478. Suspected by Richard III, he was arrested in 1483 and, while in the custody of the Duke of Buckingham, was credited by later writers with encouraging Buckingham's rebellion. Escaping to Flanders, he joined Henry Tudor and returned after Bosworth.

Under Henry VII

Henry VII made Morton Archbishop of Canterbury in 1486 and Lord Chancellor in 1487. He was the king's most trusted counsellor in finance and diplomacy, helped negotiate the marriage of Prince Arthur to Catherine of Aragon, and rebuilt much of Lambeth Palace, including its gatehouse. Tradition associates him with "Morton's Fork", the no-win argument attributed to royal tax collectors: the extravagant must be able to pay, and the frugal must have saved enough to pay.

Death and legacy

Morton died at Knole in 1500 and was buried in Canterbury Cathedral. Thomas More, later Lord Chancellor and saint, served as a page in Morton's household and praised his wisdom in Utopia. Morton also figures in Shakespeare's Richard III.
