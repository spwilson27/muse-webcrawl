Assault gun

A German Sturmgeschütz III, the most-produced assault gun of the Second World War

An assault gun (from German Sturmgeschütz) is a type of self-propelled artillery or armoured fighting vehicle: a gun or howitzer mounted on a motorised chassis, usually with the weapon fixed in a casemate superstructure rather than a rotating turret. Assault guns were designed primarily to provide direct fire support for infantry, destroying fortifications, strongpoints and — increasingly during the Second World War — enemy armour.

History

The concept matured in Germany in the 1930s, championed by Erich von Manstein, who proposed "assault artillery" battalions to accompany infantry attacks. The resulting Sturmgeschütz III, built on the Panzer III chassis, entered service in 1940 and became Germany's most-produced armoured fighting vehicle of the war. Omitting the turret made assault guns cheaper and quicker to build, allowed a larger gun on a given chassis, and gave a low silhouette well suited to ambush; the type consequently evolved into an effective tank destroyer as the war turned defensive.

The Soviet Union developed parallel vehicles — the SU-76, SU-85, SU-100, SU-122 and ISU-152 — in huge numbers, using them for infantry support and anti-tank work. Finland employed captured and German-supplied StuG IIIs with great success. Other nations generally folded the role into turreted tanks, tank destroyers or self-propelled artillery.

Post-war

After 1945 the classic assault gun faded as main battle tanks took over its roles, though related vehicles appeared, such as West Germany's Kanonenjagdpanzer, Sweden's turretless Stridsvagn 103 (a tank), and various wheeled gun systems. The term is sometimes applied loosely to modern direct-fire support vehicles.
