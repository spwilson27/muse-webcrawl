The Chassepot (pronounced "shass-poh"), officially known as the Fusil modèle 1866, was a bolt-action military breechloading rifle adopted by the French Empire in 1866. It was named after its inventor, Antoine Alphonse Chassepot, a worker in the state arms workshops. The Chassepot combined the Dreyse needle gun's ignition concept with improved obturation — a rubber ring on the bolt head that sealed the breech — giving it a substantially higher muzzle velocity and much longer effective range than the Prussian Dreyse it was designed to counter.

Design

Like the Dreyse, the Chassepot fired a combustible paper cartridge ignited by a long needle striking a percussion cap at the base of the bullet. Its 11 mm bullet left the muzzle at roughly 410 m/s, compared with about 305 m/s for the Dreyse, and its sights were graduated far beyond those of contemporary rifles. The rubber obturator ring solved the gas-leakage problem that plagued the German rifle, though rings degraded with heat and sustained firing.

Service history

The Chassepot received its "baptism of fire" at the Battle of Mentana on 3 November 1867, where French troops supporting the Papal States defeated Garibaldi's volunteers; the French commander famously reported that "the Chassepots have done wonders!" In the Franco-Prussian War (1870–1871) the rifle dramatically outclassed the Dreyse in range and accuracy, inflicting heavy casualties at battles such as Gravelotte–Saint-Privat, though its superiority could not compensate for French deficiencies in artillery, mobilization, and command. About a million Chassepots were produced. After the war, many were converted to fire metallic cartridges as the Gras rifle (modèle 1874), and captured examples were used or converted by German states.
