The Persian Constitutional Revolution (Persian: مشروطیت, Mashrutiyat) was a movement between 1905 and 1911 that led to the establishment of the first constitution and parliament (Majlis) in Qajar Persia. It was a landmark in modern Iranian history, uniting merchants, clergy, intellectuals, and tribal forces against absolute monarchy and foreign economic domination.

Background

By 1900, Persia was heavily indebted to Britain and Russia, which competed for influence in the "Great Game". Concessions to foreigners, such as the tobacco concession of 1890 (reversed after the Tobacco Protest), customs administered by Belgian officials for Russian loans, and the extravagance of the Qajar court fuelled discontent among the bazaar merchants and the ulama.

Course of the revolution

Protests in 1905 over the bastinado of Tehran sugar merchants escalated into a general demand for a "house of justice" and then a constitution. In the summer of 1906 thousands took sanctuary (bast) at the British legation in Tehran. Mozaffar ad-Din Shah conceded, signing the constitution in December 1906 shortly before his death. The first Majlis opened in October 1906, and the Supplementary Fundamental Laws of 1907 defined citizens' rights while retaining Twelver Shi'ism as the state religion.

Civil war and suppression

Mohammad Ali Shah, who succeeded in 1907, opposed the constitution. In June 1908, with the Persian Cossack Brigade under Colonel Liakhov, he bombarded the Majlis and arrested leading constitutionalists. Resistance centred on Tabriz, defended by Sattar Khan and Baqer Khan, and constitutionalist forces from Gilan and Isfahan marched on Tehran, deposing the shah in July 1909. The restored constitutional government was undermined by tribal unrest, the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907, and finally a Russian ultimatum and occupation of northern Persia in 1911, which dissolved the second Majlis.

Legacy

The revolution introduced constitutionalism, parliamentary politics, a free press, and modern political vocabulary into Iran. Its ideals and its failure shaped Iranian politics throughout the twentieth century, down to the revolution of 1979.
