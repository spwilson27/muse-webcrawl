Vocal tract

The vocal tract is the cavity in human beings and in animals where the sound produced at the sound source — the larynx in mammals — is filtered. It extends from the vocal folds (vocal cords) up through the pharynx, the oral cavity, and the nasal cavity to the lips and nostrils. In humans the vocal tract is about 17 cm long in adult males. Its constantly changing shape, controlled by the articulators (tongue, lips, jaw, soft palate, and cheeks), resonates selectively to produce the distinct sounds of speech.

Source–filter model

In the source–filter theory of speech production, the vocal folds generate a buzzing source sound rich in harmonics; the vocal tract acts as a variable acoustic filter whose resonances, called formants, amplify some frequencies and damp others. Vowel quality is determined mainly by the first two or three formants, which depend on tongue height and backness and on lip rounding. Consonants are produced by constrictions at specific places of articulation along the tract, from the glottis through the pharynx, velum, palate, alveolar ridge, teeth, and lips. Raising or lowering the velum couples or decouples the nasal cavity, producing nasal or oral sounds.

Development and variation

In human infants the larynx sits high in the throat, allowing simultaneous breathing and swallowing; it descends during the first years of life, lengthening the pharynx and creating the roughly right-angled tract of adults, which greatly expands the range of producible vowel sounds (the "quantal" vowels /i/, /a/, /u/) — at the cost of an increased risk of choking. Debates continue over how closely the Neanderthal vocal tract matched the modern human one, and hence over their speech capabilities. Other animals have differently shaped tracts; birds produce sound in the syrinx and filter it in the trachea and beak.
