Commander-in-chief

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A commander-in-chief is the person who exercises supreme command and control over a nation's armed forces. In many states the role belongs to the head of state or head of government as a constitutional office, symbolising civilian control of the military; in others it is held by the senior serving officer. The term is also used for the commander of a particular theatre or unified command.

Under Article II of the United States Constitution, the President is Commander in Chief of the Army and Navy of the United States and of the state militias when called into federal service. The provision anchors the principle that the elected civil authority directs the military, while Congress retains the powers to declare war, raise and fund forces, and make rules for their regulation — a division that has produced recurring disputes over war powers, addressed partly by the War Powers Resolution of 1973.

Other countries

In the United Kingdom the monarch is head of the armed forces and commander-in-chief in law, though command is exercised by the government through the Defence Council. In France the President is chef des armées. In India the President is the supreme commander, acting on ministerial advice. In some states the role has at times been assumed by military rulers themselves, blurring the civilian-control principle the office is meant to embody.

Civilian control

The office expresses the doctrine that the military must be subordinate to the political leadership accountable to the public. Tensions arise where commanders-in-chief intervene in operational decisions or where professional officers resist policy; classic statements of the problem include Samuel Huntington's theory of “objective control” in The Soldier and the State (1957).
