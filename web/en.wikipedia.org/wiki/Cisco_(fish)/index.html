Cisco is the common name for several species of freshwater whitefish in the genus Coregonus, most notably the lake cisco or lake herring, Coregonus artedi. Ciscoes are silvery, schooling, plankton-eating salmonids of the cold, deep lakes of northern North America, and form a key forage base for predatory fish such as lake trout, northern pike, and walleye.

The lake cisco (Coregonus artedi) is an important forage fish in the Great Lakes and Canadian Shield lakes.

Taxonomy and species flock

The Great Lakes once supported a celebrated "species flock" of deepwater ciscoes, including the bloater, kiyi, shortjaw cisco, longjaw cisco, blackfin cisco, and shortnose cisco, whose taxonomy has long been debated: some authorities treat them as full species of Coregonus, others as forms of a single complex. Several forms were driven to extinction in the mid-20th century.

Ecology

Ciscoes occupy the cold, well-oxygenated upper and middle waters of oligotrophic lakes, feeding on zooplankton and small invertebrates, and spawn in late autumn over shoals or in tributaries. Because they require cold water, they are vulnerable to warming, eutrophication, and competition or predation from introduced species such as rainbow smelt and alewife.

Fisheries

Ciscoes supported major commercial fisheries in the Great Lakes in the 19th and early 20th centuries, sold fresh, salted, and smoked (as "chubs"). Overfishing, sea lamprey predation, and invasive species caused collapses; some populations, such as the bloater, have partially recovered. In parts of the Upper Midwest, cisco (locally "tullibee") roe and smoked fish remain regional delicacies, and autumn cisco runs attract anglers and predators alike.
