Śūnyatā (Sanskrit: शून्यता; Pali: suññatā), often translated as emptiness or voidness, is a central concept in Mahayana Buddhism. It expresses the idea that all phenomena (dharmas) are empty (śūnya) of inherent, independent existence — of svabhāva, an unchanging intrinsic nature of their own.

The doctrine developed from early Buddhist teachings on impermanence (anicca) and non-self (anattā), and was systematised in the Prajñāpāramitā ("Perfection of Wisdom") sutras and in the Madhyamaka philosophy of Nāgārjuna (c. 2nd century CE).

Development

In the early discourses, "empty" was applied mainly to the absence of a self or anything pertaining to a self in the five aggregates. The later Prajñāpāramitā literature extended emptiness to all dharmas whatsoever: as the Heart Sutra puts it, "form is emptiness, emptiness is form."

Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā ("Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way") identified emptiness with dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda): because things arise only in dependence on causes and conditions, they lack any independent essence — and that lack is itself not a thing. Emptiness is therefore not nihilism; Nāgārjuna insisted that emptiness is also empty, and that the teaching is a "middle way" between eternalism and annihilationism.

Interpretations

Different Mahayana schools have read śūnyatā differently. Madhyamaka treats it as the absence of svabhāva in all phenomena. Yogācāra relates emptiness to the absence of a duality between perceiver and perceived. In East Asian Buddhism — Chan/Zen, Tiantai, and Huayan — emptiness is bound up with doctrines such as interpenetration and Buddha-nature (tathāgatagarbha), with long-running debate over how the two teachings relate.
