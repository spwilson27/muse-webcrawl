Module (mathematics)

FieldAbstract algebra
GeneralisesVector space (scalars form a ring, not necessarily a field)
ExamplesAbelian groups (ℤ-modules), ideals of a ring, vector spaces

In abstract algebra, a module over a ring is a generalisation of the notion of a vector space over a field: the scalars need only form a ring, not a field. A (left) module over a ring R consists of an abelian group (M, +) together with an operation of scalar multiplication by elements of R satisfying the familiar axioms — distributivity over both additions, compatibility with ring multiplication, and identity action of 1. Modules are central objects of study in commutative algebra and homological algebra, and much of the theory of linear algebra is developed in this generality.

Examples

If R is a field, modules over R are exactly vector spaces. If R is the ring of integers ℤ, modules are exactly abelian groups. Every ring is a module over itself, and its submodules are its (left) ideals. More generally, ideals of any ring are modules over it, and vector spaces with a linear operator acting on them correspond to modules over a polynomial ring. The set of n-tuples over a ring forms a free module.

Submodules and homomorphisms

A submodule is a subset closed under addition and scalar multiplication; quotient modules are formed analogously to quotient groups. Module homomorphisms are maps preserving both operations, and the isomorphism theorems hold as for groups and vector spaces. Unlike vector spaces, modules need not have bases: a module with a basis is called free, and phenomena such as torsion — elements annihilated by a non-zero scalar — have no vector-space analogue.

Structure theory

For finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, a complete classification exists (the structure theorem), generalising the fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups. Over general Noetherian rings, finite generation, the ascending chain condition on submodules, and exact sequences of modules provide the working tools. Modules form an abelian category, making them the natural setting for homological algebra — derived functors such as Ext and Tor are defined on module categories.
