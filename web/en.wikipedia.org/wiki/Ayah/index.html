An ayah (Arabic: آية, romanised: ʾāyah, plural: آيات ʾāyāt) is a "verse" in the Quran, one of the statements or paragraphs that make up a surah (chapter). The word literally means a "sign" or "evidence" — in the Quran it is used both for the verses themselves and for the signs of God in the natural world. The verses are conventionally counted at 6,236 (excluding the basmalas), although counts vary slightly by tradition of enumeration; the longest verse, the Verse of the Loan (Q2:282), spans a full page, while the shortest consist of a word or two, or even disconnected letters (muqaṭṭaʿāt).

Reference and numbering

Ayat are cited by surah and verse number, e.g. Q2:255 — the famous Ayat al-Kursi, the "Throne Verse," among the most memorised and inscribed passages of the Quran. Individual verses are marked in printed mushafs by a small ornamental symbol, often containing the verse number in Arabic-Indic numerals. The first revealed verses are traditionally identified as the opening of Surah Al-'Alaq (Q96), and the last revealed verse is debated, commonly held to be Q2:281 or Q5:3.

Notable individual verses

Certain ayat have acquired special names and devotional roles, including Ayat al-Kursi (Q2:255), the Verse of Light (Q24:35), and the verses of protection; verses are also classified in Islamic jurisprudence and theology as clear (muḥkam) or ambiguous (mutashābih), a distinction drawn from Q3:7, and the doctrine of abrogation (naskh) concerns relationships between verses revealed at different times.
