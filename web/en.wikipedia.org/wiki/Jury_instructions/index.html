Jury instructions

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jury instructions are the directions of law a judge gives to a jury before deliberation — explaining the elements of the charges or claims, the burden and standard of proof, how to evaluate evidence and credibility, and the verdict forms available. In US practice, counsel submit proposed instructions and argue them at a charge conference; the judge settles the final text, reads it to the jury increasingly supplemented by written copies, and instructs again if the jury asks questions during deliberation.

Importance and problems

Instructions are the legal system's designated interface between law and lay decision-making, and a prime source of appellate error: an incorrect statement of an element or defence can require reversal (instructional error reviewed for harmlessness, or plain error if unobjected). Empirical research finds lay comprehension of standard instructions poor — legalese, passive constructions and double negatives defeating jurors who nonetheless decide confidently by narrative sense — prompting plain-language reforms and "pattern" or model instructions promulgated by courts and bar committees to standardise and simplify charges. Systems differ: English judges sum up facts as well as law ("the facts are for you, the law is from me"), while many US judges may not comment on the evidence — a contrast that encodes different trusts in judicial versus lay judgment.
