Musketeer

A musketeer (French: mousquetaire) was a type of soldier equipped with a musket. Musketeers first appeared in early modern Europe as a component of infantry formations in which they worked alongside pikemen, and they became the dominant infantry type as firearms improved, until the musket with socket bayonet made the pike obsolete at the end of the 17th century.

A musketeer of the Thirty Years' War era.

History

Musketeers emerged in the 16th century, notably in the Spanish tercios, where arquebusiers and later musketeers with heavier, more powerful weapons were combined with pikemen and swordsmen. The Dutch reforms of Maurice of Nassau and the Swedish army of Gustavus Adolphus systematised volley fire and thinner, more flexible formations. Musketeers fired in ranks and then retired to reload — the countermarch — or stood to receive cavalry behind pikes. The flintlock musket and the socket bayonet, adopted around 1700, let every infantryman act as both shot and pike, and the musketeer became the standard line infantryman of the 18th century.

Equipment

A 17th-century musketeer carried a matchlock (later flintlock) musket, a forked rest for the heavy early weapons, a bandolier with wooden charge containers ("the twelve apostles"), a sword, and often a helmet or broad-brimmed hat. Rate of fire was slow — one or two shots a minute — but massed volleys were devastating at close range.

The French Musketeers of the Guard

The word is most famous for the Musketeers of the Guard (Mousquetaires de la garde), an elite company of the French royal household founded in 1622 under Louis XIII. Recruited from gentlemen, they served as the king's bodyguard and a training school for officers; Cardinal Richelieu maintained a rival company. Immortalised by Alexandre Dumas's novel The Three Musketeers (1844) — whose heroes Athos, Porthos, Aramis, and d'Artagnan were based on real musketeers — the corps was disbanded in 1776 for economy, briefly revived in 1789 and 1814–1816, then abolished. (For the corps itself, see Musketeers of the Guard.)

Elsewhere

Russia's streltsy were musketeer guards of the tsars from the 16th century until Peter the Great disbanded them after their revolts. The Ottoman Janissaries also adopted muskets. In English usage, "musketeer" generically describes any musket-armed foot soldier of the period.
