Folkestone is a coastal town and civil parish in Kent, England, on the English Channel at the southern edge of the North Downs. A fishing port and market town for centuries, it became a fashionable Victorian seaside resort and an important cross-Channel ferry and railway town. Since 1994 it has been closely associated with the nearby Channel Tunnel terminal at Cheriton.

History

The area has been settled since Mesolithic times; the East Cliff above the harbour was a major centre for the export of quern-stones in the late Iron Age and Roman periods. A nunnery founded in the seventh century by St Eanswith, granddaughter of King Æthelberht of Kent, made Folkestone an early Christian site. William Harvey, discoverer of the circulation of the blood, was born in Folkestone in 1578.

The arrival of the South Eastern Railway in the 1840s transformed the town: a harbour branch crossed a viaduct over the harbour, grand hotels and terraces were built, and the Leas promenade and cliff lift (the Leas Lift, opened 1885) served holiday-makers. Folkestone was a major embarkation port during both world wars, and more than a million soldiers passed through it on the way to the Western Front via the "Via Folkestone" route.

Transport

Folkestone Central and Folkestone West stations lie on the South Eastern Main Line, with high-speed services to London St Pancras via High Speed 1. The Eurotunnel Le Shuttle vehicle terminal at Cheriton, just west of the town, provides access to the Channel Tunnel to Coquelles near Calais. Regular ferry services to France ceased in 2000, after competition from the tunnel.

Culture and regeneration

Since the 2000s the old town's Creative Quarter, centred on the Old High Street and Tontine Street, has attracted artists and independent businesses, and the town hosts the Folkestone Triennial, a major public-art exhibition founded in 2008. The restored Harbour Arm serves as a promenade with food stalls and events.
