Fixed exchange rate system

Under a fixed exchange rate, a currency's value is tied to another currency or to a commodity such as gold.

A fixed exchange rate system is a currency regime in which a government or central bank ties the value of its currency to that of another currency, a basket of currencies, or a commodity such as gold, and maintains the peg by buying and selling its own currency in foreign-exchange markets. It contrasts with a floating exchange rate, where the market determines the currency's value.

Mechanism

The monetary authority commits to exchange its currency at a stated parity. If market pressure pushes the currency above the peg, the authority sells its own currency and accumulates reserves; if below, it buys its currency using reserves. Defending a peg therefore requires adequate foreign-exchange reserves and, ultimately, monetary policy consistent with the target — interest rates must serve the exchange rate rather than domestic goals, a constraint summarised by the "impossible trinity" of open capital markets, fixed rates, and independent monetary policy.

Forms

Regimes range from conventional pegs and pegs within horizontal bands, through crawling pegs that adjust gradually, to hard pegs such as currency boards (which back the money supply with reserves, as in Hong Kong) and dollarisation or membership of a monetary union, where a foreign currency or shared currency is used outright. The historical gold standard and the post-war Bretton Woods system (1944–1971), in which currencies were pegged to the US dollar convertible into gold, are the classic examples.

Advantages and disadvantages

Fixed rates reduce transaction costs and exchange-rate risk in trade and investment, impose anti-inflation discipline, and can anchor expectations in countries with weak institutions. Their costs include the loss of monetary-policy autonomy, vulnerability to speculative attacks — as in the 1992 ERM crisis, the 1994 Mexican crisis and the 1997 Asian crisis — and the risk of real overvaluation that erodes competitiveness until a sudden devaluation becomes unavoidable. China long maintained a tightly managed peg to the dollar; Switzerland imposed a floor on the franc against the euro from 2011 to 2015.
