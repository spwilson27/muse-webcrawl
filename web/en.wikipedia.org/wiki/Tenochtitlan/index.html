Tenochtitlan was the capital city of the Mexica (Aztec) empire, founded in 1325 on an island in Lake Texcoco in the Valley of Mexico. One of the largest cities in the world at the time of the Spanish conquest — with a population often estimated at 200,000 or more, comparable to London or Seville — it was destroyed during the siege of 1521 by Hernán Cortés and his allies, and Mexico City was built upon its ruins.

Foundation

According to Mexica tradition, the god Huitzilopochtli directed his people to found their city where they found an eagle perched on a prickly-pear cactus growing from a stone in the lake — an image that survives today on the Mexican flag and coat of arms. The city grew by land reclamation and was joined to its sister city Tlatelolco, whose great market impressed the Spanish.

Layout

The city was divided into four quadrants (campan) around the sacred precinct, whose centrepiece was the Templo Mayor, a great pyramid crowned by twin shrines to Huitzilopochtli, god of war and the sun, and Tlaloc, god of rain. Three great causeways linked the island to the mainland, and a system of canals, aqueducts (notably from Chapultepec) and floating gardens (chinampas) supplied food and fresh water; a great dike built under Nezahualcoyotl separated the brackish eastern lake from the fresher water around the city.

Conquest and aftermath

After the Spanish entry into the city in November 1519, the expulsion of the Spanish on La Noche Triste (30 June 1520), and a smallpox epidemic that killed the tlatoani Cuitláhuac, Cortés returned with brigantines and tens of thousands of indigenous allies. Following a siege of some 80 days the city fell on 13 August 1521 and its last ruler, Cuauhtémoc, was captured. The ruins of the Templo Mayor were rediscovered beside Mexico City's cathedral in 1978 with the excavation of the Coyolxauhqui stone.
