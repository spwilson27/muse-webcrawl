Unjust enrichment

Field: Private law / restitution

Key remedy: Restitution

Unjust enrichment is a legal doctrine in many common law and civil law systems under which a person who has been enriched at the expense of another, in circumstances the law regards as unjust, must make restitution of the benefit received. It is the foundation of the law of restitution and of claims such as money had and received and quantum meruit.

Elements

A claim in unjust enrichment generally requires that (1) the defendant was enriched, (2) the enrichment was at the claimant's expense, (3) the enrichment was unjust — supported by a recognised "unjust factor" such as mistake, failure of consideration, duress or illegality — and (4) the defendant has no available defence, such as change of position.

History

The doctrine has roots in the Roman law condictiones and in the maxim of Pomponius that no one should be enriched at another's expense. In English law, the modern structure was articulated in cases such as Lipkin Gorman v Karpnale Ltd [1991], in which the House of Lords recognised unjust enrichment as a distinct category of legal obligation.

Defences

The principal defence is change of position, recognised in Lipkin Gorman, which protects a good-faith recipient who has irreversibly altered their position in reliance on the receipt. Other defences include estoppel, illegality, incapacity and the passing of the benefit to third parties.
