Muisca


The Muisca raft, a gold offering figure associated with the El Dorado legend.

The Muisca (also called the Chibcha) are an indigenous people and culture of the Altiplano Cundiboyacense, Colombia, that formed the Muisca Confederation before the Spanish conquest. They spoke Muysccubun, a language of the Chibchan family, and were one of the most advanced pre-Columbian civilisations of the Americas.

The Muisca lived in a loose confederation of chiefdoms centred on the zipa of Bacatá (near modern Bogotá) and the zaque of Hunza (Tunja), with important religious centres at Sugamuxi (Sogamoso) and Lake Guatavita. Their economy rested on agriculture — maize, potatoes, quinoa and cotton — and on control of salt mines at Zipaquirá and emerald mines, traded over wide networks.

El Dorado

The Muisca ceremony at Lake Guatavita, in which a new zipa was covered in gold dust and offerings of gold and emeralds were cast into the lake, gave rise to the Spanish legend of El Dorado. Muisca goldwork, especially small votive figures called tunjos, is now showcased at the Museo del Oro in Bogotá.

Conquest

The Spanish conquistador Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada conquered the Muisca in 1536–1538. Today around 14,000 people identify as Muisca, organised in recognised indigenous councils in Cundinamarca and Bogotá.
