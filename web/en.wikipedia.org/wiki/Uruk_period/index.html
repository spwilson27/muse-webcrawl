Uruk period

The Uruk period (c. 4000 to 3100 BC; also spelled Uruk period, sometimes called the Warka period) existed from the protohistoric Chalcolithic to the Early Bronze Age in the history of Mesopotamia, after the Ubaid period and before the Jemdet Nasr period. Named after the Sumerian city of Uruk, this period saw the emergence of urban life in Mesopotamia and the Sumerian civilization.

Chronology

Archaeologists divide the Uruk period into phases based on the stratigraphy of the Eanna precinct at Uruk, principally levels XIV to IV. The Late Uruk period (c. 3400–3100 BC) witnessed the most dramatic social transformations, including the appearance of the first writing.

Urbanization

During this period Uruk grew into one of the largest settlements in the world, reaching perhaps 50,000–80,000 inhabitants within a walled area of about 6 km². Monumental architecture appeared, including the White Temple built atop a high terrace (an early ziggurat) and the limestone and cone-mosaic temples of the Eanna district.

Writing and administration

The earliest known writing, proto-cuneiform, appears in Uruk IV (c. 3300 BC) on clay tablets that record rations, land, and commodities. Cylinder seals, numerical tokens, and bevelled-rim bowls — probably used to distribute standardized rations — reflect a new bureaucratic administration.

Uruk expansion

In the later fourth millennium BC, Uruk-style material culture spread across a vast area from western Iran to southeastern Turkey and northern Syria, at sites such as Habuba Kabira, Jebel Aruda, and Tell Brak. Scholars debate whether this "Uruk expansion" represents colonies, trading enclaves, or emulation by local elites.
