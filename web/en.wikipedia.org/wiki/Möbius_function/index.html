In number theory, the Möbius function μ(n) is a multiplicative function introduced by the German mathematician August Ferdinand Möbius in 1832. It is ubiquitous in elementary and analytic number theory and most often appears as part of its namesake Möbius inversion formula. It may also be defined in terms of the prime factorisation of n:

  • μ(n) = 1 if n is a square-free positive integer with an even number of prime factors;
  • μ(n) = −1 if n is square-free with an odd number of prime factors;
  • μ(n) = 0 if n has a squared prime factor.

By convention μ(1) = 1, since the empty product has zero prime factors, an even number.

Properties

The sum of the Möbius function over all positive divisors of n is zero unless n = 1, in which case the sum is 1. This property underlies Möbius inversion: if g(n) = Σd|n f(d), then f(n) = Σd|n μ(d) g(n/d). The summatory Möbius function, the Mertens function M(x) = Σn≤x μ(n), exhibits chaotic-looking cancellation; its growth is intimately connected with the Riemann hypothesis — indeed, the statement that M(x) = O(x1/2+ε) for every ε > 0 is equivalent to it. The disproven Mertens conjecture claimed the stronger bound |M(x)| < √x.

Applications

The Möbius function appears in counting aperiodic necklaces and irreducible polynomials over finite fields, in the inclusion–exclusion principle, in formulas for Euler's totient function, and in the theory of Dirichlet series, where Σ μ(n)/ns = 1/ζ(s) for the Riemann zeta function. In physics, it arises in the study of the free-fermion gas and in certain models of statistical mechanics.
