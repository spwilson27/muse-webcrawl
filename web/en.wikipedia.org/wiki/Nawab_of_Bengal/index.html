The Nawab of Bengal was the hereditary ruler of Bengal Subah in Mughal India. Under Emperor Aurangzeb and afterwards, the office combined the functions of subahdar (governor) and diwan (revenue chief), making its holder effectively the sovereign of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa — then one of the richest provinces in the world. The Nawabs ruled from Murshidabad from 1717 until the British East India Company assumed control after the Battle of Plassey (1757) and the grant of the Diwani in 1765.

Rise of the Nawabi

Murshid Quli Khan, appointed diwan of Bengal in 1700 and later subahdar, transferred the capital from Dhaka to a new city named after himself, Murshidabad, in 1704. In 1717 the Mughal emperor Farrukhsiyar formally appointed him Nawab Nazim. He reorganised revenue administration, and Bengal's surplus famously remitted large sums to Delhi. He was succeeded by his son-in-law Shuja-ud-Din Muhammad Khan (1727–1739). Alivardi Khan seized power in 1740 and defended Bengal against repeated Maratha invasions, concluding peace by ceding Orissa's revenues (chasuth) arrangements in 1751.

Notable Nawabs

  • Murshid Quli Khan (1717–1727) — founder of the Nawabi and of Murshidabad.
  • Shuja-ud-Din (1727–1739) — added Bihar to the charge.
  • Sarfaraz Khan (1739–1740) — deposed and killed by Alivardi Khan.
  • Alivardi Khan (1740–1756) — repelled Maratha invasions; governed with ability.
  • Siraj ud-Daulah (1756–1757) — the last independent Nawab, defeated at Plassey.
  • Mir Jafar (1757–1760, 1763–1765) — installed by the Company after betraying Siraj.
  • Mir Qasim (1760–1763) — attempted reform and independence; defeated and fled.

Plassey and Company rule

Siraj ud-Daulah's capture of Calcutta in 1756 (and the "Black Hole" episode) led Robert Clive to retake the city and conspire with Mir Jafar and the Jagat Seth bankers. At the Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757), Siraj's army collapsed through treachery; he was captured and executed. Successive Company-installed Nawabs ceded ever more rights, and after Mir Qasim's defeat at Buxar (1764), the Treaty of Allahabad (1765) granted the Company the Diwani (revenue administration) of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa — the legal foundation of British rule in India. The Nawabs retained the Nizamat ( nominal government) as pensioned figureheads.

Later title

Descendants of Mir Jafar continued as Nawabs of Murshidabad on a stipend. The title of Nawab of Bengal was abolished by the British in 1880, after which the head of the family was styled Nawab Bahadur of Murshidabad. The family's Hazarduari Palace at Murshidabad, built in the 1830s, remains a major monument.
