Noise-induced hearing loss

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An audiogram showing the typical 4 kHz notch.

Noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) is a hearing impairment resulting from exposure to loud sound. People may have a loss of perception of a range of frequencies or impaired perception of sound, including sensitivity to sound or ringing in the ears (tinnitus). When exposure to hazards such as noise occurs at work and is associated with hearing loss, it is referred to as occupational hearing loss.

Causes

Hearing may deteriorate gradually from chronic, usually long-term, exposure to moderately excessive sound levels (noise-induced), or hearing loss may be caused by a single very loud impulse, such as a gunshot or explosion (acoustic trauma). Both types damage the sensory hair cells of the inner ear, particularly the outer hair cells of the cochlea, and the damage is generally permanent because these cells do not regenerate in humans.

Characteristics

NIHL typically affects both ears and first appears as a "notch" in the audiogram at 3, 4 or 6 kHz (most characteristically 4 kHz), with better hearing at lower and higher frequencies in its early stages. With continued exposure the loss spreads to neighbouring frequencies, including the speech frequencies, affecting communication. Sounds of short duration, such as impulse noise, can cause instantaneous damage.

Exposure limits

Many jurisdictions regulate occupational noise. In the United States, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration sets a permissible exposure limit of 90 dBA for an eight-hour time-weighted average with a 5 dB exchange rate, while the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health recommends 85 dBA with a 3 dB exchange rate. The European Union sets upper exposure action values of 85 dBA. Roughly 16% of adult hearing loss worldwide is attributed to occupational noise.

Prevention

Prevention follows the hierarchy of hazard controls: engineering controls to reduce noise at the source, administrative controls to limit exposure time, and hearing protection devices such as earplugs and earmuffs, which can substantially reduce exposure when fitted and worn correctly. Hearing conservation programmes include regular audiometric monitoring. Recreational risks include loud concerts, shooting sports and listening to personal audio devices at high volume; the World Health Organization has warned that over a billion young people are at risk from unsafe listening practices.

Treatment

There is no cure that restores the damaged hair cells; management includes hearing aids, assistive listening devices and auditory rehabilitation.
