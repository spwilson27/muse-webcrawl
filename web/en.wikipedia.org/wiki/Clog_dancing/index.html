Clog dancing

Clog dancing is a form of step dance in which the dancer's footwear — traditionally wooden-soled clogs — is used as a percussion instrument, striking the heel and toe against the floor to create audible rhythms. It developed in the industrial towns of northern England during the 19th century and has related traditions in Wales, the Netherlands, and Appalachia.

[Image: Morris dancers performing a clog dance in wooden-soled clogs]

Origins

Workers in the cotton mills of Lancashire wore clogs with wooden soles and iron or rubber "irons" on the floor. During breaks they danced steps and shuffles, some said to imitate the rhythms and movements of the looms. Clog dancing became a staple of music hall, with champions such as Dan Leno — a noted clog dancer before his fame as a comedian — and Charlie Chaplin, who learned steps in the halls.

Regional styles

The Lancashire and Northumbrian traditions emphasise intricate, low-to-the-floor steps performed on the spot, while Welsh clog dancing (clocsio) features athletic tricks, including dances around a lit candle and snuffing it with the sole. Dutch klompendans is usually a group folk dance. In the United States, Appalachian "clogging" fused British steps with African-American and Native American influences and is performed to bluegrass, often in smooth-soled shoes with taps.

Modern practice

Clog dancing survives in folk festivals, competitions such as the Lancashire and Cheshire Clog Dancing Championships, and within Morris dance sides. It is recognised as an ancestor of tap dance and of modern street styles that emphasise percussive footwork.
