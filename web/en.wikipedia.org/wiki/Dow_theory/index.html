Dow theory on stock market movements is a form of technical analysis that includes some aspects of sector rotation. The theory was derived from 255 editorials in The Wall Street Journal written by Charles H. Dow (1851–1902), co-founder of Dow Jones and Company. Following Dow's death, William Peter Hamilton, Robert Rhea, and E. George Schaefer organized and collectively represented Dow theory, based on Dow's editorials.

The six tenets

  1. The market has three movements — a primary trend lasting years, secondary reactions lasting weeks to months, and minor day-to-day fluctuations.
  2. Market trends have three phases — in a bull market: accumulation, public participation, and excess (distribution); bear markets show the mirror image.
  3. The stock market discounts all news — prices incorporate all available information and expectations.
  4. Stock market averages must confirm each other — the industrials and transports (originally rails) must move in the same direction for a trend signal to be valid.
  5. Trends are confirmed by volume — volume should expand in the direction of the primary trend.
  6. A trend is assumed to remain in effect until it gives definite signals that it has reversed.

Application and evidence

Practitioners watch for both averages simultaneously making new highs or lows after a secondary reaction as a buy or sell signal. Alfred Cowles III, in a famous 1934 study, found that Hamilton's published signals had roughly matched buy-and-hold returns with less risk; later studies have been mixed, and Dow theory is criticised for late signals — by design, it never attempts to catch exact tops and bottoms. Nevertheless it remains the historical root of technical analysis and of concepts such as trend-following and market breadth.
