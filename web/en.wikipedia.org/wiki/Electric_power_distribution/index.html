Electric power distribution is the final stage in the delivery of electric power: it carries electricity from the transmission system to individual consumers. Distribution substations connect to the transmission network and step voltage down — commonly to between 2.4 and 34.5 kilovolts — for primary distribution lines that feed distribution transformers near customers' premises. Those transformers reduce voltage again to the utilisation levels used by homes and businesses, such as 120/240 volts in North America or 230/400 volts in Europe.

System design

Distribution networks are usually arranged as radial feeders, though urban areas often use interconnected or looped networks for reliability. Equipment includes overhead lines on utility poles, underground cables, pole-mounted and pad-mounted transformers, voltage regulators, capacitor banks, reclosers, fuses and switchgear that isolate faults and restore service. In North America, late-19th-century systems pioneered by Edison's companies evolved from DC to AC; rural electrification programmes in the 1930s, such as those of the US Rural Electrification Administration, extended distribution to farming areas.

Modern developments

Distribution is being reshaped by rooftop solar and other distributed energy resources, which can reverse power flows; by electric vehicle charging; by smart meters and advanced metering infrastructure; and by "smart grid" automation that detects and reroutes around faults. Distribution utilities are typically regulated monopolies responsible for the wires, metering and reliability in a service territory, measured by indices such as SAIDI and SAIFI.
