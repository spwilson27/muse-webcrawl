Sunglasses

Sunglasses or sun glasses (informally called shades or sunnies) are a form of protective eyewear designed primarily to prevent bright sunlight and high-energy visible light from damaging or discomforting the eyes. They can also serve as a fashion accessory and as visual aids, with lenses that are coloured, polarized, mirrored, or photochromic.

History

Inuit peoples used flattened walrus-ivory "snow goggles" with narrow slits to reduce glare from snow. In 12th-century China, flat panes of smoky quartz were used by judges to conceal their expressions in court. James Ayscough experimented with tinted lenses in the 18th century. Mass-market sunglasses arrived in 1929 when Sam Foster sold his Foster Grants from an Atlantic City boardwalk stand. Bausch & Lomb's Ray-Ban line, developed in the 1930s for U.S. aviators, popularized the aviator style, and the Wayfarer (1952) became a cultural icon.

Protection

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation can contribute to cataracts, pterygium, photokeratitis ("snow blindness"), and possibly macular degeneration; quality sunglasses block 99–100% of UVA and UVB. Standards such as ISO 12312 classify lenses by filter category from 0 (light tint) to 4 (very dark, unsuitable for driving). Polarized lenses additionally cut reflected glare from water, snow, and roads, and are standard for fishing and winter sports.

Specialized types

Prescription sunglasses, clip-ons, and flip-ups serve wearers of corrective lenses. Athletes use wraparound and interchangeable-lens designs; welders and eclipse viewers use far darker filters. Three-dimensional cinema glasses, including passive polarized and active shutter types, are a related use of tinted eyewear. Some sunglasses have found their way into spaceflight and military use, where glare and UV exposure are extreme.
