And Then There Were None

Author: Agatha Christie

Published: 6 November 1939

Publisher: Collins Crime Club

Genre: Mystery novel

And Then There Were None is a mystery novel by the English writer Agatha Christie, described by her as the most difficult of her books to write. It was first published in the United Kingdom by the Collins Crime Club on 6 November 1939. The book is the world's best-selling mystery novel, and with more than 100 million copies sold is one of the best-selling books of all time.

Plot

Ten strangers are lured to an island off the coast of Devon under various pretexts by a mysterious host, "U. N. Owen". During dinner, a recorded voice accuses each guest of having committed a murder for which they escaped justice. One by one the guests begin to die, in ways that parallel the verses of the nursery rhyme "Ten Little Soldiers", and the soldier figurines on the dining table disappear one at a time. Cut off from the mainland, the survivors realise the killer must be one of them. In the epilogue, a confession found in a bottle reveals the identity and method of the murderer: Justice Lawrence Wargrave, who faked his own death and engineered the killings as an act of twisted justice, before shooting himself in a way designed to baffle the police.

Title

The novel was originally published under a title taken from the final line of the minstrel song that inspired its structure; that title, containing a racial slur, was changed for later editions, and the rhyme's characters were altered over successive versions ("Indians", then "Soldiers"). The current title comes from the last line of the rhyme as printed in the novel.

Reception and adaptations

The novel is widely regarded as Christie's masterpiece and a landmark of the "closed circle" mystery form. It has been adapted many times for stage — including Christie's own 1943 play, which softened the ending — and for film and television, notably René Clair's 1945 film and the BBC's 2015 miniseries.
