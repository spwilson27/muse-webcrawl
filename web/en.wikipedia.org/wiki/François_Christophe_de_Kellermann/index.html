François Christophe de Kellermann

François-Étienne-Christophe Kellermann (28 May 1735 – 23 September 1820), duc de Valmy, was a French military commander who served in the Seven Years' War, the French Revolutionary Wars and the Napoleonic Wars. He is best known for the stand at the Battle of Valmy in 1792, which saved the young French Republic from invasion, and he became one of Napoleon's Marshals of the Empire.

Born: 28 May 1735, Strasbourg, France
Died: 23 September 1820, Paris, France
Rank: Marshal of the Empire (1804)
Title: Duke of Valmy (1808)

Early career

Kellermann was born in Strasbourg into a family of Saxon origin and joined the French army as a cadet in a hussar regiment in 1752. He distinguished himself in the Seven Years' War, served in Poland in 1771–72, and rose steadily to become a maréchal de camp in 1788. He supported the Revolution and in 1791 took command of the Army of the Moselle.

Valmy

In September 1792, Kellermann advanced to join Dumouriez's Army of the North against the invading Prussian army of the Duke of Brunswick. On 20 September, at Valmy, he took position by the village windmill, endured a heavy cannonade, and — famously crying "Vive la Nation!" and waving his hat on his sword — rallied his troops to stand firm. The Prussians broke off the engagement and soon retreated across the frontier; Goethe, present with the Prussian army, remarked that a new epoch in world history had begun. Politically imprisoned for a time during the Terror, Kellermann was acquitted in 1794 and later commanded the Army of the Alps, mentoring the young Napoleon Bonaparte's Italian operations in 1796–97.

Under Napoleon and the Restoration

Too old for field command by 1804, Kellermann was made one of the first four honorary Marshals of the Empire and served as a senator and administrator of reserve forces, organising the national guard depots that fed Napoleon's armies. He was created Duke of Valmy in 1808. After the Restoration he sat in the Chamber of Peers. He died in Paris in 1820 and was buried at Valmy beside the battlefield; his name is inscribed on the Arc de Triomphe. His son François-Étienne Kellermann also became a noted cavalry general.
