Chemiosmosis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of chemiosmosis in a mitochondrion: proton gradient across the inner membrane driving ATP synthase]
Protons pumped across the membrane return through ATP synthase, making ATP.

Chemiosmosis is the movement of ions across a semipermeable membrane down their electrochemical gradient, coupled to the generation of ATP. More specifically, it is the mechanism by which most ATP is produced in cells: electron transport chains pump protons across a membrane (the inner mitochondrial membrane in eukaryotes, the thylakoid membrane in photosynthesis, the cell membrane in bacteria), and the return flow through the enzyme ATP synthase drives the synthesis of ATP from ADP and phosphate.

The theory was proposed in 1961 by British biochemist Peter Mitchell, whose chemiosmotic hypothesis was initially controversial because biochemists expected a soluble chemical intermediate. Its confirmation — the proton motive force measured directly, ATP synthase's rotary mechanism elucidated (John Walker, Paul Boyer) — earned Mitchell the 1978 Nobel Prize in Chemistry and Boyer and Walker a share of the 1997 prize. Chemiosmosis is now recognised as a universal energy-coupling principle, and its antiquity — plausibly predating cells as we know them, in alkaline hydrothermal vents — makes it central to theories of the origin of life.

Mechanism

In mitochondria, electrons from NADH and FADH₂ pass along respiratory complexes I–IV to oxygen; complexes I, III and IV pump protons into the intermembrane space, storing energy as an electrochemical gradient (Δp, about −180 mV across the membrane). ATP synthase, a rotary molecular motor, lets protons flow back through its c-ring (one full rotation per ~3 ATP), catalysing ATP formation in its head. Brown fat short-circuits the gradient with the uncoupling protein thermogenin, converting it directly to heat.
