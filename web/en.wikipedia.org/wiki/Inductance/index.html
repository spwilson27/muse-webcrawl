Inductance

Inductance is the tendency of an electrical conductor to oppose a change in the electric current flowing through it. A changing current creates a changing magnetic field, which in turn induces an electromotive force (EMF) in the conductor itself — a phenomenon known as self-inductance — described by Faraday's law of induction and Lenz's law. The SI unit of inductance is the henry (H), named after the American scientist Joseph Henry.

Definition

Inductance L is defined as the ratio of the induced voltage v to the rate of change of current i:

v = L · di/dt

Equivalently, it is the magnetic flux linkage per unit current. A conductor has an inductance of one henry if a current changing at one ampere per second induces one volt.

Self-inductance and mutual inductance

Self-inductance arises when a circuit's own magnetic field links back through the circuit, as in a coil or solenoid. Mutual inductance describes the coupling between two separate circuits: a changing current in one induces a voltage in the other. Mutual inductance is the operating principle of the transformer. The inductance of a coil depends on the number of turns, the cross-sectional area and length of the coil, and the magnetic permeability of its core material; winding a coil around a ferromagnetic core can increase its inductance by orders of magnitude.

Energy storage

An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field. The stored energy is:

E = ½ L i²

In AC circuits, inductance produces inductive reactance, XL = 2πfL, which increases with frequency and causes the current to lag the voltage by up to 90 degrees in a purely inductive circuit.

Applications

Inductors are used in filters, tuned circuits, transformers, electric motors, energy storage in switched-mode power supplies, and as chokes to block high-frequency signals while passing low-frequency or DC current.
