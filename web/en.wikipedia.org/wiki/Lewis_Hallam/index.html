Lewis Hallam

Lewis Hallam (c. 1714 – 1756) was an English actor and theatre manager who founded the first permanent professional theatre company in colonial America. With his family troupe, Hallam brought a full London repertory of plays to audiences in the American colonies beginning in 1752 and is often called a founder of the American theatre.

Early life

Hallam came from a London theatrical family and worked as an actor in England before deciding to seek his fortune in America. In 1752 he organized a company of about a dozen adult actors and his own children, and sailed for Virginia, arriving in Yorktown and opening at Williamsburg in September 1752 with The Merchant of Venice and the farce Lethe.

The Hallam Company

The troupe, later known as the American Company, toured the colonies, performing in makeshift and purpose-built playhouses in New York, Philadelphia, Charleston, and other cities, staging Shakespeare and popular eighteenth-century English plays such as Richard III, Othello, and The Beggar's Opera. The company faced hostility from religious and civic authorities opposed to the theatre but also found an eager colonial audience. In 1754 Hallam took the company to Jamaica for a more favourable climate of support; he died there in 1756.

Legacy

Hallam's widow Sarah married actor-manager David Douglass, who consolidated the company and built early American playhouses including the Southwark and Chestnut Street theatres in Philadelphia. Lewis Hallam's son, Lewis Hallam Jr., became the leading American actor of his generation, famous as a tragedian and as Jonathan in Royall Tyler's The Contrast (1787), the first American comedy professionally produced.
