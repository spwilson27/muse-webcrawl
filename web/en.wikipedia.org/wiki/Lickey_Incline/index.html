The Lickey Incline, south of Birmingham, England, is the steepest sustained main-line railway gradient in Great Britain. It climbs for about two miles (3.2 km) at a gradient of 1 in 37.7 (2.65%) between Bromsgrove and Blackwell stations on the line from Birmingham to Bristol, part of today's Cross Country Route.

History

The incline was built as part of the Birmingham and Gloucester Railway, engineered by William Moorsom, and opened in 1840. Its gradient was a bold choice for the era: most early railways avoided steep slopes, and a stationary steam engine with rope haulage was originally considered. Instead, the line was worked by locomotives, initially American-built Norris 4-2-0 engines ordered specially for the bank.

For well over a century, ascending trains were assisted by banking engines stationed at Bromsgrove, which attached at the rear of trains and pushed them to the summit at Blackwell. The most famous banker was the unique LMS 0-10-0 locomotive No. 2290, nicknamed "Big Bertha", built in 1919 and fitted with a powerful headlamp for night work. She was replaced in 1956 by BR Standard Class 9F 2-10-0 No. 92079.

Modern operation

Modern diesel and electric freight locomotives, and multiple-unit passenger trains, generally climb the incline unassisted, though banking is still occasionally used for heavy freight. The gradient remains a notable operating challenge, particularly in conditions of poor rail adhesion, and the section is a well-known spot for railway photography.
