The Black Jacobins


C. L. R. James's history of the Haitian Revolution

The Black Jacobins: Toussaint L'Ouverture and the San Domingo Revolution is a 1938 history of the Haitian Revolution by the Trinidadian historian C. L. R. James. It is widely regarded as a foundational text of African and Caribbean historiography and of the study of slave revolts, and remains in print as a classic work of Marxist historical writing.

Contents

James recounts how the enslaved people of the French colony of Saint-Domingue — the richest sugar colony in the world — rose in 1791 in the only successful slave revolt in history, defeating in turn the local planters, the Spanish, the British, and the French expedition sent by Napoleon, and establishing the independent state of Haiti in 1804. Its central figure is Toussaint Louverture, the formerly enslaved coachman who became the revolution's greatest leader, whom James likens — in his discipline, radicalism and eventual fate — to the Jacobins of the French Revolution.

Argument and significance

James presents the San Domingo slaves as a modern proletariat whose revolt was intertwined with the revolutionary upheavals in France, and argues that Caribbean history was shaped by the economics of the sugar plantation and Atlantic capitalism. A revised edition of 1963 added the appendix "From Toussaint L'Ouverture to Fidel Castro", linking the Haitian Revolution to the contemporary Cuban Revolution. The book profoundly influenced Black radical thought and anti-colonial movements, and James himself drew on his research for his 1936 play Toussaint Louverture, performed in London with Paul Robeson.
