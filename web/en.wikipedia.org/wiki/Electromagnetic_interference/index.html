Electromagnetic interference (EMI), also called radio-frequency interference (RFI) when in the radio frequency spectrum, is a disturbance generated by an external source that affects an electrical circuit by electromagnetic induction, electrostatic coupling, or conduction. The disturbance may degrade the performance of the circuit or even stop it from functioning. In the case of a data path, these effects can range from an increase in error rate to a total loss of the data.

Sources

EMI sources may be naturally occurring or human-made. Natural sources include lightning, solar flares and electrostatic discharge. Human-made sources include ignition systems, mobile phones, switched-mode power supplies, electric motors, fluorescent lights and radio transmitters. Rapidly switching currents in digital circuits are a common unintentional source of emissions.

Coupling mechanisms

Interference can be conducted along power and signal cables, or radiated through space as electromagnetic waves. At low frequencies, capacitive and inductive coupling between nearby conductors dominate; at high frequencies, structures begin to behave as antennas and radiation becomes significant. Conducted interference is often divided into differential mode and common mode components.

Effects and examples

Classic examples include buzzing in audio equipment near mobile phones, artefacts on television screens from household appliances, and disrupted radio reception near faulty power lines. In critical settings — aircraft avionics, medical devices, automotive electronics — EMI can pose safety risks, which is why devices must meet emissions and immunity standards.

Mitigation and regulation

Common countermeasures include shielding enclosures (Faraday cages), filtering on cables, ferrite beads, twisted-pair wiring, proper grounding, and careful circuit-board layout. Regulators such as the FCC in the United States and the EMC Directive in the European Union set limits on emissions and require immunity testing, ensuring that equipment can coexist — the discipline known as electromagnetic compatibility (EMC).
