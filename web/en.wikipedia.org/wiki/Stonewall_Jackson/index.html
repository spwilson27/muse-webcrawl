Stonewall Jackson

Thomas Jonathan "Stonewall" JacksonBorn: 21 January 1824, Clarksburg, Virginia
Died: 10 May 1863, Guinea Station, Virginia
Allegiance: United States; Confederate States
Service: U.S. Army (1846–1851); Confederate Army (1861–1863)
Rank: Lieutenant general (CSA)

Thomas Jonathan "Stonewall" Jackson (January 21, 1824 – May 10, 1863) was a Confederate general during the American Civil War, considered one of the most gifted tactical commanders in American military history. He served as a corps commander under General Robert E. Lee in the Army of Northern Virginia.

Early life and career

Born in Clarksburg, Virginia (now West Virginia), Jackson was orphaned young and raised by relatives. He graduated from West Point in 1846 and served with distinction in the Mexican–American War. He resigned from the army in 1851 to teach at the Virginia Military Institute in Lexington, where his stern manner earned him the nickname "Tom Fool" among cadets.

Civil War

At the First Battle of Bull Run in July 1861, Jackson's brigade stood firm against Union attacks, prompting General Barnard Bee's famous remark that Jackson stood "like a stone wall" — the origin of his nickname. In the spring of 1862, Jackson's brilliant Shenandoah Valley campaign defeated multiple Union forces and tied down troops Washington had hoped to use against Richmond. He went on to fight in the Seven Days Battles, the Second Battle of Bull Run, Antietam, and Fredericksburg, and led the daring flank march at Chancellorsville in May 1863 that routed the Union right wing.

Death and legacy

On the night of May 2, 1863, returning from a reconnaissance at Chancellorsville, Jackson was accidentally shot by his own men. His left arm was amputated, and he died of pneumonia eight days later, aged 39. His death was a severe blow to the Confederacy. Jackson remains a figure of study in military academies for his audacity, secrecy, and speed of manoeuvre, though his memory is also bound up with the Confederacy's defence of slavery, and monuments to him have been removed in recent years, including the statue on Richmond's Monument Avenue in 2020.
