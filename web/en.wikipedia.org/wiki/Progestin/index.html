Progestin

A progestin is a synthetic progestogen — a drug that mimics the effects of the natural female hormone progesterone. Progestins are used most commonly in hormonal contraception (alone or combined with an estrogen) and in menopausal hormone therapy to protect the endometrium. They are also used to treat gynecological disorders such as endometriosis and abnormal uterine bleeding, and in some cases for hormone therapy in transgender women and in the treatment of hormone-sensitive cancers.

Classification

Progestins are commonly grouped into generations based on when they were introduced, and structurally into those derived from progesterone itself (pregnanes), from 19-nortestosterone (estranes and gonanes), and from spironolactone (such as drospirenone). Different progestins vary in their androgenic, antiandrogenic, glucocorticoid, and antimineralocorticoid activity, which influences their side-effect profiles.

Examples

Common progestins include norethindrone (norethisterone), levonorgestrel, desogestrel, etonogestrel, drospirenone, medroxyprogesterone acetate, and micronized progesterone (which is bioidentical to the natural hormone).

Medical uses

Progestins form the basis of progestogen-only contraceptives — the "minipill", implants, injections, and hormonal intrauterine devices — and are the progestogenic component of combined oral contraceptives. In hormone therapy they oppose estrogen's stimulatory effect on the endometrium, preventing endometrial hyperplasia and cancer.
