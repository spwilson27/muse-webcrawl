Cava (Catalan: cava, "cellar") is a Spanish sparkling wine produced mainly in the Penedès region of Catalonia using the traditional method — a second fermentation in the bottle, as in Champagne. It is protected by a Denominación de Origen (DO) and is one of the world's best-selling traditional-method sparkling wines. The word replaced the older term champaña after Spain's entry into the European Community restricted the use of "Champagne"-derived names.

History

Sparkling wine production in Catalonia began in the 19th century; Josep Raventós of Codorníu produced the first bottles in 1872 after visiting Champagne. The industry grew around the town of Sant Sadurní d'Anoia, home to houses such as Codorníu and Freixenet, whose rivalry drove exports through the 20th century. In 2015 leading producers introduced the single-estate classification Cava de Paraje Calificado, and in 2019 a group of estates left the DO to form the Corpinnat association, though Corpinnat wines are no longer labelled Cava.

Production

The principal grapes are Macabeo (Viura), Xarel·lo and Parellada, with Chardonnay and Pinot Noir also authorised (the latter for rosé, cava rosado). Base wines are blended, bottled with yeast and sugar for the second fermentation, and aged on the lees for a minimum of nine months; Reserva requires 18 months and Gran Reserva 30 months, with vintage dating. Styles range from Brut Nature (no added sugar) to Dulce, classified by residual sugar.

Regions and consumption

Although over 95% of Cava comes from Catalonia, the DO also includes municipalities in La Rioja, Valencia, Aragon, Extremadura (Almendralejo) and elsewhere. Cava is traditionally drunk at celebrations and with food, and Spain exports well over 100 million bottles annually.
