D-Day is the day on which a combat attack or operation is to be initiated. The best-known D-Day is 6 June 1944, the day of the Normandy landings, which began the Allied effort to liberate mainland Europe from Nazi occupation during the Second World War; in popular usage "D-Day" almost always refers to that date.

Terminology

The terms D-Day and H-Hour are used for the day and hour on which a combat attack or operation is to be initiated. They designate the day and hour of the operation when the day and hour have not yet been determined, or where secrecy is essential. The letters are derived from the words they represent: "D" for the day of the invasion and "H" for the hour operations actually begin — the notation can be tracked back to the Field Order of the American First Army for the Saint-Mihiel offensive of September 1918. Days before and after are indicated as D−3, D+2 and so on, allowing whole plans to be written around a floating date.

6 June 1944

The D-Day of Operation Overlord had originally been tentatively set for 5 June 1944, but bad weather forced a 24-hour postponement; the convoy already at sea endured a miserable night before General Eisenhower, on his meteorologist's advice, gave the order: "OK, we'll go." In the early hours of 6 June, airborne forces dropped over Normandy, and at H-Hour — from about 06:30, varying by beach — the greatest armada ever assembled landed some 156,000 troops on five beaches by the end of the day.

Other notable D-Days

Many other operations had their own D-Days which never entered popular memory, including the landings on Sicily (10 July 1943), at Salerno and Anzio, in southern France (Operation Dragoon, 15 August 1944), and in the Pacific at Leyte, Iwo Jima and Okinawa. The term lives on in planning usage and, figuratively, for any momentous scheduled day.
