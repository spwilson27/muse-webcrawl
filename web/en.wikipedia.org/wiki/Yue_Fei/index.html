Yue Fei (Chinese: 岳飛; courtesy name Pengju, 鵬舉; 1103–1142) was a Chinese military general of the Southern Song dynasty who fought against the Jurchen-led Jin dynasty in the Jin–Song Wars. Renowned for his tactical brilliance, his disciplined "Yue Family Army" (岳家軍), and his execution on fabricated charges, he became China's pre-eminent folk hero and a cultural symbol of patriotism, loyalty and integrity — a reputation embodied in the four characters his mother is said to have tattooed on his back: 精忠報國, "serve the country with utmost loyalty".

Career

Born to a poor tenant-farming family in Henan, Yue Fei mastered archery, spear-fighting and military classics as a youth. He enlisted as the Jin invasions destroyed the Northern Song (1125–1127), rising from private soldier to commander through victories against bandits, rebels and Jin forces. By the mid-1130s he commanded one of the dynasty's largest field armies from his base at Ezhou, recovered lost territory including Xiangyang and the strategic approaches to the Central Plain, and built a force famed for discipline — his maxim held that it was "easy to shake a mountain, hard to shake the Yue army". In 1140, during his most successful northern offensive, his troops defeated the Jin at Yancheng and Zhuxianzhen and advanced toward Kaifeng.

Recall and death

Emperor Gaozong and his chancellor Qin Hui, favouring a negotiated peace and fearing that victories threatened both their policy and the throne's control of the army, recalled Yue Fei — legend says with twelve gold plaques in a single day. Yue was arrested on charges of disloyalty; after a two-month investigation found no evidence, he was executed in prison on 28 January 1142, together with his son Yue Yun and his son-in-law Zhang Xian. The Song then concluded the Treaty of Shaoxing, accepting vassal status and the Huai River as the border with Jin.

Rehabilitation and legend

Yue Fei was posthumously rehabilitated by Emperor Xiaozong in 1162 and lavishly honoured thereafter. His biography in the History of Song was compiled from family records by his grandson Yue Ke. Folk tradition supplied beloved episodes — the back tattoo, the poem Man Jiang Hong ("All Red the River"), traditionally attributed to him — and the Qing-era novel The Story of Yue Fei fixed his image in popular culture. At his temple in Hangzhou, statues of Qin Hui and others accused of framing him kneel in disgrace before his tomb, spat upon by visitors for centuries.
