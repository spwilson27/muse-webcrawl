Mistrial

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mistrial is a trial that is ended without a decision on the merits because of a fundamental error or circumstance that prevents a fair conclusion — including a hung jury (deadlock), serious prosecutorial or judicial misconduct, inadmissible evidence heard by the jury that cannot be "unheard", juror misconduct, or the death or incapacity of a participant. A mistrial is not an acquittal or a conviction; it normally leaves the parties as if the trial had not occurred.

Consequences

Whether the case may be retried depends on why the mistrial occurred and who sought it. Retrial after a hung jury is routine; after a mistrial granted on the defendant's motion it is usually permitted — unless the prosecution intended to goad the defendant into seeking it (Oregon v. Kennedy, 1982) — while a mistrial declared without the defendant's consent requires "manifest necessity" (United States v. Perez, 1824) or double jeopardy bars retrial. Mistrials cost heavily: witnesses rehearsed twice, victims re-traumatised, evidence deteriorating — so appellate courts prefer curative instructions, severance and other fixes where the error is reparable, and grant mistrials for prejudice that cannot otherwise be undone (massive publicity, coerced confessions aired, tainted juries).
