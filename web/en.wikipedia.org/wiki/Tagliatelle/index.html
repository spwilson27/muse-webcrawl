Tagliatelle

Tagliatelle (Italian: [taʎʎaˈtɛlle]) are a traditional type of pasta from the Emilia-Romagna and Marche regions of Italy. Individual pieces of tagliatelle are long, flat ribbons that are similar in shape to fettuccine but are typically made with egg pasta dough in a ratio of one egg per 100 grams of flour, and are usually about 6–7 mm wide.

Name and legend

The name derives from the Italian verb tagliare, "to cut". A popular legend attributes the pasta's invention to a court chef in 1487 who was inspired by the blonde hair of Lucrezia Borgia on the occasion of her marriage, but this story was spread as a humorous invention by the Bolognese artist Augusto Majani in 1931.

Official measure

In 1972 the Italian Academy of Cuisine deposited an official specimen and recipe at the Bologna chamber of commerce: the cooked tagliatella of Bologna is set at 7 mm in width (1/12,270 of the height of the Asinelli tower), corresponding to about 8 mm uncooked. A golden model of the measure is displayed in the Palazzo della Mercanzia.

Serving

Tagliatelle are classically served with ragù alla bolognese, but also suit other meat, mushroom and game sauces; the porous texture of hand-rolled egg pasta holds rich sauces well. A variant cut slightly narrower is called tagliolini (or tajarin in Piedmont).
