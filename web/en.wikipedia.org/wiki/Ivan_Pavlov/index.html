Ivan Pavlov

Born26 September 1849, Ryazan, Russian Empire
Died27 February 1936, Leningrad, USSR
Known forClassical conditioning; Pavlov's dogs
AwardsNobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1904)

Ivan Petrovich Pavlov (1849–1936) was a Russian and Soviet physiologist known chiefly for his work on classical conditioning — "Pavlov's dogs". His experiments on digestion, for which he won the 1904 Nobel Prize, led him to a systematic science of conditioned reflexes that profoundly influenced psychology and behaviourism.

Early life and digestion research

The son of a priest, Pavlov studied at St Petersburg University and the Military Medical Academy, becoming professor of physiology. Using surgically prepared dogs with gastric fistulas and a "Pavlov pouch", he mapped the nervous control of digestion, publishing The Work of the Digestive Glands (1897).

Conditioned reflexes

Noticing dogs salivated before food arrived, Pavlov studied how a neutral stimulus (a bell or metronome) paired with food came to elicit salivation alone. He defined acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalisation and discrimination, and developed concepts of excitation and inhibition and experimental neurosis. His final decades, funded by the Soviet state despite his criticism of it, were spent extending the theory to psychiatry and human behaviour ("second signal system").

Legacy

"Pavlovian" conditioning became a cornerstone of learning theory and behaviour therapy; the phrase "Pavlov's dog" entered everyday language for automatic responses. Pavlov died in Leningrad in 1936, working almost to the end.
