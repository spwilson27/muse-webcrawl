Willard Van Orman Quine (1908–2000) was an American philosopher and logician, one of the dominant figures in 20th-century Analytic philosophy, who spent his career at Harvard University. Trained in mathematical logic (his dissertation completed under Whitehead's supervision), he visited the Vienna Circle in the 1930s and became the leading advocate of a naturalised, science-centred philosophy.

Thought

His essay "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) attacked the analytic–synthetic distinction and reductionism, proposing instead confirmational holism: our beliefs face the tribunal of experience as a corporate body — a "web of belief" whose edges are observation and whose interior, including logic and mathematics, is in principle revisable. Word and Object (1960) advanced the indeterminacy of translation (the "gavagai" thought experiment) and ontological relativity. Quine's naturalised epistemology proposed studying knowledge as a chapter of empirical psychology, and his work on set theory (the NF system), ontology ("to be is to be the value of a bound variable") and the philosophy of language shaped generations of analytic philosophers.

Style and legacy

Known for aphoristic prose, logical rigour and wide travel, Quine trained or influenced figures including Donald Davidson and Daniel Dennett. His scepticism about meanings, analyticity and the mental remains a standing challenge — and provocation — within philosophy of language and mind.
