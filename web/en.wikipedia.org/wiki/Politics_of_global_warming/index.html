Politics of global warming

The politics of global warming concern the public debate, policy choices and international negotiations surrounding climate change — in particular how far, how fast and by whom greenhouse-gas emissions should be reduced, and how societies should adapt to warming already under way.

International framework

Global climate politics are organised through the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (1992). The Kyoto Protocol (1997) set binding emission targets for developed countries only, while the Paris Agreement (2015) committed nearly all states to nationally determined contributions aimed at keeping warming "well below" 2 °C and pursuing 1.5 °C. Annual Conferences of the Parties (COPs) negotiate finance, loss and damage, and carbon markets.

Domestic politics

Within countries, climate policy cuts across traditional party lines in some states but is sharply polarised in others — notably the United States, where attitudes to climate action divide strongly by party. Fossil-fuel industries, renewable-energy firms, insurers and farmers lobby over the costs and benefits of action. Instruments debated include carbon taxes, emissions trading, subsidies for clean energy, efficiency standards and outright bans.

Controversy and movements

Although the scientific consensus that human activity is warming the climate is robust, political controversy persists over the speed of transition, the fairness of burdens between rich and poor countries, and energy security. Climate activism — from the school strikes of Fridays for Future to litigation against governments and oil companies — has become a significant political force. See also climate change mitigation.
