Disruptive innovation

In business theory, disruptive innovation is an innovation that creates a new market and value network or enters at the bottom of an existing market and eventually displaces established market-leading firms, products, and alliances. The term was defined and the phenomenon analysed by the American scholar Clayton Christensen and his collaborators beginning in 1995 and in the book The Innovator's Dilemma (1997).

Theory

Christensen distinguished sustaining innovations, which improve products along dimensions mainstream customers value, from disruptive innovations, which initially underperform on those dimensions but offer other advantages — typically simplicity, convenience, or lower price. Disruptions begin either in low-end footholds, serving customers overserved by incumbents (as minimill steelmakers did to integrated mills, and discount retailers to department stores), or in new-market footholds, competing against non-consumption (as personal computers did against mainframes, and transistor radios against vacuum-tube sets). Incumbents rationally ignore such entrants because their best customers do not want the early products and their margins are unattractive; by the time the entrant's performance improves enough for the mainstream, the incumbent's position is undermined.

Examples and evidence

Canonical cases include hydraulic excavators displacing cable-actuated ones, 5.25-inch disk drives displacing 8-inch drives, digital photography displacing film, and streaming video displacing video-rental chains. Later work by Christensen and co-authors cautioned that not every successful challenger is a true disruption — ride-hailing, for example, they argued began as a sustaining innovation relative to taxis — and empirical tests of the theory's predictive accuracy have produced mixed results. Jill Lepore's 2014 critique in The New Yorker challenged the case-study evidence and the theory's popular reception; Christensen responded that the theory was being misapplied as a generic label for competitive change.

Impact

Despite the debates, "disruption" became a central concept in management practice and technology journalism, shaping corporate strategy, venture investment, and government policy debates on education, health care, and media.
