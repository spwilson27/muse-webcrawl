International Sign

International Sign (IS) is a pidgin sign language used in a variety of different contexts, particularly by the World Federation of the Deaf (WFD) at international meetings such as its congress, the Deaflympics, and events of the European Union of the Deaf, as well as informally when travelling and socialising across language borders. Earlier names for substantially the same practice include Gestuno, a name proposed in the 1970s when a WFD commission attempted to standardise a vocabulary.

Nature of the language

International Sign is not a fully conventionalised natural language but a flexible contact variety. Its lexicon draws heavily on signs that are iconic — visually motivated by their referents — and on vocabulary shared among or borrowed from European and North American sign languages, especially signs spread through international deaf institutions. Skilled IS users rely extensively on strategies common to sign languages: use of space, classifier constructions, depiction, gesture, mouthing of words from spoken languages, and negotiation of meaning with the audience. Comprehension therefore depends heavily on shared context and the signer's skill.

Use and interpretation

Because IS is not any community's native language, conferences provide interpretation into and out of IS by trained interpreters, alongside national sign languages. The WFD and the World Association of Sign Language Interpreters have developed standards and training for IS interpreting. Research describes IS as a pidgin with a core lexicon but considerable variation between signers and settings, and scholars debate whether repeated, more standardised uses — especially in recorded media and among interpreters — are driving it toward greater stability.

History

Deaf people from different countries have communicated across language barriers at international gatherings since the nineteenth century, beginning with the first world congresses of the deaf. In 1973 the WFD published a dictionary, Gestuno: International Sign Language of the Deaf, listing about 1,500 signs chosen by committee; the artificial vocabulary never fully caught on, and natural usage — which proved more flexible — persisted under the name International Sign.
