Manfred Symphony

Manfred Symphony
in B minor, Op. 58
Composer: Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky
Composed: 1885
Premiere: 23 March 1886, Moscow
Movements: 4

The Manfred Symphony in B minor, Op. 58 is a program symphony by Russian composer Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky, written between May and September 1885. It is based on the poem Manfred by Lord Byron, following Tchaikovsky's earlier works Romeo and Juliet and Francesca da Rimini in the Byron-inspired vein. The idea was suggested by the composer Mily Balakirev, who had earlier proposed the subject to Hector Berlioz, who declined it.

Tchaikovsky initially felt little enthusiasm for the subject, but after close engagement with Byron's poem while staying in the Swiss Alps — the poem's setting — he became absorbed in the project. The work is the largest of his symphonies in scale and scoring, and the only one without a number; Tchaikovsky did not count it among his numbered symphonies.

Movements

The symphony has four movements:

  1. Lento lugubre — Manfred wanders in the Alps, tormented by guilt over a forbidden love.
  2. Vivace con spirito — the appearance of the Alpine fairy in a rainbow.
  3. Andante con moto — a pastorale depicting the simple life of the mountain folk.
  4. Allegro con fuoco — an infernal banquet in the subterranean palace of Arimanes; Manfred dies, forgiven.

A recurring idée fixe, first heard at the opening, represents Manfred himself and unifies the work. The finale reuses material from the first movement before the hero's death and a quiet concluding chorale.

Reception

Tchaikovsky's own opinion of the symphony fluctuated; he called it one of his best works in some letters and expressed a wish to destroy parts of it in others. It has generally been performed less frequently than his numbered symphonies, though it has been championed by conductors such as Arturo Toscanini.
