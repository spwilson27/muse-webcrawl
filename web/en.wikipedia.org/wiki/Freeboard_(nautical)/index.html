Freeboard (nautical)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia · For the engineering term, see Freeboard.

In sailing and boating, freeboard is the distance from the waterline to the upper deck level, measured at the lowest point where water can enter the boat or ship — formally, on a merchant ship, the vertical distance between the summer load waterline and the top of the freeboard deck. Greater freeboard gives more reserve buoyancy, keeps decks drier in waves, and provides more enclosed volume for cargo, but it also raises the centre of gravity and windage, so ship design balances freeboard against stability and cost.

Regulation and load lines

Minimum freeboards for merchant ships are assigned under the International Convention on Load Lines (1966), administered by the International Maritime Organization. The assigned freeboard determines the position of the load line marks (Plimsoll marks) painted amidships, which show the deepest the ship may legally be loaded in different water densities and seasons (tropical, summer, winter, North Atlantic winter, and fresh water). Tables in the convention compute tabular freeboard from the ship's length and type, with corrections for hull depth, sheer, superstructure, and other features. The system descends from the British Merchant Shipping Act of 1876, championed by Samuel Plimsoll to end the scandal of overloaded, unseaworthy "coffin ships".

Small craft

In small boats, freeboard is measured from the waterline to the gunwale or deck edge. Low-freeboard designs (such as racing dinghies and many canoes) are lighter and have less windage but ship water easily; high-freeboard boats ride drier and carry more but are more affected by wind. A boat with insufficient freeboard for its load risks swamping — excess freeboard for its beam, conversely, can make a vessel stiff or cranky in handling.
