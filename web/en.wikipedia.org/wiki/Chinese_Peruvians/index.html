Chinese Peruvians (Spanish: chino-peruanos) are Peruvian citizens of Chinese ancestry, forming one of the largest and oldest Chinese diaspora communities in the Americas. Their history begins in the mid-19th century, when Peru imported tens of thousands of indentured laborers from China — known as coolies — to replace slave labour on coastal sugar and cotton plantations and in the guano fields of the Chincha Islands.

History

Between roughly 1849 and 1874 over 100,000 Chinese workers arrived under harsh, often deadly conditions. Many who completed their contracts settled in Peru, working as merchants, cooks and market gardeners. Anti-Chinese riots punctuated the late 19th and early 20th centuries, but the community endured, and Chinese immigrants continued to arrive after the Second World War and again from the 1990s.

Today millions of Peruvians claim some Chinese ancestry, concentrated in Lima's Barrio Chino (Chinatown) and on the northern coast. Chifa — the Chinese-Peruvian cuisine that blends Cantonese cooking with Peruvian ingredients — is one of Peru's most popular foods, and the Cantonese-Spanish hybrid vocabulary of the early migrants left lasting traces in Peruvian Spanish. Notable Chinese Peruvians include the politician and Fujimori-era official Luz Salgado and athletes, entrepreneurs and artists of Sino-Peruvian descent.

Culture

The community maintains Chinese associations, temples and festivals, including Lunar New Year celebrations in Lima's Chinatown, alongside full participation in Peruvian public life; intermarriage is common and most Chinese Peruvians speak Spanish as their first language.
