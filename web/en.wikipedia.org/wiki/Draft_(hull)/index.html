Draft (hull)

The draft or draught of a ship's hull is the vertical distance between the waterline and the bottom of the hull (keel). Draft determines the minimum depth of water a ship or boat can safely navigate. The related term air draft is the maximum height of a ship above the waterline, and matters for passing under bridges.

The more heavily a vessel is loaded, the deeper it sinks into the water and the greater its draft. After construction, the shipyard creates a table showing how much water the vessel displaces at different drafts. Draft can also affect sailing performance: a deeper draft generally increases stability and lateral resistance.

Measurement

Draft is measured and marked on the bow and stern by draft marks — numerals painted on the hull whose size and spacing allow the immersed depth to be read. In metric practice, draft marks are typically 10 cm tall numerals spaced 20 cm apart; in imperial practice, numerals are 6 inches tall spaced 12 inches apart. Draft measurements may be corrected for trim (the difference between forward and aft draft), list, and water density.

Terms

  • Draft (forward) and draft (aft): measured at the perpendiculars at bow and stern.
  • Mean draft: the average of forward and aft drafts, corrected for the position of the midship section.
  • Trim: the difference between the aft and forward drafts.
  • Extreme draft: measured to the lowest point of the vessel, such as a sonar dome or propeller tip, where these extend below the keel.

Operational significance

Draft limits where a ship can operate: ports, canals and locks impose maximum drafts. The largest container ships and bulk carriers are classed by the waterways they can transit — for example, "Panamax" and "Malaccamax" limits are governed substantially by draft. Loading a vessel beyond its marks (set by its load line, or Plimsoll mark) is unsafe and illegal, because it leaves insufficient freeboard and reserve buoyancy.
