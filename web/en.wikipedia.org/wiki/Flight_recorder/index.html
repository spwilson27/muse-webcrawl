A flight recorder is an electronic recording device placed in an aircraft for the purpose of facilitating the investigation of aviation accidents and incidents. The device may be referred to colloquially as a "black box", although recorders are painted bright orange to aid recovery.

Components

The system comprises two units: the flight data recorder (FDR), which records parameters such as airspeed, altitude, heading and control inputs — modern units capture thousands of parameters for at least 25 hours — and the cockpit voice recorder (CVR), which records the last two hours of cockpit audio. Both are typically mounted in the tail, the most survivable part of the airframe.

History

Early devices used photographic film and metal foil. Australian scientist David Warren conceived the modern crash-survivable combined recorder in the 1950s after investigating Comet jetliner crashes. Recorders became mandatory on commercial aircraft during the 1960s.

Survivability and recovery

Recorders are built to withstand impact forces of 3,400 g and fires of 1,100 °C, and carry underwater locator beacons that transmit for at least 90 days.
