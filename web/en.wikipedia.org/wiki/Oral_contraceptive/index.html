Oral contraceptive

Oral contraceptives, commonly known as birth control pills or simply "the Pill", are medications taken by mouth for the purpose of preventing pregnancy. There are two main types: the combined oral contraceptive pill, containing an estrogen and a progestin, and the progestogen-only pill (the "minipill"). They are among the most widely used methods of contraception worldwide and appear on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Mechanism of action

Combined pills act principally by suppressing gonadotropin release, thereby preventing ovulation; they also thicken cervical mucus and alter the endometrium. Progestogen-only pills act mainly on cervical mucus, and newer formulations also reliably inhibit ovulation. With perfect use, combined pills are over 99% effective over a year of use; typical-use effectiveness is lower, around 91–93%, chiefly because of missed doses.

History

The Pill grew out of research by Gregory Pincus and John Rock, funding by Katharine McCormick, and activism by Margaret Sanger. The first pill, Enovid (norethynodrel with mestranol), developed by the chemist Carl Djerassi's contemporaries and the pharmaceutical company Searle, was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for contraceptive use in 1960. Its introduction had profound social effects, contributing to the sexual revolution and to major changes in women's education, employment and family patterns.

Health effects

Benefits beyond contraception include regulation of menstrual cycles, reduced menstrual pain and blood loss, and reduced risks of ovarian and endometrial cancers. Risks include a small increased risk of venous thromboembolism, especially with certain progestins and in smokers over 35, and contraindications such as migraine with aura and uncontrolled hypertension. Modern pills contain far lower hormone doses than the original formulations.
