Aneuploidy

Aneuploidy is the presence of an abnormal number of chromosomes in a cell, for example 45 or 47 human chromosomes instead of the usual 46. It differs from polyploidy, in which entire extra sets of chromosomes are present. Aneuploidy is caused mainly by nondisjunction — the failure of homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids to separate properly during meiosis or mitosis — and it is a leading known cause of miscarriage and of congenital conditions.

Trisomy 21, the cause of Down syndrome, is the best-known autosomal aneuploidy compatible with survival.

Types

A missing chromosome is a monosomy (2n−1); an extra chromosome is a trisomy (2n+1); two extra chromosomes may constitute a tetrasomy or double trisomy. In humans, autosomal monosomies are almost always lethal early in development, and only three autosomal trisomies — of chromosomes 21 (Down syndrome), 18 (Edwards syndrome), and 13 (Patau syndrome) — commonly survive to birth. Sex-chromosome aneuploidies such as Turner syndrome (45,X), Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), and trisomy X are generally milder.

Causes and risk

Meiotic nondisjunction risk increases with maternal age, particularly for Down syndrome. Errors in mitosis after fertilisation produce mosaic aneuploidy, in which only some cells are affected. Aneuploidy is also a hallmark of many cancers, where chromosome instability generates abnormal karyotypes that can drive tumour evolution.

Detection

Aneuploidy is detected by karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH), chromosomal microarray, and prenatal screening methods including cell-free DNA testing (NIPT), with diagnostic confirmation by amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling.
