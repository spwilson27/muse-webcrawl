Janus kinase (JAK) is a family of intracellular, non-receptor tyrosine kinases that transduce cytokine-mediated signals via the JAK-STAT pathway. The name derives from Janus, the two-faced Roman god of beginnings, because each JAK protein possesses two near-identical phosphate-transferring domains: one exhibits kinase activity, while the other (a pseudokinase domain) negatively regulates it.

Family

Mammals have four JAK family members: JAK1, JAK2, JAK3 and TYK2. JAK1, JAK2 and TYK2 are ubiquitously expressed, whereas JAK3 is expressed primarily in haematopoietic cells. JAK proteins range from 120 to 140 kDa and contain seven Janus homology (JH) domains, JH1 through JH7, with JH1 being the active kinase domain and JH2 the regulatory pseudokinase domain.

Function

JAKs associate with the cytoplasmic tails of cytokine receptors (including receptors for interleukins, interferons, erythropoietin, thrombopoietin and many growth factors). When a cytokine binds, the receptor subunits rearrange, bringing associated JAKs together so they phosphorylate each other and the receptor. The phosphotyrosine sites then recruit STAT transcription factors, which are phosphorylated by JAKs, dimerise, and move to the nucleus to activate gene transcription.

Clinical significance

Loss-of-function mutations in JAK3 cause severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID). The gain-of-function JAK2 V617F mutation, discovered in 2005, is a driver of myeloproliferative neoplasms such as polycythaemia vera, essential thrombocythaemia and primary myelofibrosis. Small-molecule JAK inhibitors ("jakinibs") — including tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib and the JAK2 inhibitor ruxolitinib — are approved for rheumatoid arthritis, ulcerative colitis, atopic dermatitis, alopecia areata and myelofibrosis, among other indications.

History

The JAK genes were identified in 1989 through PCR-based searches for new tyrosine kinases; they were initially dubbed "just another kinase" before the more elegant Janus backronym was adopted. Their central role in cytokine signalling was established in the early 1990s through studies of interferon signalling.
