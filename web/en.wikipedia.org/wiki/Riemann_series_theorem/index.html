Riemann series theorem

The Riemann series theorem (also called the Riemann rearrangement theorem), named after 19th-century German mathematician Bernhard Riemann, says that if an infinite series of real numbers is conditionally convergent, then its terms can be arranged in a permutation so that the new series converges to an arbitrary given value, or diverges.

Statement

A series converges conditionally if it converges but the series of absolute values diverges. For such a series, the theorem states that a rearrangement of its terms can be made to sum to any real number, or to diverge to positive or negative infinity, or to oscillate. This stands in sharp contrast to absolutely convergent series, whose sum is unchanged by any rearrangement.

Proof idea

The proof separates the positive and negative terms of the series. Because the series is conditionally convergent, the sums of the positive terms and of the negative terms each diverge (to +∞ and −∞ respectively), while the individual terms tend to zero. One then takes terms from the positive subsequence until the partial sum first exceeds the target value, then terms from the negative subsequence until it first falls below the target, and so on. Since the terms shrink to zero, the partial sums converge to the chosen target.

History and significance

The result was published by Riemann in his 1854 Habilitationsschrift on trigonometric series, which appeared posthumously in 1867. The theorem is a striking illustration of how the behaviour of infinite processes can differ from finite intuition: addition of infinitely many numbers is not commutative in the same way as finite addition. A related result, the Lévy–Steinitz theorem, generalises the phenomenon to series in finite-dimensional vector spaces.
