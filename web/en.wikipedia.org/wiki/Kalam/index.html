Kalām

Kalām (Arabic: علم الكلام, ʿilm al-kalām, "science of discourse") is the Islamic tradition of rational theology: the disciplined use of reasoning and dialectic to articulate and defend the doctrines of Islam, including the existence and attributes of God, free will and predestination, and the nature of the Qur'an.

History

Kalām arose in the eighth century amid debates with other faiths and within Islam itself. The early Muʿtazilite school, centred in Basra and Baghdad, championed human free will, divine justice and the createdness of the Qur'an. In reaction, the Ashʿarite school of Abū al-Ḥasan al-Ashʿarī (873–935) developed a middle position using rational argument to defend more traditional doctrines, and Ashʿarism became the dominant theological school of Sunni Islam, alongside the Māturīdite school of Transoxiana.

Methods and topics

Practitioners, the mutakallimūn, built arguments from atomism, theories of accidents and proofs of the world's temporal origination (such as the "kalām cosmological argument", later influential in Christian and modern philosophy of religion). Thinkers such as al-Ghazālī both criticised the philosophers and absorbed kalām methods into Sunni orthodoxy.
