Asia Minor, also known as Anatolia, is the large peninsula in Western Asia that forms the majority of the modern Republic of Turkey. Bounded by the Black Sea to the north, the Aegean Sea to the west, and the Mediterranean Sea to the south, it has been a crossroads of civilizations linking Europe and Asia for millennia.

Name

"Asia Minor" (Latin: Asia Minor, "lesser Asia") distinguished the peninsula from the greater continent of Asia, a usage that arose after "Asia" — originally the name of the Roman province in the peninsula's west — was extended eastward. "Anatolia" comes from the Greek Anatolē, meaning "sunrise" or "east," referring to its position east of Greece; it was also the name of a Byzantine theme and later an Ottoman eyalet.

Geography

The peninsula consists of a high central plateau (averaging about 1,000 m) ringed by mountain ranges — the Pontic Mountains along the Black Sea and the Taurus Mountains along the Mediterranean — with fertile coastal plains, especially in the west, where rivers such as the Hermus and Maeander cut broad valleys to the Aegean. The climate ranges from Mediterranean on the coasts to continental steppe in the interior.

History

Anatolia was home to some of the earliest Neolithic settlements, such as Çatalhöyük, and to the Hittite Empire of the Bronze Age. Classical antiquity saw Greek cities on the Aegean coast (Ionia), the kingdoms of Lydia, Phrygia, and Urartu, and then Persian, Hellenistic, and Roman rule. It was a core territory of the Byzantine Empire until the Seljuk Turkish victory at Manzikert in 1071 opened it to Turkic settlement. The Ottoman Empire arose in northwestern Anatolia in the late thirteenth century. After the First World War, the Turkish War of Independence (1919–1923) secured the peninsula as the heartland of the Republic of Turkey, whose capital, Ankara, lies on the central plateau; the 1923 population exchange between Greece and Turkey reshaped its demography.
