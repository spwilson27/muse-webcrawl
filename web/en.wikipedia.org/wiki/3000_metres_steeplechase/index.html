The 3000 metres steeplechase is the most common distance for the steeplechase in track and field. It is an obstacle race over 3000 metres in which athletes must clear 28 fixed barriers and 7 water jumps. The event has been part of the men's Olympic programme since 1920 and was added for women at the 2008 Beijing Games.

Format

Over the standard distance runners complete seven and a half laps of a 400-metre track. Each full lap after the first partial lap contains four barriers and one water jump. The barriers are 914 mm high for men and 762 mm for women and, unlike sprint hurdles, do not fall over when struck. The water jump consists of a barrier followed by a pit 3.66 m square filled with water up to 70 cm deep at the barrier, sloping up to track level at the far end; jumping further out means landing in shallower water and losing less momentum. Depending on the placement of the water jump inside or outside the bend, lap length is adjusted so the total distance remains 3000 metres.

History

The steeplechase derives from cross-country races run across the Irish and English countryside over natural obstacles — stone walls, hedges, and streams — and its name is borrowed from horse racing. It was contested over varying distances in the early Olympics before standardising at 3000 metres in 1920. Kenyan runners have dominated the men's event in recent decades, winning every Olympic title from 1984 to 2016, while the Moroccan Soufiane El Bakkali ended that streak at Tokyo 2020 and Paris 2024.

Technique

Elite steeplechasers combine distance-running endurance with hurdling efficiency. Most athletes hurdle the dry barriers without touching them but step on the water-jump barrier to propel themselves as far as possible across the pit. Because a poor barrier clearance costs more time than in sprint hurdles, athletes practise "barrier efficiency" extensively, and fatigue in the final laps frequently causes falls at the water jump.
