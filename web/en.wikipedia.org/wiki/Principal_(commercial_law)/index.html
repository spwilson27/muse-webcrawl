Principal (commercial law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In commercial law, a principal is a party that has authorized another party — an agent — to act on its behalf in dealings with third parties, such that the acts of the agent, within the scope of the authority granted, are legally the acts of the principal. The body of rules governing this three-party relationship is called the law of agency.

Types of principal

A principal may be disclosed, where the third party knows the agent is acting for a principal and knows that principal's identity; partially disclosed (or unidentified), where the third party knows a principal exists but not who it is; or undisclosed, where the third party believes the agent is acting on the agent's own behalf. The classification matters because it determines who may sue and be sued on the contract: an undisclosed principal may generally sue and be sued once discovered, subject to exceptions, while the agent of an undisclosed principal is also personally liable.

Rights and duties

The principal is bound by contracts made by the agent acting within actual authority — express or implied — and, in many common-law jurisdictions, by acts within the agent's apparent (ostensible) authority where the principal's manifestations induced the third party reasonably to believe the agent was authorised. The principal owes the agent duties including payment of agreed remuneration, reimbursement of expenses properly incurred, and indemnification against liabilities incurred within authority. The agent, in turn, owes the principal fiduciary duties of loyalty, obedience, care, disclosure and accounting.

Related uses

In related commercial contexts "principal" also denotes the main party to a transaction acting on its own account — for example, a dealer trading as principal rather than as broker — and, in the law of crime, the main perpetrator of an offence as distinguished from an accessory.
