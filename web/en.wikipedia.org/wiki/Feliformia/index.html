Feliformia is a suborder within the order Carnivora consisting of "cat-like" carnivorans, including cats (large and small), hyenas, mongooses, civets, and related taxa. The other suborder of Carnivora is Caniformia, the "dog-like" carnivorans.

Characteristics

Feliforms tend to have shorter rostra (snouts) and fewer teeth than caniforms, and many possess retractile or semi-retractile claws. Their auditory bullae are double-chambered, formed from two bones joined by a septum. Feliforms are generally more specialized as ambush predators and obligate meat-eaters, though some lineages (such as many viverrids and the aardwolf) are omnivorous or insectivorous.

Classification

Extant families of Feliformia include:

  • Felidae — true cats: lions, tigers, leopards, cheetahs, domestic cats and others
  • Hyaenidae — hyenas and the aardwolf
  • Herpestidae — mongooses
  • Eupleridae — Madagascan carnivorans such as the fossa
  • Viverridae — civets and genets
  • Prionodontidae — the Asiatic linsangs
  • Nandiniidae — the African palm civet

Extinct groups include the Nimravidae ("false sabre-toothed cats"), the Barbourofelidae, and the Percrocutidae.

Evolution

Feliforms and caniforms diverged from a common carnivoran ancestor roughly 40–50 million years ago in the Eocene. Early feliforms were arboreal, civet-like animals similar to the fossil Tapocyon and the nimravids. Feliforms historically ranged across the Americas, Africa, Europe, and Asia, though nimravids and barbourofelids died out by the late Miocene–Pliocene.
