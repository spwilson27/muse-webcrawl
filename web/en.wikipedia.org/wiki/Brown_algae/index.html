Brown algae (class Phaeophyceae) are a large group of multicellular algae, comprising about 300 genera and roughly 1,500–2,000 species, found predominantly in cold marine waters. They include many familiar seaweeds, such as kelps, rockweeds and the free-floating Sargassum. Their characteristic brown or olive colour comes from the pigment fucoxanthin, which masks the green of chlorophyll.

Structure and life cycle

Brown algae range from small filamentous forms like Ectocarpus to giant kelps such as Macrocystis pyrifera, which can grow more than 45 metres long and form underwater forests. Many have differentiated tissues: a holdfast anchoring them to rock, a stipe (stem-like structure) and blades (leaf-like photosynthetic organs), sometimes with gas-filled floats called pneumatocysts. Their life cycles typically involve alternation of generations between a diploid sporophyte and a haploid gametophyte.

Ecology and uses

Kelp forests are among the most productive marine ecosystems, sheltering fish, sea otters and invertebrates. Brown algae are harvested for alginates — thickening agents used in food, textiles and pharmaceuticals — and are eaten directly in many cuisines, notably the Japanese kombu (Saccharina japonica). Historically, kelp ash was a major source of soda and iodine.

Classification

Brown algae belong to the stramenopiles (heterokonts), a lineage whose plastids derive from a secondary endosymbiosis with a red alga. They are only distantly related to land plants, having evolved multicellularity independently.
