Cable landing point

A cable landing point is the location where a submarine or other underwater cable makes landfall. The term is most often used for the points where submarine telecommunications cables and submarine power cables come ashore. The landing will often be at a cable landing station, a building that houses the equipment for terminating the cable and connecting it to terrestrial networks, though in some cases the cable may be buried and connected onward to a station further inland.

Selection criteria

Landing points are chosen for gentle, sandy seabed approaches free of shipping lanes, fishing grounds, and anchorages; stable geology; proximity to population centres and existing network infrastructure; and available power and security. Ideally the cable route avoids rocky outcrops and steep underwater slopes that could abrade or suspend the cable. Beach manholes and buried ducts carry the cable from the surf zone to the landing station, minimizing exposure to storms and human activity.

Infrastructure

At the landing station, optical signals from the submarine cable are received, regenerated or amplified as needed, and handed off to domestic backbone networks. The station provides power feed equipment that supplies the constant current used by undersea repeaters, along with network management systems and backup power. Major landing sites may terminate many cables; well-known clusters include those near Bude in Cornwall (UK), Tuckerton and Wall Township in New Jersey (US), and Chiba and Okinawa in Japan.

Security

Because a large share of intercontinental internet traffic passes through a small number of landing points, they are considered critical infrastructure. Concerns include accidental damage by anchors and trawlers — the most common cause of cable faults — as well as espionage and sabotage. States typically restrict access to landing stations and may monitor the facilities.
