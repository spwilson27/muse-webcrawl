Ran Min (Chinese: 冉閔; died 352 CE), also known as Shi Min (石閔), formally Prince Daowu of (Ran) Wei, was a Chinese military leader and ruler during the Sixteen Kingdoms period. Of Han Chinese descent, he was adopted into the ruling Shi family of the Jie-led Later Zhao dynasty. In 350 he seized power, massacred the Shi clan, and founded the short-lived state of Ran Wei, with its capital at Ye (modern Handan, Hebei).

Rise to power

Ran Min was famed as a fierce cavalry commander under the Later Zhao emperor Shi Hu, who had adopted his father into the imperial family and raised him as a grandson of the dynasty's founder. After Shi Hu's death in 349, a succession struggle among the Shi princes gave Ran Min the opportunity to take control of the army and the capital.

The anti-Jie edicts and downfall

Ran Min is best known — and remains highly controversial — for a series of edicts ordering the killing of the Jie and other non-Han peoples in the capital region, in which tens of thousands reportedly died, partly in retaliation for Jie oppression of the Han population and partly amid civil war. His campaigns against the Xianbei state of Former Yan failed, and in 352 he was defeated and captured at Liantai by the Former Yan prince Murong Ke. He was executed after, according to tradition, he defiantly told the Yan ruler that his seizure of power was a response to Jie tyranny. Ran Wei collapsed the same year.

Legacy

Chinese historiography has judged Ran Min in sharply opposed ways: condemned in many traditional accounts as a brutal usurper whose massacres plunged the north into chaos, and praised in some modern popular writing as a Han national hero. Historians note that the scale and framing of the events are difficult to separate from later ethnically charged retellings.
