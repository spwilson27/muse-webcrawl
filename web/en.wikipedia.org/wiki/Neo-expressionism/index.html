Neo-expressionism

Neo-expressionism is a style of late modernist or early-postmodern painting and sculpture that emerged in the late 1970s. Its adherents in Germany were sometimes called the Neue Wilden ("new wild ones"). Neo-expressionism was a reaction against the cool, impersonal abstractions of conceptual art and minimalism, and marked a return to portraying recognisable objects, such as the human body, in a rough and violently emotive manner, using vivid colours and clashing harmonies.

Characteristics

Neo-expressionist works are typically large in scale, executed rapidly with thick impasto, distorted figures and mythic or historical subject matter. The style revived the gestural intensity of German Expressionism and drew on primitivism, graffiti and art brut.

Artists and centres

In Germany the movement centred on Georg Baselitz, Anselm Kiefer, Jörg Immendorff, A. R. Penck and Markus Lüpertz, whose paintings confronted German history and identity. In Italy the Transavanguardia of Sandro Chia, Francesco Clemente and Enzo Cucchi pursued similar goals, while in the United States Julian Schnabel, David Salle and Jean-Michel Basquiat were associated with the return to expressive figuration.

Reception

The international exhibition Zeitgeist (Berlin, 1982) consolidated the movement, which dominated the art market of the early 1980s. Critics were divided: supporters saw a restoration of painting's expressive power, while detractors condemned the works as macho, reactionary or commercially calculated. Interest waned later in the decade, though the movement's leading figures retained major reputations.
