Phở (pronounced roughly "fuh") is a Vietnamese soup dish consisting of broth, rice noodles (bánh phở), herbs, and meat (usually beef, sometimes chicken). Phở is a popular food in Vietnam, where it is served in households, street stalls, and restaurant chains nationwide, and it is considered one of Vietnam's national dishes.

History

Phở originated in the early 20th century in northern Vietnam, in Hanoi and Nam Định, during the French colonial period. It is believed to reflect a mix of Vietnamese, Chinese, and French influences: rice noodles and spices recall Chinese cookery, while the use of beef — little eaten in Vietnam before colonisation — and the clear consommé-style broth suggest French influence. After the partition of Vietnam in 1954, phở spread south with migrating northerners, and after 1975 it was carried worldwide by the Vietnamese diaspora.

Preparation

The broth is the heart of the dish: beef (or chicken) bones are simmered for hours with charred ginger and onion and spices such as star anise, cloves, cinnamon, cardamom, and fish sauce. The soup is ladled over flat rice noodles and thinly sliced meat — rare beef cooks in the hot broth in the bowl — and served with fresh herbs, bean sprouts, lime, chillies, and sauces such as hoisin and sriracha, which are especially characteristic of the southern style.

Variations

The two classic varieties are phở bò (beef) and phở gà (chicken). Regional styles differ: Hanoi phở tends toward a clear, delicate broth with fewer garnishes, while southern (Saigon) phở is sweeter and served with a larger plate of herbs and bean sprouts.
