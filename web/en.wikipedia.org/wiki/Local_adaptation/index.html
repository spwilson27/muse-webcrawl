Local adaptation

Local adaptation is a mechanism in evolutionary biology in which a population of organisms evolves to be more well-suited to its local environment than other members of the same species that live elsewhere. It is the result of divergent natural selection across habitats acting on heritable variation, and is opposed by gene flow, genetic drift, and the absence of variation. Local adaptation is a key process in ecological speciation and in the maintenance of genetic diversity within species.

Divergent environments drive local adaptation

Evidence

The classic test is the reciprocal transplant experiment, in which individuals from different populations are raised in each other's environments (and their own) in common-garden and field settings; a population is locally adapted if it has higher fitness at home than foreign populations do there ("local vs. foreign" criterion), or higher fitness at home than away ("home vs. away" criterion). Meta-analyses of hundreds of such experiments, especially in plants, find local adaptation to be common but not universal, and stronger in large populations and across strong environmental gradients. Well-known examples include heavy-metal tolerance in grasses near mines, edaphic races on serpentine soils, host races of insects, Darwin's finches' beak variation among islands, and freshwater adaptation in sticklebacks.

Conditions and constraints

Local adaptation requires selection strong enough to overcome gene flow from other populations; where migration is high, populations may instead evolve phenotypic plasticity, or adaptation may be confined to a few strongly selected loci. Temporal variation in conditions, small population size, and mutation limitation also constrain it. With environmental change — especially climate change — previously adaptive trait combinations can become maladaptive, a concern motivating "assisted gene flow" proposals in forestry and conservation.

Genomic approaches

Genome scans for outlier loci between populations, environmental association analyses, and QST–FST comparisons are used to detect the genetic signatures of local adaptation, though distinguishing selection from demographic history remains methodologically difficult.
