Chromosomal crossover

Chromosomal crossover, or crossing over, is the exchange of genetic material between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes during meiosis. It occurs in prophase I, when paired homologues (bivalents) synapse and exchange corresponding segments, producing recombinant chromosomes that combine alleles from both parents. The visible points of exchange are called chiasmata.

Mechanism

Crossing over is initiated by programmed double-strand breaks made by the enzyme Spo11. The broken ends are resected and invade the homologous chromosome, forming displacement loops and Holliday junctions. Resolution or dissolution of these intermediates yields either crossover products — reciprocal exchanges of flanking markers — or non-crossover gene conversions. Proteins such as MLH1 and MLH3 mark the sites destined to become crossovers, and most organisms regulate their number and spacing, a phenomenon known as crossover interference.

Consequences

Crossover shuffles alleles into new combinations, increasing genetic diversity in offspring and allowing natural selection to act on combinations of traits. Crossovers also play a mechanical role: together with sister-chromatid cohesion they hold homologues together until anaphase I, ensuring accurate segregation. Failures in crossover formation can cause nondisjunction and aneuploid gametes.

Discovery and mapping

The phenomenon was described cytologically by Frans Alfons Janssens in 1909 and linked to genetic recombination by Thomas Hunt Morgan and his students; Harriet Creighton and Barbara McClintock demonstrated in 1931 that genetic crossing over corresponds to physical exchange of chromosome segments. Recombination frequencies between genes formed the basis of the first genetic maps by Alfred Sturtevant.

Mitotic and unequal crossover

Rare crossovers in mitosis can produce mosaic tissues and loss of heterozygosity. Misalignment of repetitive sequences can cause unequal crossing over, generating duplications and deletions, as in the red–green colour vision gene arrays and the globin gene family.
