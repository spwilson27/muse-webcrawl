Deconstruction (building)

Deconstruction (building)
DefinitionSelective dismantlement of building components for reuse, recycling and waste management
Contrasted withDemolition
Recovered materialsTimber, brick, fixtures, metals, architectural salvage

In the context of physical construction, deconstruction is the selective dismantlement of building components, specifically for reuse, repurposing, recycling, and waste management. It differs from demolition, in which a site is cleared of its building by the most expedient means. Deconstruction has also been called "construction in reverse", and in its most complete form nearly all materials from a building are salvaged rather than sent to landfill.

Process

A deconstruction project typically begins with a salvage survey identifying valuable or hazardous materials. Workers then remove fixtures, fittings, and finishes, followed by non-structural elements and finally the structure itself, often in the reverse order of construction. Soft stripping of interiors precedes careful hand or mechanical dismantling of framing, roofing, and masonry.

Benefits and challenges

  • Diverts waste from landfill and reduces demand for virgin materials and the carbon emissions of new manufacture.
  • Recovers old-growth timber, handmade brick, and period fixtures that command premium prices as architectural salvage.
  • Creates more jobs per unit of material handled than mechanical demolition.
  • Challenges include higher labour costs, longer schedules, hazardous materials such as asbestos and lead paint, and building designs that were never intended to come apart.

Design for deconstruction

Advocates promote "design for disassembly" in new buildings: mechanical rather than chemical connections, standardised components, and material passports documenting what a building contains, so that its parts can be recovered at end of life.
