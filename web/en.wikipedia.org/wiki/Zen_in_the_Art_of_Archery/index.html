Zen in the Art of Archery (German: Zen in der Kunst des Bogenschießens) is a book by German philosophy professor Eugen Herrigel, published in 1948, describing his experiences studying kyūdō, Japanese archery, under master Awa Kenzō in the 1920s. It was one of the first books to introduce Zen Buddhism to Western audiences after the Second World War and became a classic of spiritual literature.

Content

Herrigel recounts his six years of training: learning to draw the great Japanese longbow (yumi) with correct breathing, suppressing conscious effort until the shot "falls from the archer like snow from a bamboo leaf". The central lesson is that the release should happen without intention — the master's famous dictum that "It shoots", not "you shoot". The book presents archery as a contemplative discipline in which technique is a vehicle for self-transcendence.

Influence and criticism

The book inspired the title of Robert Pirsig's Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance (1974) and influenced generations of Western practitioners of Japanese arts. Later scholarship, notably by Yamada Shōji, has questioned the fidelity of Herrigel's account: Awa Kenzō was not a Zen master, his idiosyncratic teaching drew on other sources, and translation through an interpreter may have Zen-inflected the lessons retrospectively. The book remains influential despite these debates.
