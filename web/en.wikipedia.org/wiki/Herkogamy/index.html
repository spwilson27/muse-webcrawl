Herkogamy
Field: Plant reproductive biology
Definition: Spatial separation of anthers and stigma within a flower
Related: Dichogamy; heterostyly; self-incompatibility

Herkogamy

Herkogamy (or hercogamy) is a common strategy employed by hermaphroditic angiosperms to reduce sexual interference between male (anthers) and female (stigma) function in a single flower and to promote outcrossing. It is the spatial separation of the anthers and stigma within a flower, so that self-pollen is less likely to reach the flower's own stigma.

Forms

In approach herkogamy the stigma is positioned above or beyond the anthers, so a visiting pollinator contacts the stigma first; in reverse herkogamy the anthers are uppermost, favouring pollen export. Separation may be fixed or involve movements of the style or stamens during flowering. Herkogamy frequently occurs together with dichogamy (separation of male and female function in time) and with physiological self-incompatibility.

Significance

By reducing self-pollination and the resulting inbreeding depression, herkogamy is one of several floral mechanisms thought to have contributed to the evolutionary success of the flowering plants. It is widespread in families such as the Iridaceae, Fabaceae, and Lamiaceae.
