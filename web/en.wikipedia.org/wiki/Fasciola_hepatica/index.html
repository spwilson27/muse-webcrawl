Fasciola hepatica

Kingdom: Animalia
Phylum: Platyhelminthes
Class: Trematoda
Order: Plagiorchiida
Family: Fasciolidae
Genus: Fasciola
Species: F. hepatica

Fasciola hepatica, also known as the common liver fluke or sheep liver fluke, is a parasitic trematode (flatworm) that infects the livers of various mammals, including humans, in which it causes the disease fasciolosis. It is distributed worldwide wherever sheep and cattle are raised and suitable freshwater snail hosts are present.

Morphology

The adult fluke is a flat, leaf-shaped worm about 30 mm long and 13 mm wide, grey-brown in colour, with a characteristic cone-shaped head end bearing an oral sucker; a larger ventral sucker (acetabulum) anchors it in the bile ducts. Like other trematodes it is hermaphroditic, and a single adult can produce up to about 20,000 eggs per day, which pass out with the host's bile into the intestine and are shed in the faeces.

Life cycle

Eggs hatch in water into miracidia, which must penetrate an amphibious freshwater snail of the family Lymnaeidae (such as Galba truncatula) within hours. Inside the snail the parasite multiplies through sporocyst and redia stages, emerging as free-swimming cercariae that encyst as metacercariae on aquatic vegetation such as watercress. The definitive host becomes infected by eating the vegetation. The young flukes excyst in the duodenum, burrow through the gut wall and across the liver parenchyma — a migratory phase of six to eight weeks that can cause significant tissue damage — before settling in the bile ducts to mature.

Disease and treatment

Fasciolosis causes substantial economic losses in sheep and cattle through liver condemnation, reduced milk and wool yields, and death in heavy infections ("liver rot"). In humans, the migratory phase may cause fever, right upper-quadrant pain, and eosinophilia, while the chronic phase can obstruct the bile ducts. The drug of choice is triclabendazole, which is effective against both immature and adult flukes; resistance to it has been reported in livestock in several countries. Prevention rests on snail-habitat drainage, fencing animals from wet pastures, and avoiding raw watercress from endemic areas.
