Longitudinal stability is the stability of an aircraft in the longitudinal, or pitching, plane. It describes the tendency of an aircraft to return to a trimmed angle of attack after a disturbance in pitch, such as a gust or a control input. It is one of the three principal components of aircraft stability, alongside lateral stability (rolling) and directional stability (yawing).

Static longitudinal stability

An aircraft is said to have positive static longitudinal stability if, following a disturbance that increases its angle of attack, aerodynamic forces generate a nose-down pitching moment that tends to restore the original attitude — and vice versa for a decrease. This restoring tendency arises principally from the horizontal tailplane (stabilizer), which sits well behind the centre of gravity and acts like the feathers of an arrow: a change in angle of attack changes the tail's lift, producing a moment about the centre of gravity.

The strength of static stability is quantified by the static margin, the distance between the aircraft's neutral point (the aerodynamic centre of the whole aircraft) and its centre of gravity, usually expressed as a percentage of the mean aerodynamic chord. A centre of gravity ahead of the neutral point gives positive static stability; the larger the margin, the more stable — but also the less manoeuvrable — the aircraft.

Dynamic longitudinal stability

Static stability alone does not guarantee satisfactory behaviour: an aircraft may return toward trim yet oscillate with growing amplitude. Dynamic stability concerns the motion over time. The two characteristic longitudinal oscillation modes are the short-period mode, a heavily damped, rapid pitching oscillation, and the phugoid, a long-period, lightly damped exchange between airspeed and altitude at nearly constant angle of attack. Good design requires both modes to be damped, or at least acceptably stable.

Design factors

  • Centre of gravity position — the dominant factor; forward CG increases stability, aft CG reduces it.
  • Tail volume — the size of the horizontal tail and its distance from the CG (the tail moment arm).
  • Wing position and downwash — the wing's wake reduces the effective angle of attack at the tail.
  • Fuselage and nacelles — generally destabilising, since their lift acts ahead of the CG.
  • Elevator and trim systems — stick-free stability also depends on elevator hinge moments and whether the elevator floats.

Relaxed static stability — a deliberately unstable or near-neutral airframe stabilised by a fly-by-wire flight control system — is used in modern fighter aircraft to reduce trim drag and improve manoeuvrability.
