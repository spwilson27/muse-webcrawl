High cross


Muiredach's Cross, Monasterboice

A high cross or standing cross (Irish: cros ard) is a free-standing Christian cross made of stone, often richly decorated, found in Ireland, Britain, and other regions evangelized from Ireland between the 7th and 12th centuries. The most distinctive feature of many is a ring surrounding the intersection of the arms and stem, producing the form now commonly called the Celtic cross.

History and decoration

The earliest high crosses developed from carved standing stones and cross-slabs. The great Scripture crosses of the 9th–10th centuries, such as Muiredach's Cross at Monasterboice, the Cross of the Scriptures at Clonmacnoise, and the crosses at Castledermot, are covered with panels depicting biblical scenes, intended to teach stories to a largely illiterate audience. Earlier crosses often feature interlace, spiral, and key patterns, and bosses or figural carving concentrated on the Crucifixion.

Distribution

In Ireland the crosses stood at monasteries and served as preaching stations, boundary markers, and objects of devotion. In Anglo-Saxon England the tradition produced monuments such as the Ruthwell Cross and Bewcastle Cross in the north, with runic inscriptions and vine-scroll ornament. Related carved crosses occur in Wales, Scotland (including Pictish cross-slabs), Cornwall, and the Isle of Man. The form was revived in the 19th century during the Celtic Revival and is now used extensively for grave markers.
