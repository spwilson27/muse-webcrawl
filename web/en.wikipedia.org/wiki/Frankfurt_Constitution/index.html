Frankfurt Constitution

Adopted28 March 1849
DrafterFrankfurt Parliament (National Assembly)
VenueSt Paul's Church (Paulskirche), Frankfurt am Main
StatusNever implemented

The Frankfurt Constitution (German: Frankfurter Verfassung), also known as the Paulskirche Constitution, was the constitution drafted by the democratically elected Frankfurt Parliament during the German revolutions of 1848–49. Adopted on 28 March 1849, it provided for a unified German federal nation-state under a hereditary emperor, a two-chamber parliament (the Reichstag, with a Volkshaus elected by universal male suffrage), and an extensive catalogue of fundamental rights for all Germans — one of the earliest modern German rights charters.

Fate

The Assembly offered the imperial crown to Frederick William IV of Prussia, who refused it in April 1849, reportedly unwilling to accept a crown "from the gutter". Although 28 German states accepted the constitution, the great powers Austria and Prussia rejected it, the so-called Imperial Constitution Campaign of uprisings in its defence was crushed, and the rump parliament was dispersed at Stuttgart. The German Confederation was restored.

Despite its failure, the Frankfurt Constitution became a landmark of German liberal and democratic tradition; its fundamental-rights section strongly influenced later German constitutions, including the Weimar Constitution of 1919 and the Basic Law of 1949.
