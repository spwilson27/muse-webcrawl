Veche

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Novgorod veche, by Apollinary Vasnetsov

A veche (Russian: вече) was a popular assembly in medieval Slavic countries, and in several towns of Kievan Rus' and the later Rus' republics it became the supreme governing body. In the Novgorod Republic (1136–1478) and the Pskov Republic (1348–1510), the veche stood above princes and councils: it elected the mayor (posadnik), the military commander (tysyatsky) and even the archbishop, invited and expelled princes, and decided questions of war, peace, law and taxation.

Operation

The Novgorod veche was summoned by ringing the veche bell — whose removal by Ivan III in 1478 became the symbol of the republic's fall. In principle all free male householders of the city could attend, gathering in Yaroslav's Court across the river from the Kremlin; in practice the great boyar landowning families increasingly dominated proceedings, and by the later period a smaller Council of Lords prepared most decisions. Decisions were taken by acclamation — by which side shouted louder — and contested votes could degenerate into brawls on the bridge over the Volkhov, sometimes settled by pitched fights between districts.

History

Veche assemblies are attested across the Rus' lands from the 10th and 11th centuries, reflecting older Slavic tribal assemblies. In most principalities they withered as princely power grew, but in the northwest, where princes were weak and the merchant city strong, the veche became sovereign: in 1136 Novgorodians arrested and expelled Prince Vsevolod, an event often taken as the founding of the republic. Novgorod invited princes thereafter by contract (ryad), forbidding them to own land in Novgorod territory or dismiss officials without trial. Moscow absorbed Novgorod in 1478 and Pskov in 1510, extinguishing the veche tradition; the institution has since been romanticised in Russian historiography as an indigenous, democratic alternative to autocracy.
