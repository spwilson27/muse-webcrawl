Centre Pompidou

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Centre Pompidou's west facade: the transparent escalator tube snaking up the inside-out building, colour-coded pipes in red, blue, green and yellow]

The Centre Pompidou, formally the Centre national d'art et de culture Georges-Pompidou, is a complex building in the Beaubourg area of the 4th arrondissement of Paris, housing the Musée National d'Art Moderne — Europe's largest museum of modern art — a vast public library (the BPI), the IRCAM centre for music and acoustic research, and exhibition and performance spaces.

Designed by Renzo Piano, Richard Rogers and Gianfranco Franchini and opened on 31 January 1977, it is the monument of high-tech architecture: all structural and service elements — colour-coded pipes, ducts and the famous external escalator tube — are worn on the outside, freeing the floors within. President Georges Pompidou conceived it as a democratic machine for culture; Parisians nicknamed it 'Beaubourg' and, less kindly, the refinery.

The building and its argument

The competition design (681 entries) scandalised and then captivated Paris: blue for air, green for water, yellow for electricity, red for circulation — a building as diagram, half of its footprint left as the sloped piazza where street performers still gather. The radicalism held: the Pompidou proved a museum could be a popular urban living room, and its success launched both its architects toward the Pritzker stratosphere.

Renovation

After a quarter-century of wear from success, the Centre closed in stages from 2023 for a major renovation (the so-called metamorphosis programme), with full reopening projected for around 2030; the collection tours the world meanwhile, and the satellite centres (Metz since 2010, Málaga, Brussels' Kanal, Shanghai's West Bund Museum partnership) carry the flag.
