Republic of Genoa

The Republic of Genoa (Italian: Repubblica di Genova; Ligurian: Repúbrica de Zêna) was a medieval and early modern maritime republic in north-western Italy, centred on the city of Genoa and the coast of Liguria. Established around 1005, it became one of the great Italian maritime republics, rivalling Venice, and endured for roughly eight centuries until it was conquered by Napoleon in 1797 and replaced by the Ligurian Republic.

History

Genoa grew rich on Mediterranean trade during the Crusades, winning trading quarters in the Levant and the Black Sea. Genoese colonies and factories included Pera (Galata) opposite Constantinople, Caffa in the Crimea, and the islands of Chios and Lesbos. The republic fought a long series of wars with Venice over eastern trade, culminating in the War of Chioggia (1378–1381), which checked Genoese power. Genoa also held Corsica until ceding it to France in 1768, shortly before Napoleon's birth there.

Government and finance

From 1339 the republic was headed by a Doge, modelled on Venice's, though factional strife between noble families frequently destabilised the state and led to periods of foreign domination by France or Milan. Genoese bankers were among the most powerful financiers in Europe: the Bank of Saint George (founded 1407), one of the oldest chartered banks in the world, administered much of the republic's revenue and colonies. In the 16th century, under the statesman-admiral Andrea Doria, Genoa aligned with Habsburg Spain, and Genoese finance underpinned the Spanish monarchy; the explorer Christopher Columbus was a native of the city.

Decline and fall

The shift of trade to the Atlantic, Spanish bankruptcies and rivalry with France eroded Genoese wealth. In 1797 Napoleon reorganised Italy and the republic was abolished in favour of the French client Ligurian Republic. After the Congress of Vienna (1815), Genoa and Liguria were annexed to the Kingdom of Sardinia.
