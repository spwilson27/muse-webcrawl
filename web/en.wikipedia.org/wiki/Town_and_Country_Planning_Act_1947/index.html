The Town and Country Planning Act 1947 (10 & 11 Geo. 6. c. 51) was an Act of Parliament in the United Kingdom passed by the post-war Labour government of Clement Attlee. Together with the New Towns Act 1946, it was the foundation of modern town and country planning in Britain. The Act came into effect on 1 July 1948 — the so-called "appointed day" — and effectively nationalised the right to develop land.

Provisions

The Act required all development of land — building, engineering, mining or other operations, and material changes of use — to receive planning permission from the local planning authority, deciding in accordance with a development plan. County and county borough councils were made the planning authorities, and were required to survey their areas and prepare development plans showing land use designations for twenty years ahead.

Landowners were compensated for the loss of development value by a one-off £300 million global fund, administered by the Central Land Board. In return, a development charge of 100% of the betterment — the increase in land value attributable to the grant of planning permission — was payable to the Central Land Board when development took place. In this way the Act attempted to capture the whole of the development value of land for the state.

The Act also gave councils wider compulsory purchase powers, allowed them to acquire land for planning purposes at existing-use value, protected listed buildings and trees, and provided for the control of outdoor advertising. Agriculture and forestry were largely exempt from the definition of development.

Background

The Act built on wartime reports on reconstruction: the Barlow Report (1940) on the distribution of industry, the Scott Report (1942) on rural land use, and the Uthwatt Report (1942) on compensation and betterment, which recommended state capture of development values. The Town and Country Planning Act 1944 had already given powers to rebuild blitzed cities, and the 1947 Act followed as the comprehensive peacetime measure, introduced by the Minister of Town and Country Planning, Lewis Silkin.

Subsequent history

The development charge proved difficult to administer and discouraged land coming forward for development. The Conservative government abolished the charge and the Central Land Board by the Town and Country Planning Act 1953, and later legislation of 1959 restored market-value compensation for compulsory purchase. The system's core — development plans and development control by planning permission — nevertheless survived and remains the basis of planning law in England and Wales, consolidated most recently in the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. The Act is also credited with enabling the green belt policy and the protection of the countryside around British cities.
