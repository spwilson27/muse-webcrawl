Andragogy

Andragogy addresses how adults learn differently from children.

Andragogy refers to methods and principles used in adult education — the theory and practice of teaching adult learners. The word derives from the Greek andr- (man, adult) and agōgos (leading). It was first used by the German educator Alexander Kapp in 1833, and was popularised in the English-speaking world by Malcolm Knowles, who from the 1970s presented andragogy as a distinct model of learning opposed to pedagogy, the teacher-centred instruction of children.

Knowles's assumptions

Knowles proposed that adult learners are characterised by: (1) an increasingly self-directed self-concept; (2) a growing reservoir of experience that serves as a resource for learning; (3) readiness to learn oriented to the tasks of their social roles; (4) a problem-centred orientation, seeking knowledge for immediate application rather than postponed use; and (5) predominantly internal motivation. From these assumptions follow principles of design: adults should be involved in planning and evaluating their instruction, experience should provide the basis for learning activities, and content should have immediate relevance to their work or lives.

Criticism and use

Critics note that the child/adult contrast is overdrawn — children can be self-directed and adults sometimes need direct instruction — and that Knowles himself later reframed andragogy and pedagogy as ends of a continuum rather than a dichotomy. Nonetheless, andragogy remains influential in corporate training, continuing professional development, and adult and community education worldwide.
