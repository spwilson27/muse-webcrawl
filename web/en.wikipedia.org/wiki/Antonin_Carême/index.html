Marie-Antoine Carême (8 June 1784 – 12 January 1833), known as Antonin Carême, was a French chef who is regarded as the founder of French haute cuisine and the first celebrity chef. Celebrated as "the king of chefs, and the chef of kings", he cooked for Talleyrand, Napoleon, Tsar Alexander I, and the future George IV, and codified French cooking in influential books.

Early life

Abandoned by his impoverished parents in revolutionary Paris, Carême began work as a kitchen boy and at sixteen was apprenticed to the celebrated pâtissier Sylvain Bailly, where his talent for elaborate architectural pastry constructions — pièces montées copied from architectural engravings he studied at the national library — made his reputation.

Career

Working for the diplomat Talleyrand for over a decade, Carême cooked for the Congress of Vienna and for Europe's rulers, acquiring an international clientele that included the Prince Regent in Brighton and Tsar Alexander I in St Petersburg, and ended his career in the service of Baron James de Rothschild in Paris. He systematized the mother sauces, refined service and kitchen organization, popularized the tall chef's hat (the toque), and insisted that cooking was both a science and an art.

Writings and legacy

His works include Le Pâtissier royal parisien (1815), Le Maître d'hôtel français (1822), and the unfinished five-volume L'Art de la cuisine française au dix-neuvième siècle (1833–1835, completed by Plumerey). Exhausted by his work and possibly affected by years of charcoal fumes, he died at 48. Carême's codification of grande cuisine shaped professional cooking until it was reformed by Auguste Escoffier at the end of the century.
