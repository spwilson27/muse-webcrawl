The 1900 Galveston hurricane, also known as the Great Galveston hurricane and the Galveston Storm of 1900, is the deadliest natural disaster in United States history and the fifth-deadliest Atlantic hurricane overall. The hurricane struck the island city of Galveston, Texas, on 8 September 1900 with estimated winds of 145 mph (230 km/h), a Category 4 storm on the modern Saffir–Simpson scale. The storm surge inundated the entire island; between 6,000 and 12,000 people lost their lives, with the most commonly cited figure being 8,000.

Meteorological history

The storm was first detected as a tropical wave over the tropical Atlantic in late August 1900. It passed through the Leeward Islands and near Jamaica as a weak tropical storm, crossed western Cuba, and emerged into the Gulf of Mexico, where it strengthened rapidly over warm water. Ship reports and the observations of the United States Weather Bureau office in Galveston, headed by Isaac Cline, tracked the storm as it approached the Texas coast, but the technology and understanding of the time prevented an accurate forecast of its intensity or landfall point.

Impact

Galveston, then one of the largest cotton ports in the United States and Texas's premier commercial city, sat barely above sea level — its highest point was only about 8.7 feet (2.7 m) above the Gulf. The storm surge, estimated at 8 to 15 feet (2.4 to 4.6 m), swept completely over the island. More than 3,600 houses were destroyed and a wall of debris accumulated on the island's Gulf side. Bridges to the mainland were washed away, and telegraph lines were down, so news of the disaster reached the outside world only days later.

The dead were collected by work gangs and, after burial at sea proved impractical because bodies washed back ashore, cremated on immense funeral pyres. Martial law was declared to prevent looting.

Aftermath

In the storm's wake, Galveston undertook one of the most ambitious civil-engineering projects of the era: a 17-foot (5.2 m) seawall was built along the Gulf shore beginning in 1902, and the grade of the entire city behind it was raised by as much as 17 feet using sand dredged from the harbour — some 500 city blocks and more than 2,100 buildings were lifted. The city government was reorganised under a commission form of government that became a model adopted by hundreds of American cities. Nevertheless, Galveston's commercial primacy passed permanently to Houston, whose dredged ship channel opened in 1914.

The Galveston hurricane remains the benchmark against which American hurricane disasters are measured. Author Erik Larson popularised its story in the 1999 book Isaac's Storm.
