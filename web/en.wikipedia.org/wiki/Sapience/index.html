Sapience

Not to be confused with sentience.


Rodin's The Thinker, an emblem of human reflection

Sapience is the property of an organism or machine, most characteristically a human being, of possessing wisdom, sound judgement and the capacity for reflective, self-aware reasoning. The word derives from the Latin sapientia ("wisdom") and the verb sapere ("to taste; to be wise"). It gives the human species its scientific name, Homo sapiens — "wise man" — given by Carl Linnaeus in 1758.

Distinction from sentience

Sapience is conventionally distinguished from sentience. Sentience is the capacity to have subjective experiences and feelings — to perceive, feel pleasure or pain. Sapience, by contrast, involves higher cognition: self-awareness, abstract reasoning, metacognition (thinking about one's own thinking), language, moral judgement and long-range planning. An entity may be sentient without being sapient, as most non-human animals are generally held to be; whether a machine could be either remains contested.

Usage

The term is used in philosophy, cognitive science, animal ethics and in discussions of artificial intelligence and personhood. In science fiction, "sapience" is often the criterion for recognising an alien species or artificial mind as a person with moral and legal standing. Some ethicists argue that sapience, rather than species membership, is the appropriate basis for certain rights, while others ground moral status in sentience alone, since the capacity to suffer does not depend on intelligence.

The related adjective sapient simply means "wise". Because the boundary of sapience is vague, it is sometimes treated as a matter of degree rather than a binary property, with great apes, cetaceans and some other animals displaying elements of self-recognition, tool use and problem-solving that approach it.
