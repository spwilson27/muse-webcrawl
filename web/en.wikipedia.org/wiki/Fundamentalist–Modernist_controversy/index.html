Fundamentalist–Modernist controversy

The Fundamentalist–Modernist controversy was a religious and cultural conflict that divided American Protestantism in the early 20th century, roughly from the 1910s to the 1930s. On one side stood fundamentalists, who insisted on the literal truth and inerrancy of the Bible and opposed theological liberalism; on the other stood modernists, who sought to adapt Christian teaching to modern science, historical criticism of the Bible, and evolutionary theory.

Origins

The controversy grew out of 19th-century developments: Darwin's theory of evolution, German higher criticism of the Bible, and the social gospel movement. Between 1910 and 1915, conservative writers published a series of pamphlets titled The Fundamentals: A Testimony to the Truth, funded by oil magnates Lyman and Milton Stewart, which gave the fundamentalist movement its name. The essays defended doctrines such as biblical inerrancy, the virgin birth, substitutionary atonement, the bodily resurrection, and the authenticity of miracles.

Major battles

The conflict played out within the Northern Baptist Convention and the Presbyterian Church in the United States of America. Harry Emerson Fosdick's 1922 sermon "Shall the Fundamentalists Win?" became a manifesto for the modernist side, while J. Gresham Machen's Christianity and Liberalism (1923) argued that liberal theology was not Christianity at all but a different religion. Machen later founded Westminster Theological Seminary (1929) and the Orthodox Presbyterian Church (1936) after conservatives lost the denominational struggle.

The most public episode was the Scopes Monkey Trial of 1925 in Dayton, Tennessee, where William Jennings Bryan defended a state law against teaching evolution and Clarence Darrow cross-examined him on the witness stand. Although Scopes was convicted, press coverage portrayed fundamentalists as anti-intellectual, and the movement withdrew from mainstream public life for several decades, building its own churches, colleges, and publishing networks.

Legacy

The controversy permanently split American Protestantism into mainline and evangelical camps. After the 1930s, fundamentalists largely abandoned the Northern mainline denominations, while a more moderate "neo-evangelical" movement emerged in the 1940s and 1950s under figures such as Billy Graham. The fault lines first drawn in this period continue to shape American religious and political culture.
