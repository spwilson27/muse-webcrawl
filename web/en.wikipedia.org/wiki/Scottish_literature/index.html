Scottish literature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Scottish literature is literature written in Scotland or by Scottish writers. It includes works composed in English, Scottish Gaelic, Scots, Brythonic, French, Latin, and other languages. The earliest surviving major text is the 14th-century epic Brus by John Barbour. Scottish literature flourished in the Middle Scots period of the makars, reached a golden age with Robert Burns in the 18th century, and was transformed in the 19th century by Walter Scott, whose historical novels influenced writing across Europe.

Early and medieval literature

Early Scottish poetry was composed in Brythonic, Gaelic, Norse, and Latin. John Barbour's Brus (1375) celebrated Robert the Bruce and the Wars of Independence. In the 15th and early 16th centuries the court poets known as makars — Robert Henryson, William Dunbar, and Gavin Douglas — produced sophisticated verse in Middle Scots; Douglas's Eneados (1513) was the first complete translation of Virgil's Aeneid into a vernacular of the British Isles. The period also produced the Arthurian and romance works of the Scottish Chaucerians, and the comic flyting tradition.

Early modern and the Reformation era

The Reformation and the departure of the court to London in 1603 reduced patronage for Scots writing, and prose and poetry increasingly followed English models. Ballads and chapbooks preserved a vigorous oral tradition. Meanwhile Gaelic poetry flourished, from the classical bardic tradition to the vernacular verse of Iain Lom and, in the 18th century, Alasdair mac Mhaighstir Alasdair.

The eighteenth century and Burns

The Scottish Enlightenment made Edinburgh a centre of letters. Allan Ramsay revived Scots vernacular poetry; Robert Fergusson bridged the way to Robert Burns (1759–1796), Scotland's national poet, whose poems and songs in Scots and English — "Auld Lang Syne", "Tam o' Shanter", "To a Mouse" — achieved worldwide fame. James Macpherson's Ossian poems sparked international interest and controversy over their authenticity.

The nineteenth century

Walter Scott's Waverley novels (from 1814) effectively created the historical novel and reshaped Scotland's image abroad. James Hogg's Private Memoirs and Confessions of a Justified Sinner (1824) is now regarded as a classic of psychological fiction. Later in the century Robert Louis Stevenson, from Edinburgh, wrote Treasure Island and Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde, while Margaret Oliphant and George MacDonald were major figures, and the "Kailyard" school sentimentalised Scottish rural life — a tendency later writers reacted against.

Twentieth century and the Scottish Renaissance

The Scottish Renaissance of the 1920s, led by Hugh MacDiarmid, sought to revive Scots as a literary language and reconnect Scottish writing with European modernism. Edwin Muir, Sorley MacLean (writing in Gaelic), and Lewis Grassic Gibbon — whose Sunset Song (1932) is among the most loved Scottish novels — were central figures. Post-war writers include Muriel Spark, William McIlvanney, and the poets Edwin Morgan, Iain Crichton Smith, and Liz Lochhead.

Contemporary literature

From the 1980s, writers such as James Kelman, Irvine Welsh (Trainspotting, 1993), Janice Galloway, A. L. Kennedy, and Ian Rankin brought Scottish fiction international prominence, often using urban Scots voices. Gaelic literature continues with poets such as Derick Thomson and Aonghas MacNeacail. Scotland's literary heritage is celebrated in Edinburgh, designated UNESCO's first City of Literature in 2004.
