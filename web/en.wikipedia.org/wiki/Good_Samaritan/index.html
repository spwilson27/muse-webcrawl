Good Samaritan

The parable of the Good Samaritan is told by Jesus in the Gospel of Luke (Luke 10:25–37). It is about a traveller (implied to be Jewish) who is stripped of clothing, beaten, and left half dead alongside the road. First a Jewish priest and then a Levite come by, but both avoid the man. Finally, a Samaritan happens upon the traveller and helps him.

The parable

Jesus tells the story in reply to a lawyer who asks, "And who is my neighbour?" A man travelling from Jerusalem to Jericho is set upon by robbers. A priest and a Levite pass by on the other side, but a Samaritan — a member of a group despised by Jews — binds the man's wounds with oil and wine, carries him to an inn, and pays the innkeeper for his care. Jesus ends by commanding, "Go and do likewise."

Interpretation and legacy

The parable is a central text on compassion that crosses ethnic and religious enmity: the "neighbour" is defined by mercy shown, not by kinship. Its imagery has passed into everyday language — a "good Samaritan" is anyone who helps a stranger in need — and into law: Good Samaritan laws in many jurisdictions protect voluntary rescuers from liability, while some legal systems impose a duty to rescue. Hospitals, charities, and relief organisations worldwide bear the Samaritan name.
