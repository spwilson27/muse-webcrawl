Autoclaved aerated concrete (AAC) is a lightweight, precast, cellular concrete building material, suitable for producing concrete masonry unit-like blocks. Composed of quartz sand, calcined gypsum, lime, cement, water and aluminium powder, AAC products are cured under heat and pressure in an autoclave. Invented in the mid-1920s in Sweden, AAC simultaneously provides structure, insulation, and fire and mould resistance.

Production

When mixed, the aluminium powder reacts with calcium hydroxide to release hydrogen gas, which forms millions of tiny, uniformly distributed air cells — the "rising" process, likened to baking a cake. After initial setting, the green cake is wire-cut into blocks or panels and steam-cured in an autoclave at around 180–190 °C under pressure for 10–12 hours, forming the mineral tobermorite that gives the material its strength.

Properties and uses

AAC is roughly one-fifth the weight of ordinary concrete, offers good thermal insulation and workability — it can be sawn, nailed and routed with ordinary woodworking tools — and is used for internal and external walls, floors and roof panels in residential, commercial and industrial construction. It is produced as blocks, wall and floor panels, and lintels, often steel-reinforced for structural applications.
