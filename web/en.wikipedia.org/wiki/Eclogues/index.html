The Eclogues (Latin: Eclogae), also called the Bucolics, is the first of the three major works of the Latin poet Virgil. It consists of ten pastoral poems in dactylic hexameter, modelled on the Idylls of the Greek poet Theocritus, composed between about 42 and 39 BC. The poems are peopled by herdsmen who converse and sing in a stylised rural landscape ("Arcadia"), while frequently alluding to the turmoil of the Roman civil wars and to Virgil's patrons, including Maecenas and the young Octavian (later Augustus).

The poems

  • Eclogue 1 — a dialogue between Tityrus, secure on his farm thanks to a "god" in Rome (Octavian), and Meliboeus, dispossessed by land confiscations for veterans of the civil wars.
  • Eclogue 2 — the shepherd Corydon laments his unrequited love for the boy Alexis.
  • Eclogue 3 — a singing contest between Menalcas and Damoetas.
  • Eclogue 4 — the "Messianic" eclogue, prophesying the birth of a child who will usher in a golden age.
  • Eclogue 5 — lament and apotheosis of the shepherd Daphnis.
  • Eclogue 6 — the song of Silenus, recounting the creation of the world and myths.
  • Eclogue 7 — another singing match between Corydon and Thyrsis.
  • Eclogue 8 — the songs of Damon (despairing in love) and Alphesiboeus (a love charm).
  • Eclogue 9 — dispossessed shepherds lament the loss of their songs and land near Mantua.
  • Eclogue 10 — the poet Gallus wastes away with unrequited love in Arcadia.

The Fourth Eclogue

The fourth poem, addressed to the consul Gaius Asinius Pollio and announcing that "a great order of ages is born anew" under the return of the Virgin (Justice) and a miraculous child, was in late antiquity and the Middle Ages read by Christians — including Constantine's circle, Lactantius and Augustine — as a prophecy of the birth of Christ. This reading made Virgil a "pagan prophet" figure and helps explain his immense medieval authority, culminating in Dante choosing Virgil as his guide in the Divine Comedy.

Influence

The Eclogues founded the European pastoral tradition: imitated by Calpurnius Siculus and Nemesianus in Latin, they inspired the Renaissance pastoral of Sannazaro, Mantuan, Spenser's Shepheardes Calender, Milton's Lycidas, and the entire genre of pastoral romance and drama. Virgil's Arcadia became one of the most enduring imaginative landscapes in Western literature.
