Abstinence-only sex education

Abstinence-only sex education, also termed abstinence-only-until-marriage education, is a form of sex education that teaches abstaining from sexual activity outside marriage as the only morally acceptable and fully effective option, and that typically excludes or limits information about contraception and safer sex. It is contrasted with comprehensive sex education, which covers both abstinence and contraception.

Definition

Under the 1996 US federal definition (the "A–H" criteria of Title V of the Social Security Act), funded programmes must teach, among other points, that abstinence outside marriage is the expected standard of behaviour, that it is the only certain way to avoid pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections, and that sexual activity outside a monogamous marriage is likely to have harmful effects. Programmes meeting these criteria may not provide information on contraception except to emphasise failure rates.

Evidence

Large evaluations have generally found that abstinence-only programmes do not delay sexual debut, reduce partner numbers, or increase condom use. A 2007 congressionally mandated study by Mathematica Policy Research of four Title V programmes found no significant impacts on abstinence or sexual behaviour. Systematic reviews by the Cochrane Collaboration and UNESCO found insufficient evidence that abstinence-only programmes reduce HIV risk or unintended pregnancy, while comprehensive programmes showed modest protective effects without hastening sexual debut. Some trials of specific abstinence interventions with younger adolescents (e.g., Jemmott et al., 2010) did find short-term delays in self-reported sexual initiation.

Policy history

Federal abstinence funding in the United States expanded from 1981 (Adolescent Family Life Act) and peaked in the 2000s; the Obama administration shifted funding toward evidence-based teen pregnancy prevention, while later legislation rebranded abstinence funding as "sexual risk avoidance education." Public support and school practice vary widely by state, and many countries outside the US rely on comprehensive models recommended by UNESCO and WHO.
