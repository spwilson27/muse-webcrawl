Behavioural addiction, also called process addiction, is the compulsion to repeatedly engage in a rewarding behaviour — one that is not related to ingesting a psychoactive substance — despite experiencing harmful or negative consequences. Proposed examples include gambling, video gaming, compulsive shopping, exercise, internet and social-media use, sexual behaviour and eating.

Classification

Whether behaviours can be truly "addictive" has been debated for decades. Two cases have achieved formal diagnostic recognition. Gambling disorder was reclassified in the DSM-5 (2013) from the impulse-control disorders to "Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders", the first non-substance condition in that chapter. Gaming disorder was included in the ICD-11 (adopted 2019) under disorders due to addictive behaviours, alongside gambling disorder. Other candidate addictions — compulsive sexual behaviour, buying-shopping disorder — appear in ICD-11 under impulse-control disorders rather than as addictions, reflecting unresolved scientific debate.

Features and mechanisms

Behavioural addictions are described as sharing core features with substance addictions: salience (the behaviour dominates thinking), craving, tolerance-like escalation, loss of control, continued use despite harm, and relapse. Neuroscientific models emphasise the brain's reward system, especially dopaminergic signalling and incentive sensitisation; compulsive behaviours can produce measurable adaptations in reward circuitry, though the extent of the parallel with drugs remains contested. Griffiths' "components model" lists salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict and relapse as the defining components of any addiction.

Treatment

Treatments borrow from substance-addiction practice: cognitive behavioural therapy, motivational interviewing, self-help groups (e.g. Gamblers Anonymous), and sometimes medication such as opioid antagonists for gambling disorder. Regulation of product design — for example gambling and video-game "loot boxes" — is an active public-policy debate.

Criticism

Critics warn against pathologising everyday enthusiasms, noting that heavy engagement with games, sex or shopping often reflects other problems (depression, anxiety, ADHD) or simply high involvement, and that diagnostic thresholds vary widely between studies, producing implausibly high prevalence estimates.
