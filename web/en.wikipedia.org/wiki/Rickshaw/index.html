Rickshaw

ClassificationHuman-powered (or motorised) passenger vehicle
OriginJapan, c. 1869
TypesPulled rickshaw; cycle rickshaw; auto rickshaw

A rickshaw is a mode of human-powered transport by which a runner draws a two-wheeled cart that seats one or two people. The word derives from the Japanese jinrikisha (人力車), literally "human-powered vehicle". Rickshaws have since evolved into the cycle rickshaw (pedicab), in which the driver pedals, and the motorised auto rickshaw, widespread across Asia.

History

The pulled rickshaw was invented in Japan around 1869, in the early Meiji period, and spread rapidly: within a few years thousands operated in Tokyo, and the design was exported throughout Asia — to Shanghai, Hong Kong, Singapore, Calcutta and beyond — becoming the dominant form of urban hire transport in many Asian cities by the late 19th century. Faster and cheaper than the palanquin and horse carriage, it employed large numbers of working-class men; in Japan their numbers peaked in the tens of thousands before motor vehicles displaced them in the early 20th century.

Types

  • Pulled rickshaw — the original two-wheeled, human-drawn design. These survive mainly for tourists in places such as Kyoto and Asakusa (Tokyo), and in Kolkata, where hand-pulled rickshaws remained a working transport into the 21st century.
  • Cycle rickshaw — a bicycle with a passenger seat behind (in South Asia) or in front (in Vietnam, where it is called the cyclo). Cycle rickshaws are ubiquitous in Bangladesh — Dhaka is often called the "rickshaw capital of the world" — and in India, Indonesia and elsewhere; pedicabs serve tourist areas in Western cities too.
  • Auto rickshaw — a motorised three-wheeler, known as a tuk-tuk in Thailand, bajaj in Indonesia, mishuk in Bangladesh and auto in India; a staple of urban and rural transport across Asia and increasingly Africa and Latin America.
  • Electric rickshaw — battery-powered variants (e-rickshaws) that have spread rapidly in India and Bangladesh since the 2000s.

Social and cultural aspects

The rickshaw has long been a symbol both of Asian city life and of the exploitation of its pullers, whose work is exhausting and poorly paid; reformers repeatedly sought to ban hand-pulled rickshaws, and post-war Japan and China largely eliminated them. In Bangladesh and India, by contrast, cycle rickshaws remain an essential, pollution-free transport system supporting millions of livelihoods, and Bangladeshi rickshaw art — the vivid painting of the vehicles — is a recognised folk art form.
