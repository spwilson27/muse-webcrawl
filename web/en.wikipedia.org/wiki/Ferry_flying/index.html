Ferry flying

Ferry flights move aircraft to where they are needed, often over long distances.

Ferry flying is the flying of aircraft for the purpose of returning it to base, delivering it to a customer, moving it from one base of operations to another, or moving it to or from a maintenance facility for repairs, overhaul, or other work. A ferry flight carries no passengers or cargo for revenue, though it may carry extra fuel, ferry tanks, positioning crew, and equipment needed for the journey.

Practice

Ferry flights are common when airlines take delivery of new aircraft from manufacturers, when aircraft are returned to lessors, sold, or sent for storage or scrapping, and when light aircraft are delivered across oceans to buyers abroad. Because many small aircraft lack the range for long over-water legs, ferry pilots may install temporary long-range fuel tanks in the cabin and route via staging points such as Canada, Greenland, Iceland, and the Azores for North Atlantic crossings.

Regulation

Special flight permits (ferry permits) may be issued to allow an aircraft that does not meet full airworthiness requirements to be flown to a place where repairs can be made, subject to conditions. Ferry flying over long distances in single-engine aircraft is considered a specialised and comparatively risky occupation, and experienced ferry pilots command professional fees and insurance arrangements accordingly.

History

During the Second World War, organisations such as the British Air Transport Auxiliary and the US Women Airforce Service Pilots ferried military aircraft from factories to operational units, freeing combat pilots for front-line duty.
