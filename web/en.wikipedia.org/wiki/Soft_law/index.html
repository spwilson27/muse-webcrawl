Soft law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Soft law is a term for quasi-legal instruments and norms that do not have the binding force of hard law but nonetheless shape state and institutional behaviour: declarations, resolutions, codes of conduct, guidelines, action plans and standards adopted by international bodies and networks. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights began as soft law (a General Assembly declaration) before hardening into custom and treaties; UNFCCC-era climate pledges, the OECD's guidelines and tax standards, and the Basel Committee's banking standards are governing soft law of enormous practical effect.

Why states choose it

Soft forms lower sovereignty costs (no ratification, easier amendment, no formal breach), allow precision or ambiguity on demand, include non-state actors and technical bodies, and serve as incubators: norms are tested, generate practice and opinio juris, and may harden into custom or treaty. Critics see an accountability gap — law's discipline without law's legitimacy, "regulation by communiqué" through clubs (G7, G20, standard-setters) that outruns democratic oversight — and a compliance gap papered over by reporting rituals. The hard/soft binary is itself dissolving into a spectrum analysis in the "international public law" and global administrative law literatures, which study how bindingness, precision and delegation vary independently.
