Vienna Dioscurides

The Vienna Dioscurides is an early 6th-century Byzantine illuminated manuscript of De materia medica by Dioscorides in Greek. It is an important and rare example of late antique scientific text, copied in Constantinople around 512/515 AD, and is now held in the Austrian National Library in Vienna (manuscript Codex medicus Graecus 1). The manuscript contains some 383 full-page paintings of plants, executed in a naturalistic style derived from classical models, as well as miniatures of animals and a famous series of frontispieces showing Dioscorides and other physicians.

History

The codex was produced for the imperial princess Juliana Anicia, daughter of the western emperor Anicius Olybrius, in gratitude — according to the dedication miniature and poem — for her founding of a church of the Theotokos in the Honoratae district of Constantinople around 512–515. It rearranges Dioscorides' pharmacological text alphabetically by substance, a format more convenient for consultation. The manuscript remained in Constantinople for a millennium, accumulating readers' notes in Greek, Arabic, Persian, Turkish, and Hebrew. In the 1560s it was purchased for the Habsburg imperial library in Vienna by the ambassador Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq from the heirs of the physician Moses Hamon.

Contents and illustrations

The plant miniatures, generally one per page facing the relevant text, are notable for their naturalism, which descends from the lost illustrated herbal of Crateuas, physician to Mithridates VI of Pontus. Frontispieces include an author portrait of Dioscorides, a scene of the mandrake's discovery, and a group portrait of seven famous physicians of antiquity (the "Seven Physicians"). Some images, such as the mandrake pulled by a dog, carry folklore themes traceable to classical antiquity.

Influence

The Vienna Dioscurides is the oldest substantially preserved illustrated herbal and the principal surviving witness to the classical tradition of botanical illustration. Copies and adaptations — including a 10th-century Byzantine copy and later Arabic translations of Dioscorides — transmitted many of its images into medieval Islamic and European herbals, where they influenced plant illustration for a thousand years.
