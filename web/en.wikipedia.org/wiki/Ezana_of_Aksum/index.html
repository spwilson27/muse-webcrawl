Ezana of Aksum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Gold coin of Ezana bearing the Christian cross, among the first Christian coinages]

Ezana (active 320s–360s AD) was a king (negus) of the Kingdom of Aksum in the Horn of Africa, the first Aksumite ruler to embrace Christianity — making his empire one of the earliest Christian states in the world. Succeeding his father Ella Amida as a child under the regency of his mother, Ezana campaigned from Nubia (his inscriptions boast of defeating the Noba and reaching the Nile–Atbara confluence) to South Arabia, minted Aksum's coinage first with pagan disc-and-crescent and then, from about the 340s, with the cross — among the earliest Christian coinage anywhere — and received the shipwrecked Syrian missionary Frumentius, whom Athanasius of Alexandria consecrated as Aksum's first bishop. His trilingual inscriptions (Ge'ez, Sabaean, Greek) are the voice of an African empire speaking to three worlds.

Empire and inscriptions

Ezana's royal inscriptions — more numerous than any other Aksumite king's — chart an expansionist reign: wars against the Beja, the Afar and the peoples of Meroë's former realm (his soldiers destroyed Meroë's remaining settlements and set up a throne at the Nile confluence, a boasts-inscription that is also a key source for Nubia's collapse). Aksumite power reached across the Red Sea into Himyarite Yemen, and its coinage circulated from Egypt to India. The mature stelae fields of Aksum belong to this era of confidence.

Conversion

The conversion story — told by Rufinus from Frumentius' own circle — has the young Syrian captive rise as royal cupbearer and tutor to the boy-king, gathering the Christian merchants of the ports, then sailing to Alexandria for a bishop: himself. Ezana's coins mark the public change in the 340s; his later inscriptions invoke 'the Lord of Heaven' and Christ rather than the old gods (Almaqah, Astar). Ethiopia's church, born in this reign and severed from the Mediterranean world by the 7th century's upheavals, preserved the Aksumite Christianity — its Ge'ez liturgy, canon and calendar — into the modern age.
