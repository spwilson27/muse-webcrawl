Computational origami

Computational origami is a branch of computer science concerned with algorithms for solving origami (paper-folding) problems and for designing foldable structures. The field applies techniques from computational geometry, graph theory, and complexity theory to questions about what can be folded, how efficiently fold sequences can be found, and how folding can be used in engineering.

History and key results

The mathematical study of origami was systematised by the Huzita–Hatori axioms, which describe the folds possible with a single simultaneous fold; origami constructions are strictly more powerful than compass-and-straightedge constructions (they can trisect angles and double the cube). Computational questions were taken up by researchers such as Erik Demaine, whose work includes the universality results that any polygonal shape can be folded from a large enough sheet of paper, and algorithms for fold-and-cut problems — the fold-and-cut theorem, proved by Demaine, Demaine, and Lubiw, states that any straight-line pattern of cuts can be produced by folding paper flat and making a single straight cut.

Design algorithms

  • TreeMaker, by Robert J. Lang, computes crease patterns for origami bases with many flaps of specified lengths, using circle/river packing optimisation.
  • Origamizer, by Tomohiro Tachi, generates crease patterns that fold into arbitrary three-dimensional polyhedral surfaces.
  • Software and theory for rigid origami — folding in which panels remain rigid and only creases flex — relevant to deployable structures and mechanisms.

Complexity and foldability

Deciding whether a given crease pattern can be folded flat is NP-hard in general. The problem of assigning mountain/valley folds (governed locally by Maekawa's theorem and Kawasaki's theorem) is NP-complete for general maps, and the related map-folding problem for simple folds has been studied since the 20th century. Bern and Hayes showed that checking flat foldability is NP-hard even in restricted settings.

Applications

Computational origami informs the design of deployable space structures (such as the Miura fold used for solar-panel arrays), foldable medical devices such as stents, self-assembling robots and materials, and DNA origami at the nanoscale, where scaffolded DNA strands are folded into designed shapes.
