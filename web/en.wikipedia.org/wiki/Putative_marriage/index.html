Putative marriage

A putative marriage is a marriage that is legally invalid but was entered into in good faith by one or both parties who believed it to be valid. The doctrine, originating in canon law, protects the good-faith spouse — the "putative spouse" — and the children of the union, who are treated as legitimate.

Causes of invalidity

A marriage may be putative where it is void or voidable because of an impediment unknown to at least one party: a prior undissolved marriage of one spouse, a defect in the required form or licence, consanguinity, or lack of capacity. The essential element is good faith — an honest and reasonable belief that the marriage was valid — which must exist at the time of the ceremony and, in many systems, persist until the impediment is discovered.

Legal effects

Under the doctrine, the putative spouse may acquire rights similar to those of a legal spouse, such as a share of property acquired during the union, maintenance, and inheritance rights in some jurisdictions. In the canon law of the Catholic Church (canon 1061 §3), an invalid marriage is called putative if it was celebrated in good faith by at least one party, until both parties become certain of its nullity. Civil-law jurisdictions influenced by this tradition, and community-property states such as California and Louisiana in the United States, recognise versions of the putative spouse doctrine.
