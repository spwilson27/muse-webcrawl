Theatre of ancient Greece

The theatre of ancient Greece was a theatrical culture that flourished in ancient Greece from around 700 BC, reaching its height in Athens in the 5th century BC. Athenian tragedy, comedy and the satyr play form the foundation of Western theatre.

Origins and festivals

Drama developed from religious festivals in honour of Dionysus, especially the City Dionysia, at which playwrights competed with three tragedies and a satyr play, and the Lenaia, which emphasised comedy. Tradition credits Thespis with stepping out of the chorus to become the first actor (hence "thespian"). The great tragedians were Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides; the leading comic playwright was Aristophanes.

Theatres and performance

Greek theatres were open-air structures built into hillsides, comprising a circular orchestra, a raised stage with a skene (stage building), and tiered seating (theatron) for thousands. Actors, all male, wore masks and performed with a chorus that sang and danced. Well-preserved examples include the theatre at Epidaurus and the Theatre of Dionysus in Athens.

Influence

Greek drama established conventions of plot, character and dramatic structure analysed by Aristotle in his Poetics, and it remains central to Western literature and performance.
