The Advanced Configuration and Power Interface (ACPI) is an open standard that operating systems use to discover and configure computer hardware components and to perform power management — for example, putting unused devices to sleep, controlling processor performance states, and enumerating devices on the motherboard. First released in December 1996 by Hewlett-Packard, Intel, Microsoft, Phoenix Technologies, and Toshiba, ACPI is now maintained by the UEFI Forum and has replaced the older APM and Plug and Play BIOS specifications.

Overview

ACPI moves power management from the BIOS, which in the APM era acted behind the operating system's back, into the OS itself ("OSPM" — Operating System-directed Power Management). Firmware describes the hardware to the OS through tables, including the RSDP, RSDT/XSDT, FADT, and the DSDT, which contains code written in ACPI Machine Language (AML) that the operating system's interpreter executes to control devices, thermal zones, and power resources.

Power states

The specification defines global states: G0 (working), G1 (sleeping, subdivided into S1–S4), G2 (soft off, S5), and G3 (mechanical off). Sleeping states include S3 ("suspend to RAM", commonly just "sleep") and S4 ("suspend to disk", or hibernation). Devices and processors have their own D-state and C/P-state hierarchies managed under the same framework.

Adoption

ACPI is supported by all modern x86 operating systems, including Windows, Linux, and the BSD family, and has been extended to ARM-based systems, particularly servers. Poorly written firmware AML was a recurring source of compatibility problems in the standard's early years.
