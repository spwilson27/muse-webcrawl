Abbot

An abbot is the male superior of a monastery or abbey in various Christian traditions, especially the Benedictine, Cistercian, and Trappist orders. The word derives from the Aramaic abba, "father", through the Greek abbas and Latin abbas. The female equivalent is an abbess.

Origins

The title was first used for respected hermits and monks in the Egyptian desert, such as Anthony the Great. As communities formed, the abbot became the spiritual father and administrator of the whole house. The Rule of Saint Benedict (c. 530) gave the office its classic form: the abbot is elected by the community, holds office for life, and governs with the counsel of the brethren.

Duties and authority

An abbot oversees the spiritual life, discipline, property, and external relations of his monastery. In major abbeys he may enjoy quasi-episcopal authority; heads of certain historic abbeys, called mitred abbots, wear a mitre and crozier and rank as prelates. An abbot is normally elected by the professed monks of the house and blessed by the diocesan bishop.

Other traditions

In Eastern Orthodoxy the corresponding superior is often called an archimandrite or hegumen. In Buddhism, the head of a monastery is also commonly translated "abbot" in English. In England, the abbots of great houses sat in the House of Lords until the Dissolution of the Monasteries under Henry VIII.
