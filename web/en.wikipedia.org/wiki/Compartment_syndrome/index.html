Compartment syndrome

Compartment syndrome is a condition in which increased pressure within a closed compartment of the body compromises the circulation and function of the tissues within that space. It most commonly occurs in the compartments of the leg or forearm, whose muscles and nerves are enclosed by strong, inelastic layers of fascia. Compartment syndrome is usually caused by bleeding or swelling after an injury, such as a fracture. If untreated, the pressure can cause permanent muscle and nerve damage.

There are two main types: acute compartment syndrome, a surgical emergency typically following trauma, and chronic exertional compartment syndrome, in which symptoms arise during exercise and resolve with rest.

Signs and symptoms

The classic features of acute compartment syndrome are sometimes summarised as the "five Ps": pain, pallor, paresthesia, paralysis, and pulselessness. The earliest and most reliable sign is pain that is severe and out of proportion to the injury, worsened by passive stretching of the muscles in the compartment. The compartment feels tense or "wood-like" on palpation. Numbness and weakness develop as nerves and muscles become ischaemic; loss of pulses is a late sign. In chronic exertional compartment syndrome, aching, tightness, and sometimes numbness develop at a predictable point during exercise and subside within minutes of stopping.

Causes

Acute compartment syndrome typically follows long-bone fractures (especially of the tibia), crush injuries, severe contusions, burns, tight casts or dressings, and reperfusion after vascular injury. Bleeding or oedema inside the fascial compartment raises the intracompartmental pressure, compressing veins and eventually capillaries and arteries, reducing tissue perfusion and causing ischaemia, which produces still more swelling — a self-reinforcing cycle.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is primarily clinical. Where doubt exists, intracompartmental pressure can be measured directly with a needle and manometer; a difference between diastolic blood pressure and compartment pressure (delta pressure) of 30 mmHg or less is commonly used as a threshold supporting intervention.

Treatment

Acute compartment syndrome requires urgent fasciotomy — surgical incision of the fascia to release the pressure — ideally performed within six hours of onset to prevent irreversible tissue death. Splints and casts should be loosened or removed and the limb kept at heart level. Chronic exertional compartment syndrome may be managed by activity modification, physiotherapy, and gait retraining, or by elective fasciotomy in athletes.
