Procrastination

Procrastination is the act of unnecessarily delaying or postponing something despite knowing that there will be negative consequences for doing so. It is a human experience that involves delay in everyday chores or even putting off tasks such as attending an appointment, submitting a job report or school assignment, or broaching a stressful issue with a partner. It is often perceived as a negative trait due to its hindering effect on one's productivity, associated with depression, low self-esteem, guilt, and feelings of shame. However, it can also be considered a prudent response to certain demands that could present risky or negative outcomes or require waiting for new information to arrive.

Psychological causes

Psychological research, notably by Piers Steel and others, treats procrastination less as a time-management failure than as a problem of emotion regulation: people delay tasks that evoke boredom, anxiety, insecurity, or resentment, gaining short-term mood repair at the cost of long-term goals. Steel's Temporal Motivation Theory relates procrastination to expectancy, task value, impulsiveness, and delay — highly impulsive people are particularly prone, especially when rewards are distant.

Types

Researchers commonly distinguish passive procrastinators, who are paralysed by indecision and fail to complete tasks on time, from active procrastinators, who deliberately work under pressure and often perform comparably to non-procrastinators. Chronic procrastination affects roughly a fifth of adults and is associated with poorer health, stress, and delayed medical treatment.

Management

Strategies include breaking tasks into small concrete steps, implementation intentions ("if–then" plans), deadlines and pre-commitment devices, self-compassion for past delays, and reducing task aversiveness. Cognitive-behavioural therapy has been adapted into specific treatments for chronic procrastination.
