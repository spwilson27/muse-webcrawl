Permeable paving

Permeable paving (also called pervious or porous paving) is a range of surface materials and construction techniques that allow rainwater to infiltrate through the surface of pavements into an underlying stone reservoir and soil, rather than running off into drains. It is a central technique of sustainable drainage systems (SuDS) and low-impact development.

Types

Common types include pervious concrete and porous asphalt, in which fine aggregate is reduced so that interconnected voids pass water; permeable interlocking concrete pavers and grid systems of concrete or plastic, whose joints or cells are filled with gravel or soil; and resin-bound gravel. Typical construction places the surface over an open-graded crushed-stone base that temporarily stores water while it soaks into the subgrade.

Benefits and limitations

Permeable pavements reduce stormwater runoff volumes and peak flows, recharge groundwater, and filter pollutants such as oils and metals, improving the quality of water reaching streams. They are widely used for car parks, footpaths, driveways, and low-traffic roads. Their main limitation is clogging: fine sediment gradually fills the pores, so periodic vacuum sweeping is required to maintain infiltration. They are unsuitable where the water table is very high, soils are contaminated, or heavy loads demand conventional structural pavement.
