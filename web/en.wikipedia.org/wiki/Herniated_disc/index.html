Herniated disc

A herniated disc, also known as a slipped disc, is a medical condition affecting the spine in which a tear in the outer, fibrous ring (annulus fibrosus) of an intervertebral disc allows the soft, central portion (nucleus pulposus) to bulge out beyond the damaged ring. Herniation most often occurs in the lower back (lumbar spine) and the neck (cervical spine).

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms depend on whether the protruding material presses on a spinal nerve. Lumbar herniation classically causes back pain with pain radiating down the leg (sciatica), numbness or weakness in the affected dermatome. Cervical herniation causes neck and arm pain. Most herniations are painless incidental findings; a rare emergency, cauda equina syndrome, involves bladder or bowel dysfunction and requires urgent surgery.

Causes

Herniation usually follows age-related degeneration of the disc, which loses water content and becomes prone to tearing; it may be precipitated by strain or trauma but often occurs without any obvious injury. Risk factors include heavy lifting, obesity, smoking and sedentary work.

Treatment

Most cases resolve with conservative treatment: analgesics, physical therapy and remaining active, typically within a few weeks. Epidural steroid injections may help some patients. Surgery, usually microdiscectomy, is reserved for persistent severe pain, progressive neurological deficit, or cauda equina syndrome.
