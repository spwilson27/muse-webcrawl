Legal liability

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Legal liability is the state of being legally responsible for something, such as a debt, an obligation or the consequences of one's acts or omissions. Liability may be civil (owed to a private party, remedied by damages or injunction) or criminal (owed to the state, sanctioned by fine or punishment), contractual or tortious, personal or vicarious, and joint, several or joint-and-several among multiple defendants.

Civil liability generally requires a legal duty, breach of it, causation and loss. Regimes vary in their fault requirements: negligence liability requires fault; strict liability attaches without proof of fault (for defective products, abnormally dangerous activities, or employers' liability for employees' torts — vicarious liability). Liability may be limited by statute (caps on damages, corporate limited liability shielding shareholders beyond their investment) or excluded or apportioned by agreement and insurance. The concept is foundational across private law: to say a party is liable is to say the law will compel them to answer for the matter in question.
