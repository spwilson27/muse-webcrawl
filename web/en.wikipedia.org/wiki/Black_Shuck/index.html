Black Shuck

Black Shuck, Old Shuck, Old Shock or simply Shuck is the name given to a ghostly black dog said to roam the coastline and countryside of East Anglia, in eastern England. Descriptions vary: the creature is sometimes said to be as large as a calf, with one huge flaming eye or two red glowing eyes, and its appearance is often taken as an omen of death.

The Bungay and Blythburgh incident

The most famous account dates to Sunday 4 August 1577, during a great storm. According to the contemporary pamphlet A Straunge and Terrible Wunder by Abraham Fleming, a black dog burst into St Mary's Church in Bungay, Suffolk, during the service, killing two worshippers. The same day a similar creature reportedly appeared at Holy Trinity Church, Blythburgh, killing three people and leaving scorch marks on the church door that survive today and are known locally as "the devil's fingerprints".

Folklore

Black Shuck belongs to the wider British tradition of spectral black dogs, but in some Norfolk and Suffolk tales he is not malevolent: he may guide lost travellers home or guard women walking alone at night. The name may derive from the Old English scucca, meaning "demon" or "fiend".
