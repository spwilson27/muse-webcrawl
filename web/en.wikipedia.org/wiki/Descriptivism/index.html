Descriptivism

Descriptivism may refer to several related positions:

  • In linguistics, linguistic descriptivism is the practice of describing language as it is actually spoken and written by its users, without imposing judgments of correctness. It is opposed to linguistic prescriptivism, which prescribes how language ought to be used.
  • In ethics, descriptivism is the view that moral terms can be defined in terms of descriptive, natural properties (see ethical naturalism).
  • In the philosophy of language, descriptivism is a theory of names holding that a proper name's meaning is given by an associated description or cluster of descriptions.

Linguistic descriptivism

Professional linguists are descriptive in method: they observe usage — including dialects, slang, and errors by prescriptive standards — and formulate the rules speakers actually follow. Dictionaries compiled on descriptive principles, such as Webster's Third New International Dictionary (1961), record usage rather than legislate it, a stance that provoked a famous public controversy over standards.

Descriptivists do not claim that anything goes in every context: registers, style, and standard varieties exist and can be described, and learners may be taught the standard. The descriptive claim is that the standard is one variety among others, not the embodiment of logic or correctness itself.

History in linguistics

The descriptive stance emerged with modern linguistics in the early 20th century — in the structuralism of Leonard Bloomfield and in fieldwork traditions documenting unwritten languages — displacing the philological and prescriptive orientation of earlier grammar writing.

Descriptivism about names

In philosophy, descriptivism (associated with Frege, Russell, and later Searle's cluster theory) held that a name refers via a description the speaker associates with it. Saul Kripke's Naming and Necessity (1972) attacked the theory with modal and semantic arguments, advancing a causal-historical picture of reference instead; descriptivist theories were subsequently revised in two-dimensional and other forms.
