Einstein synchronisation

Einstein synchronisation (or Poincaré–Einstein synchronisation) is a convention for synchronising clocks at different places within a single inertial frame of reference, using signals that travel at the speed of light. It was introduced by Henri Poincaré and made central to the special theory of relativity by Albert Einstein in his 1905 paper on the electrodynamics of moving bodies.

The procedure

A signal is sent from clock 1 at time t1 to clock 2, where it arrives at time t2 and is immediately reflected back, reaching clock 1 at time t3. The clocks are defined to be synchronous if t2 = (t1 + t3)/2 — that is, if the arrival time at the distant clock is taken to be exactly halfway between the emission and return times. This definition assumes the two-way speed of light is isotropic and equal in both directions, something that cannot be verified by measurement of the one-way speed alone, since that measurement itself presupposes synchronised clocks.

Conventionality

The conventionality of simultaneity, debated by philosophers such as Hans Reichenbach, arises from this: alternative synchronisation conventions can be adopted, with a parameter ε between 0 and 1 replacing the value ½, without changing any observable consequences. Einstein synchronisation uses ε = ½, making the one-way speed of light equal in both directions and giving the standard form of the Lorentz transformations. Under Einstein synchronisation, simultaneity is frame-dependent, leading to the relativity of simultaneity.
