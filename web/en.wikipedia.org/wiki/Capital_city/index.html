Capital city

A capital city or just capital is the municipality holding primary status in a country, state, province, department, or other subnational division, usually as its seat of the government. A capital is typically a city that physically encompasses the government's offices and meeting places; the status as capital is often designated by its law or constitution. In some jurisdictions, including several countries, different branches of government are located in different settlements, and in a few cases, the official capital is one city, while government functions are exercised from another city.

In some cases, a distinction is made between the official (constitutional) capital and the seat of government, which is another place. In English, "capital" and "seat of government" are often used interchangeably, but in some other languages the two terms may have different meanings. Capitals are typically the largest and most important cities, although that is not always the case: some capitals are planned cities, built specifically to serve as capitals, such as Canberra in Australia, Brasília in Brazil, and Washington, D.C. in the United States.

Etymology

The word capital derives from the Latin word caput (genitive capitis), meaning "head". In several modern languages, the capital city is also referred to using words derived from this Latin root. The related term "capital city" is often used in English to distinguish a political capital from an economic or cultural capital.

Capitals in history

Historically, the major economic centre of a state or region has often also become the focal point of political power, and became a capital by conquest or federation. Examples include ancient Babylon, Athens, Rome, and Constantinople. Since the end of the Middle Ages, capitals have tended to be fixed in location, because with the increase of literacy and written records, it became more important for the capital to be in a permanent location for keeping records and administration.

Planned capitals

A number of capitals have been purpose-built as planned cities. Examples include:

  • Canberra, Australia — designated in 1908 and established as capital in 1913, as a compromise between Sydney and Melbourne.
  • Brasília, Brazil — inaugurated in 1960, built in the country's interior.
  • Washington, D.C., United States — founded in 1790 as a federal district not part of any state.
  • Islamabad, Pakistan — built in the 1960s to replace Karachi.
  • Abuja, Nigeria — officially became the capital in 1991, replacing Lagos.
  • Naypyidaw, Myanmar — became the administrative capital in 2005.

Unusual capital situations

Some countries have more than one capital. South Africa has three capital cities: Pretoria (executive), Cape Town (legislative), and Bloemfontein (judicial). The Netherlands has Amsterdam as its constitutional capital, while the seat of government is in The Hague. A few countries have no official capital city at all; the Swiss Confederation has no de jure capital, with Bern serving as the de facto seat of government.

Capitals outside the territory

In a few cases, a capital is located outside the territory it governs, often due to historical occupation or dispute. For example, during the Second World War, several governments-in-exile were based in London. The official capital of the Philippines during the Spanish colonial period was Intramuros, within Manila.
