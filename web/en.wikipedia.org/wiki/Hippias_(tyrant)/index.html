Hippias of Athens (Greek: Ἱππίας; c. 570 – 490 BC) was the last tyrant of Athens. The elder son of Peisistratos, he succeeded his father in about 527 BC and ruled jointly alongside his younger brother Hipparchus. His expulsion in 510 BC ended the Peisistratid tyranny and paved the way for the democratic reforms of Cleisthenes.

Rule

The brothers initially continued their father's largely moderate and constitution-respecting government, patronising poets such as Simonides and Anacreon and maintaining Athens' festivals and building programmes. After Hipparchus was assassinated in 514 BC by the lovers Harmodius and Aristogeiton — an attack provoked by a personal insult rather than politics — Hippias' rule turned harsh and suspicious: he executed many citizens and imposed heavy taxes, building a reputation for oppression that later Athenian tradition magnified.

Expulsion and exile

The exiled Alcmaeonid clan, having won influence at Delphi, persuaded Sparta to intervene. After an initial Spartan failure, King Cleomenes I besieged Hippias on the Acropolis in 510 BC; when his children were captured while trying to escape, Hippias agreed to leave Attica within five days. He retired to Sigeion, then to the Persian court. In 490 BC the aged Hippias accompanied the Persian expedition against Greece, landing at Marathon in the hope of being restored — a hope destroyed by the Athenian victory there. He is said to have died shortly afterwards, at Lemnos.
