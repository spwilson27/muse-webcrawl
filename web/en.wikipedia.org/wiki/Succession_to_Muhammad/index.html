The succession to Muhammad refers to the question of who should lead the Muslim community after the death of the Prophet Muhammad in 632 CE. It is the central dividing issue in the history of Islam, underlying the split between Sunnis and Shias.

The Saqifah

According to the mainstream account, while the Prophet's family prepared his body for burial, a group of companions gathered at the Saqifah (portico) of the Banu Sa'ida clan in Medina, where Abu Bakr was recognised as leader and pledged allegiance to by those present. Ali ibn Abi Talib, the Prophet's cousin and son-in-law, did not attend the meeting; Shia tradition holds that Muhammad had designated Ali as his successor at Ghadir Khumm and views the Saqifah as a usurpation.

Sunni and Shia views

Sunnis hold that the caliph is chosen by consultation (shura) among the qualified, and they accept Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali — the Rashidun — as the rightly guided successors. Shias hold that leadership (imamah) was divinely appointed to Ali and his descendants through the line of Fatima, beginning with the Imams of Ahl al-Bayt.

Legacy

The dispute over the succession shaped Islamic theology, law, and political history, and it remains a defining difference between the two largest branches of Islam.
