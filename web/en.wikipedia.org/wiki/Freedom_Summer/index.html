Freedom Summer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Freedom Summer, also known as the Mississippi Summer Project, was a 1964 campaign in the United States organised by the Council of Federated Organizations (COFO) — a coalition led by the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) and CORE — to register as many African American voters as possible in Mississippi, where Black citizens had been systematically disenfranchised. About 1,500 volunteers, most of them white northern college students, joined hundreds of Black activists to run voter registration drives, "Freedom Schools", and community centres, and to organise the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party.

The campaign

Only about 6.7 percent of eligible Black Mississippians were registered to vote in 1964, the lowest rate in the nation, because of poll taxes, literacy tests, and violent intimidation. Volunteers, trained in nonviolence at Western College for Women in Oxford, Ohio, canvassed door to door, taught prospective voters to complete registration forms, and established some 41 Freedom Schools serving roughly 2,500 students with a curriculum of Black history and civic education designed by activist Charles Cobb. The project also supported the Freedom Democratic Party, which challenged the seating of Mississippi's all-white delegation at the 1964 Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City; Fannie Lou Hamer's televised testimony about being beaten for attempting to register became a defining moment of the era.

Violence and the Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner murders

The campaign met fierce resistance. On June 21, 1964, civil rights workers James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner were murdered by Ku Klux Klan members, with the complicity of local law enforcement, near Philadelphia, Mississippi; their bodies were found on August 4 after a massive FBI search (code-named MIBURN). Over the summer, dozens of volunteers were beaten, more than a thousand people were arrested, and scores of Black churches, homes, and businesses were bombed or burned.

Legacy

Although fewer than 2,000 new voters were registered during the summer itself, the national attention Freedom Summer brought to Mississippi's disenfranchisement helped build momentum for the Voting Rights Act of 1965, which transformed Southern politics. The project also reshaped the civil rights movement internally, strengthening Black-led organising in Mississippi while straining the coalition that had run it.
