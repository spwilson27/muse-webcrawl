American Sign Language (ASL) is a natural language that serves as the predominant sign language of Deaf communities in the United States and most of anglophone Canada. It is a complete, complex visual language expressed through movements of the hands combined with facial expressions and postures of the body. Contrary to a common misconception, ASL is not a signed version of English and is not mutually intelligible with British Sign Language, despite the spoken languages of the two countries being largely the same.

History

ASL emerged in 1817 at the American School for the Deaf in Hartford, Connecticut, co-founded by Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet and the French Deaf teacher Laurent Clerc. Clerc's French Sign Language merged with local sign varieties — including the sign language of Martha's Vineyard — and with home sign systems brought by students, forming the basis of modern ASL. For this reason ASL shares a large proportion of its vocabulary roots with French Sign Language.

Linguistics

The linguist William Stokoe demonstrated in 1960 that ASL has a fully fledged phonology: signs are built from a limited set of handshapes, locations, movements, palm orientations and non-manual markers. ASL grammar makes extensive use of signing space for pronouns and verb agreement, classifiers for describing movement and location, and facial expressions that function as grammatical markers (for example, raised brows for yes–no questions). Topic-comment structure is common, and the language differs markedly from English word order.

Use and recognition

ASL is the language of instruction at Gallaudet University in Washington, D.C., the world's only university designed for Deaf students. Several U.S. states formally recognise ASL, and it is widely accepted for foreign-language credit in schools and universities. Estimates of the number of ASL users in the United States vary greatly because the U.S. Census does not count sign language use directly.
