Human skin

The human skin is the outer covering of the body and is the largest organ of the integumentary system. In an average adult it covers an area of about 2 square metres and weighs around 4–5 kg. The skin protects the body against pathogens, excessive water loss, ultraviolet radiation and mechanical injury, and is important in insulation, temperature regulation, sensation and the synthesis of vitamin D.

Structure

Skin is composed of three main layers. The epidermis, the outermost layer, is a keratinised stratified squamous epithelium, continuously renewed from stem cells in its basal layer; it contains melanocytes, which produce the pigment melanin responsible for skin colour. Beneath it lies the dermis, dense connective tissue containing blood vessels, nerves, hair follicles, sebaceous glands and sweat glands, which gives the skin strength and elasticity. The deepest layer, the hypodermis (subcutaneous tissue), consists largely of fat and connective tissue, cushioning and insulating the body.

Variation and conditions

Human skin colour varies from very dark brown to very pale, an adaptation balancing protection of folate stores against ultraviolet light with the need for cutaneous vitamin D synthesis; it is conventionally described using the Fitzpatrick scale. Hair density, sweat-gland distribution and skin thickness also vary by body site and population. Diseases of the skin — including eczema, psoriasis, acne, infections and skin cancers such as melanoma and basal cell carcinoma — are among the most common of all human ailments; the branch of medicine dealing with them is dermatology.
