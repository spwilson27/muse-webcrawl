Kingston upon Hull, usually abbreviated to Hull, is a port city and unitary authority area in the East Riding of Yorkshire, England. It lies upon the River Hull at its confluence with the Humber Estuary, about 25 miles (40 km) inland from the North Sea.

History

The town grew around a port established by the monks of Meaux Abbey in the late 12th century to export wool. Edward I acquired it in 1293, renamed it "King's town upon Hull", and granted a charter in 1299; Hull became a major Hanseatic trading port for wool, wine, and timber. In 1642 Hull's governor, Sir John Hotham, famously closed the city's Beverley Gate to Charles I—an act often seen as a spark of the English Civil War—and the city withstood two royalist sieges.

Hull's MP William Wilberforce was a leading parliamentary campaigner for the abolition of the slave trade. In the 19th and 20th centuries the city became one of Britain's great fishing and whaling ports, its distant-water trawler fleet working Arctic and North Atlantic grounds until the Cod Wars and industry decline of the 1970s. Hull was heavily bombed in the Second World War (the "Hull Blitz"), suffering the highest proportion of damaged housing of any British city.

Modern Hull

Today the Port of Hull handles around ten million tonnes of cargo a year, with ferry services to Rotterdam and Zeebrugge, and the Humber is a centre for offshore wind manufacturing, notably the Siemens Gamesa blade factory. The University of Hull was founded in 1927. The poet Philip Larkin, librarian there for 30 years, is the city's most famous literary figure. Hull was the UK City of Culture in 2017, an event credited with major investment and a cultural revival; landmarks include the Humber Bridge (once the world's longest single-span suspension bridge), The Deep aquarium, and Hull Minster.
