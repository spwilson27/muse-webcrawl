Ecocentrism

Ecocentrism
Ecocentrism locates intrinsic value in ecosystems themselves.
FieldEnvironmental philosophy and ethics
Contrasts withAnthropocentrism; biocentrism
Key thinkersAldo Leopold; Arne Næss; J. Baird Callicott; Holmes Rolston III
Related movementsDeep ecology, land ethic, rights of nature

Ecocentrism is a term used in environmental ethics and philosophy to denote a nature-centred system of values: the view that ecosystems, species and ecological processes — the whole of nature, including non-living components — possess intrinsic value independent of their usefulness to human beings. It is opposed to anthropocentrism, which locates overriding value in humans and human interests, and differs from biocentrism, which centres value on individual living organisms: ecocentrism is holistic, valuing the integrity of ecological wholes even when that conflicts with the welfare of individual organisms.

Origins

The term "ecocentric" was used by Aldo Leopold in his posthumous A Sand County Almanac (1949), and the outlook has older roots — in Romantic nature writing, in John Muir's preservationism, and in indigenous cosmologies that treat land as kin. Twentieth-century ecology supplied the scientific frame: if organisms are constituted by their relations within ecosystems, then the system, not just the organism, seems a proper object of moral concern.

The land ethic

Leopold's "land ethic" is the canonical statement: "A thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community. It is wrong when it tends otherwise." Leopold argued that ethics had successively extended to slaves, women and other once-excluded classes, and should now extend to soils, waters, plants and animals — "the land". Philosopher J. Baird Callicott developed the land ethic into a systematic communitarian ethic grounded in Darwinian accounts of moral sentiment and in modern ecology's picture of interdependence.

Varieties and theory

Arne Næss's deep ecology (1973) combined ecocentric egalitarianism — the equal right of all beings to live and blossom — with advocacy of reduced human interference. Holmes Rolston III defended objective intrinsic value in nature, including value in species lines and ecosystem processes. Ecocentrists debate internal tensions: culling invasive animals to protect an ecosystem is ecocentrically justified but conflicts with animal-rights biocentrism; critics coined the charge of "environmental fascism" for supposed willingness to sacrifice individuals to wholes, a charge ecocentrists reject as a caricature of Leopold's ethic of membership.

Criticism

Critics argue that "the ecosystem" is too fluid and scientifically contested to bear moral weight, that holistic value is vague when ecosystems conflict, and that ecocentrism can license neglect of human poverty (social ecologist Murray Bookchin's critique). Others defend a moderate, pluralist environmental ethic in which ecocentric value is real but balanced against human and animal values.

Policy influence

Ecocentric ideas underlie intrinsic-value language in the Convention on Biological Diversity, the Earth Charter, and the "rights of nature" movement: Ecuador's 2008 constitution grants nature legally enforceable rights, Bolivia passed a Law of the Rights of Mother Earth (2010), and New Zealand granted legal personhood to the Whanganui River (2017). Wilderness preservation statutes and rewilding projects also typically rest on partly ecocentric justifications.
