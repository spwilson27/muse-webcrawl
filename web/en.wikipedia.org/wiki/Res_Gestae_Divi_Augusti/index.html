Res Gestae Divi Augusti

AuthorAugustus
DateCompleted shortly before 14 CE
LanguageLatin, with Greek translation
Best copyMonumentum Ancyranum, Ankara
SubjectAugustus's deeds, honours and benefactions

The Res Gestae Divi Augusti ("The Deeds of the Divine Augustus") is a monumental inscription recording the accomplishments of the first Roman emperor, Augustus, written by Augustus himself shortly before his death in 14 CE. According to the text's own introduction and the historian Suetonius, the original was inscribed on bronze pillars set up in front of the Mausoleum of Augustus in Rome. Copies were distributed throughout the empire; the most complete surviving version is the Monumentum Ancyranum, carved in Latin and Greek on the walls of the Temple of Augustus and Rome in Ancyra (modern Ankara, Turkey).

Contents

The text consists of 35 chapters and an appendix summarising Augustus's expenditures. It falls into three main parts: the honours and offices Augustus received (chapters 1–14), his gifts and benefactions to the Roman people, the army and the treasury, including public works and games (chapters 15–24), and his military and political achievements (chapters 25–35) — the conquests that extended the empire's frontiers, the foreign kings and peoples who sought Roman friendship, and the restoration of the republic, as Augustus chose to present it.

Character of the text

The Res Gestae is a carefully crafted work of political self-presentation. Augustus lists facts — offices held, money spent, wars waged — in a seemingly plain, enumerative style, while omitting anything discreditable: Mark Antony is never named, the proscriptions and civil wars are veiled, and Augustus presents his accumulation of power as the free gift of the Senate and people of Rome. His famous claim that he "restored the republic" in 28–27 BCE anchors the constitutional fiction of the Principate.

Survival and discovery

The Ankara inscription was brought to European attention in 1555 by the embassy of Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq, ambassador of Ferdinand I to the Ottoman court. Further copies were identified at Apollonia and Pisidian Antioch in Galatia, where Greek and Latin fragments allowed scholars to reconstruct passages lost at Ankara. Together the copies preserve nearly the whole text.

Significance

The Res Gestae is one of the most important primary sources for the reign of Augustus and for the ideology of the early empire. Epigraphers sometimes call it "the queen of inscriptions". Its statistics — the sums donatives cost, the numbers of soldiers settled, the temples built — provide data found nowhere else, while its silences are as instructive as its statements.
