Lee Navigation

The Lee Navigation is a canalised river navigation in eastern England, running from Hertford Castle Weir in Hertfordshire, through the Lee Valley, to Bow Creek at Leamouth in east London, where it joins the River Thames. It is one of the oldest artificial navigations in England, having been progressively improved by Acts of Parliament dating back to 1425, and its construction is regarded as a landmark in the history of civil engineering.

The navigation carries the River Lea (spelled "Lea" when referring to the natural river) south for about 28 miles (45 km). Together with the Stort Navigation, which joins it at Feildes Weir, it forms part of the canal network connecting London with East Anglia.

History

The River Lea was navigable in Roman times and was improved in the medieval period. An Act of 1425 appointed commissioners to maintain the navigation, and in 1571 the first pound lock in England built to a documented design, Waltham Cut, was constructed. Later improvement Acts of 1766 and 1855 created a straighter, deeper channel capable of taking barges of up to 130 tons. The engineer John Smeaton was among those consulted on early improvement schemes.

Course

The navigation begins at Hertford and passes Ware, Stanstead Abbotts, Rye House, Hoddesdon, Broxbourne, Cheshunt, Waltham Abbey, Enfield, Edmonton, Tottenham, Walthamstow, Leyton, Stratford, Bromley-by-Bow and Poplar before reaching the Thames at Leamouth. Much of the lower navigation flows through the Lee Valley Regional Park.

Locks

The navigation is controlled by a series of locks, including Hertford Lock, Ware Lock, Stanstead Lock, Hardmead Lock, Feildes Weir Lock, Dobbs Weir Lock, Carthagena Lock, Aqueduct Lock, Cheshunt Lock, Waltham Common Lock, Waltham Town Lock, Rammey Marsh Lock, Enfield Lock, Ponders End Lock, Pickett's Lock, Stonebridge Lock, Tottenham Lock, Pond Lane Flood Gates, and Old Ford Lock. Bow Locks give access to the tidal Bow Creek and the Limehouse Cut.

Use today

Commercial traffic largely ceased in the 1980s, but the navigation remains heavily used by leisure craft, rowing clubs, anglers and walkers on the Lea Valley Walk. The towpath forms part of National Cycle Route 1. The navigation is managed by the Canal & River Trust.
