Diesel locomotive

A diesel locomotive is a type of railway locomotive in which the prime mover is a diesel engine. Most diesel locomotives are diesel-electric: the engine drives an alternator whose output powers electric traction motors on the axles. Others use hydraulic or mechanical transmission.

History

Experimental diesel locomotives appeared in the early twentieth century, following Rudolf Diesel's patents. Diesel-electric switchers entered service in the 1920s–30s, and streamlined passenger units such as the EMD E-units, followed by the ubiquitous EMD F-units and GP-series road switchers, displaced steam across North America during the 1940s and 1950s — the process known as dieselisation.

Characteristics

Diesel locomotives offered far better fuel efficiency and availability than steam, needing no water stops or lengthy preparation. Modern units produce several thousand horsepower, use AC traction motors and microprocessor control, and include genset and hybrid variants for lower emissions.
