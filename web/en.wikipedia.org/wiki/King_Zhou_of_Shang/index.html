King Zhou of Shang

King Zhou (Chinese: 紂王; pinyin: Zhòu Wáng) was the pejorative posthumous name given to Di Xin (帝辛), the last king of the Shang dynasty of ancient China, conventionally dated to have reigned from about 1075 to 1046 BC. In Chinese tradition he became the archetype of the corrupt, tyrannical last ruler whose misrule forfeits the Mandate of Heaven.

Reign

Di Xin succeeded his father Di Yi as king of Shang. Early accounts describe him as intelligent, strong and eloquent, but later tradition — largely written by the Zhou dynasty that overthrew him — portrayed a ruler devoted to drinking, women and extravagant cruelty. He is said to have built a lake of wine and a forest of meat, imposed sadistic punishments such as the "roasting pillar", and been led astray by his consort Daji.

Fall

In about 1046 BC, King Wu of Zhou defeated the Shang army at the Battle of Muye. Accounts relate that Shang troops, including many slaves, defected to the Zhou. Di Xin retreated to his capital, Yin, donned his finest jade garments and set fire to his palace, perishing in the flames. The victorious Zhou founded a new dynasty and justified the conquest by the doctrine of the Mandate of Heaven.

Legacy

The very name "Zhou" (紂) was assigned by his conquerors as a term of disgrace, and his crimes were progressively embellished by Confucian-era historians. Modern scholars caution that the traditional portrait is heavily propagandistic, though the broad outline of the Shang collapse is accepted. King Zhou and Daji figure prominently in the Ming-dynasty novel Investiture of the Gods (Fengshen Yanyi), which recasts his fall as a war among gods and immortals.
