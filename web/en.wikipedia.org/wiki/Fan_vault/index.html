Fan vault

A fan vault is a form of vault used in the Gothic style in which the ribs are all of the same curve and spaced equidistantly, in a manner resembling a fan. The initiation and propagation of this design element is strongly associated with England, as the earliest examples were built there, and it is regarded as a hallmark of the Perpendicular Gothic style.

Structure

In a fan vault, ribs of identical curvature radiate from a single point (usually on a wall shaft or corbel), spreading out to form a half-conoid or "fan". The spaces between the ribs are filled with deeply cut panels, often decorated with tracery. Unlike earlier rib vaults, in which ribs were structural and webs infill, fan vaults are typically built of jointed stone panels in which rib and panel are carved from the same stones, making the vault effectively a carved stone shell.

Examples

The earliest surviving fan vaults date from around the 1350s, in the cloisters of Gloucester Cathedral. Famous later examples include:

  • King's College Chapel, Cambridge — the largest fan vault in the world, constructed between 1512 and 1515 by master mason John Wastell.
  • St George's Chapel, Windsor Castle
  • Henry VII Lady Chapel, Westminster Abbey — with elaborate pendant bosses hanging from the vault.
  • Bath Abbey and Peterborough Cathedral (retrochoir).
  • Sherborne Abbey — among the finest parish-church fan vaults.

Revival

The form was revived in the Gothic Revival of the 18th and 19th centuries, and occasionally copied in modern buildings, usually in reinforced concrete or plaster rather than jointed stone.
