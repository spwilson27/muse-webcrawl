Amateur astronomy

Amateur astronomy is a hobby where participants enjoy observing or imaging celestial objects in the sky using the unaided eye, binoculars, or telescopes. Even though scientific research may not be their primary goal, some amateur astronomers make contributions to doing citizen science, such as by monitoring variable stars, double stars, sunspots, or occultations of stars by the Moon or asteroids, or by discovering transient astronomical events, such as comets, galactic novae or supernovae in other galaxies.

Amateur astronomers do not use the field of astronomy as their primary source of income or support, and usually have no professional degree or advanced academic training in the subject. Most amateurs are hobbyists, while others have a high degree of experience in astronomy and may often assist and work alongside professional astronomers.

Beginnings and equipment

Typical entry points are naked-eye constellation learning, binocular observing and a small telescope, often a Dobsonian reflector, which offers large aperture at low cost. Modern practice increasingly includes astrophotography with digital cameras and computer-controlled mounts, and remote observing via internet-accessible telescopes.

Activities

Common pursuits include observing the Moon and planets, deep-sky objects from catalogues such as the Messier list, meteor showers, solar observing with safe filters, and completing observing programs run by societies such as the Astronomical League. Public outreach — sidewalk astronomy and star parties — is a major part of the hobby's culture.

Contributions to science

Amateurs have a long record of discovery: the comet Hale–Bopp was co-discovered by amateur Alan Hale; amateur observations coordinated by organizations such as the AAVSO (founded 1911) supply long-baseline light curves professionals cannot obtain alone. Supernova hunting, asteroid astrometry and exoplanet transit photometry are also areas where amateurs contribute data to professional research.

Societies and events

Local astronomy clubs are found worldwide, and annual gatherings such as the Texas Star Party and Stellafane (Vermont, meeting since the 1920s) anchor the hobby's social calendar. Magazines such as Sky & Telescope and Astronomy have served the community for decades.
