Casein

Casein (from Latin caseus, "cheese") is a family of related phosphoproteins (αS1, αS2, β, and κ-casein) that are commonly found in mammalian milk, comprising about 80% of the proteins in cow's milk and between 20% and 60% of the proteins in human milk. Sheep and cow milk have a relatively high casein content; other milks, including human milk, have relatively low casein content.

Structure

In milk, caseins are organised into colloidal aggregates called casein micelles, stabilised by calcium phosphate and a surface layer of κ-casein. Caseins are proline-rich, largely unstructured proteins, which makes them resistant to denaturation by heat but readily precipitated by acid or by the enzyme rennet — the basis of curd and cheese formation.

Uses

Casein is the principal protein of cheese and is also used as a food additive, a protein supplement, and industrially in glues, paints, plastics and fibres. Casein paint was widely used by artists, and casein glue was historically used in woodworking and early aircraft construction. Caseinates (such as sodium caseinate) are used as emulsifiers in processed foods.

Health

Some people are allergic to casein as a milk protein. The peptide casomorphin released during digestion of A1 β-casein has been the subject of research and popular debate, though health authorities have not established adverse effects of A1 versus A2 milk.
