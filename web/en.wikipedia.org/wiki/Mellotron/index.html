Mellotron
TypeElectro-mechanical polyphonic tape-replay keyboard
ManufacturerMellotronics / Streetly Electronics (Birmingham, England)
Introduced1963 (Mk I); M400 from 1970
MechanismOne strip of magnetic tape per key, each with a pre-recorded sound (approx. 8 seconds)
Famous soundsFlutes ("Strawberry Fields Forever"), strings, choir

The Mellotron is an electro-mechanical keyboard instrument that produces sound by playing back strips of magnetic tape, one for each note. Developed in Birmingham, England, in the early 1960s from the American Chamberlin, the Mellotron became a signature sound of progressive rock and psychedelic music. Pressing a key pushes its tape against a playback head and capstan; each tape holds about eight seconds of sound, and the distinct attacks, wow and flutter of the mechanism give the instrument its haunting, unstable tone.

History

Brothers Leslie, Frank and Norman Bradley of Mellotronics acquired the rights to Harry Chamberlin's tape-replay keyboard and in 1963 launched the Mellotron Mk I, a large dual-manual console aimed at home entertainment and dance bands. The Mk II followed, and the instrument came to wider attention when the Beatles used its flute tapes — played by Paul McCartney — on "Strawberry Fields Forever" (1967). The Moody Blues, with Mike Pinder (a former Mellotronics employee) on keyboards, used it as an orchestral substitute in songs such as "Nights in White Satin".

M400 and progressive rock

The single-manual M400 of 1970 was smaller, cheaper and more reliable, with a removable frame holding 35 tapes (typically flute, strings and cello, or choir in later sets). It became a staple of progressive rock: King Crimson, Genesis (Tony Banks), Yes (Rick Wakeman), and Led Zeppelin (John Paul Jones) all featured it prominently. Its tapes could not sustain notes indefinitely and the instrument was temperamental on tour, which only added to its mystique.

Decline and revival

The arrival of polyphonic synthesisers in the late 1970s and digital samplers in the 1980s displaced the Mellotron, and production of new instruments effectively ceased. Interest revived in the 1990s and 2000s: restored originals commanded high prices, sample libraries recreated the classic tape banks, and new hardware — including the Swedish-built Mellotron M4000D digital model — brought the sound back to studios and stages.
