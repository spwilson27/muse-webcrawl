The Paris meridian is a meridian line running through the Paris Observatory in Paris, France — now longitude 2°20′14.03″ east of the IERS Reference Meridian (the modern successor of the Greenwich meridian). For more than two centuries it was Greenwich's chief rival for adoption as the world's prime meridian, and it was the reference meridian for French cartography and for the original definition of the metre.

The meridian line in the Cassini room of the Paris Observatory.

History

The Paris Observatory was founded in 1667, and its first director, Giovanni Domenico Cassini, began defining the meridian through it in 1669. Generations of the Cassini family surveyed France along the meridian, producing the first modern topographic map of a country (the Carte de Cassini). In the 1790s, Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain measured the meridian arc from Dunkirk to Barcelona (passing through Paris) to define the metre as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the Equator along that meridian.

Rivalry with Greenwich

At the International Meridian Conference in Washington, D.C., in 1884, delegates voted to adopt the Greenwich meridian as the international prime meridian; France abstained and continued to use the Paris meridian. France adopted Greenwich-based time in 1911 — phrased, to save face, as "Paris mean time, retarded by 9 minutes 21 seconds" — and French charts switched to Greenwich for navigation in 1914. The offset between the two meridians is 2°20′, corresponding to 9 minutes 21 seconds of time.

The line today

The meridian is marked across Paris by the Arago medallions, 135 bronze plaques set into pavements and buildings along the line, installed in 1994 in honour of the astronomer François Arago (who re-measured the meridian); the project was designed by Dutch artist Jan Dibbets. The line also features in popular culture, notably in The Da Vinci Code, which calls it the "Rose Line" — a fictional name.
