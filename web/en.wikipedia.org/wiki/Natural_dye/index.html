Natural dye

Wool dyed with natural dyes

Natural dyes are dyes or colorants derived from plants, invertebrates, or minerals. The majority of natural dyes are vegetable dyes from plant sources — roots, berries, bark, leaves, and wood — and other biological sources such as fungi and lichens. Until the mid-19th century they provided nearly all the colours used for textiles, food and art.

Common sources

  • Madder (Rubia tinctorum): a red dye from the roots, giving Turkey red on cotton.
  • Indigo: a blue dye from Indigofera species and from woad (Isatis tinctoria) in Europe.
  • Weld (Reseda luteola): one of the clearest and fastest natural yellows.
  • Cochineal: a brilliant crimson from scale insects living on prickly pear cacti in Mexico and Peru.
  • Tyrian purple: an extremely costly purple extracted from murex sea snails, reserved in antiquity for royalty.
  • Logwood, cutch, ochres and iron blacks: widely traded dyewoods and mineral pigments.

Process

Most natural dyes are adjective dyes: they require a mordant, usually a metallic salt such as alum, iron or chrome, to bind the colourant to the fibre. Substantive dyes such as indigo, applied by vat dyeing, bond without a mordant. Dyeing was historically a skilled, guild-regulated trade, and dye plants were major articles of international commerce.

Decline and revival

The synthesis of mauveine by William Henry Perkin in 1856 launched the synthetic dye industry, and by the early 20th century natural dyes had been almost entirely displaced commercially. Interest revived from the late 20th century among artisan dyers, the natural fibres movement and researchers seeking lower-impact textile processes.
