Open-source software

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Logos of well-known open-source projects arranged in a grid]

Open-source software (OSS) is computer software released under a licence in which the copyright holder grants users the rights to use, study, change, and distribute the software and its source code to anyone and for any purpose. It is typically developed collaboratively and publicly.

The term 'open source' was adopted in 1998 (following Netscape's release of its browser source code) as a more business-friendly framing of the older free-software movement; the Open Source Initiative maintains the Open Source Definition and approves licences such as MIT, Apache 2.0, and the GNU GPL.

Impact

Open source now underlies most of the world's computing: the Linux kernel, web servers (Apache, nginx), browsers (Firefox, Chromium), programming ecosystems (Python, GCC, LLVM), and cloud infrastructure (Kubernetes, Docker). Companies contribute for shared maintenance, talent, and standards influence; studies find the majority of commercial codebases contain open-source components.

The model faces sustainability questions — critical projects maintained by few volunteers (the 2024 xz Utils backdoor scare and 2021 Log4Shell incident highlighted the risks) — spurring corporate and government funding initiatives.

Licensing

Permissive licences (MIT, BSD, Apache) allow proprietary derivatives; copyleft licences (GPL) require derivatives to remain open. Licence choice is a central strategic and legal decision for projects.
