The MD5 message-digest algorithm is a widely used hash function producing a 128-bit hash value, typically expressed as a 32-digit hexadecimal number. Designed by Ronald Rivest in 1991 and published as RFC 1321 in 1992, MD5 was intended as a secure replacement for the earlier MD4. It has since been shown to be severely compromised: collision attacks are practical, and MD5 is no longer considered suitable for cryptographic security such as digital signatures.

History

MD5 was designed by Rivest at MIT in 1991 and standardised in April 1992. Doubts about its collision resistance emerged as early as 1996, when collisions were found in its compression function. In 2004 researchers demonstrated full MD5 collisions, and by 2008 a forged X.509 certificate had been created using an MD5 chosen-prefix collision. In 2012 the Flame malware exploited an MD5 collision to forge a Microsoft code-signing certificate.

Algorithm

MD5 processes a variable-length message, padded so that its length is congruent to 448 modulo 512 bits, in successive 512-bit blocks. A 128-bit state, divided into four 32-bit words, is updated over four rounds of sixteen operations each, using nonlinear functions, per-step constants and message words, in a Merkle–Damgård construction. The final state is the digest.

Example

The MD5 hash of the empty string is d41d8cd98f00b204e9800998ecf8427e, and even a small change to a message produces a completely different digest, illustrating the avalanche effect.

Applications

Despite its cryptographic weaknesses, MD5 remains common for non-security purposes such as checksums for detecting accidental data corruption, fingerprinting files, and as a component of legacy protocols and password-storage schemes that are no longer recommended.
