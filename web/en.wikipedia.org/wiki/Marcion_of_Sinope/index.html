Marcion of Sinope
Bornc. 85 AD, Sinope, Pontus
Diedc. 160 AD, Rome
Known forFounding Marcionism; first New Testament canon

Marcion of Sinope (c. 85 – c. 160 AD) was an early Christian theologian and bishop's son from Pontus who came to Rome around 140 AD. He taught that the God of the Old Testament was a distinct, lesser deity — a just but wrathful demiurge — while the God revealed by Jesus was a previously unknown God of love and mercy. Expelled from the Roman church in 144 AD, he founded a rival church whose influence spread widely.

Teachings

Marcion rejected the Old Testament entirely and produced the earliest known Christian biblical canon: a version of the Gospel of Luke shorn of material he considered Judaizing interpolations, together with ten of Paul's letters, preceded by his own Antitheses, which contrasted the Old and New Testaments.

Legacy

Marcionite churches persisted into the fifth century, especially in the East. His challenge is widely credited with prompting the mainstream church to define its own canon of the New Testament. Tertullian devoted a five-book work, Adversus Marcionem, against him. Adolf von Harnack controversially praised Marcion as a reformer before Luther.
