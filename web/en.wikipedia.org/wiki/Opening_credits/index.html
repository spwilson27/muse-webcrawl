Opening credits

In a motion picture, television program, or video game, the opening credits or opening titles are shown at the very beginning and list the major participants in the production: the title, studio, principal cast, director, producers, and other key creative personnel. They contrast with the closing credits, which give a much fuller listing at the end.

History

In early cinema, nearly all credits appeared at the start of the film, often on static title cards, since audiences were expected to leave as the film ended. From the 1930s through the 1950s, elaborate credit sequences with distinctive typography and artwork became common; designer Saul Bass became famous in the 1950s for animated title sequences for films such as those of Alfred Hitchcock and Otto Preminger. Over time, most credits migrated to the end of films, and many modern works open with only the title — sometimes delayed well into the picture — or with minimal credits.

Television

Television series traditionally open with a title sequence combining theme music, imagery, and the names of the regular cast and creators. These sequences help establish tone and brand for a show. In recent years, many series have shortened or even skipped opening sequences to maximize story time, a trend accelerated by streaming services that offer "skip intro" features.

Conventions

The order and form of billing in opening credits are frequently determined by contracts and guild rules; the director's credit customarily appears last in film opening credits, while the writer's placement is governed by Writers Guild rules. A "cold open" — a scene before the titles — is common in television comedies and dramas.
