Battle of Mons

Date23 August 1914
LocationMons, Belgium
ResultGerman tactical success; British retreat in good order
BelligerentsBritish Empire vs. German Empire
CommandersJohn French; Horace Smith-Dorrien vs. Alexander von Kluck

The Battle of Mons was the first major engagement of the British Expeditionary Force (BEF) in the First World War, fought on 23 August 1914 near Mons, Belgium, as part of the Battle of the Frontiers. The BEF held off the German First Army for a day before the retreat of the French Fifth Army on its flank forced a general withdrawal — the beginning of the Great Retreat to the Marne.

Background

The BEF, some 70,000 men under Field Marshal Sir John French, deployed along the Mons–Condé canal on the left of the French armies executing Plan XVII. Advancing into Belgium under the Schlieffen Plan, General von Kluck's First Army, roughly 160,000 strong in the immediate sector, expected to sweep past a much smaller force.

The battle

German infantry attacking in close order across the canal were met by the rapid, accurate rifle fire for which the professional British regulars were famous — the musketry standard of fifteen aimed rounds a minute led some German units to report facing massed machine guns. The British held the canal line all day, inflicting heavy casualties, but the salient around Mons became untenable once the French fell back, and the BEF withdrew overnight.

Aftermath

British casualties were about 1,600 men; German losses were considerably higher. The subsequent fighting retreat, including the rear-guard actions at Le Cateau, ended at the Marne in September. Mons later acquired legendary embellishments, including the story of the "Angels of Mons" said to have covered the retreat.
