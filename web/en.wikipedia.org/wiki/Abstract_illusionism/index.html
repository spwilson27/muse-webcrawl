Abstract illusionism

Abstract illusionism is a style of painting that emerged in the United States in the mid-1970s. It combines abstract, often geometric or gestural forms with the illusionistic devices of trompe-l'œil — above all cast shadows and simulated three-dimensional depth — so that painted shapes appear to float in front of, or project from, the picture surface.

Characteristics

Where classical trompe-l'œil imitates recognisable objects, abstract illusionism imitates the behaviour of light on invented forms: ribbons, bars, slashes of paint, and hovering planes rendered with drop shadows, gradients, and perspective cues. The result plays illusionistic space against the flatness demanded by modernist abstraction, producing a deliberately ambiguous reading.

History

The term was popularised by the art historian and curator Barbara Rose, who organised the exhibition "Abstract Illusionism" in the late 1970s. Associated artists include James Havard, often credited as a pioneer of the style, Al Held, Jack Lembeck, George D. Green, and Allan D'Arcangelo in some phases of his work. The movement was taken up by the Louis K. Meisel Gallery and others interested in successors to Photorealism.

Criticism and legacy

Critics were divided: some welcomed the wit and technical finish, others saw a decorative compromise between abstraction and realism. Abstract illusionism fed into the broader pattern and decoration tendencies of the late 1970s and anticipated later painters' renewed interest in illusion, virtual space, and digital-looking effects.
