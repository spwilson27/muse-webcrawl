Art pottery

Art pottery vases from the American art pottery movement

Art pottery is a term for ceramic ware made primarily for aesthetic rather than purely functional purposes, usually produced in a studio or small workshop setting where the design, throwing, decorating and glazing are treated as an integrated artistic act. The movement flourished from the 1870s through the 1920s in Britain, Europe and the United States, and is closely associated with the Arts and Crafts movement.

History

The roots of art pottery lie in the reaction against mass-produced, heavily ornamented industrial ceramics of the mid-Victorian era. In Britain, firms such as Minton, Doulton and the Martin Brothers began producing studio-made wares with hand-painted decoration, while the rediscovery of Japanese ceramics following the opening of Japan influenced the restrained forms and glazes of potters such as Bernard Leach.

In the United States, the movement took off after the Centennial Exposition of 1876 in Philadelphia. Maria Longworth Nichols founded Rookwood Pottery in Cincinnati in 1880, and potteries such as Grueby, Van Briggle, Teco and Newcomb College produced matte-glazed vessels that became defining objects of the American Arts and Crafts interior. In Europe, studios worked within the currents of Art Nouveau and later Art Deco.

Characteristics

Art pottery is distinguished by hand-thrown or hand-finished forms, experimental glazes (notably matte, crystalline and lustre finishes), and painted or modelled decoration drawn from nature. Pieces were often signed by the artist, reflecting the elevated status of the potter as an artist rather than an anonymous factory worker.
