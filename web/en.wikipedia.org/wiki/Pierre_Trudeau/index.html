Pierre Elliott Trudeau (October 18, 1919 – September 28, 2000) was a Canadian politician, statesman, and lawyer who served as the 15th prime minister of Canada from 1968 to 1979 and again from 1980 to 1984. He also briefly served as leader of the Opposition in 1979–1980, and as minister of justice and attorney general from 1967 to 1968. Trudeau ranks as one of Canada's most consequential and polarising prime ministers; he is the father of Justin Trudeau, the 23rd prime minister.

Early life and career

Born in Montreal to a wealthy Franco-Scottish family, Trudeau studied law at the Université de Montréal and later at Harvard, the London School of Economics and Sciences Po. In the 1950s he became a leading critic of Quebec premier Maurice Duplessis and co-founded the intellectual journal Cité Libre. He entered federal politics in 1965 and became minister of justice under Lester B. Pearson, where he liberalised laws on divorce, abortion and homosexuality, famously remarking that "there's no place for the state in the bedrooms of the nation."

Prime ministership

Trudeau won the Liberal leadership in 1968 amid the enthusiasm dubbed "Trudeaumania". As prime minister he championed a "Just Society", official bilingualism (Official Languages Act, 1969) and multiculturalism. During the October Crisis of 1970, he invoked the War Measures Act after kidnappings by the Front de libération du Québec. In his final term he patriated the Canadian constitution and entrenched the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms in the Constitution Act, 1982. His National Energy Program and his confrontational stance toward Quebec nationalism remained deeply controversial, particularly in Western Canada and Quebec. He retired in 1984.

Legacy

Trudeau's vision of a bilingual, multicultural, rights-based federal Canada, governed with a strong central government, continues to shape Canadian politics. Montreal's international airport is named in his honour.
