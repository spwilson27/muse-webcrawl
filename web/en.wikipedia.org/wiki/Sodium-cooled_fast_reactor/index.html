Sodium-cooled fast reactor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sodium-cooled fast reactor (SFR) is a fast neutron reactor design cooled by liquid sodium. It is one of the designs pursued under the Generation IV International Forum for advanced nuclear reactors, valued for its ability to breed fuel and to burn long-lived actinides from spent nuclear fuel.

Design

Liquid sodium has excellent thermal conductivity and a high boiling point (883 °C), allowing the primary coolant to operate near atmospheric pressure while achieving high outlet temperatures and good thermal efficiency. Because the reactor uses fast (unmoderated) neutrons, it can fission actinides that thermal reactors cannot, potentially closing the fuel cycle.

History

Experimental sodium-cooled reactors include the American EBR-I (which in 1951 first produced electricity from nuclear power) and EBR-II, the French Phénix and Superphénix, the Japanese Monju, and Russia's BN-600 and BN-800 reactors.

Challenges

Sodium reacts vigorously with water and burns in air, requiring intermediate coolant loops and careful leak detection. A positive sodium void coefficient in large cores is also a safety consideration.
