The ends justify the means

"The ends justify the means" is a phrase commonly used to describe a strategy or policy in which the morality of an action is judged solely by its outcome: if the outcome is good, then the action taken to reach it is morally acceptable, no matter how questionable the action itself might be. The phrase is often associated with consequentialism in ethics, the view that the consequences of one's conduct are the ultimate basis for any judgment about the rightness or wrongness of that conduct.

Origins

Similar ideas appear in ancient and early modern writing. The Roman poet Ovid wrote in his Heroides "exitus acta probat" — the outcome justifies the deeds. The phrase is also strongly associated with the Italian political theorist Niccolò Machiavelli, whose work The Prince (written in 1513) argued that rulers must be prepared to act against conventional morality when necessary to maintain the state, although the exact phrase does not appear in his writings. The Jesuit casuist Hermann Busenbaum stated a related maxim in 1645: "when the end is legitimate, the means are also legitimate."

Interpretations and criticism

The phrase is frequently used negatively, to criticise actions taken in pursuit of a goal that are considered immoral or excessive. Deontological ethical theories reject the maxim, holding that some actions are wrong in themselves regardless of their consequences. Critics also note that the maxim can be used to rationalise almost any behaviour after the fact, and that harmful means often corrupt the ends they are meant to serve.
