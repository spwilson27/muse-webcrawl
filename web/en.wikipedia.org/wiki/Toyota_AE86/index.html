The Toyota AE86 is a small, lightweight, front-engine/rear-wheel-drive model line manufactured by Toyota from 1983 to 1987 as part of the fifth-generation Corolla (E80) range. Sold in Japan as the Corolla Levin (with fixed headlights) and Sprinter Trueno (with pop-up headlights), it was one of the last affordable rear-wheel-drive cars of its type, and its balance, rev-happy engine, and simple construction later made it an icon of drifting and of Japanese car culture.

Technical description

The AE86 was powered by the 1.6-litre 4A-GE engine, a twin-cam, sixteen-valve four-cylinder producing about 130 PS (128 hp) in Japanese specification, driving the rear wheels through a five-speed manual gearbox, with an optional limited-slip differential. MacPherson-strut front suspension, a live rear axle with a five-link arrangement, near-even weight distribution, and a kerb weight around 970–1,000 kg gave the car handling that enthusiasts prize for adjustability and feedback rather than outright speed.

Motorsport and drifting

The AE86 was competitive in Group A and Group N touring-car racing, winning class championships in Europe and Japan, and was campaigned in rallying as well. It is most celebrated, however, for its role in the birth of drifting: Keiichi Tsuchiya, the "Drift King," honed his technique in an AE86, and the car became synonymous with mountain-pass (touge) driving in Japan.

In popular culture

The AE86's fame outside enthusiast circles rests largely on the manga and anime Initial D (from 1995), whose protagonist Takumi Fujiwara delivers tofu in his father's panda-black-and-white Sprinter Trueno. The series triggered a surge in prices and demand for surviving cars worldwide. Toyota itself acknowledged the lineage when it named the AE86's modern spiritual successor, the Toyota 86 (2012), after it.
