Homology modeling

Homology modeling, also known as comparative modeling of protein, refers to constructing an atomic-resolution model of a "target" protein from its amino acid sequence and an experimental three-dimensional structure of a related homologous protein (the "template"). Homology modeling relies on the observation that a protein's structure is more highly conserved than its sequence: proteins sharing significant sequence similarity almost always fold into similar three-dimensional shapes.

Method

The procedure generally proceeds in four steps. First, a template is identified, usually by searching sequence databases with tools such as BLAST or the more sensitive profile methods HHsearch. Second, the target sequence is aligned to the template; the quality of this alignment is the single most important factor in model accuracy. Third, the model is built by copying the coordinates of aligned template residues and modeling the regions that differ, especially loops, using knowledge-based or energy-based methods. Fourth, the model is refined and assessed, often with statistical potentials or geometry checks such as Ramachandran analysis.

Accuracy and applications

When the target and template share more than about 50 percent sequence identity, models can approach the accuracy of low-resolution experimental structures. Between 30 and 50 percent identity, models are usually correct in overall fold but contain errors in side-chain packing and loops. Below roughly 30 percent identity—the "twilight zone"—alignment errors become severe and models less reliable. Homology models are used in drug discovery (for virtual screening and structure-based drug design), in planning experiments such as site-directed mutagenesis, and in interpreting the effects of genetic variants.

Popular software tools include MODELLER, SWISS-MODEL, Rosetta, and I-TASSER. The biennial CASP experiments benchmark prediction methods; since CASP14 in 2020, deep-learning methods such as AlphaFold2 have largely superseded classical homology modeling for single-chain proteins, though template-based modeling remains useful for complexes, ligands, and rapid analyses.
