Crossmodal perception or multisensory perception is the perception that involves interactions between two or more different sensory modalities. Crossmodal perception is the single, integrated experience that human and other animal nervous systems produce from overlapping pieces of information arriving through separate senses — seeing a speaker's lips while hearing their voice, or tasting food while smelling it.

Research in the field examines how the brain combines information across vision, hearing, touch, smell, and taste, and how one modality can alter perception in another. Crossmodal effects range from automatic illusions, which demonstrate mandatory integration, to correspondences such as the universal tendency to match the word "bouba" with a rounded shape and "kiki" with a spiky one.

Examples

  • McGurk effect: Hearing a syllable such as /ba/ while watching a face articulate /ga/ produces perception of a third syllable, /da/ — vision irreversibly changes what is heard.
  • Ventriloquism effect: Perceived location of a sound is pulled toward a simultaneous visual event, which is why dialogue in films seems to come from actors' mouths rather than from speakers.
  • Rubber hand illusion: Synchronous stroking of a visible rubber hand and one's own hidden hand leads to a felt sense of ownership of the fake hand, showing vision–touch integration in body perception.
  • Sound symbolism (bouba/kiki): Across languages and cultures, people match certain sounds to rounded or angular shapes, first noted by Köhler in 1929 and popularized by Ramachandran and Hubbard.
  • Flavour: What is called taste is mostly a multisensory construct of taste, retronasal smell, texture, temperature, and even sound (e.g., the crispness of a chip).

Mechanisms

Neuroscience research shows that integration occurs both in classically multisensory structures such as the superior colliculus and in areas once considered unisensory, including primary visual and auditory cortices. Integration follows principles summarized by Stein and Meredith: signals that are close in space and time, and that are individually weak, are integrated most strongly (the principle of inverse effectiveness). Modern accounts model the process as Bayesian causal inference: the brain estimates whether signals share a common cause, fusing them when they probably do and segregating them when they probably do not.

Crossmodal correspondences

Crossmodal correspondences are the consistent associations most people make between features in different senses — high-pitched sounds with small, bright, elevated objects; sweetness with roundness; loudness with brightness. Unlike synesthesia, which is idiosyncratic and experienced consciously, correspondences are shared statistical regularities, possibly learned from the environment, from language, or from structural features of the nervous system. They are exploited in product design, food science, and interface design.

Development and plasticity

Infants show early crossmodal matching, such as looking longer at a face whose movements match a heard vowel sound. After sensory loss, crossmodal plasticity recruits the deprived cortex: in blind individuals, occipital cortex responds to touch (Braille) and sound, one basis for sensory-substitution devices that translate images into soundscapes or tactile arrays.

Applications

Crossmodal research informs the design of alarms and cockpits (redundant audio-visual signals), virtual reality and teleoperation (matching visual and haptic feedback), food and beverage formulation, advertising, and assistive technology.
