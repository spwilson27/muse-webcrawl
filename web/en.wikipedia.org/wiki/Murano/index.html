Murano is a series of islands linked by bridges in the Venetian Lagoon, about 1.5 kilometres north of Venice, Italy. Like Venice itself, it is organised as an archipelago — seven individual islands divided by canals and linked by bridges. It has a population of around 4,100 and is world-famous for its glassmaking.

History

Murano was originally settled by the Romans and later by refugees from the mainland during the barbarian invasions of the 5th and 6th centuries. In 1291, the Venetian Republic ordered all glass furnaces in Venice to be moved to Murano, officially because of the risk of fire to the city's wooden buildings — and, it is often suggested, to isolate the glassmakers and protect the secrets of their trade. Glassmasters were forbidden to leave the Republic, though they enjoyed high social status; their daughters could marry into the Venetian nobility.

Murano glass became one of Europe's most prized luxury goods: the island's craftsmen developed or refined techniques including crystalline glass (cristallo), enamelled glass, aventurine glass with gold flecks, multicoloured millefiori and imitation gemstones. By the 18th century the industry declined under foreign competition, but it revived in the 19th century and remains the island's economic mainstay. The Museo del Vetro (Glass Museum), founded in 1861, occupies the Palazzo Giustinian.

Sights

Notable sights include the Church of Santa Maria e San Donato, known for its 12th-century Byzantine apse and mosaic floor, the Church of San Pietro Martire, and the island's lighthouse. Murano is also often held to be the birthplace of the composer Tomaso Albinoni.
