Point-and-shoot camera

A point-and-shoot camera, also known as a compact camera, is a still camera designed primarily for simple operation. Most use focus-free or autofocus lenses and automatic systems for setting the exposure options; the user need only compose the picture and press the shutter button. Point-and-shoot cameras are distinguished from single-lens reflex (SLR) and mirrorless cameras by their fixed, non-interchangeable lenses, smaller sensors, and emphasis on portability and convenience rather than manual control.

Film compacts

Simple cameras for the mass market date to the Kodak Brownie of 1900, but the modern point-and-shoot emerged with the compact 35 mm cameras of the 1960s–1980s, such as the Rollei 35, the Olympus Trip 35, and the Minox 35. From the late 1970s, autofocus compacts — beginning with the Konica C35 AF (1977) — added automatic focusing, motorised film advance, auto exposure, and built-in flash, making snapshot photography effectively foolproof. Disposable (single-use) cameras, introduced in the 1980s, were an extreme form of the type.

Digital compacts

Digital point-and-shoot cameras dominated consumer photography in the 2000s, typically offering small sensors (1/2.3" and similar formats), retractable zoom lenses, and pocketable bodies. Subclasses included ultra-compacts, "superzoom" or bridge cameras with very long zoom ranges, rugged waterproof models, and premium fixed-lens compacts with larger sensors aimed at enthusiasts, such as the Ricoh GR and Fujifilm X100 series, which retain manual controls and a devoted following.

Decline and survival

Sales of compact cameras collapsed in the 2010s as smartphone cameras matched or exceeded their convenience and approached their image quality; annual shipments fell from well over 100 million units around 2010 to a small fraction of that within a decade. The category survives in niches — long-zoom travel cameras, waterproof models, premium fixed-lens cameras, and instant cameras — while the term point-and-shoot remains in general use for any effortless automatic camera.

The name derives from the computing idiom point and click: the user simply points the device and operates a single control.
