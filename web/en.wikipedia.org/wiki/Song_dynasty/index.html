Song dynasty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Detail of the Qingming scroll, showing bustling city life in Kaifeng]

The Song dynasty (960–1279) ruled China during one of the most economically and technologically dynamic periods of the pre-modern world. Founded by Zhao Kuangyin (Emperor Taizu), the Song reunified most of China proper, governed through a meritocratic civil service selected by examination, and presided over a commercial revolution: paper money, maritime trade, coal-fuelled iron production, printing, gunpowder weapons and cities of over a million people. Militarily weaker than its northern neighbours, the Song lost the north to the Jin in 1127 and the whole empire to the Mongols in 1279.

Northern and Southern Song

The Northern Song (960–1127), capital Kaifeng, paid tribute to the Khitan Liao rather than risk war, and debated reform under Wang Anshi's New Policies (from 1069). In 1127 Jurchen Jin forces captured Kaifeng and two emperors (the Jingkang incident). The Southern Song (1127–1279), capital Hangzhou, flourished commercially even in reduced territory — its navy, tax revenues and sea trade were formidable — while philosophers such as Zhu Xi systematised Neo-Confucianism, which dominated East Asian thought for centuries after.

Fall

Allied at first with the Mongols against Jin, the Song then faced the Mongol Empire alone. After a forty-year resistance — among the longest defences against Mongol conquest anywhere — Hangzhou fell in 1276 and the last loyalist court perished at the naval Battle of Yamen in 1279, where, tradition holds, the official Lu Xiufu leapt into the sea holding the child emperor.
