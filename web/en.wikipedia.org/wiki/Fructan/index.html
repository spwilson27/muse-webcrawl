Fructan

A fructan is a polymer of fructose molecules. Fructans with a short chain are known as fructooligosaccharides; longer-chain fructans occur in many plants as storage carbohydrates. The best-known fructan is inulin, a mixture of fructose polymers found in chicory root, Jerusalem artichoke and many other plants. Fructans also occur in grasses and cereals, including wheat, and are produced by some bacteria and fungi.

Types

Fructans are classified by the linkage between fructose units. Inulins have predominantly β(2→1) bonds and are linear; levans have β(2→6) bonds; graminans, found in cereals and grasses, are branched mixtures of both. Plant fructans usually terminate in a glucose residue derived from the sucrose molecule from which synthesis began.

Function and uses

In plants, fructans are stored in vacuoles, especially in stems, roots and bulbs, and serve as reserves of energy; they are thought also to contribute to tolerance of cold and drought by protecting membranes. Roughly 15 percent of flowering plant species accumulate fructans rather than, or in addition to, starch.

Humans lack enzymes to digest fructans, so they pass to the large intestine, where gut bacteria ferment them, producing gas and short-chain fatty acids. Inulin and fructooligosaccharides are used as prebiotic food ingredients and soluble fibre, and as sugar and fat replacers. Because of their fermentability, fructans are classed among the FODMAPs and can provoke symptoms in people with irritable bowel syndrome.
