Parables of Jesus

The parables of Jesus are short allegorical stories told by Jesus in the New Testament Gospels, forming a central and distinctive feature of his teaching. Found principally in the three Synoptic Gospels (Matthew, Mark, and Luke), the parables number more than thirty depending on how they are defined and counted. Drawing on everyday scenes of farming, household life, commerce, and masters and servants in first-century Galilee and Judea, they teach about the Kingdom of God, God's mercy, forgiveness, prayer, wealth, and readiness for judgement.

Notable parables

  • The Good Samaritan (Luke 10) — a despised Samaritan helps a wounded traveller, redefining "neighbour" as one who shows mercy.
  • The Prodigal Son (Luke 15) — a forgiving father welcomes back his lost son; paired with the parables of the Lost Sheep and the Lost Coin.
  • The Sower (Matthew 13; Mark 4; Luke 8) — seed falling on different soils pictures different responses to the word of God.
  • The Mustard Seed and the Leaven — the kingdom's small beginnings and great growth.
  • The Talents / Minas — servants entrusted with money picture stewardship and accountability.
  • The Pharisee and the Tax Collector — humility in prayer.
  • The Workers in the Vineyard, the Rich Fool, the Ten Virgins, the Sheep and the Goats, and the Wicked Tenants.

Interpretation

Early and medieval interpreters typically read the parables allegorically, finding a meaning in every detail. Modern scholarship, beginning with Adolf Jülicher in the late 19th century, argued each parable makes essentially one main point; later scholars such as C. H. Dodd and Joachim Jeremias stressed their eschatological urgency — the parables as announcements that God's reign was breaking into the present. Contemporary approaches also read them as provocative literary art that subverts expectations: heroes are Samaritans, shepherds leave ninety-nine sheep for one, and fathers run to embrace disgraced sons. The Gospels themselves sometimes give the parables a deliberately veiling function ("to those outside, everything comes in parables"), and several include interpretive explanations for the disciples.

Influence

The parables have profoundly shaped Western language and culture, giving expressions such as "good Samaritan," "prodigal son," "pearl of great price," "separating the sheep from the goats," and "the blind leading the blind," and they remain central texts in Christian preaching, ethics, and art.
