Salmonidae is a family of ray-finned fish, the only living family currently placed in the order Salmoniformes. It includes salmon (both Atlantic and Pacific species), trout, chars, freshwater whitefishes, and graylings, which collectively are known as the salmonids. The Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) gives the family and order their names.

Salmonids are native to the cool waters of the Northern Hemisphere but have been widely introduced elsewhere for aquaculture and sport fishing. Many species are anadromous, migrating from the sea to freshwater to spawn, although some complete their whole life cycle in fresh water.

Characteristics

Salmonids are slender, soft-finned fish with a small fleshy adipose fin on the back behind the dorsal fin — a hallmark of the family. They have small cycloid scales, a single row of sharp teeth on each jaw, and a lateral line. Body size ranges from a few centimetres in some whitefish relatives to over a metre and more than 30 kg in Chinook salmon and taimen.

Classification

The family is divided into three subfamilies:

  • Coregoninae — the whitefishes (genera Coregonus, Prosopium, Stenodus);
  • Thymallinae — the graylings (genus Thymallus);
  • Salmoninae — salmon, trout and chars (genera Salmo, Oncorhynchus, Salvelinus, Hucho, and relatives).

Together the family contains roughly 230 species. Molecular studies have reshaped the classification, moving the Pacific salmon and trout into Oncorhynchus and suggesting that several nominal genera intergrade.

Life cycle

Most salmonids spawn in cool, well-oxygenated gravel beds. Females excavate a nest, or redd, in which eggs are fertilised and buried. Alevins hatch with a yolk sac, later becoming free-swimming fry and parr. Anadromous species undergo smoltification — physiological changes preparing them for life in salt water — before migrating to the ocean, then returning to their natal streams to spawn. Pacific salmon of the genus Oncorhynchus are semelparous, dying after a single spawning, whereas Atlantic salmon may survive to spawn again.

Importance to humans

Salmonids support major commercial and recreational fisheries and are among the most important farmed fish worldwide; Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout aquaculture is a multi-billion-dollar industry centred on Norway, Chile, Canada, and Scotland. Wild stocks have declined because of habitat loss, dams, overfishing, and climate change, and many populations are protected under conservation legislation.
