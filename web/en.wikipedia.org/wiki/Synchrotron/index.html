Synchrotron

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Aerial photograph of a synchrotron facility: a large circular ring building]
A synchrotron light source from the air.

A synchrotron is a type of circular Particle accelerator in which the magnetic field bending the particle beam is synchronised (ramped) with the particles' increasing energy, keeping them on a fixed circular orbit while radiofrequency cavities accelerate them at each turn. Invented in 1944–45 (Vladimir Veksler in the USSR; Edwin McMillan in the US), it superseded the cyclotron for high energies and culminates (so far) in the Large Hadron Collider.

Synchrotrons have a second life as light sources: electrons bent in storage rings emit intense synchrotron radiation — a nuisance in colliders (energy loss) but a gift as the world's brightest X-ray sources, used by tens of thousands of researchers yearly for crystallography, materials science, chemistry and imaging. Facilities such as the ESRF (Grenoble), APS (Argonne), SPring-8 (Japan) and Diamond (UK) are user laboratories of whole scientific nations; fourth-generation diffraction-limited rings sharpen the beams further.

Principle

Unlike the cyclotron's constant field and spiralling orbit, the synchrotron holds radius constant by strengthening the dipole field as momentum rises, and maintains phase stability — particles oscillating stably around the synchronous phase (McMillan–Veksler) — which permits practically unlimited turns and energies, limited by magnet strength, ring size and (for electrons) synchrotron radiation losses scaling as E⁴/r.
