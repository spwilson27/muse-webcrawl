The Tsardom of Russia (Russian: Русское царство, Russkoye tsarstvo, later also Российское царство, Rossiyskoye tsarstvo) was the centralised Russian state from the assumption of the title of tsar by Ivan IV in 1547 until the foundation of the Russian Empire by Peter the Great in 1721. It grew out of the Grand Duchy of Moscow and became one of the largest states in the world, stretching from the Baltic and the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth in the west across Siberia to the Pacific Ocean in the east.

History

Ivan IV and expansion

Ivan IV ("the Terrible") was crowned tsar on 16 January 1547, a title asserting equality with emperors and independence from the Mongol-Tatar khanates. His reign saw the conquest of the khanates of Kazan (1552) and Astrakhan (1556), opening the Volga to Russian control, and the beginning of the conquest of Siberia with Yermak Timofeyevich's expedition against the Khanate of Sibir in the early 1580s. At home Ivan's later reign was marked by the Oprichnina, a reign of terror against the boyar aristocracy, and by the disastrous Livonian War (1558–1583) against a coalition including Poland–Lithuania and Sweden.

Time of Troubles

The extinction of the Rurik dynasty in 1598, with the death of Feodor I, plunged Russia into the Time of Troubles (1598–1613): famine, civil war, a succession of pretenders known as the False Dmitris, and Polish and Swedish intervention, including a Polish garrison in Moscow. The crisis ended when a national uprising under Kuzma Minin and Dmitry Pozharsky liberated Moscow, and the Zemsky Sobor elected Michael Romanov tsar in 1613, founding the Romanov dynasty, which ruled Russia until 1917.

The Romanovs and Siberia

Under the early Romanovs — Michael, Alexis, and Feodor III — Russia recovered, codified its laws in the Sobornoye Ulozhenie of 1649 (which finalised the institution of serfdom), fought wars with Poland–Lithuania that brought Smolensk and eastern Ukraine under Russian control after the Pereyaslav Agreement of 1654 with the Cossack Hetmanate, and endured the Old Believers' schism (Raskol) in the church. Russian explorers and Cossacks crossed Siberia with remarkable speed, reaching the Pacific by 1639; Semyon Dezhnev sailed through the Bering Strait in 1648, and by the 1650s Russians were on the Amur River, clashing with Qing China until the Treaty of Nerchinsk (1689) fixed the border.

Peter the Great

Peter I (reigned 1682–1725) transformed the tsardom: he built a regular army and a Baltic navy, founded Saint Petersburg in 1703, defeated Sweden in the Great Northern War (1700–1721), and reformed administration, the church, the calendar, dress, and taxation along Western lines. On 2 November 1721 (22 October, Old Style), following the Treaty of Nystad, the Senate conferred on Peter the titles of Emperor of All Russia, the Great, and Father of the Fatherland — the conventional end of the Tsardom and the beginning of the Russian Empire.

Government and society

The tsardom was an autocracy in which the tsar's power was theoretically unlimited, supported by the boyar Duma, the chancelleries (prikazy), and occasionally the Zemsky Sobor, an assembly of the land. Society was rigidly stratified, and by the mid-17th century the peasantry was bound in hereditary serfdom. The Russian Orthodox Church was a pillar of the state; after the reforms of Patriarch Nikon and the ensuing schism, and especially under Peter, it came firmly under state control.
