Nansen passports, originally called certificates of identity, were internationally recognised refugee travel documents issued by the League of Nations to stateless refugees following the First World War. They were devised in 1922 by Fridtjof Nansen, the League's first High Commissioner for Refugees, and are regarded as a landmark in the development of international refugee law and protection.

History

The upheavals of war, revolution, and the collapse of empires left millions displaced; among them were some 800,000 Russians made stateless when the Soviet government revoked the citizenship of émigrés in 1921. Without identity papers, refugees could not legally cross borders, work, or reside anywhere. The Nansen passport system, adopted by an intergovernmental conference in July 1922, allowed host governments to issue standardised identity certificates recognised eventually by more than fifty states. The scheme was later extended to Armenian, Assyrian, and other refugee groups fleeing persecution and genocide.

Use and legacy

An estimated 450,000 Nansen passports were issued, enabling refugees to travel, resettle, and rebuild their lives. Famous holders included the composer Igor Stravinsky, the painter Marc Chagall, and the dancer Anna Pavlova. The Nansen passport was the forerunner of the travel documents provided for by the 1951 Refugee Convention, and the Nansen International Office for Refugees received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1938 for its work.
