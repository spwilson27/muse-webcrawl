Imperial examination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The imperial examination, or keju, was a civil-service examination system in Imperial China designed to select the best potential candidates to serve as administrative officials. Originating under the Sui dynasty (conventionally 605 CE) and systematised under the Tang and Song, it endured, with interruptions, until 1905 — for roughly 1,300 years the chief ladder of social mobility in China and the model for Vietnam, Korea and (partly) Japan.

Candidates progressed through local, provincial and metropolitan examinations (juren, jinshi degrees) on the Confucian classics, in Ming and Qing times through the rigid “eight-legged essay” format; the highest could proceed to a palace examination presided over by the emperor. Success rates were tiny, degrees quasi-sacred, and the system shaped Chinese education, literature and family strategy. Historians debate its fairness (examinations were open in principle, but preparation favoured the wealthy) and its effects: it integrated the empire around a shared ideology and staffed a professional bureaucracy a millennium before Europe's merit services, while its classical curriculum arguably discouraged technical learning. It directly influenced the British and other Western civil-service reforms of the nineteenth century.
