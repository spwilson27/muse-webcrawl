Henry VIII

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Hans Holbein's famous portrait of Henry VIII in his later, massive majesty]

Henry VIII (28 June 1491 – 28 January 1547) was King of England from 1509 until his death, famous for his six marriages and for initiating the English Reformation — separating the Church of England from papal authority — in his pursuit of an annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon so that he might marry Anne Boleyn and father a male heir. His reign saw the dissolution of the monasteries, the union of England and Wales, the strengthening of the navy, and the executions of two of his queens, Thomas More, Thomas Cromwell and many others. He was succeeded by his son Edward VI.

Break with Rome

Convinced his marriage to his brother's widow Catherine was cursed (she had borne one surviving child, Mary, and no son), Henry sought an annulment the Pope, under the influence of Catherine's nephew Charles V, would not grant. Between 1532 and 1534 Parliament passed the Acts in Restraint of Appeals and of Supremacy, making Henry Supreme Head of the Church of England. Anne Boleyn, crowned in 1533, bore Elizabeth but no surviving son; she was executed in 1536 on charges of adultery and treason that most historians regard as fabricated. Jane Seymour gave Henry his son Edward and died in 1537; three further marriages (Anne of Cleves, annulled; Catherine Howard, executed 1542; Catherine Parr, who outlived him) followed.

Later reign and legacy

Under Thomas Cromwell the monasteries were dissolved (1536–1541) and their lands sold, reshaping English landownership; pilgrimages of protest (the Pilgrimage of Grace, 1536) were crushed. Henry's wars with France and Scotland consumed treasure; debasement of the coinage fuelled inflation. In popular memory he remains the archetypal tyrant-king; historians debate a charismatic Renaissance prince against a pathologically suspicious despot — and credit (or blame) his reign with launching England's Protestant future.
