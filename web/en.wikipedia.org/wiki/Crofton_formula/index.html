Crofton formula

The Crofton formula, named after Morgan Crofton (1826–1915), is a classic result of integral geometry relating the length of a curve to the expected number of times a "random" line intersects it.

Statement

Suppose a curve γ in the plane is rectifiable, and consider the set of oriented lines, each specified by the angle θ of its normal and its signed distance p from the origin. Let nγ(θ, p) be the number of times the corresponding line intersects γ. Then, with dp dθ as the invariant measure on the space of lines,

∫ nγ(θ, p) dp dθ = 2 × length(γ).

In other words, the integral of the intersection count over all lines equals twice the length of the curve. Crofton published the result in 1868 in his article "On the Theory of Local Probability" for the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society.

Applications and generalisations

The formula lets one measure lengths by counting intersections, an idea applied in stereology and materials science to estimate lengths and surface areas in samples. It generalises to higher dimensions and more general spaces: in the Poincaré–Crofton generalisation, integrals of intersection numbers recover volumes and areas of surfaces, and analogous formulae hold in hyperbolic and spherical geometry.
