School of Salamanca

The School of Salamanca was a Renaissance movement of Spanish scholastic theologians, jurists, and economists centred on the University of Salamanca in the 16th and early 17th centuries. Led initially by the Dominican Francisco de Vitoria, its members revitalized Thomist scholasticism and applied it to the new moral, legal, and economic problems posed by the Spanish conquest of the Americas and the expansion of commerce and empire.

Law and rights

Vitoria's lectures on the Indies questioned the legal titles by which Spain claimed the New World and defended the natural rights of indigenous peoples, who, he argued, possessed true dominion over their lands. Domingo de Soto, Martín de Azpilcueta, Tomás de Mercado, and later the Jesuit Francisco Suárez developed theories of natural law, international law (ius gentium), just war, and the rights of peoples that profoundly influenced later European legal thought, including Hugo Grotius.

Economics

The Salamanca scholars made pioneering contributions to economic theory: they articulated a subjective theory of value, analyzed money and inflation in connection with the influx of American silver, defended private property and free trade, and formulated an early version of the quantity theory of money. For these reasons they are often regarded as forerunners of classical and Austrian economics.
