Patter

Patter is a prepared and practiced speech that is designed to accustom listeners to a particular mode of speech or production, most familiarly the rapid-fire, glib talk of magicians, showmen, auctioneers, carnival barkers, and salespeople. The word derives from the hurried, repetitive mumbling of prayers — patter comes from pater, as in the Pater Noster, recited quickly and mechanically.

A magician's patter guides the audience's attention while the method stays hidden.

In magic

In conjuring, patter is the running commentary that accompanies a trick. It serves several purposes: it entertains, builds a story around the effect, misdirects attention from secret moves, and paces the performance so that crucial actions pass unnoticed in a stream of talk. Magicians often script and rehearse their patter as carefully as their sleights, and trade literature publishes ready-made patter for standard effects.

Patter songs

The term is also known from the patter song, a comic song form characterized by a rapid-fire delivery of tongue-twisting lyrics sung at high speed. The form is most associated with the operettas of Gilbert and Sullivan — "I am the very model of a modern Major-General" from The Pirates of Penzance is the archetype — and with the earlier comic songs of music hall. Stephen Sondheim and other modern writers for musical theatre have used patter songs to display verbal virtuosity and comic agitation.

Related senses

By extension, "patter" can mean the specialized jargon or cant of a trade, and in slang, "the patter" was the flash language of thieves and showmen recorded in 19th-century glossaries. Glasgow dialect is also famously nicknamed "the patter." In performance more generally, any smooth, persuasive, and continuous talk — from a stand-up comedian's set-ups to a street vendor's pitch — may be described as patter.
