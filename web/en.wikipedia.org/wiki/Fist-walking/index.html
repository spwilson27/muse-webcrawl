Fist-walking is a form of quadrupedal locomotion in which an animal walks with its body weight supported on the backs of its clenched fingers rather than on its palms or knuckles. It is the characteristic terrestrial gait of orangutans, whose extremely long, curved fingers — adapted for grasping branches — make palm-down walking awkward.

Description

In fist-walking the hand is closed into a loose fist and weight is borne on the dorsal surface of the middle phalanges, with the wrist held in a neutral or slightly flexed position. The gait is typically slow and deliberate, used for short distances on the ground or along large branches, since orangutans are predominantly arboreal.

Comparison with knuckle-walking

African apes — chimpanzees, bonobos, and gorillas — practise knuckle-walking, supporting weight on the middle phalanges with the fingers extended rather than clenched. The two gaits differ in wrist posture and in which part of the hand contacts the ground: fist-walkers curl the fingers fully, keeping the long digits folded out of the way, whereas knuckle-walkers keep them semi-extended. Fist-walking also occurs occasionally in captive apes and, in a convergent form, in giant anteaters, which walk on their knuckles/fists to protect their large digging claws.

In orangutans

Orangutans spend most of their lives in trees, clambering with all four limbs in a varied repertoire sometimes called "quadrumanous scrambling." On the ground — males travel terrestrially more often than females — they fist-walk with a rolling, unhurried gait. Their hand anatomy, with a reduced thumb and elongated fingers, reflects a trade-off favouring hook-like grips over terrestrial support.

Evolutionary significance

Whether the last common ancestor of great apes was a knuckle-walker, and whether human bipedalism evolved from a knuckle-walking ancestor, are long-running debates in palaeoanthropology. Fist-walking in orangutans demonstrates that the great-ape hand has evolved several distinct solutions to moving a heavy body over the ground, cautioning against treating knuckle-walking as a single primitive condition.
