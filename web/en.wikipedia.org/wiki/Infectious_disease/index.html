Infectious disease

Infectious diseases, also known as transmissible or communicable diseases, are illnesses caused by pathogenic microorganisms — bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites or prions — that can be transmitted, directly or indirectly, from one person or animal to another. Infections may be asymptomatic, mild, or life-threatening; they remain among the leading causes of death worldwide, particularly in low-income settings.

Transmission

Pathogens spread by several routes: direct contact, respiratory droplets and aerosols, contaminated food and water, fomites, blood and body fluids, sexual contact, and vectors such as mosquitoes and ticks. The chain of infection links a reservoir, a portal of exit, a mode of transmission, a portal of entry and a susceptible host.

Prevention and control

Key measures include vaccination, sanitation and clean water, hand hygiene, safe food handling, vector control, antimicrobial stewardship, screening and isolation of infectious cases. Germ theory, established in the 19th century by figures such as Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch, underpins modern infection control; Koch's postulates formalised the causal link between a microbe and a disease.

Treatment

Bacterial infections are treated with antibiotics, viral infections with antivirals where available, fungal infections with antifungals, and parasitic diseases with antiparasitic drugs. Antimicrobial resistance is a growing global threat.

Burden

Major infectious diseases include respiratory infections, HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, malaria, diarrhoeal diseases and hepatitis. Epidemics and pandemics — such as the 1918 influenza pandemic and COVID-19 — have shaped history and driven the development of public health.
