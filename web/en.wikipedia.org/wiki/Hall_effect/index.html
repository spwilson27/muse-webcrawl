Hall effect

The Hall effect is the production of a potential difference (the Hall voltage) across an electrical conductor, transverse to an electric current in the conductor and to an applied magnetic field perpendicular to the current. It was discovered by Edwin Hall in 1879 while he was working on his doctoral thesis at Johns Hopkins University.

Explanation

When a current-carrying conductor is placed in a magnetic field perpendicular to the current, the Lorentz force deflects the charge carriers to one side of the conductor. Charge accumulates until the resulting electric field balances the magnetic force, producing a measurable transverse voltage. The sign of the Hall voltage reveals whether the dominant charge carriers are negative (electrons) or positive (holes), and its magnitude allows the carrier density to be determined: VH = IB / (nqt), where I is the current, B the magnetic field, n the carrier density, q the carrier charge and t the thickness.

Applications and variants

Hall effect sensors are widely used for proximity sensing, position and speed measurement, current sensing and in brushless DC motors. Related phenomena include the quantum Hall effect, discovered in 1980, in which the Hall resistance of a two-dimensional electron system is quantised in exact steps — an effect so precise it is used to define electrical resistance standards — and the anomalous and spin Hall effects.
