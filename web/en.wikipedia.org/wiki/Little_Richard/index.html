Richard Wayne Penniman (December 5, 1932 – May 9, 2020), known professionally as Little Richard, was an American singer, pianist, and songwriter. He was an influential figure in popular music and culture for seven decades. Described as the "Architect of Rock and Roll", Richard's most celebrated work dates from the mid-1950s, when his charismatic showmanship and dynamic music, characterized by frenetic piano playing, pounding backbeat and powerful raspy singing voice, laid the foundation for rock and roll.

Career

Born into a devout family in Macon, Georgia, Penniman sang gospel from childhood and was influenced by Sister Rosetta Tharpe and the boogie-woogie pianist Esquerita. After winning a talent contest he recorded for RCA Victor and Peacock Records with modest success. His breakthrough came in 1955 at Specialty Records: "Tutti Frutti", with its explosive opening cry of "A-wop-bop-a-loo-bop-a-wop-bam-boom!", became an instant hit. A string of classics followed — "Long Tall Sally", "Rip It Up", "Lucille", "Jenny, Jenny", "Keep A-Knockin'" and "Good Golly, Miss Molly" — selling millions and influencing virtually every rock musician who followed.

In 1957, at the height of his fame, Richard abruptly quit rock and roll during a tour of Australia, interpreting a fireball in the sky (the launch of Sputnik 1) as a sign from God. He enrolled in Bible college, was ordained a minister, and recorded gospel music. He returned to secular performing in 1962, touring Britain with the young Beatles and Rolling Stones among his devoted fans and opening acts. Later decades brought continued touring, film and television appearances, and widespread recognition of his foundational role.

Legacy

Little Richard was among the first class of inductees into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1986. His music and stagecraft shaped Elvis Presley, the Beatles, James Brown, Prince and countless others; his flamboyant, androgynous style also helped open popular music to new expressions of identity. He died of bone cancer on 9 May 2020.
