Word formation

In linguistics, word formation is the creation of a new word, in the sense of both "newly created (coined) in a language" and "newly extracted from an existing word". Word formation is sometimes contrasted with semantic change, which is a change in a single word's meaning. The boundary between word formation and semantic change can be difficult to define: a new use of an old word can be seen as a new word derived from an old one.

Types of word formation

Derivation

Derivation builds new words by adding affixes to existing forms: happy → unhappy, happiness. Derivational affixes may change a word's part of speech or its meaning, unlike inflectional affixes, which mark grammatical features without creating a new lexeme. See affix and morphology.

Compounding

Compounding combines two or more free morphemes into a new word, as in blackboard, toothpaste and keyboard. Compounds may be written solid, hyphenated or open, and their meaning is not always fully predictable from their parts.

Conversion

Conversion (zero derivation) shifts a word to a new word class without any change of form: the noun email becomes the verb to email.

Blending and clipping

A blend fuses parts of two words, as smog (smoke + fog) and brunch (breakfast + lunch). Clipping shortens a word without changing its meaning: influenza → flu, advertisement → ad. Acronyms and initialisms are formed from the initial letters of phrases, such as laser and NATO.

Back-formation and borrowing

Back-formation creates a shorter word by removing a supposed affix, as edit from editor. Languages also acquire new words by borrowing from other languages, and by coinage — the outright invention of a term, often for a brand or product name.
