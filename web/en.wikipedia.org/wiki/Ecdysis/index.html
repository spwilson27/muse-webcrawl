Ecdysis

Ecdysis
Also calledMoulting (of the cuticle)

Ecdysis is the moulting of the cuticle in many invertebrate animals of the clade Ecdysozoa — including arthropods (insects, crustaceans, arachnids, myriapods), nematodes, tardigrades, onychophorans, and several smaller groups. Because the rigid external cuticle cannot grow, the animal must periodically shed it and replace it with a new, larger one in order to increase in size; the discarded cuticle is called the exuviae. The term derives from the Greek ekdysis, "stripping off."

The process

Ecdysis unfolds in stages. In the pre-moult phase (apolysis), the epidermis detaches from the old cuticle and secretes a new cuticle beneath it, while moulting fluid digests and recycles much of the old inner cuticle. The animal then splits and escapes from the remaining old shell — the act of ecdysis proper — often by swallowing air or water to swell the body. Afterwards, the soft new cuticle expands to a larger size and hardens (sclerotises) over hours or days. Animals are especially vulnerable to predators during and just after moulting, and many hide or cease feeding at this time.

Hormonal control

In arthropods, ecdysis is orchestrated by steroid hormones called ecdysteroids, principally ecdysone and its active form 20-hydroxyecdysone, released in response to prothoracicotropic hormone from the brain; juvenile hormone determines whether a moult produces another juvenile stage or an adult. In insects, a cascade of peptide hormones — ecdysis-triggering hormone and eclosion hormone — coordinates the stereotyped motor programme that frees the animal from its old cuticle. The number of moults varies: many insects moult four to eight times, while some crustaceans continue moulting throughout life.
