Quantum teleportation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of the quantum teleportation protocol: an entangled pair, a Bell measurement, and classical communication]
The teleportation protocol transfers a state using entanglement plus two classical bits.

Quantum teleportation is a technique for transferring the quantum state of a system to another location using Quantum entanglement and classical communication. Proposed in 1993 by Charles Bennett and five co-authors, and first demonstrated with photons in 1997 (by Anton Zeilinger's and Francesco De Martini's groups), teleportation does not transport matter or energy, nor does it permit faster-than-light communication: the transferred information is useless until two classical bits from the sender arrive.

In the protocol, Alice and Bob share an entangled pair. Alice performs a joint (Bell-state) measurement on the unknown state and her half of the pair — destroying the original state — and sends Bob the two-bit result; Bob applies one of four corrections to his particle, which thereby assumes the unknown state exactly. The no-cloning theorem guarantees the original is destroyed in the process, so teleportation never copies a state. Extensions of the protocol underpin quantum repeaters, the proposed quantum internet, and fault-tolerant quantum computing schemes; teleportation has been demonstrated over metropolitan fibre networks and, via the Micius satellite, over 1,400 km ground-to-space.

Demonstrations

Teleportation has been achieved with photons, trapped ions, superconducting qubits, and between light and matter; in 2020–2023, teleportation between non-neighbouring nodes of small quantum networks was demonstrated in Delft and elsewhere. Each is a prerequisite technology for distributing entanglement — the resource on which long-distance quantum communication depends.
