War of the League of Cambrai

The War of the League of Cambrai (1508–1516), sometimes known as the War of the Holy League after 1511, was fought during the Italian Wars. The League of Cambrai was an anti-Venetian alliance formed in December 1508 by Pope Julius II, Louis XII of France, Emperor Maximilian I, and Ferdinand II of Aragon to curb the expansion of the Republic of Venice in northern Italy. After Venice's mainland empire was shattered, the alliance dissolved into shifting coalitions — Pope Julius turned against France with a "Holy League" that included his former Venetian enemies — until peace settlements in 1516 restored a version of the status quo, confirming French control of Milan and Venetian survival.

Background

Venice had expanded aggressively on the Italian mainland (Terraferma), alarming the Papacy and the great powers. Julius II, the "warrior pope", brokered the League of Cambrai (10 December 1508) agreeing a partition of Venetian territory: Romagna to the Pope, Milan's claims to France, Verona and the Veneto ports to the Emperor, and Apulian ports to Spain.

Course of the war

France crushed the Venetian army at Agnadello (14 May 1509); Venice lost almost all its mainland possessions within weeks but recovered Padua and held out through siege, while diplomacy detached the Pope and Spain from the league. Alarmed by French dominance, Julius formed the Holy League (1511) with Venice, Spain, England, and Swiss mercenaries. France won bloodily at Ravenna (1512), where its commander Gaston de Foix was killed, but was expelled from Milan after the Swiss victory at Novara (1513) — the "battle of the giants". England invaded northern France (Battle of the Spurs, 1513) while Scotland's intervention ended disastrously at Flodden. On Julius's death (1513) negotiations stalled until Francis I of France won the great victory of Marignano (13–14 September 1515) over the Swiss, recovering Milan.

Outcome

The treaties of Noyon and Brussels (1516) and the Concordat of Bologna settled the war: France kept Milan, Venice regained most of its Terraferma, and the Swiss cantons gained lasting exemptions and a perpetual peace with France. The war demonstrated both the military supremacy of combined-arms armies and the limits of coalition warfare driven by competing territorial appetites.
