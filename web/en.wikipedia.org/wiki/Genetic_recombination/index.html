Genetic recombination

Genetic recombination is the exchange of genetic material between different molecules of DNA, or between different parts of the same molecule, producing combinations of alleles that differ from those found in either parent. It is a fundamental source of genetic diversity in offspring and a key mechanism of DNA repair.

Types

Homologous recombination occurs between similar or identical DNA sequences. In eukaryotes it takes place during meiosis as chromosomal crossover between paired homologous chromosomes, and it also repairs double-strand breaks. Site-specific recombination, by contrast, occurs at short specific sequences and is catalyzed by enzymes such as integrases; non-homologous recombination can join unrelated sequences and may cause translocations.

Molecular mechanism

In the Holliday model, recombination begins with a break in one DNA molecule, strand invasion of a homologous duplex, formation of a Holliday junction, and its resolution by endonucleases. Enzymes such as RecA in bacteria and Rad51 in eukaryotes mediate the search for homology and strand exchange.

Applications

Recombination frequency between genes underlies genetic linkage mapping, measured in centimorgans. In biotechnology, homologous recombination enables gene targeting and genome editing, while site-specific systems such as Cre–loxP are used to manipulate DNA in living organisms.
