Google Maps

Google Maps is a web mapping platform and consumer application offered by Google. It offers satellite imagery, aerial photography, street maps, 360° interactive panoramic views of streets (Street View), real-time traffic conditions, and route planning for traveling by foot, car, bike, air (in beta) and public transportation. As of 2020, Google Maps was being used by over one billion people every month around the world.

History

Acquisitions and launch

Google Maps began as a C++ desktop program at Where 2 Technologies, a Sydney-based company founded by brothers Lars and Jens Eilstrup Rasmussen and others. In October 2004, the company was acquired by Google, which converted it into a web application. After additional acquisitions of a geospatial data visualization company and a real-time traffic analyzer, Google Maps was launched in February 2005. The service's front end utilizes JavaScript, XML, and Ajax. Google Maps first conformed to the then-common practice of letting users pan and zoom continuously over a tiled map — a design that made it far faster and smoother than competing services such as MapQuest.

Growth and features

Google announced satellite imagery in April 2005, and launched Google Earth as a companion desktop product later that year. In 2007, Google introduced Street View, offering street-level panoramic photographs in five U.S. cities, a feature that has since expanded to thousands of locations worldwide. A mobile application for Android followed in 2008, and an iOS version in 2012 after Apple replaced Google Maps with its own mapping app as the default on iOS 6 — an episode that became a notable controversy.

Google's acquisition of the social navigation company Waze in 2013 added crowdsourced incident reporting. In 2018, Google merged its mapping APIs into the Google Maps Platform and restructured pricing for commercial users. In August 2018, Google Maps rolled out a 3D globe view, projecting the map onto a spherical model rather than a flat Mercator projection at small scales.

Recent developments

In the 2020s Google added features including augmented-reality navigation (Live View), eco-friendly routing options, information about COVID-19-related restrictions during the pandemic, and increasingly detailed transit and cycling layers. In 2023 Google began rolling out "Immersive View," which combines aerial and Street View imagery into photorealistic 3D models of cities and landmarks. The service also began integrating generative AI features for local discovery.

Features

  • Route planning: directions for driving, walking, cycling and public transit, with real-time traffic and estimated arrival times.
  • Street View: panoramic, street-level imagery collected by cars, trekkers and other platforms.
  • Business listings: Google Business Profiles provide hours, reviews, photos and contact information for local businesses.
  • Offline maps: users can download regions for use without a connection.
  • Location sharing and timeline: optional sharing of real-time location and a history of places visited.
  • Google Maps Platform: APIs for embedding maps, geocoding, places and routes in third-party applications, widely used by ride-hailing, delivery and real-estate services.

Data and imagery

Google Maps combines data from numerous sources, including government agencies, commercial providers such as those supplying satellite imagery, and user contributions via community editing programs. The service formerly allowed user edits through Google Map Maker (discontinued in 2017), and now accepts contributions through the app and the Local Guides program. Road data accuracy varies by country, and the depiction of disputed borders may differ depending on the user's location, in line with local laws.

Criticism and controversies

Street View raised privacy concerns from its launch, prompting Google to automatically blur faces and license plates. The service's collection of Wi-Fi network data by Street View cars led to regulatory investigations and fines in several countries. Google Maps has also been involved in disputes over place names and international borders, and navigation errors have occasionally led drivers into hazardous situations. The 2012 switch by Apple to its own maps, which were initially error-prone, briefly drove renewed adoption of the Google Maps iOS app.
