Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer Studios Inc. v. Grokster, Ltd., 545 U.S. 913 (2005), is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision in which the Court unanimously held that defendant peer-to-peer file-sharing companies Grokster and StreamCast (maker of Morpheus) could be sued for inducing copyright infringement for acts of infringement by third parties using their software.

Background

A group of 28 entertainment companies, led by Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, sued Grokster and StreamCast, whose free software allowed users to share files — overwhelmingly copyrighted music and films — over peer-to-peer networks. The plaintiffs argued the defendants should be secondarily liable for users' infringement. The defendants relied on Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios (1984), the "Betamax case", which held that a technology capable of substantial noninfringing uses cannot give rise to contributory liability merely from its distribution. The District Court and the Ninth Circuit ruled for the defendants, finding their decentralised software had substantial noninfringing uses and that the companies had no actual knowledge of specific infringements at a time they could act on it.

Decision

Justice David Souter's unanimous opinion introduced and applied the inducement rule: "one who distributes a device with the object of promoting its use to infringe copyright, as shown by clear expression or other affirmative steps taken to foster infringement, is liable for the resulting acts of infringement by third parties." The Court found substantial evidence of inducement: the companies had advertised to former Napster users, made no effort to develop filtering tools, and built advertising-based business models whose revenue grew with infringing traffic. The Court stressed that mere knowledge that a product could be misused, or ordinary acts incident to product distribution, were not enough; the Sony safe harbor was left intact. Separate concurrences by Justices Ginsburg and Breyer disagreed about the strength of the evidence and the scope of Sony's protection.

Aftermath

On remand, the district court found Grokster liable and issued a permanent injunction; Grokster settled for $50 million and shut down its service in November 2005. StreamCast litigated further and also eventually shut down. The decision is regarded as a defining statement of secondary liability in copyright law, balancing the protection of copyright against the risk of chilling technological innovation.
