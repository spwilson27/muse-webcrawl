Paul Sérusier (9 November 1864 – 7 October 1927) was a French painter who was a pioneer of abstract art and an inspiration for the avant-garde Nabis movement, Synthetism and Cloisonnism.

Life and work

Sérusier was born in Paris to a prosperous family and studied at the Académie Julian. In the summer of 1888 he travelled to Pont-Aven in Brittany, where he worked under the supervision of Paul Gauguin. On a cigar-box lid, in the Bois d'Amour, he painted a small landscape following Gauguin's radical advice to use pure, unmixed colour — "How do you see these trees? They are yellow. So, put in yellow." Returning to Paris, Sérusier showed the little panel to his fellow students, who excitedly adopted it as their "Talisman"; it became the founding icon of the Nabis ("prophets") group that formed around Sérusier, Maurice Denis, Pierre Bonnard, Édouard Vuillard and others.

He spent long periods in Brittany, drawn to its archaic religious culture, and painted Breton landscapes and peasant scenes in flat areas of vivid colour. He developed an interest in theosophy and esoteric thought, studied the colour and proportion theories of the Benedictine monks of Beuron, and late in life published ABC de la peinture (1921), a short treatise on his theories. From 1921 he taught at the Académie Ranson. He died in Morlaix in 1927.

Legacy

The Talisman (1888), now in the Musée d'Orsay, is regarded as one of the earliest manifestos of abstract painting, and Sérusier's role as the transmitter of Gauguin's Synthetist ideas to the Parisian avant-garde secured his place in the history of modern art.
