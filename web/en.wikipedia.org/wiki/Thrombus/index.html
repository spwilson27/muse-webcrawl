Thrombus

Thrombus
SpecialtyVascular medicine, haematology

A thrombus (plural thrombi), colloquially a blood clot, is the final product of blood coagulation in haemostasis: a solid or semi-solid mass formed from the constituents of the blood within a living blood vessel or the heart. It consists of platelets and red blood cells enmeshed in cross-linked fibrin. The process of its formation is called thrombosis. A thrombus that breaks free and travels is an embolus.

Formation

Thrombosis is promoted by the three factors of Virchow's triad: endothelial injury, abnormal blood flow (stasis or turbulence), and hypercoagulability. Arterial thrombi, formed under high flow, are rich in platelets ("white thrombi"); venous thrombi, formed in stasis, are rich in red cells ("red thrombi"). Microscopically, alternating pale and dark layers — the lines of Zahn — mark formation in flowing blood and distinguish a thrombus from a post-mortem clot.

Outcomes

A thrombus may resolve by fibrinolysis, organise and recanalise, propagate, or embolise. Depending on location, thrombosis causes deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, or stroke. Prevention and treatment use anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, thrombolytics, thrombectomy, and compression, according to the site and cause.
