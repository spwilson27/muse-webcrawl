NURBS

Non-uniform rational B-splines (NURBS) are a mathematical model for representing curves and surfaces in computer graphics, computer-aided design (CAD) and manufacturing. By generalising both B-splines and Bézier curves, NURBS can represent exactly both free-form shapes — such as car body panels — and analytic shapes such as circles, spheres and surfaces of revolution.

Concepts

A NURBS curve is defined by a set of weighted control points, a knot vector and a degree. The non-uniform spacing of knots allows local control and the insertion of sharp features; the rational weights attached to control points pull the curve toward or push it away from them and are what make exact conic sections possible: with suitable weights a NURBS curve reproduces a circular arc precisely, something a polynomial B-spline cannot do. NURBS surfaces extend the construction to two parameters, typically rendered as a grid of control points.

History and use

The mathematics descends from work on splines and from Pierre Bézier's and Paul de Casteljau's systems at French car makers in the 1960s, unified and generalised by researchers including Kenneth Versprille in the 1970s. NURBS became the standard geometry of industrial CAD kernels and exchange formats such as IGES and STEP, and are supported in graphics APIs and packages from AutoCAD and Rhino to Maya and Blender. Designers manipulate surfaces indirectly by moving control points, changing weights or inserting knots, while the rendered surface passes near — though generally not through — the control polygon.

Properties

NURBS offer local modification (moving one control point affects only a neighbourhood), smoothness adjustable by degree and knot multiplicity, and affine invariance. Their main drawbacks are the storage and computation overhead of weights and the difficulty of operations such as trimming and joining surfaces — problems addressed in research by alternative representations such as T-splines and subdivision surfaces.
