The Tuileries Palace (French: Palais des Tuileries) was a royal and imperial palace in Paris which stood on the right bank of the River Seine, next to the Louvre. It was the usual Parisian residence of most French monarchs, from Henry IV to Napoleon III, until it was burned by the Paris Commune in 1871. Its name came from the tile factories (tuileries) that had previously occupied the site.

History

Building began in 1564 under Catherine de' Medici to designs by Philibert de l'Orme, and the palace was extended over the following centuries until its façade stretched some 266 metres, closing the western end of the Louvre courtyard. Louis XIV resided there while Versailles was being built; Louis XVI was brought there from Versailles in October 1789 and the palace was stormed by a mob on 10 August 1792, ending the monarchy. Napoleon I made it his principal residence, as did the restored Bourbons, Louis-Philippe, and Napoleon III.

Destruction and aftermath

On 23 May 1871, during the suppression of the Paris Commune, retreating Communard forces set the palace ablaze; the fire gutted the building, leaving only the shell. After years of debate the ruins were demolished in 1883, leaving the Tuileries Garden open to the Louvre's Cour Napoléon. Only the Pavillon de Flore and Pavillon de Marsan survive. Proposals to rebuild the palace have been advanced periodically, notably by a national committee formed in 2003, but none has been adopted.
