Sign (linguistics)

For Saussure, the sign unites a signifier with a signified.

In linguistics and semiotics, a sign is something that stands for something else and carries meaning within a system of communication. The modern theory of the sign derives chiefly from Ferdinand de Saussure's Course in General Linguistics (1916) and, in a different form, from the philosophy of Charles Sanders Peirce. The linguistic sign is the basic unit of langue, the shared system underlying individual speech.

Saussure's dyadic model

Saussure defined the sign as the union of a signifier (the sound-image, or form) and a signified (the concept). Neither component exists independently in the sign; they are like the two sides of a sheet of paper. Crucially, the bond between them is arbitrary: there is no natural reason why the sequence of sounds in tree should denote that concept, as the variety of the world's languages shows. Signs acquire value not from any intrinsic content but from their differences from other signs in the system.

Peirce's triadic model

Peirce defined a sign as a three-way relation between a representamen (the sign vehicle), an object, and an interpretant (the effect or further sign it generates). He classified signs as icons (related by resemblance), indices (related by causal or spatiotemporal connection, like smoke for fire) and symbols (related by convention). This typology, extended by Charles Morris and others, underlies modern semiotics.

In sign languages

In the linguistics of sign languages, "sign" simply denotes the lexical unit — the manual equivalent of a word — analysed into parameters such as handshape, location, movement and orientation, as first demonstrated by William Stokoe.
