The Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) is a self-report questionnaire that assigns people to one of sixteen personality types according to their stated preferences on four dichotomies. Developed by Katharine Cook Briggs and her daughter Isabel Briggs Myers from the ideas of Carl Jung, it became one of the world's most widely used personality instruments in business, education and counselling, though its scientific validity is disputed by many psychologists.

The four dichotomies

The questionnaire sorts preferences along four axes: Extraversion (E) – Introversion (I), the direction of one's energy and attention; Sensing (S) – Intuition (N), how one takes in information (concrete facts versus patterns and possibilities); Thinking (T) – Feeling (F), how one makes decisions (logic versus values); and Judging (J) – Perceiving (P), how one organises life (structure versus spontaneity). The four letters combine into 16 types, each described in the MBTI literature with characteristic strengths and occupations. An elaborated version of the theory adds a hierarchy of eight "cognitive functions" (e.g. introverted intuition) for each type.

History and use

Briggs began classifying personalities after reading Jung in the 1920s; Myers developed the pencil-and-paper indicator during the Second World War, hoping to help women entering the workforce find suitable jobs. The Educational Testing Service published it in 1962, and from 1975 Consulting Psychologists Press (now The Myers-Briggs Company) marketed it commercially. An estimated two million people take it each year, and it has long been used by corporations, universities and government agencies for team-building and career counselling.

Criticism

Academic assessments are largely critical. The test dichotomises traits that are normally distributed (most people score near the middle), so small changes flip types: studies find a large fraction of test-takers receive a different type on retesting after only weeks. Reviews (including a 1991 US National Academy of Sciences committee report and analyses by Pittenger and others) find insufficient evidence that MBTI scores predict job performance, and professional guidelines discourage its use in hiring. Defenders respond that the instrument is meant as a framework for self-understanding, not clinical diagnosis, and note that its continuous scores correlate substantially with four of the Big Five dimensions. The type descriptions' generality also exposes them to the Barnum effect.
