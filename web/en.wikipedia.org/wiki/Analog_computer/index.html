Analog computer

An analog computer or analogue computer is a type of computation machine (computer) that uses physical phenomena such as electrical, mechanical, or hydraulic quantities behaving according to the mathematical principles in question (analog signals) to model the problem being solved. In contrast, digital computers represent varying quantities symbolically and by discrete values of both time and amplitude (digital signals).

Analog computers were widely used in scientific and industrial applications between the 1950s and 1970s.

Analog computers were once widely used in scientific and industrial applications because, at the time, they were faster than digital computers. They became less common after the introduction of digital computers, although as of 2023 they remain in use in some specialised applications, such as flight simulators and university teaching. Complex mechanisms using gears and levers, such as planimeters and slide rules, made early contributions to analog computation, as did mechanical integrators and differential analysers.

History

The Antikythera mechanism, an orrery dating from about 100 BC, is regarded as one of the earliest analog computers. The slide rule, invented around 1620–1630, performed multiplication and division by adding and subtracting lengths. In 1876 James Thomson described the mechanical integrator, and his brother Lord Kelvin built machines based on it. Vannevar Bush completed the first large-scale general-purpose mechanical differential analyser at MIT in 1931. Electronic analog computers using operational amplifiers flourished after the Second World War, with companies such as Electronic Associates Inc. producing large patch-panel systems used for simulation of aircraft, missiles, and industrial processes.

Components

Key components of electronic analog computers include operational amplifiers configured as summers, integrators, and coefficient potentiometers; function generators for nonlinear functions; and multipliers. Programs were set up by interconnecting components on a removable patch panel, and results were read from voltmeters, chart recorders, or oscilloscopes.

Advantages and limitations

Analog computers solve differential equations continuously and in parallel, making them naturally suited to real-time simulation. Their accuracy, typically around 0.1 percent, is limited by component tolerances, drift, and noise, whereas digital computers offer arbitrary precision. Hybrid computers combined analog speed with digital logic and memory during the transition era.
