Sociology of science

The sociology of science is a subfield of sociology concerned with the social conditions and effects of science, and with the social structures and processes of scientific activity. It examines how social factors shape scientific knowledge, institutions, careers, and the distribution of credit among scientists.

History

Robert K. Merton is widely considered the founder of the field. His work in the 1930s–1970s identified the normative structure of science — communalism, universalism, disinterestedness, and organised scepticism (CUDOS) — and phenomena such as the Matthew effect, by which eminent scientists receive disproportionate credit. Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962) influenced later work by emphasising paradigms and scientific communities.

Approaches

From the 1970s, the sociology of scientific knowledge (SSK) and the "strong programme" associated with David Bloor and Barry Barnes argued that scientific beliefs themselves, true and false alike, require sociological explanation. Laboratory studies by Bruno Latour, Steve Woolgar, and Karin Knorr Cetina examined science in the making, while Harry Collins studied expertise and replication. Feminist and postcolonial scholars have analysed how gender and power shape research priorities and authority.

Topics

Major topics include scientific careers and reward systems, citation and bibliometrics, peer review, scientific controversies, the organisation of disciplines, and science policy.
