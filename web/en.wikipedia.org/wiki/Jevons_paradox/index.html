Jevons paradox

Jevons paradox

In economics, the Jevons paradox (sometimes the Jevons effect) occurs when technological progress increases the efficiency with which a resource is used (reducing the amount necessary for any one use), but the falling effective cost of using the resource increases its overall demand, causing total resource consumption to rise rather than fall. Governments and environmentalists often assume efficiency gains will lower resource consumption, ignoring the possibility of the paradox arising.

In 1865, the English economist William Stanley Jevons observed that technological improvements that increased the efficiency of coal use led to the increased consumption of coal in a wide range of industries. He argued that, contrary to common intuition, technological progress could not be relied upon to reduce fuel consumption.

History

Jevons set out the argument in The Coal Question (1865), written amid anxiety that Britain's coal reserves would soon be exhausted. Observing that James Watt's steam engine — far more efficient than Newcomen's — had been followed not by falling but by soaring coal demand, Jevons wrote that "it is a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth." Cheaper steam power made steam profitable in new applications (railways, shipping, iron smelting), dwarfing the per-engine saving.

The idea was revived in the 1980s by Daniel Khazzoom and Leonard Brookes, and formalised by Harry Saunders (1992), who showed that microeconomic models with neoclassical growth readily produce "backfire."

Mechanism and the rebound effect

An efficiency improvement lowers the implicit price of a service (lighting, transport, computing). Consumers respond by using more of the service (direct rebound), spending the money saved on other goods (indirect rebound), and the wider economy adjusts through growth and new applications (economy-wide effects). When the total rebound exceeds 100%, consumption rises — "backfire," the Jevons paradox proper. Empirical estimates of direct rebound for household energy services in developed economies are often in the range of 10–30%, so full backfire is uncommon at the micro level, though economy-wide estimates are higher and more contested.

Historical cases frequently cited include lighting (each cheaper lumen technology — candles, kerosene, gas, incandescent, fluorescent, LED — coinciding with more total light consumption), aviation fuel efficiency, irrigation efficiency sometimes increasing total water abstraction, and computing, where efficiency gains have accompanied explosive growth in computation and energy demand.

Khazzoom–Brookes postulate

The Khazzoom–Brookes postulate, developed independently by Daniel Khazzoom and Leonard Brookes in the 1980s during debates over energy-efficiency standards, holds that energy-efficiency improvements at the microeconomic level tend to increase energy consumption at the macroeconomic level. Saunders subsequently named and analysed it, linking it with the Jevons paradox. The postulate is invoked in arguments that efficiency standards alone cannot decarbonise an economy and must be paired with pricing or supply constraints.

Evidence and debate

Reviews of empirical studies generally find partial rebound rather than full backfire for most end-uses in wealthy economies, with direct effects typically well under 100%; however, for energy services with unsatisfied demand in developing economies, rebound can be large, and economy-wide general-equilibrium studies report wide ranges. Critics of the paradox's policy use note that falling consumption of specific resources (e.g., coal in some countries) has been achieved when efficiency combined with prices, regulation, and substitutes. The topic remains actively debated in ecological economics and energy policy.

Policy implications

If rebound is significant, efficiency policy alone delivers less saving than engineering estimates predict. Proposed responses pair efficiency with carbon pricing, caps, or resource taxes — the approach Jevons himself gestured toward (he discussed taxes on coal) — and prioritise absolute emission limits over intensity targets, so that efficiency gains translate into lower totals rather than cheaper expansion.
