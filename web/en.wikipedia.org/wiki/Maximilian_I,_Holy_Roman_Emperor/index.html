Maximilian I (22 March 1459 – 12 January 1519) was Holy Roman Emperor from 1508 until his death and King of the Romans from 1486. A member of the House of Habsburg, he greatly expanded the Habsburg territories through war and dynastic marriage, earning the saying Bella gerant alii, tu felix Austria nube — "Let others wage war; you, fortunate Austria, marry." He has been called "the last knight" (der letzte Ritter) for his chivalric self-image combined with a thoroughly modern use of artillery, propaganda and the printing press.

Burgundian inheritance

In 1477 Maximilian married Mary of Burgundy, heiress to Charles the Bold, who had been killed at the Battle of Nancy. The marriage brought the wealthy Burgundian Netherlands and Franche-Comté into the Habsburg orbit and began a long struggle with France over the Burgundian inheritance. Mary's early death in a riding accident in 1482 left Maximilian regent for their son, Philip the Handsome, in the face of Flemish revolts that at one point saw him imprisoned by the citizens of Bruges (1488).

Emperor

Elected King of the Romans in 1486 during his father's lifetime, Maximilian became sole ruler on Frederick III's death in 1493. He pursued administrative reform of the Empire — an Imperial Chamber Court (Reichskammergericht), imperial circles and a common penny tax were established at the Diet of Worms in 1495 — and fought a long series of wars in Italy against France during the Italian Wars, forming the League of Cambrai (1508) and later shifting alliances in characteristic fashion. Prevented by Venetian resistance from travelling to Rome for coronation, he assumed the title "Elected Roman Emperor" (Erwählter Römischer Kaiser) in 1508 with papal consent — the style used by his successors thereafter.

Maximilian's own marriages and those he arranged laid the foundation of Habsburg greatness: his son Philip married Joanna of Castile, uniting the Burgundian lands with the Spanish crowns in the person of his grandson Charles V, and the double marriage treaty of Vienna (1515) with the Jagiellonians promised Bohemia and Hungary, which fell to the Habsburgs in 1526.

Patronage and image

An energetic self-publicist, Maximilian commissioned vast woodcut projects from Albrecht Dürer and others — the Triumphal Arch and Triumphal Procession — as well as the semi-autobiographical chivalric works Theuerdank, Freydal and the Weisskunig. He founded the tradition of the Vienna Boys' Choir (1498) and standardised the Landsknecht mercenary infantry. He died at Wels in January 1519 and was buried at Wiener Neustadt beneath the altar steps of St George's Chapel, so that priests celebrating mass would stand above his heart.
