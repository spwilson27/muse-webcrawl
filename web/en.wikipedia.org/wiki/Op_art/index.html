Op art

Op art, short for optical art, is a style of visual art that uses optical illusions. Op artworks are abstract, with many better-known pieces created in black and white. Typically, they give the viewer the impression of movement, hidden images, flashing and vibrating patterns, or swelling and warping.

Origins and development

Roots lie in the geometric abstraction of Josef Albers and Victor Vasarely, whose Zebra (1937) is often cited as an early example. The movement crystallised in the 1960s; the landmark 1965 Museum of Modern Art exhibition The Responsive Eye brought it to wide public attention. Bridget Riley's black-and-white paintings such as Movement in Squares (1961) became emblematic.

Methods

Op artists exploit the fallibility of the eye: precise arrangements of lines, grids and contrasting colours produce effects such as afterimages, moiré patterns and apparent motion in peripheral vision. The work is typically impersonal and systematically planned rather than expressive.
