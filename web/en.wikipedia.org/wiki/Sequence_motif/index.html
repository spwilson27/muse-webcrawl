Sequence motif

In genetics, a sequence motif is a nucleotide or amino-acid sequence pattern that is widespread and is conjectured to have a biological significance — for instance, marking a protein binding site, a structural feature, or a site of modification. Motifs are usually identified by comparing aligned sequences of related genes or proteins and finding positions that are conserved more than chance would predict.

Representation

Motifs are represented in several ways: as consensus sequences using ambiguity codes (for example, the TATA box of promoters), as regular expressions or patterns such as those in the PROSITE database, and probabilistically as position weight matrices or profile hidden Markov models, which capture how often each base or residue occurs at each position. Sequence logos provide a common graphical summary of such information.

Examples and discovery

Well-known motifs include the Pribnow and TATA boxes in promoters, the Shine–Dalgarno sequence in bacterial translation, splice-site consensus sequences, and protein motifs such as the zinc finger and the Walker A (P-loop) motif of ATP-binding proteins. Computational motif-discovery tools such as MEME search sets of unaligned sequences for recurring patterns, while databases such as JASPAR and Pfam catalog experimentally and computationally derived motifs used to annotate new sequences.
