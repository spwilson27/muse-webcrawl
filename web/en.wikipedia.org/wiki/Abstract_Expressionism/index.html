Abstract Expressionism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A vast drip painting in the manner of Jackson Pollock: skeins of black, white and aluminium paint webbed across an unprimed canvas]

Abstract Expressionism is a post–Second World War American art movement, centred in New York, in which artists made large, spontaneous, emotionally charged abstract paintings. The term, earlier applied to Kandinsky, was fixed to the New York School of Jackson Pollock, Willem de Kooning, Mark Rothko, Barnett Newman and others, and the movement's international triumph in the 1950s shifted the centre of the art world from Paris to New York.

The movement divides loosely into action painting — Pollock's poured 'drip' canvases, de Kooning's slashing figures — and colour-field painting, the immersive floating rectangles of Rothko and Newman's 'zips'. Critics Clement Greenberg and Harold Rosenberg championed rival readings: formal self-criticism versus existential act.

Origins

Refugee Surrealists in wartime New York, the WPA Federal Art Project's employment of future stars, and Arshile Gorky's bridge between European modernism and the new painting prepared the ground. Pollock's first drip paintings of 1947 and the famous Life magazine spread of 1949 ('Is he the greatest living painter in the United States?') made the movement public property.

Reception and legacy

Promoted abroad by the Museum of Modern Art's touring exhibitions (Cold War cultural diplomacy historians still debate), Abstract Expressionism dominated the 1950s until Pop and Minimalism reacted against its heroic subjectivity. Its scale, ambition and market — Pollock's No. 5, 1948 and de Kooning's Interchange rank among the most expensive paintings ever sold — continue to define modern art's mythology.
