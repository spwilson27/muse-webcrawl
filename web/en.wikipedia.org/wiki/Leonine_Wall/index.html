Leonine Wall

The Leonine Wall (Italian: Mura Leonine) is a medieval fortification that encircles the Vatican Hill area of Rome, Italy, enclosing what became known as the Leonine City — the district of Borgo, including St Peter's Basilica and the Vatican. It was built in the 9th century on the orders of Pope Leo IV, after whom it is named.

Construction

In 846, Muslim raiders sailed up the Tiber and plundered the basilicas of St Peter and St Paul, which then stood outside Rome's Aurelian Walls. To protect the Vatican, Leo IV ordered a new circuit of walls, built between 848 and 852 with funding that included contributions from the Frankish emperor. The wall ran about three kilometres, with dozens of towers, from the Tiber near Castel Sant'Angelo around the hill and back to the river. Leo consecrated the completed circuit in June 852 with a ceremonial procession and sprinkling of holy water.

Later history

The Leonine City remained a distinct fortified enclave; later popes repaired and extended the walls, and Pope Paul III and Pius IV added bastions in the 16th century. The enclosed Borgo district, with the Vatican palaces and St Peter's, became the core of the modern Vatican City, whose territory was formalised by the Lateran Treaty of 1929. Parts of the wall, including the Passetto corridor linking the Vatican to Castel Sant'Angelo, survive today.
