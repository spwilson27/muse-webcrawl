Fionn mac Cumhaill (pronounced roughly "fin ma-cool"; anglicised Finn McCool) is the central hero of the Fenian Cycle of Irish mythology — a warrior, hunter, seer and leader of the Fianna, the warrior band in the service of the High King of Ireland. His deeds, and those of his followers, are recounted chiefly in the Acallam na Senórach and in a great body of later folk tradition in Ireland and Scotland.

Early life and the Salmon of Knowledge

Fionn was the posthumous son of Cumhall, leader of the Fianna, who was killed in battle by Goll mac Morna, head of a rival clan within the band. The infant — born Deimne — was hidden in the wilderness of Sliabh Bladhma and raised in secret by two women, the druidess Bodhmall and the warrior Liath Luachra, for fear Goll would kill him. His fair hair earned him the name Fionn, "the fair".

As a youth Fionn served the poet Finn Eces, who had fished for seven years to catch the Salmon of Knowledge (an Bradán Feasa), which had eaten the hazelnuts of wisdom fallen into the Boyne. Ordered to cook the fish without eating any, Fionn burned his thumb on it and instinctively put the thumb in his mouth — receiving the salmon's knowledge in that one taste. Thereafter, whenever Fionn chewed or pressed his thumb, he gained prophetic insight (imbas), a device used throughout the tales.

Leader of the Fianna

At Samhain Fionn won back his father's place by slaying Aillen, the fire-breathing being of the Aos Sí who burned Tara each year, using a magical spear that kept him awake against Aillen's sleep-inducing music. Acknowledged by Goll and appointed leader of the Fianna by King Cormac mac Airt, he became famous for his generosity, his hounds Bran and Sceólang, and his followers — his son the poet Oisín, his grandson Oscar, the swift Caoilte, and the handsome Diarmuid. Later tales darken: the aged Fionn's jealousy drives the pursuit of Diarmuid and Gráinne, and tradition quarrels over his role in Diarmuid's death and the Fianna's ruin at the Battle of Gabhra.

In tradition

Fionn became the archetypal folk hero of Ireland and Gaelic Scotland: landscape features are ascribed to him, including, in a famous cross-channel tale, the Giant's Causeway, built as stepping-stones toward Scotland. A popular tradition held that Fionn is not dead but sleeps in a cave beneath Dublin, to awake in Ireland's hour of need. Through Macpherson's Ossian poems his cycle influenced European Romantic literature, and in the 19th century the Fenian Brotherhood and Irish Republican Brotherhood took their name from his band.
