Bacalao

Dried salt cod, the basis of bacalao dishes
TypeSalted fish dish
Place of originIberian Peninsula
Main ingredientsSalt cod, olive oil, potatoes, peppers

Bacalao (Spanish: bacalao; Portuguese: bacalhau) is the culinary name for dried and salted cod, and by extension for the many dishes prepared from it across Spain, Portugal, and their former colonial territories. Salt cod has been a staple food in the Iberian Peninsula for centuries, valued because it could be transported and stored for long periods without refrigeration.

History

Cod fishing off the Grand Banks of Newfoundland was pursued by Basque and Portuguese fishermen from the 15th century onward. Salting and drying the catch on shore allowed ships to carry large quantities of protein-rich fish back to Europe. Salt cod became a Lenten staple in Catholic countries, where meat was forbidden on Fridays and during Lent, and it spread through Spanish and Portuguese trade routes to the Caribbean, Latin America, West Africa, and the Philippines.

Preparation

Before cooking, salt cod must be desalted by soaking in water for 24 to 48 hours, with several changes of water. Once rehydrated, the fish is poached, fried, stewed, or baked. Classic preparations include the Basque bacalao al pil pil, in which the fish is emulsified in olive oil and garlic; bacalao a la vizcaína, stewed in a sauce of peppers and tomatoes; and the Portuguese bacalhau à Brás, shredded cod scrambled with eggs, onions, and matchstick potatoes. In Puerto Rico, bacalaítos are crispy cod fritters.

Cultural significance

In Portugal bacalhau is sometimes said to have 365 traditional recipes, one for every day of the year, and it is the centerpiece of the traditional Christmas Eve dinner in many families. Similar traditions exist in Spain, where bacalao features prominently in regional cuisines of the Basque Country, Catalonia, and Valencia.
