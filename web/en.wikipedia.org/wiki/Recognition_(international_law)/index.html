Recognition (international law)

Recognition in international law is a unilateral political act by a state (or group of states) acknowledging the existence of another entity as a state, or of a regime as the government of a state, together with the legal consequences that flow from that acknowledgment. Recognition may be express, for example by a formal declaration or the establishment of diplomatic relations, or implied by conduct such as concluding a bilateral treaty.

Recognition is distinct from the existence of a state as a matter of fact. Several entities exercise effective control over territory and population while enjoying little or no recognition, and some widely recognized states have at times lacked an effective government.

Theories of recognition

Two classical theories compete in the literature. Under the constitutive theory, associated with writers such as Oppenheim and Kelsen, an entity becomes a state in the legal sense only when it is recognized by existing states; recognition is a constitutive act that creates statehood. Under the declaratory theory, recognition merely declares or acknowledges a statehood that already exists once certain factual criteria are met, and is legally superfluous even if politically vital.

The declaratory view is reflected in Article 3 of the Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States (1933), which provides that the political existence of a state is independent of recognition by other states. The Convention's Article 1 lists the classic criteria of statehood: a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states.

Recognition of states and of governments

International practice distinguishes recognition of states from recognition of governments. New states arise through secession, dissolution, decolonization, or merger; their recognition is a discretionary political decision, though premature recognition of a secessionist entity may itself be an unlawful intervention. Recognition of governments, by contrast, concerns which regime is treated as entitled to act for an existing state. Since the late twentieth century many states, following the British practice announced in 1980, no longer formally recognize governments at all, instead deciding on a case-by-case basis which regime to deal with.

Recognition is also described as de facto or de jure. De facto recognition is provisional and limited, extended to an authority whose control is effective but whose permanence is doubted; de jure recognition is full and definitive. The United Kingdom, for example, accorded de facto recognition to the Soviet government in 1921 and de jure recognition in 1924.

Collective recognition and non-recognition

Recognition may be collective, as when admission to the United Nations system or coordinated declarations by regional organizations effectively confirm statehood, although UN membership is formally a separate act and is not itself recognition by each member. Conversely, states may be legally obliged not to recognize situations created by serious breaches of peremptory norms. The classic example is the Stimson Doctrine of 1932, under which the United States refused to recognize territorial changes effected by force in Manchuria. The obligation of non-recognition has been affirmed by the International Court of Justice, notably in the Namibia advisory opinion (1971), and by the UN Security Council in relation to Rhodesia, the South African bantustans, and Northern Cyprus.

Contemporary examples

  • Kosovo — recognized by roughly half of UN member states following its 2008 declaration of independence from Serbia.
  • Palestine — recognized by a large majority of UN members and holding non-member observer state status at the UN since 2012.
  • Taiwan (Republic of China) — recognized by only a small number of states, most of which recognize the People's Republic of China as the sole legitimate government of China, yet maintaining extensive unofficial relations worldwide.
  • Somaliland — effectively self-governing since 1991 but recognized by no UN member state for most of that period.
  • Northern Cyprus — recognized only by Turkey; its 1983 declaration of independence was declared legally invalid by the Security Council.

Effects of recognition

Recognition typically unlocks the full range of interstate relations: diplomatic and consular relations, treaty-making, access to the recognizing state's courts, immunity for state property, and eligibility for membership of international organizations. Non-recognition does not necessarily render an entity a legal nullity: unrecognized regimes may still bear obligations under international humanitarian law and human rights law, and their acts may be given effect in foreign courts where necessary to avoid injustice to private parties, as in the Namibia opinion's exception for acts such as the registration of births and marriages.
