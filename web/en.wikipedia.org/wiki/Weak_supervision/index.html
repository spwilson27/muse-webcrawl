Weak supervision

Weak supervision is a paradigm in machine learning in which noisy, limited, or imprecise sources of labels are used to provide supervision signals for training predictive models. Rather than relying on large quantities of hand-labeled training data, weak supervision combines multiple weak sources—such as heuristic rules, knowledge bases, distant supervision signals, or the outputs of other models—to generate probabilistic training labels at scale.

Motivation

Labeling training data by hand is often expensive, slow, or requires scarce expert knowledge, particularly in domains such as medicine, law, and intelligence analysis. Weak supervision seeks to reduce this bottleneck by allowing practitioners to encode their knowledge as labeling functions: small programs or heuristics that assign noisy labels to subsets of the data. The disagreements and correlations among these labeling functions can then be modeled to estimate their accuracies and produce denoised probabilistic labels.

Approaches

A prominent framework for weak supervision is Snorkel, developed at Stanford, in which users write labeling functions and a generative model learns to combine their outputs without access to ground truth labels. The resulting probabilistic labels are used to train a downstream discriminative model, which can generalize beyond the coverage of the labeling functions. Other approaches include distant supervision, in which labels are inferred from an external knowledge base; crowdsourcing aggregation; and semi-supervised learning, which combines a small labeled set with a large unlabeled one.
