Media literacy

Media literacy is the ability to access, analyze, evaluate, create, and act using all forms of communication. Expanding the notion of literacy beyond reading and writing, media literacy education aims to empower people to be critical thinkers and makers, effective communicators, and active citizens. Definitions by bodies such as NAMLE (the National Association for Media Literacy Education) and UNESCO emphasize critical inquiry into media messages, their purposes, techniques, and effects.

Core concepts

Widely taught key concepts (drawn from the Center for Media Literacy and Canadian media education traditions) include:

  • Media are constructions, not transparent reflections of reality.
  • Audiences negotiate and interpret meaning differently.
  • Media have commercial implications and embedded values and points of view.
  • Each medium has its own language, forms, and conventions.
  • Media messages are produced for purposes such as profit, persuasion, and power.

History

Media education began in the 1930s–60s as "protectionist" inoculation against popular culture and propaganda—for example through the British film-society movement and Leavisite literary criticism, and the U.S. Institute for Propaganda Analysis (1937). It shifted toward critical appreciation (Cultural Studies influence), then toward empowerment and participation in the digital era. Milestones include the UNESCO Grünwald Declaration (1982) on media education; the integration of media literacy into school curricula in countries such as the UK, Canada, and Australia; and a renewed focus after 2016 on misinformation, leading to related frameworks for news literacy and digital literacy, lateral reading, and verification skills.

Practices and frameworks

Media literacy pedagogy uses inquiry-based questions—Who made this? Why? What techniques attract attention? How might different people understand it differently? What values are represented or omitted?—and production activities in which students create media. Scholarly traditions include W. James Potter's cognitive model of media literacy and Renee Hobbs's work on digital and media literacy competencies (access, analyze, create, reflect, act).
