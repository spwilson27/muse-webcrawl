The Euler angles are three angles introduced by Leonhard Euler to describe the orientation of a rigid body with respect to a fixed coordinate system. Any orientation in three-dimensional space can be obtained by composing three successive rotations about specified axes, and the Euler angles are the magnitudes of those rotations. They are fundamental in rigid-body dynamics, aerospace engineering (where roll, pitch and yaw are Euler angles of the Tait–Bryan type), robotics, crystallography and computer graphics.

Conventions

Twelve conventions exist, depending on the sequence of axes used. The "classical" Euler angles use a z–x–z sequence: a rotation φ about the z-axis, then θ about the new x-axis (the line of nodes), then ψ about the new z-axis — the convention used for tops and gyroscopes in mechanics. The Tait–Bryan angles use three different axes (e.g. z–y–x), giving yaw, pitch and roll as used for aircraft and ships. Angles may be defined intrinsically (about the moving axes) or extrinsically (about the fixed axes); the two descriptions are equivalent with reversed order.

Euler's rotation theorem

Underlying the parametrisation is Euler's rotation theorem: any displacement of a rigid body with one point fixed is equivalent to a single rotation about some axis through that point. The three Euler angles, combined into rotation matrices or quaternions, encode this rotation in a form convenient for computation.

Gimbal lock

Euler-angle representations have a singularity: when the middle rotation aligns the first and third axes (for example, pitch of ±90° in the yaw–pitch–roll convention), one degree of freedom is lost — the phenomenon of gimbal lock. At such a configuration two of the angles produce rotation about the same physical axis, and the orientation cannot be smoothly tracked by the angles alone. For this reason spacecraft and 3D graphics often use quaternions internally, converting to Euler angles only for human-readable display.

Applications

Euler angles describe aircraft attitude (heading, pitch, bank), the orientation of crystallites in texture analysis (the Bunge convention), joint angles in robotics and biomechanics, and camera orientation in computer vision and graphics. In quantum mechanics the same rotations, applied to spinors, connect with the rotation group SO(3) and its double cover SU(2).
