Nonviolent Communication (NVC) is an approach to communication that seeks to improve compassion and collaboration in society. It was developed by clinical psychologist Marshall Rosenberg beginning in the 1960s and 1970s. The Center for Nonviolent Communication, founded by Rosenberg in 1984, defines NVC as a way of communicating that "helps people connect with themselves and others in a way that makes compassionate giving natural."

Overview

NVC models communication as a four-step process: (1) observing without evaluating; (2) identifying and expressing feelings; (3) connecting feelings to underlying universal human needs; and (4) making clear, concrete requests rather than demands. It also emphasizes empathic listening — hearing the feelings and needs behind another person's words, including behind criticism or anger.

Applications

The method has been applied in mediation, education, parenting, psychotherapy, business, and restorative justice, and in conflict zones. Rosenberg mediated between warring groups in settings including Rwanda, Burundi, Nigeria, Malaysia, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, Israel and Palestine.

Reception

Supporters point to case studies and practitioner reports of improved relationships and de-escalation. Critics note the limited base of rigorous empirical studies, and some find the formulaic language artificial or open to misuse as a covert way of controlling conversations. Limited research — including a 2021 systematic review — suggests positive effects on empathy, though evidence remains preliminary.
