Fibonacci

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A portrait painting of Leonardo Fibonacci, bearded medieval scholar]

Leonardo Bonacci (c. 1170 – c. 1240/50), known as Fibonacci, was an Italian mathematician from the Republic of Pisa, considered to be 'the most talented Western mathematician of the Middle Ages'.

His Liber Abaci (1202, 'The Book of Calculation') introduced the Hindu–Arabic numeral system to Europe, arguing its superiority over Roman numerals for commerce and calculation. The book also presented the sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8... — the Fibonacci sequence, known earlier in Indian mathematics — arising in his (idealised) rabbit-population problem, each term the sum of the previous two.

Legacy

The ratio of consecutive Fibonacci numbers approaches the golden ratio φ ≈ 1.618; the sequence and its relatives appear in phyllotaxis (leaf and seed arrangements), art mythology, and computer science (Fibonacci heaps, search techniques, the classic recursion example).

Fibonacci travelled the Mediterranean with his customs-official father, learning the mathematics of Islamic and Byzantine trade — his book synthesised these sources for Latin readers, a key transmission in European mathematics' revival.
