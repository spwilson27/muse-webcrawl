The Yardbirds

The Yardbirds are an English rock band formed in London in 1963. They are celebrated as a launchpad for three of rock's greatest guitarists — Eric Clapton, Jeff Beck and Jimmy Page — and as pioneers of blues rock, psychedelic rock and early hard rock and heavy metal.

History

The band emerged from the British rhythm and blues boom, taking over the Rolling Stones' residency at Richmond's Crawdaddy Club. With Keith Relf on vocals and harmonica, Chris Dreja, Jim McCarty and Paul Samwell-Smith, the Yardbirds began as a blues covers band; their live album Five Live Yardbirds (1964) captured their energetic "rave-up" style. Clapton, a blues purist, left in 1965 over the pop direction of the hit single "For Your Love," and was replaced by Jeff Beck, whose experimentation with feedback, fuzz and Eastern scales drove hits such as "Heart Full of Soul," "Evil Hearted You" and "Shapes of Things." Bassist-turned-guitarist Jimmy Page joined in 1966, briefly sharing lead guitar with Beck. After Beck's departure, the four-piece Page-led band toured into 1968 before splitting; Page formed the New Yardbirds, which soon became Led Zeppelin.

Legacy

The Yardbirds' innovations in guitar distortion and improvisation profoundly influenced hard rock and heavy metal. They were inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1992. A reformed Yardbirds, led by McCarty, has toured and recorded since the 1990s.
