Germanic paganism

Germanic paganism is the collective term for the pre-Christian religions of the Germanic peoples, whose practices are documented — fragmentarily and often by hostile outsiders — from the Roman Iron Age until Christianisation completed the process in the early medieval period, last of all in Scandinavia. It was a polytheistic tradition focused on a pantheon of gods, ritual sacrifice (blót), and sacred places in the landscape.

Sources

Evidence comes from Tacitus's Germania (c. 98 AD), runic inscriptions, place names, later medieval literature such as the Icelandic Poetic Edda and Prose Edda, sagas, and the writings of Christian missionaries and historians like Bede and Adam of Bremen. Archaeology — bog bodies, sacrificed weapons (as at Illerup Ådal), hoards, and cult buildings — supplements the texts.

Deities and cosmology

Principal deities included Odin (Wodan), the god of wisdom, war, and poetry; Thor (Donar), wielder of the hammer Mjölnir and protector against giants; Freyr and Freyja, Vanir deities of fertility; and Tyr, Baldr, Frigg, and Heimdall. Cosmology envisioned a world tree, Yggdrasil, and an eventual world-ending battle, Ragnarök. Regional and temporal variation was considerable: the continental and Anglo-Saxon traditions differed in emphasis from the later Norse religion, which is by far the best documented.

Practice and decline

Worship centred on sacrifices of animals, goods, and occasionally humans, seasonal feasts, processions, and divination; sacred groves, springs, and temple buildings (such as the one described at Uppsala) served as cult sites. Christianisation proceeded from the 4th century among the Goths to the 12th century in Sweden. In the modern era, revivals of Germanic pagan belief, often called Heathenry or Ásatrú, have emerged since the 1970s.
