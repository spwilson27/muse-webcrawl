Anandamide (ANA), also known as N-arachidonoylethanolamine (AEA), is a fatty-acid neurotransmitter belonging to the class of endocannabinoids. It was the first endocannabinoid to be discovered, isolated from porcine brain in 1992 by Raphael Mechoulam's group, including Lumír Hanuš and William Devane. Its name derives from the Sanskrit ananda, "bliss" or "delight", plus amide.

Physiological functions

Anandamide binds to the same cannabinoid receptors (CB1 and CB2) activated by THC, the psychoactive compound in cannabis, though it is a partial agonist and is much shorter-lived. It is synthesised on demand from membrane phospholipids rather than stored in vesicles, and acts largely as a retrograde messenger modulating neurotransmitter release. Anandamide signalling is implicated in mood, appetite, pain perception, memory, fertility and reward; elevated levels after endurance exercise have been linked to the "runner's high". It also activates the TRPV1 vanilloid channel at higher concentrations.

Metabolism

Anandamide's action is terminated mainly by the enzyme fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH), which hydrolyses it to arachidonic acid and ethanolamine. FAAH inhibitors raise anandamide levels and have been investigated as analgesic and anxiolytic drugs.

Occurrence

Anandamide and related N-acylethanolamines occur in most animal tissues and also in foods such as chocolate (in trace amounts) and truffles, though dietary amounts are not thought to be psychoactive.
