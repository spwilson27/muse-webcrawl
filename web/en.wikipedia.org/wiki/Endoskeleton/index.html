Endoskeleton

The human endoskeleton

An endoskeleton is the internal support structure of an animal, composed of mineralised tissue, as opposed to an exoskeleton, which is external. Endoskeletons provide support and protection for the body, an attachment surface for muscles, and — because they are internal — can grow with the animal without moulting.

In vertebrates

The vertebrate endoskeleton consists of bone and cartilage. In humans it comprises the axial skeleton (skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum) and the appendicular skeleton (limbs and girdles) — 206 bones in a typical adult. Bones also serve as a mineral store (calcium and phosphate) and contain marrow that produces blood cells. The earliest vertebrate endoskeletons were cartilaginous; bone evolved later, a fact reflected in sharks and rays, whose skeletons remain largely cartilage.

In invertebrates

Some invertebrates possess endoskeletons of a very different kind. Echinoderms (starfish, sea urchins) have an endoskeleton of calcareous ossicles beneath the skin, which in sea urchins fuse into a rigid test. Sponges are supported internally by spicules of silica or calcium carbonate embedded in their tissues. Cephalopods such as cuttlefish have an internal shell (the cuttlebone) that functions in buoyancy.

Comparison with exoskeletons

Endoskeletons can support much larger body sizes than exoskeletons, permit continuous growth, and allow muscles to attach on the outside of the skeleton, giving mechanical advantages for large land animals. Exoskeletons, by contrast, offer armour-like protection but constrain size and must be shed for growth in arthropods.
