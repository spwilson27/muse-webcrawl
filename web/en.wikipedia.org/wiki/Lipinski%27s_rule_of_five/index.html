Lipinski's rule of five

Lipinski's rule of five

Formulated by: Christopher A. Lipinski

Year: 1997

Field: Medicinal chemistry, drug discovery

Purpose: Evaluate druglikeness and oral bioavailability

Lipinski's rule of five, also known as Pfizer's rule of five or simply the rule of five (RO5), is a rule of thumb used to evaluate druglikeness or determine if a chemical compound with a certain pharmacological or biological activity has chemical and physical properties that would make it likely to be an orally active drug in humans. The rule was formulated by Christopher A. Lipinski in 1997, based on the observation that most orally administered drugs are relatively small and moderately lipophilic molecules.

The rule

The rule describes molecular properties important for a drug's pharmacokinetics in the human body, including their absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME). Lipinski's rule states that, in general, an orally active drug has no more than one violation of the following criteria:

  • Not more than 5 hydrogen bond donors (the total number of nitrogen–hydrogen and oxygen–hydrogen bonds).
  • Not more than 10 hydrogen bond acceptors (all nitrogen or oxygen atoms).
  • A molecular mass less than 500 daltons.
  • A calculated octanol-water partition coefficient (Clog P) that does not exceed 5.

All numbers in the rule are multiples of five, which is the origin of the name "rule of five".

Criticism and extensions

The rule does not predict whether a compound is pharmacologically active. It is also important to keep in mind that compounds which are substrates for biological transporters are an exception to the rule. Various extensions have been proposed, such as Veber's rules, which add criteria for polar surface area and rotatable bond count, and "lead-like" criteria that are stricter and intended for lead compounds rather than drug candidates.
