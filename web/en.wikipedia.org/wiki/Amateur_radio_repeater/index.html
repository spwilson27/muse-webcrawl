Amateur radio repeater

Type: Repeater station
Typical bands: 2 metres, 70 centimetres
Purpose: Extending range of mobile and handheld stations

An amateur radio repeater is an electronic device in the amateur radio service that receives a radio signal on one frequency and simultaneously retransmits it on another, usually from an elevated site such as a hilltop, tower or tall building. Repeaters allow low-power handheld and mobile stations to communicate over distances far beyond their normal line-of-sight range, and they form the backbone of local VHF and UHF amateur communication.

Operation

A repeater listens on its input frequency and rebroadcasts everything it hears on its output frequency, separated by a standard offset — for example 600 kHz on the 2-metre band and 5 MHz on the 70-centimetre band. Users transmit on the input and listen on the output. Most repeaters require the user to send a subaudible CTCSS tone or a 1750 Hz tone burst to activate them, and controllers add identification in Morse code or voice, courtesy tones and time-out timers.

Equipment

A typical repeater consists of a receiver, transmitter, controller, power supply and a duplexer — a set of sharp filters that lets one antenna serve both transmission and reception at once. Linked repeater systems join several machines by radio links or the internet (as in EchoLink and IRLP), enabling regional or worldwide coverage, while digital systems such as DMR, D-STAR and System Fusion carry voice in digital form through networked reflectors.

Use

Repeaters support everyday local conversation, nets, and emergency communication: during disasters they are heavily used by groups such as RAYNET and ARES. Frequency coordination bodies assign repeater channels to avoid interference between neighbouring systems.
