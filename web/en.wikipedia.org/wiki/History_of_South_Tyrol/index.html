History of South Tyrol

The history of South Tyrol, the largely German-speaking Alpine province of Bolzano in northern Italy, is defined by its transfer from Austria to Italy after the First World War, the Fascist Italianisation campaign and the post-war autonomy settlement that made it a frequently cited model for resolving ethnic conflict.

From Raetia to the Habsburgs

The region, inhabited in antiquity by Raetian tribes, was conquered by Rome and later settled by Bavarians from the 6th century. It formed the southern part of the County of Tyrol, named after Tyrol Castle near Merano, and passed to the Habsburgs in 1363. Through the prince-bishoprics of Brixen and Trent and the County of Tyrol, the area remained in the Austrian sphere for over five centuries, fighting French invasion under Andreas Hofer in 1809.

Annexation by Italy

Under the secret Treaty of London (1915), Italy was promised the Tyrol up to the Brenner watershed. The Treaty of Saint-Germain (1919) awarded the territory south of the Brenner Pass to Italy despite its overwhelmingly German-speaking population, as a strategic "natural frontier". Under Fascism, an aggressive Italianisation programme banned German in schools and public life, Italianised place and family names, and promoted Italian immigration to Bolzano. The 1939 Hitler–Mussolini "Option" agreement pressured South Tyroleans to emigrate to the Reich or accept assimilation; most who voted to leave never did, or returned after 1945.

Autonomy

The Gruber–De Gasperi Agreement of 1946 promised autonomy, but the 1948 statute gave it to the joint region of Trentino-Alto Adige, where Italians were the majority, fuelling grievance and, in the 1960s, a bombing campaign by the South Tyrolean Liberation Committee. Austria brought the dispute to the United Nations. A "package" of measures led to the Second Autonomy Statute of 1972, which transferred most powers to the province itself, guaranteed linguistic proportionality in public employment and funded cultural autonomy. Austria and Italy formally declared the dispute closed in 1992. South Tyrol is today one of Italy's wealthiest provinces, with German, Italian and Ladin language groups coexisting under the autonomy system.
