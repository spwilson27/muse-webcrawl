Assonance

Assonance shapes the sound of verse from medieval epics to modern hip-hop.
Assonance
TypeFigure of speech; sound device
DefinitionRepetition of vowel sounds in nearby words
RelatedConsonance, alliteration, rhyme

Assonance is the repetition of identical or similar vowel sounds in nearby words, usually in verse. It is sometimes called "vowel rhyme" or "imperfect rhyme": the vowel sounds match while the surrounding consonants differ, as in "Hear the mellow wedding bells" (Edgar Allan Poe). Assonance differs from consonance (repeated consonant sounds) and from full rhyme, which requires matching vowel and following consonant sounds in stressed syllables.

Use in poetry

Assonance was a structural device in Old Norse, Old Irish and medieval Spanish verse, where assonant rhyme (asonancia) organised the romances and epics such as the Poema del Cid. In English poetry it is used more loosely for musicality, mood and emphasis, appearing prominently in the work of poets such as Emily Dickinson, W. B. Yeats and Dylan Thomas.

In popular music

Hip-hop lyricists use dense internal assonance and multisyllabic vowel patterns to create rhythmic flow; songwriters likewise rely on vowel matching between lines where exact rhyme would be predictable. Assonance also appears in prose for euphony and memorability, as in proverbial phrases.
