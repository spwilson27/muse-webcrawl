Classified information

Classified information is material that a government body deems to be sensitive information that must be protected. Access is restricted by law or regulation to particular groups of people with the necessary security clearance and need to know, and mishandling of the material can incur criminal penalties.

A document marked with classification banners indicating restricted access.

Classification levels

Many governments use tiered systems. The United States system, established by executive order, has three levels: Confidential (disclosure could damage national security), Secret (could cause serious damage), and Top Secret (could cause exceptionally grave damage), with additional controls such as Sensitive Compartmented Information (SCI). The United Kingdom uses OFFICIAL, SECRET, and TOP SECRET. NATO uses designations such as NATO RESTRICTED, NATO CONFIDENTIAL, NATO SECRET, and COSMIC TOP SECRET.

Handling and declassification

Classified material must be stored in approved facilities, marked with classification banners, and destroyed by approved methods. Most systems provide for automatic or systematic declassification after a set period — in the U.S., generally 25 years — and for public requests under freedom-of-information laws. Unauthorized disclosure ("leaks") has featured in major public controversies, from the Pentagon Papers in 1971 to the disclosures by Chelsea Manning and Edward Snowden. Critics in many countries argue that over-classification undermines democratic accountability.
