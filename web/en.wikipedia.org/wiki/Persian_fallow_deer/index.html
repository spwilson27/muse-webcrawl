Persian fallow deer

Persian fallow deer
Scientific name: Dama mesopotamica
Conservation status: Endangered
Range: Iran; reintroduced in Israel
Family: Cervidae

The Persian fallow deer (Dama mesopotamica) is a deer species native to the Middle East, once ranging from the Levant and Anatolia across Mesopotamia to western Iran. It is larger than the European fallow deer, with bigger, less palmate antlers in the male, and is treated either as a full species or as a subspecies of the fallow deer (Dama dama mesopotamica).

Decline and rediscovery

Overhunting and habitat loss reduced the deer drastically, and it was long thought extinct. It was rediscovered in 1956 in Khuzestan Province, southwestern Iran, when a small population was found in the dense riverine forests along the Dez and Karkheh rivers. Iranian conservation authorities, with support from international zoos, established captive breeding programmes that became a model for species rescue.

Conservation

The deer is classified as Endangered. Wild and semi-wild populations persist in protected areas of Khuzestan, including the Dez and Karkheh wildlife refuges. Beginning in 1978, animals from the breeding programme were reintroduced to Israel, where a population now lives in the Carmel and western Galilee region. Threats include poaching, habitat fragmentation, drought, and competition with livestock.

Description

Stags stand about 95–110 cm at the shoulder and weigh 100–120 kg. The coat is reddish-brown with white spots in summer; males bear broad, flattened antlers, though less widely palmate than those of the European fallow deer.
