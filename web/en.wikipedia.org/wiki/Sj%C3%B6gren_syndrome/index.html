Sjögren syndrome

Sjögren syndrome (also spelled Sjögren's syndrome) is a long-term autoimmune disease that primarily affects the body's moisture-producing glands, most notably the tear glands and salivary glands. It is named after the Swedish ophthalmologist Henrik Sjögren, who described it in 1933.

Specialty: Rheumatology, immunology
Symptoms: Dry eyes, dry mouth, fatigue, joint pain
Onset: Usually middle age, more common in women
Treatment: Symptomatic; artificial tears, saliva substitutes, immunosuppressants in severe cases

Signs and symptoms

The hallmark symptoms are dry eyes (keratoconjunctivitis sicca) and dry mouth (xerostomia), caused by lymphocytic infiltration and destruction of the lacrimal and salivary glands. Other symptoms include fatigue, joint and muscle pain, dry skin, vaginal dryness, chronic cough, and dental decay resulting from reduced saliva.

Causes

The exact cause is unknown. It is believed to involve a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers such as viral infections. The condition may occur on its own (primary Sjögren syndrome) or alongside other autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis or systemic lupus erythematosus (secondary Sjögren syndrome).

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on symptoms, blood tests for autoantibodies such as anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB, the Schirmer test of tear production, and sometimes a biopsy of the minor salivary glands of the lip.

Treatment

There is no cure; treatment focuses on relieving dryness with artificial tears, saliva substitutes and stimulants such as pilocarpine, and on managing systemic inflammation. People with the condition have a modestly increased risk of lymphoma.
