Orobanchaceae, the broomrape family, is a family of flowering plants in the order Lamiales. It is notable as the only plant family that includes the full spectrum of nutritional modes: free-living photosynthetic plants, hemiparasites (which photosynthesise but draw water and nutrients from host roots), and holoparasites (which lack chlorophyll and depend entirely on a host). Molecular phylogenetic work greatly expanded the family in the APG systems, transferring most parasitic genera formerly placed in the Scrophulariaceae into the Orobanchaceae.

Description

Members are mostly herbaceous annuals or perennials. Parasitic species attach to host roots by a specialised organ, the haustorium. Flowers are zygomorphic (bilaterally symmetric), typically two-lipped tubes borne in spikes or racemes, and the fruit is a capsule with numerous tiny seeds — in the parasitic genera, seeds germinate only on detecting chemical signals (strigolactones) released by host roots.

Genera and significance

Important genera include Orobanche and Phelipanche (broomrapes), Striga (witchweeds), the hemiparasitic Castilleja (Indian paintbrushes), Pedicularis (louseworts), Euphrasia (eyebrights) and Rhinanthus (rattles), and the holoparasitic toothworts (Lathraea). Striga species are devastating pests of cereals — maize, sorghum, millet and rice — in sub-Saharan Africa, and broomrapes seriously damage legumes, sunflower and tomato crops around the Mediterranean, making the family of great agricultural importance. Hemiparasites such as Rhinanthus are used in grassland restoration because they suppress vigorous grasses and favour wildflower diversity.

Evolution of parasitism

Parasitism appears to have arisen once in the family, with holoparasitism evolving several times from hemiparasitic ancestors. Parasitic members show progressive reduction of the plastid genome and loss of photosynthetic genes, an evolutionary trajectory studied as a model for the transition to heterotrophy in plants.
