Paschen's law

Paschen's law
Named afterFriedrich Paschen
Announced1889
QuantityBreakdown voltage as a function of pressure × gap length (pd)
Key featureA minimum breakdown voltage for each gas (e.g. ~327 V for air)

Paschen's law describes the voltage required to start an electrical discharge or arc between two electrodes in a gas as a function of pressure and the distance between the electrodes. Discovered by the German physicist Friedrich Paschen in 1889, the law states that the breakdown voltage depends only on the product of gas pressure p and gap distance d, not on each separately.

The Paschen curve

Plotting breakdown voltage against pd yields a curve with a characteristic minimum — the Paschen minimum — which for air at standard conditions lies near 327 volts. To the right of the minimum, more gas molecules mean more collisions that rob electrons of energy, so higher voltage is needed; to the left, there are too few molecules, electrons rarely collide, and breakdown again requires higher voltage. This is why near-vacuum can be an excellent insulator and why aircraft and spacecraft electrical design must consider low-pressure altitudes where breakdown is easiest.

Mechanism and use

Breakdown proceeds by avalanche ionization: free electrons accelerated by the field ionize gas molecules, producing more electrons (Townsend discharge). Paschen's law guides the design of gas-filled surge protectors, high-voltage equipment, plasma devices and fluorescent lamps, and it constrains electrical systems operating at high altitude or in partial vacuum.
