Mozarabic art

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The church of San Miguel de Escalada (10th century), a classic example of Mozarabic architecture in León.

Mozarabic art (Spanish: arte mozárabe) is the art and architecture produced between the 8th and 11th centuries in the Iberian Peninsula in connection with the Mozarabs — Christians who had lived under Muslim rule in al-Andalus — and with the Christian kingdoms of the north, where Mozarabic refugees and monks brought the artistic traditions of the south. It forms a distinctive phase of Spanish pre-Romanesque art, blending Visigothic, Asturian, Carolingian, and Islamic elements.

Architecture

Mozarabic churches, most of them small monastic buildings erected in the Duero valley and the foothills of the Cantabrian mountains during the 10th-century repopulation (repoblación), are characterized by the horseshoe arch (often slightly more closed than its Visigothic predecessor and framed by an alfiz), ribbed and caliphal-style domes derived from Córdoba, lobed arches, sculpted modillions (modillones de rollos) under the eaves, and interiors divided into compartmentalized spaces. Masonry is typically of ashlar or rubble, with brick used in some regions.

Outstanding examples include San Miguel de Escalada and Santiago de Peñalba in León, Santa María de Lebeña in Cantabria, San Juan de la Peña in Aragón, San Baudelio de Berlanga in Soria (famous for its palm-tree-like central pillar and wall paintings), and Santa María de Melque near Toledo. Scholars debate how many of these buildings were actually built by Mozarab immigrants rather than by local Christian communities influenced by Andalusi craftsmanship, and the related label "art of the Repoblación" is sometimes preferred.

Manuscripts and painting

The most famous Mozarabic artworks are the illuminated manuscripts of the Commentary on the Apocalypse by Beatus of Liébana, copied in northern monasteries in the 10th and 11th centuries. The Beatus manuscripts are renowned for their bold, flat, brilliantly coloured miniatures — in vivid reds, yellows, and blues — with stylized figures framed by horseshoe arches and banded grounds, a style that fuses insular, Carolingian, and Islamic elements. Church wall paintings, such as those at San Baudelio and San Juan de la Peña, share this vivid, anti-naturalistic language.
