Estate (law)

In common law, an estate is the net worth of a person at any point in time, alive or dead. It is the sum of a person's assets — legal rights, interests and entitlements to property of any kind — less all liabilities at that time. The term is also used in several more specialised senses in property law, probate and bankruptcy.

Estates in land

In English land law, an "estate" historically denotes the nature and duration of a person's interest in land. The principal freehold estates are the fee simple (potentially infinite duration, freely alienable), the fee tail (descending only to lineal heirs of the body) and the life estate (lasting for the holder's lifetime). Leasehold estates — terms of years — stand alongside them. The doctrine of estates, rooted in feudal tenure, was largely codified in England by the Law of Property Act 1925, which reduced the legal estates to the fee simple absolute in possession and the term of years absolute.

Estate of a deceased person

On death, a person's estate passes to their executors or administrators, who collect the assets, pay debts and taxes (such as estate tax or inheritance tax), and distribute the remainder to beneficiaries under the will or the rules of intestacy. This process is called probate or estate administration.

Bankruptcy estate

In bankruptcy law, the "estate" is the pool of the debtor's property that becomes subject to administration for the benefit of creditors — for example, the bankruptcy estate created under section 541 of the United States Bankruptcy Code on the filing of a petition.
