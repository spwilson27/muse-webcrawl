Nom de guerre

A nom de guerre (French for "war name") is a pseudonym adopted by a person, especially a soldier, guerrilla, resistance fighter, or revolutionary, to conceal their identity, protect their family, or embody an aspiration. Related terms include nom de combat and, for writers, nom de plume.

Historical use

The practice became systematic in the French army, where from the seventeenth century soldiers traditionally received a nom de guerre recorded alongside their real name in regimental rolls — often a nickname such as "La Fleur" or "Sans-Souci." The custom persists in the French Foreign Legion, where recruits enlist under a declared identité déclarée.

Resistance and revolutionary movements

During the Second World War, members of resistance movements across occupied Europe operated under noms de guerre; Jean Moulin, for example, used the names "Rex" and "Max." Revolutionary leaders often became known primarily by their war names: "Che" Guevara took his nickname from his Argentine speech habits, Pol Pot's name derives from his underground alias, and Vietnamese leader Hồ Chí Minh ("He Who Enlightens") was itself a revolutionary pseudonym.

Modern usage

Today the term is used loosely for any alias adopted in conflict or clandestine activity, and by extension for stage-like aliases used by hackers, activists, and members of armed groups.
