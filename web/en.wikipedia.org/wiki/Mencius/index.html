Mencius (372–289 BC), known in Chinese as Mengzi, was a Chinese Confucian philosopher, the most famous after Confucius himself and honoured as the 'Second Sage'. The book bearing his name records his dialogues with kings and rival thinkers, defending Confucius's teaching with the doctrine that human nature is innately good: everyone has 'sprouts' of virtue — compassion, shame, deference, approval/disapproval — that need only cultivation, as shown by the spontaneous alarm anyone feels seeing a child about to fall into a well.

Political thought

Mencius taught that the people are the most important element in a state, the ruler the least; a king who mutilates benevolence and righteousness is a 'mere fellow' whose removal is not regicide — a justification of rebellion that made his book suspect to autocrats (the Ming founder tried to censor it). His mother, who moved house three times for his education, is a Chinese cultural icon of parenting.
