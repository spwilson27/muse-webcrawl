Dedekind domain

[Image: Richard Dedekind]
Richard Dedekind, who introduced ideal theory in number rings

In abstract algebra, a Dedekind domain (or Dedekind ring) is an integral domain that has the property that every non-zero proper ideal factors uniquely into a product of prime ideals. The concept generalizes the fundamental theorem of arithmetic from the integers to more general rings, and arose from Richard Dedekind's work on algebraic number theory in the 1870s, where unique factorization of elements fails in rings of algebraic integers but unique factorization of ideals holds.

Characterizations

An integral domain R is a Dedekind domain if and only if it satisfies three conditions: it is Noetherian, it has Krull dimension one (every non-zero prime ideal is maximal), and it is integrally closed in its field of fractions. Equivalently, a Noetherian domain is Dedekind if and only if every localization at a prime ideal is a discrete valuation ring or a field.

Examples

Every principal ideal domain, and hence every unique factorization domain of dimension one, is a Dedekind domain; this includes the ring of integers and polynomial rings in one variable over a field. The most important examples are the rings of integers of number fields. Not every Dedekind domain has unique factorization of elements: in the ring Z[√−5], for instance, 6 = 2·3 = (1+√−5)(1−√−5) are genuinely different factorizations, but the corresponding ideals factor uniquely into prime ideals. The extent to which unique factorization fails is measured by the ideal class group; a Dedekind domain is a unique factorization domain if and only if its class group is trivial.

Fractional ideals

The natural setting for the theory is that of fractional ideals, which form an abelian group under multiplication. In a Dedekind domain the quotient of this group by the subgroup of principal fractional ideals is the ideal class group, a central object of algebraic number theory; for rings of integers of number fields it is always finite.
