The Chumashan languages are a group of Native American languages spoken by the Chumash peoples along the southern California coast, from approximately San Luis Obispo in the north to Malibu in the south, and inland to the edge of the San Joaquin Valley, as well as on the northern Channel Islands. Chumashan is generally treated as a small language family whose external relationships remain undemonstrated, though it has been tentatively linked to proposed groupings such as Hokan.

Languages

The family is conventionally divided into Northern Chumash (Obispeño), Central Chumash (including Purisimeño, Ineseño, Barbareño, and Ventureño), and Southern or Island Chumash (including Cruzeño, spoken on the Channel Islands). The languages were closely related but generally not mutually intelligible. Franciscan missionaries recorded vocabulary and texts after the founding of missions in the region from the 1770s onward, and the linguist John Peabody Harrington amassed extensive documentation in the early twentieth century working with the last fluent speakers.

Extinction and revitalisation

Spanish missionisation, disease, and later American settlement devastated Chumash communities, and the languages fell out of daily use during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Mary Yee, the last native speaker of Barbareño, died in 1965. In recent decades, Chumash descendants have pursued language revitalisation using archival recordings and Harrington's notes; a dictionary of Samala (Ineseño) and language classes have been produced by the Santa Ynez Band of Chumash Indians.

Features

Chumashan languages are known for their rich consonant systems, including glottalised consonants and aspirated series, and for extensive consonant harmony (sibilant harmony), in which sibilants within a word must agree in their place of articulation. Their phonologies and morphologies have been of considerable interest to linguistic theory.
