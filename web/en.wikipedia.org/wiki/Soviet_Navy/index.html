The Soviet Navy (Russian: Военно-морской флот СССР, Voyenno-morskoy flot SSSR) was the naval warfare uniform service branch of the Soviet Armed Forces. Often referred by Western writers with the abbreviation VMF, the Soviet Navy was, for much of the Cold War, the second most powerful navy in the world, charged with disrupting NATO sea lines of communication and protecting the Soviet Union's maritime flanks.

History

The navy grew out of the remnants of the Imperial Russian Navy after the Revolution of 1917 and the Civil War. In the interwar period it remained a largely coastal force of cruisers, destroyers, and submarines. During the Second World War — the Great Patriotic War — its surface fleet played a limited role, but naval infantry, river flotillas, and lend-lease-supported operations contributed to the defence of Leningrad, the Black Sea campaigns, and the Arctic convoys.

Under Admiral Sergey Gorshkov, commander-in-chief from 1956 to 1985, the navy was transformed into a blue-water, nuclear-powered force. The Soviet Union built large numbers of nuclear attack and ballistic-missile submarines, missile-armed cruisers and destroyers, and naval aviation, and maintained a standing squadron in the Mediterranean. The ballistic-missile submarine force became a central leg of Soviet nuclear deterrence.

Structure

The navy was organised into four fleets — the Northern Fleet (headquartered at Severomorsk), the Pacific Fleet (Vladivostok), the Black Sea Fleet (Sevastopol), and the Baltic Fleet (Baltiysk and Kronshtadt) — plus the Caspian Flotilla. It also controlled naval aviation, naval infantry (marines), and coastal artillery and rocket troops.

Dissolution

With the dissolution of the Soviet Union in December 1991, the navy's ships, bases, and personnel were divided principally among Russia, which inherited the bulk of the fleet as the Russian Navy, and Ukraine, whose share of the Black Sea Fleet was settled by treaty in 1997. Other successor states received smaller coastal forces.
