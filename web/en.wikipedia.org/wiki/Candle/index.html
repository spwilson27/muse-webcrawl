Candle
MaterialWax (beeswax, paraffin, soy), wick
UseLighting, fragrance, religious ritual, decoration
Luminous intensityApproximately 1 candela

A candle is an ignitable wick embedded in wax, or another flammable solid substance such as tallow, that provides light, and in some cases, a fragrance. A candle can also provide heat or a method of keeping time.

A person who makes candles is traditionally known as a chandler. Mass production of candles began in the mid-19th century with the introduction of cheap paraffin wax, a by-product of petroleum refining, and stearin.

History

Early candles were made in many independent civilisations using available materials: the ancient Egyptians used rushlights of reed soaked in animal fat; the Romans developed the true dipped candle from tallow; in China, candles were made from whale fat and later from insects and seeds; in India from cinnamon fruit wax. Beeswax candles were used in Europe from the Middle Ages, prized for burning cleanly without a smoky flame, and were widely used for church ceremonies.

Construction and burning

A candle works as the flame's heat melts the top layer of wax, which is drawn up the wick by capillary action and vaporised to fuel the flame. Modern candles are made from paraffin wax, beeswax, soy wax, and other plant waxes, with cotton or paper wicks; lead-core wicks were phased out over health concerns. The SI unit of luminous intensity, the candela, is named after the candle and roughly corresponds to the light of one common candle.

Uses

Candles remain used for religious ceremonies, vigils, festive occasions such as birthdays and Hanukkah, romantic and decorative settings, emergency lighting during power failures, and in scented form for aromatherapy.
