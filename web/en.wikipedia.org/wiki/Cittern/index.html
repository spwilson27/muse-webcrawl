Cittern

The cittern or cithren is a plucked stringed instrument that was popular in Europe during the Renaissance. Like the lute, to which it is related, it has a flat-backed, pear- or teardrop-shaped body, but it is strung with metal (wire) strings and played with a plectrum, giving it a bright, ringing tone.

History

The cittern developed in the early 16th century, probably from the medieval citole. It typically had four courses of double strings tuned in a re-entrant fashion that made it easy to play in a few popular keys, which made it a favourite amateur instrument — cheaper and simpler than the lute. Citterns hung in barbers' shops for waiting customers to play, a detail Shakespeare alluded to. Composers such as Thomas Morley and Anthony Holborne published music for it.

By the mid-18th century the cittern had largely fallen from fashion, though related folk instruments survive, notably the Portuguese guitar (guitarra portuguesa), used in fado, and the Corsican cetara. The modern cittern was revived in the 1970s by folk musicians and luthiers such as Stefan Sobell, typically with five courses and used in Celtic music.
