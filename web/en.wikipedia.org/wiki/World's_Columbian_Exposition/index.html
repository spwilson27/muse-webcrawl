The World's Columbian Exposition, also known as the Chicago World's Fair, was a world's fair held in Chicago in 1893 to celebrate the 400th anniversary of Christopher Columbus's arrival in the Americas. Centred on a grand Court of Honor with white neoclassical buildings around a lagoon — the "White City" — the fair drew some 27 million visitors and is regarded as a landmark in American architecture, urban planning and popular culture.

Design

Director of Works Daniel Burnham coordinated architects including Richard Morris Hunt, Charles Follen McKim and Henry Van Brunt, with landscape architect Frederick Law Olmsted laying out the Jackson Park grounds. The principal buildings were clad in white-painted staff, a temporary plaster-like material, and lit by thousands of electric lights using Westinghouse alternating current — a spectacular demonstration of AC power after the "war of the currents". The Republic statue by Daniel Chester French presided over the basin. The Women's Building, designed by Sophia Hayden, showcased women's achievements.

Attractions and legacy

The Midway Plaisance introduced the original Ferris Wheel, built by George Washington Gale Ferris Jr. to rival the Eiffel Tower, along with ethnological exhibits and amusements that defined later amusement parks. New products introduced or popularised included the hamburger, Cracker Jack, and Shredded Wheat. The fair's Beaux-Arts classicism strongly influenced the City Beautiful movement and American civic design. Most structures were temporary; the Palace of Fine Arts, rebuilt in stone, survives as the Griffin Museum of Science and Industry. The fair is also remembered as the setting for the crimes of serial killer H. H. Holmes.
