Ancient law

[Image: Fragments of the Twelve Tables displayed in a museum — Rome's earliest written law code, traditionally dated to 451–450 BC]

Ancient law refers to the legal systems of the ancient world, including the law codes of Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Hebrew Bible, Greece and Rome. The earliest surviving written laws date from the third millennium BC in Mesopotamia.

Mesopotamia

Sumerian and Babylonian kings promulgated codes such as those of Ur-Namma (c. 2100 BC) and Hammurabi (c. 1750 BC), written in cuneiform and displayed publicly on stelae. They covered crime, family, property, trade and slavery, frequently using talionic penalties (see lex talionis) and class-differentiated fines.

Greece and Rome

Greek law developed in the city-states, with lawgivers such as Draco and Solon at Athens and a distinctive reliance on large lay juries. Roman law began with the Twelve Tables (c. 450 BC) and grew through the work of magistrates and jurists into a sophisticated system later codified under Justinian in the Corpus Juris Civilis, which became foundational for the civil law tradition. The office of tribune of the plebs was one of its distinctive constitutional creations.

Other traditions

Important ancient legal traditions also include Hebrew law (the Torah's covenant code), Egyptian law administered by pharaonic officials and temples, Chinese law under the Legalist-influenced imperial codes, and Indian law reflected in the Dharmashastra literature.

Study

The modern study of ancient law combines legal history, philology and archaeology; it is associated with scholars such as Henry Maine, whose Ancient Law (1861) famously described a movement "from status to contract".
