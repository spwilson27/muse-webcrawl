Existence precedes essence

"Existence precedes essence" (French: l'existence précède l'essence) is the central slogan of existentialism, coined by the French philosopher Jean-Paul Sartre in his 1945 lecture Existentialism Is a Humanism. The phrase means that a human being first exists — is thrown into the world — and only afterward defines himself or herself through choices and actions. There is no predetermined human nature or essence given by God or biology that fixes what a person must be.

Explanation

Sartre illustrated the contrast with a manufactured object such as a paper-knife: for an artefact, essence precedes existence, because the artisan conceives its design and purpose before making it. If God is conceived as a supernatural artisan, human beings would likewise have an essence preceding their existence. Sartre, arguing from an atheistic standpoint, held that no such divine blueprint exists: "Man is nothing else but what he makes of himself."

The doctrine carries two consequences Sartre emphasised: radical freedom and radical responsibility. Because there is no excuse in determinism, heredity, or divine command, each person is wholly responsible for what he or she becomes — a condition Sartre described with the words "condemned to be free," accompanied by anguish and the temptation of "bad faith" (self-deception about one's freedom).

Background and criticism

Ideas resembling the slogan appear earlier in Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, and Heidegger, though Heidegger distanced himself from Sartre's formulation in his Letter on Humanism (1947), arguing that reversing a metaphysical statement remains a metaphysical statement. Critics have objected that the slogan exaggerates human freedom by neglecting social, biological, and historical constraints; Sartre himself later qualified the view in the Critique of Dialectical Reason (1960).
