Hydroponics

Hydroponics is a method of growing plants without soil, using a water-based solution rich in mineral nutrients. The roots may be supported by an inert medium such as perlite, gravel, mineral wool, or coconut coir, or suspended directly in the nutrient solution or in air misted with it (aeroponics). The word derives from the Greek hydro (water) and ponos (labour).

Techniques

Common systems include the nutrient film technique (NFT), in which a thin stream of solution flows along channels; deep water culture, where roots hang in oxygenated solution; ebb-and-flow (flood and drain) systems; drip systems; wick systems; and aeroponics. Nutrient composition, pH (usually 5.5–6.5), and electrical conductivity are monitored to keep the solution optimal.

Advantages and disadvantages

Hydroponics offers faster growth, higher yields per unit area, precise nutrient control, greatly reduced water use through recirculation, and freedom from soil-borne pests and weeds; it is widely used for commercial lettuce, tomatoes, herbs, and strawberries, and is researched for spaceflight. Drawbacks include high initial and energy costs, the need for electricity and technical skill, and the rapid spread of water-borne disease through shared reservoirs.

History

Although floating gardens existed earlier, modern hydroponics developed from plant-nutrition experiments in the 19th and early 20th centuries; the term was coined in 1937 by William Frederick Gericke, who publicized soilless tomato growing at the University of California.
