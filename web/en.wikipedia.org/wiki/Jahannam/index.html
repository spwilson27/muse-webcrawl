Jahannam (Arabic: جهنم, jahannam) is the Islamic concept of Hell, the place of punishment in the afterlife for unbelievers and evildoers. The term derives from the Hebrew Gehenna (Ge Hinnom), the valley outside Jerusalem associated with fire and punishment, and it appears frequently in the Quran, which describes Jahannam as a fire prepared for those who reject God and commit grave sins.

Description

Quranic descriptions depict Jahannam as a blazing fire with seven gates, each gate assigned a portion of the damned (Quran 15:43–44). Its punishments include scalding water, garments of fire, the bitter fruit of the tree of Zaqqum, and chains; its keepers are stern angels led by the guardian Malik. The Quran alternates these warnings with descriptions of Jannah, the garden of Paradise, as part of its eschatological teaching about the Day of Judgment (Yawm al-Qiyamah).

Levels and theology

Later Islamic tradition elaborated seven levels of Jahannam, from the uppermost — sometimes reserved for sinful believers who would eventually be released — to the deepest pit for hypocrites. Muslim theologians debated the duration of punishment: the mainstream (Ash'ari and Maturidi) view holds that Muslim sinners may be punished temporarily and eventually admitted to Paradise through God's mercy and the intercession of Muhammad, while unbelievers remain eternally. Some theologians, such as Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim, were associated with controversial discussions of whether Hell itself might ultimately be emptied or its torment end, though interpretations of their views remain disputed.

In the afterlife journey

In popular eschatology, the dead pass through the Barzakh and, after resurrection and judgment, cross the bridge of al-Sirat over Jahannam; the righteous pass swiftly to Paradise while the wicked fall into the fire below.
