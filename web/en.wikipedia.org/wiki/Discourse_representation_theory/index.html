Discourse representation theory

Discourse representation theory (DRT) is a framework in formal semantics, developed by Hans Kamp in the early 1980s, for representing the meaning of natural language discourses — sequences of sentences — rather than isolated sentences. Its central innovation is the discourse representation structure (DRS), a two-part representation consisting of a set of discourse referents and a set of conditions on them, usually drawn as a box.

Motivation

DRT was designed to handle phenomena that classical predicate logic treats awkwardly, above all anaphora across sentence boundaries and the semantics of indefinite noun phrases. In the famous example "A man walks in the park. He whistles.", the indefinite "a man" in the first sentence licenses the pronoun "he" in the second, behaving as if it had existential force with scope extending beyond its own sentence. In DRT, the indefinite introduces a discourse referent into the DRS, which remains accessible to later sentences; the whole discourse is then interpreted by embedding the DRS into a model.

Construction and interpretation

Comprehension proceeds by a construction algorithm that processes sentences one at a time, updating the current DRS with new referents and conditions. A DRS is true in a model if there is an embedding function mapping its discourse referents to individuals in the model that satisfies all of its conditions. Negation, conditionals, and quantification create subordinate DRSs (sub-boxes), and accessibility relations between boxes regulate which referents pronouns may pick up, correctly ruling out anaphora into, for example, the scope of a negation in "It is not true that a man walks. *He whistles."

Related work

DRT is closely related to Irene Heim's file change semantics, developed independently at about the same time. Extensions of the framework include Segmented DRT, which models rhetorical relations between discourse segments, and treatments of presupposition, tense, and plurals.
