Viral vector vaccine

A viral vector vaccine is a vaccine that uses a modified, harmless virus (the vector) to deliver genetic material encoding an antigen from a target pathogen into the recipient's cells. The cells produce the antigen and present it to the immune system, generating antibody and T-cell responses without causing the target disease. The vector itself is engineered to be replication-deficient (non-replicating) or, in some designs, replication-competent.

Vectors used

Common vectors include adenoviruses (human serotypes such as Ad26 and Ad5, and chimpanzee adenoviruses such as ChAdOx1), vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), measles virus and poxviruses such as modified vaccinia Ankara (MVA). A key challenge is pre-existing immunity to the vector, which can blunt the response; rare serotypes, non-human vectors and heterologous prime–boost regimens (using different vectors for each dose) are used to overcome it.

Examples

The first licensed viral vector vaccine for human use was the VSV-based Ebola vaccine Ervebo (rVSV-ZEBOV), approved in 2019. During the COVID-19 pandemic, adenovirus-vectored vaccines — the Oxford–AstraZeneca vaccine (ChAdOx1), Johnson & Johnson's Janssen vaccine (Ad26), Russia's Sputnik V (Ad26/Ad5 prime–boost) and CanSino's Convidecia (Ad5) — were administered in billions of doses worldwide. The platform is also used in veterinary vaccines and is under study for HIV, malaria, tuberculosis and cancer immunotherapy.
