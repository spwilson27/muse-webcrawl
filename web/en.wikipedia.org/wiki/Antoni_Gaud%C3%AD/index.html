Antoni Gaudí i Cornet (25 June 1852 – 10 June 1926) was a Catalan architect from Spain, the leading figure of Catalan Modernisme. His highly individual, nature-inspired style produced some of Barcelona's most famous buildings, seven of which are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Life and career

Gaudí was born in the province of Tarragona, the son of a coppersmith, and studied architecture in Barcelona, graduating in 1878. His early work drew on Gothic and Moorish revivals, but he soon developed an organic language of curved stone, twisting iron, coloured tile (trencadís) and hyperbolic geometry. With the patronage of industrialist Eusebi Güell he built the Palau Güell, the crypt at Colònia Güell and Park Güell. Casa Batlló (1904–06) and Casa Milà "La Pedrera" (1906–12) crowned his mature style.

Sagrada Família

From 1883 Gaudí worked on the expiatory church of the Sagrada Família; from 1914 he devoted himself to it exclusively, living on site in his final years. He was struck by a tram on 7 June 1926 and died three days later; construction of the basilica continues into the 21st century following his models and drawings.

Style and legacy

Gaudí's structures imitate natural forms — trees, bones, shells — and integrate craft, sculpture and engineering. Long dismissed by modernist orthodoxy, his work was championed by Salvador Dalí and is now among the most visited architecture in the world. A cause for his beatification has been opened by the Catholic Church.
