Color (or colour) is the visual perception based on the electromagnetic spectrum. It arises from the way light interacts with objects — through absorption, reflection, emission and interference — and from the response of the observer's visual system. Though the spectrum is continuous, human perception categorises it into named colours such as red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet.

Physics and perception

Visible light spans wavelengths of roughly 380 to 750 nanometres. The retina contains three types of cone cells sensitive to short, medium and long wavelengths, giving most humans trichromatic vision; signals from the cones are combined into opponent channels (red–green, blue–yellow, light–dark) in the brain. Because perception depends on this physiology, two physically different spectra — metamers — can appear as the same colour. Colour is therefore a property of perception, not of light itself.

Colour models and reproduction

Artists and scientists describe colour with attributes of hue, saturation and lightness. Additive mixing of red, green and blue light (RGB) underlies screens, while subtractive mixing of cyan, magenta, yellow and black inks (CMYK) is used in printing. Standardised systems such as CIE 1931 colour spaces and colour charts allow precise specification and reproduction.

Language and culture

Languages differ in how many basic colour terms they have, and colour carries strong cultural and symbolic associations — in art, heraldry, signalling and design. Disorders of colour vision, most commonly red–green colour blindness, affect about 8% of men.
