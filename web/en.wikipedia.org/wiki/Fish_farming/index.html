Fish farming or pisciculture involves commercial breeding of fish, most often for food, in fish tanks or artificial enclosures such as fish ponds. It is a particular type of aquaculture, which is the controlled cultivation and harvesting of aquatic animals such as fish, crustaceans, molluscs and so on, in natural or pseudo-natural environments. A facility that releases juvenile fish into the wild for recreational fishing or to supplement a species' natural numbers is generally referred to as a fish hatchery.

Methods

Traditional pond culture, practised in China for over two thousand years, raises species such as carp in fertilised ponds. Modern intensive farming uses floating sea cages for salmon and sea bass, flow-through raceways for trout, and recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS) that filter and reuse water indoors, allowing production close to markets with tight environmental control. Integrated multi-trophic aquaculture combines fed species with extractive species such as shellfish and seaweed that absorb waste nutrients.

Importance

With wild capture fisheries at or near their sustainable limits, aquaculture has been the fastest-growing food production sector for decades and now provides about half of the fish consumed worldwide. China is by far the largest producer. Farmed salmon, shrimp, tilapia and pangasius are major internationally traded commodities.

Issues

Fish farming is criticised for the environmental impact of waste nutrients and chemicals, the spread of disease and sea lice to wild fish, escapes of farmed fish, and the use of wild-caught fish as feed for carnivorous species such as salmon. Feed conversion has improved markedly, and feeds increasingly use plant proteins and algal or insect oils. Animal welfare, antibiotic use and the siting of sea cages remain active areas of regulation and research.
