Dracunculiasis, also called Guinea-worm disease, is a parasitic infection caused by the nematode Dracunculus medinensis. People become infected by drinking unfiltered water containing tiny crustaceans (copepods) that harbour the worm's larvae. About a year later the mature female worm, which may approach a metre in length, creates a painful blister — usually on the leg or foot — and emerges through the skin; the burning pain drives sufferers to immerse the limb in water, where the worm releases larvae and the cycle continues.

Treatment

There is no drug that kills the worm and no vaccine. The traditional treatment, used since antiquity, is to wind the emerging worm slowly around a stick or gauze a few centimetres a day over weeks, taking care not to break it; the method is often cited as a possible origin of the Rod of Asclepius symbol of medicine. Care centres on wound cleaning, antisepsis and preventing secondary bacterial infection.

Eradication campaign

Dracunculiasis is on the verge of becoming the second human disease — after smallpox — to be eradicated, and the first achieved without a vaccine or drug. When the campaign led by the Carter Center and the WHO began in 1986, an estimated 3.5 million cases occurred annually in 20 countries in Africa and Asia. Interventions centred on filtering drinking water through cloth, providing safe water sources, health education and case containment. Annual human cases have since fallen to a mere handful in a few central African countries, though infections found in dogs and other animals in Chad and elsewhere have complicated the final stage of eradication.
