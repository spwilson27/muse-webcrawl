Zohar

The Zohar (Hebrew: זֹהַר, Zohar, "Splendor" or "Radiance") is a foundational work in the literature of Jewish mystical thought known as Kabbalah. It is a group of books including commentary on the mystical aspects of the Torah and scriptural interpretations, as well as material on mysticism, mythical cosmogony, and mystical psychology. Written largely in Aramaic, the Zohar appeared in Spain in the 13th century and was published by the Jewish scholar Moses de León, who attributed it to the 2nd-century rabbi Shimon bar Yochai.

Contents and structure

The Zohar consists of several sections, the most important being the commentary on the Torah framed as discussions among Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai and his companions as they travel through the Galilee. Other parts include the Sifra di-Tzeniuta ("Book of Concealment"), the Idrot ("Assemblies"), and the later Tikkunei Zohar and Zohar Hadash. Its teachings revolve around the nature of God (Ein Sof), the ten sefirot through which God interacts with creation, the soul, and the religious significance of the commandments.

Authorship debate

The work's attribution to the Tannaitic sage Shimon bar Yochai was questioned as early as the medieval period. Modern scholarship, beginning with Gershom Scholem, has concluded on linguistic and historical grounds that the main body of the Zohar was composed in 13th-century Castile, most likely by Moses de León and possibly a circle of kabbalists around him. Despite this, many traditional communities continue to regard the work as ancient and authoritative.

Influence

After its printing in the 16th century, the Zohar became the central text of Kabbalah, shaping Lurianic Kabbalah in Safed, Hasidic Judaism, and later Jewish mystical and scholarly study. Its weekly portions are still read and studied widely.
