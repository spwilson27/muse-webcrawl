Andrés Bonifacio y de Castro (30 November 1863 – 10 May 1897) was a Filipino revolutionary leader, often considered the "Father of the Philippine Revolution". He founded the secret society Katipunan, which in August 1896 launched the revolt against Spanish colonial rule. He is commemorated as a national hero of the Philippines, with his birthday, Bonifacio Day, a public holiday on 30 November.

Early life

Bonifacio was born in Tondo, Manila, to a mestizo family. Orphaned at 14, he supported his siblings through odd jobs, working variously as a clerk, salesman and warehouseman for foreign trading firms while educating himself by reading widely — including Rizal's novels, histories of the French Revolution and the lives of American presidents.

The Katipunan

In 1892 Bonifacio joined Rizal's Liga Filipina and, after its suppression, founded the Kataas-taasang, Kagalanggalangang Katipunan ng mga Anak ng Bayan (KKK) on 7 July 1892, dedicated to outright independence through armed revolution. As its Supremo, he organised its expansion across Manila and neighbouring provinces. The society's discovery by the Spanish authorities in August 1896 forced the Cry of Pugad Lawin, the opening act of the revolution.

Death

As the revolution spread, a rival faction led by Emilio Aguinaldo gained control. At the Tejeros Convention of March 1897 Bonifacio was displaced, and after repudiating the proceedings he was arrested, tried by a military court on charges of sedition and treason, and executed with his brother Procopio on 10 May 1897 near Mount Nagpatong in Maragondon, Cavite.

Legacy

The Bonifacio Monument in Caloocan and the city of Bonifacio Global City bear his name. Historians debate whether he should be regarded as the first president of the Philippines, a title he effectively held as head of the Katipunan's revolutionary government.
