Helium mass spectrometer

A helium mass spectrometer is an instrument commonly used to detect and locate small leaks in sealed containers or vacuum systems. It is a type of mass spectrometer tuned to detect helium, which is used as a tracer gas: because helium atoms are small, chemically inert, non-toxic and present in the atmosphere only in trace amounts (about 5 ppm), even minute leaks can be measured with high sensitivity. The technique was developed during the Manhattan Project to test vacuum equipment for uranium enrichment plants.

A helium mass spectrometer leak detector

Operation

The test object is evacuated and connected to the detector, or placed in a chamber filled with helium. Helium entering through any leak is drawn into the instrument's vacuum system, ionised, and accelerated into a magnetic field. The spectrometer is tuned to the mass-to-charge ratio of helium-4 (m/z = 4), and the ion current is proportional to the helium partial pressure, giving a quantitative leak rate, commonly expressed in mbar·L/s, Pa·m³/s, or std cc/s. Modern instruments can detect leak rates as small as 10−12 Pa·m³/s.

Methods

Common test configurations include the vacuum method (object evacuated inside, helium sprayed outside), the pressure or "bombing" method (object pressurised with helium, then placed in a vacuum chamber), and sniffing (helium applied inside the object and a probe samples the exterior). Helium leak testing is used widely in refrigeration and air-conditioning manufacture, semiconductor and vacuum technology, aerospace, automotive fuel systems, and packaging.
