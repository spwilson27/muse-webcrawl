Operator (biology)

Operator
FieldMolecular biology / genetics
TypeDNA regulatory sequence
FunctionBinding site for a repressor protein within an operon
Classic exampleThe operator of the lac operon of E. coli

In genetics, an operator is a segment of DNA to which a repressor protein binds. It is a classic regulatory sequence forming part of an operon, a cluster of genes transcribed together in bacteria and archaea. By binding to the operator, the repressor physically blocks RNA polymerase from binding to the adjacent promoter, or from proceeding along the DNA, thereby switching off transcription of the operon's structural genes.

The lac operon model

The best-known example is the lac operon of Escherichia coli, described by François Jacob and Jacques Monod in 1961. When lactose is absent, the LacI repressor binds the operator and prevents transcription of the genes needed to metabolise lactose. When lactose is present, its isomer allolactose binds the repressor, changing its shape (an inducer effect) so that it releases the operator and the genes are transcribed. The lac system in fact has three operator sites (O1, O2 and O3) that cooperate through DNA looping to produce tight repression.

Other operators

The trp operon of E. coli is regulated by a repressor that binds its operator only when activated by tryptophan (a corepressor), and the lambda phage operators OL and OR govern the switch between lysogenic and lytic growth. Operators have defined a general principle of gene regulation: short DNA sequences serving as binding sites for regulatory proteins, now studied as part of the broader field of gene regulatory networks.
