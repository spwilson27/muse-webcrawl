Dyscalculia

Dyscalculia is a learning disability in mathematics. People with dyscalculia have a poor understanding of number concepts, number sense, and arithmetic, often struggling to learn number facts and procedures despite normal intelligence and adequate schooling. Its prevalence is estimated at roughly 3–7% of the population, comparable to dyslexia, with which it frequently co-occurs.

Characteristics

Common signs include difficulty counting, comparing quantities, remembering multiplication tables, telling the time on analogue clocks, handling money, and estimating distances or durations. Many people with dyscalculia rely on finger counting well beyond the age at which peers have memorised arithmetic facts.

Causes

Research associates dyscalculia with atypical functioning of the intraparietal sulcus, a region involved in representing numerical magnitude. Both genetic and environmental factors appear to contribute, and acquired forms ("acalculia") can follow brain injury or stroke.

Diagnosis and support

Diagnosis is usually made through standardised tests of arithmetic and number processing that reveal performance substantially below age expectations. Support strategies include multisensory teaching, concrete manipulatives, targeted practice of number bonds, and classroom accommodations such as extra time and access to calculators.
