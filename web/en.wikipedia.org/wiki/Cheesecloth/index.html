Cheesecloth

Cheesecloth is a loose-woven gauze-like carded cotton cloth used primarily in cheese making and cooking. It is also used in various other applications, including theatrical productions, where it is often dyed and draped to create ghostly effects, and in printmaking, where it is known as tarlatan when heavily starched.

Grades

Cheesecloth is available in at least seven different grades, from open to extra-fine weave. Grades are distinguished by the amount of material per square yard, and range from grade 10 (the most open weave, roughly 8 threads per inch in each direction) to grade 90 (the finest standard grade, roughly 44 by 36 threads per inch). Higher-grade cheesecloth is denser and better suited to straining fine particles; lower grades drain quickly and are used for wrapping and pressing cheese curd.

Uses

The primary use of cheesecloth is in cheese making, where it lines moulds, holds curds together while whey drains, and is wrapped around wheels of cheese during pressing and ageing. In the kitchen it is used to strain stocks and broths, to bundle herbs into a bouquet garni, to drain yogurt into labneh or Greek-style yogurt, to squeeze liquid from grated vegetables, and to wrap fruit in puddings such as the traditional British Christmas pudding.

Beyond food, cheesecloth serves in agriculture and beekeeping, in dusting and polishing furniture, in automotive detailing, and in the graphic arts, where tarlatan wipes excess ink from an intaglio plate. Because it is inexpensive and biodegradable, unbleached cotton cheesecloth is also a common material for eco-friendly household cleaning.

History

Gauze-like fabrics have been woven in India and the Middle East for millennia, and the English name derives straightforwardly from the cloth's association with cheese production in dairies. Industrial production of standardised grades developed in the nineteenth century as commercial cheese making expanded in Europe and North America.
