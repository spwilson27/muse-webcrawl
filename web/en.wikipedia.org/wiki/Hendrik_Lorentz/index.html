Hendrik Antoon Lorentz (18 July 1853 – 4 February 1928) was a Dutch theoretical physicist who shared the 1902 Nobel Prize in Physics with Pieter Zeeman for the discovery and theoretical explanation of the Zeeman effect. He derived the Lorentz transformation of the special theory of relativity, as well as the Lorentz force, which describes the combined electric and magnetic forces acting on a charged particle in an electromagnetic field. Lorentz was also responsible for the Lorentz oscillator model, the Lorentzian theory of electrons, and his name is given to the Lorentz–Lorenz formula and many other concepts.

According to the biography published by the Nobel Foundation, "It may well be said that Lorentz was regarded by all theoretical physicists as the supreme leader of their field, always exercising an unrivalled influence." Albert Einstein praised him as a mentor and "a living work of art"; the ideas Lorentz developed — length contraction, local time, and the transformation equations that bear his name — formed the immediate mathematical foundation on which Einstein built special relativity in 1905.

Early life and career

Lorentz was born in Arnhem, Gelderland, the son of a nursery owner. He studied physics and mathematics at Leiden University, where he was strongly influenced by the astronomer Frederik Kaiser. In 1875, at the age of 22, he received his doctorate with a thesis on the theory of the reflection and refraction of light, refining Maxwell's electromagnetic theory. In 1878 he became professor of theoretical physics at Leiden, occupying a chair created specially for him — one of the first such chairs in Europe. He remained at Leiden for most of his career, moving in 1912 to a research position in Haarlem while continuing to lecture at Leiden.

Electron theory and the Zeeman effect

In the 1890s Lorentz developed his "theory of electrons", treating electric charge as carried by discrete charged particles within matter and showing how Maxwell's equations in a vacuum could be applied at the microscopic level. In 1896 his former student Pieter Zeeman discovered that spectral lines split in a magnetic field; Lorentz's electron theory explained the effect quantitatively and predicted the polarization of the split components, work for which the two shared the 1902 Nobel Prize. The force law he formulated — F = q(E + v × B) — remains fundamental to electrodynamics and plasma physics.

Lorentz transformations and relativity

Attempting to reconcile electrodynamics with the failure of experiments (notably Michelson–Morley) to detect motion through the luminiferous aether, Lorentz introduced the hypothesis of length contraction (1889–1892, independently proposed by George FitzGerald) and, by 1904, the full set of coordinate transformations now called the Lorentz transformations, including the "local time" variable. Henri Poincaré refined and named the transformations, and Einstein's special relativity reinterpreted them physically in 1905, dispensing with the aether. Lorentz accepted Einstein's theory while retaining a philosophical fondness for an aether framework.

Later years

Lorentz chaired the first Solvay Conference in 1911 and remained the elder statesman of European physics, presiding over the Solvay meetings until his death. After the First World War he served the Netherlands as chairman of the committee overseeing the Zuiderzee Works, applying hydrodynamic calculations to the design of the Afsluitdijk. He died in Haarlem in February 1928; his funeral was attended by dignitaries from across Europe, and Ernest Rutherford delivered a graveside oration.
