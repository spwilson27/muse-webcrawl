Punctation of Olmütz

The Punctation of Olmütz (German: Olmützer Punktation), also known as the Agreement of Olomouc, was a diplomatic agreement signed on 29 November 1850 at Olmütz (Olomouc) in Moravia between the Kingdom of Prussia and the Austrian Empire. It is often called the "Humiliation of Olmütz" (Schande von Olmütz), because Prussia was forced to abandon its attempt to unify Germany under its own leadership and to accept the restoration of Austrian predominance in the German Confederation.

Background

After the Revolutions of 1848, Prussia's foreign minister Joseph von Radowitz promoted the Erfurt Union, a plan for a "narrower union" of German states under Prussian leadership that would exclude Austria. Austria, recovered from revolution under Chancellor Felix of Schwarzenberg, insisted that the German Confederation of 1815 be restored in full. The two powers' rivalry came to a head over Hesse-Kassel and the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein, and by the autumn of 1850 Austria, backed by the threat of Russian intervention under Tsar Nicholas I, was mobilising. Prussia, militarily unprepared and diplomatically isolated, decided to give way.

Terms

Negotiated by Prussian minister Otto Theodor von Manteuffel with Schwarzenberg at Olmütz, the punctation provided that Prussia would renounce the Erfurt Union, demobilise its army, and consent to the restoration of the Confederation's Federal Diet at Frankfurt. The Hesse question was to be settled jointly, and the Holstein conflict was to be handled by the Confederation. A congress of ministers at Dresden would discuss possible reforms, but these came to nothing, and the Confederation was restored essentially in its pre-1848 form.

Consequences

The agreement preserved the Austro-Prussian "dualism" in Germany for another sixteen years and confirmed Russian influence in Central European affairs. In Prussia, the humiliation was keenly felt by nationalists and conservatives alike; the young Otto von Bismarck, then a Prussian delegate, defended the settlement in the chamber as the only realistic course, but the lesson — that the German question would be decided by power — shaped his later policy. The rivalry resumed and was finally resolved by the Austro-Prussian War of 1866, in which Prussia defeated Austria and dissolved the German Confederation.
