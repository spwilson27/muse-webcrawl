History of plant science

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Botanical illustration was the record-keeping of early plant science.

The history of plant science, or history of botany, runs from ancient herbal medicine through the classificatory work of Theophrastus and Linnaeus, the discovery of photosynthesis and plant cells, Mendel's genetics, and the molecular plant biology of the modern era, including the model organism Arabidopsis thaliana.

Ancient and medieval botany

Plants were catalogued for medicine and agriculture in Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Chinese (the Shennong Bencaojing) and Indian traditions. Theophrastus of Eresos (c. 371–287 BCE), a pupil of Aristotle, wrote the Enquiry into Plants and On the Causes of Plants, distinguishing some 500 species and founding botany as a discipline. Dioscorides' De Materia Medica (c. 60 CE) remained the standard pharmacopoeia for 1,500 years; medieval herbals and Islamic scholars such as Ibn al-Baitar extended it.

Renaissance to Linnaeus

Printed herbals (Brunfels, Fuchs, Gerard), the first botanical gardens (Pisa and Padua, 1540s) and global exploration flooded Europe with new species. John Ray advanced natural classification; Nehemiah Grew and Marcello Malpighi founded plant anatomy; Rudolf Camerarius demonstrated plant sexuality experimentally (1694). Carl Linnaeus's Species Plantarum (1753) fixed binomial nomenclature and a sexual system of classification, later superseded by the natural systems of Jussieu and de Candolle.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Stephen Hales's Vegetable Staticks (1727), Jan Ingenhousz's discovery of photosynthesis (1779) and Julius von Sachs's experimental physiology founded modern plant physiology. Schleiden's cell theory (1838), Darwin's work on climbing plants and insectivorous plants, and the rediscovery of Mendel's laws in 1900 — Mendel himself worked on peas — launched plant genetics. The 20th century added phytohormones, the Calvin cycle, Barbara McClintock's transposons, Norman Borlaug's Green Revolution wheats, and in 2000 the first plant genome sequence, that of Arabidopsis.
