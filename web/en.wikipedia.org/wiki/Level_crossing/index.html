Level crossing

A level crossing is an intersection where a railway line crosses a road, path, or another railway at the same level, as opposed to using a bridge or tunnel. The term is used internationally; in North America the equivalent term is grade crossing, and in Australia they are also called railway crossings.

Safety equipment

Crossings may be passive, relying on signs such as the crossbuck (St Andrew's cross) and road markings, or active, fitted with flashing lights, bells, and barriers or gates that descend as a train approaches. Half barriers block only the entry side of the road, while full barriers block both lanes. Because trains cannot stop quickly, level crossing collisions are often severe, and safety campaigns urge road users never to zig-zag around barriers.

History

Early railways crossed roads at grade as a matter of course, with crossing keepers and hand-operated gates at busy sites. As traffic grew, many countries began programmes to replace crossings with bridges, and new high-speed lines are generally built without any level crossings at all.

By country

Practices vary: the United States has over 200,000 crossings, many of them passive; the United Kingdom classifies crossings by type and user; Japan uses distinctive yellow-and-black barriers and audible alarms. See also railway safety.
