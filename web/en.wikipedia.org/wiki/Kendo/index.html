Kendo

Kendoka in bogu, wielding shinai

Kendo (Japanese: 剣道, "way of the sword") is a modern Japanese martial art descended from the classical swordsmanship (kenjutsu) of the samurai. Practitioners, called kendoka, fight with bamboo swords (shinai) while wearing protective armour (bogu), striking at designated targets — the head (men), wrist (kote), torso (dō) and throat (tsuki) — accompanied by a spirited shout (kiai).

History

Kendo's roots lie in the sword schools of feudal Japan. In the 18th century the development of bamboo practice swords and padded armour allowed full-contact training, and similar training methods were systematised during the Meiji era as gekken. After a ban under the Allied occupation following the Second World War, kendo was re-established in 1952 with the founding of the All Japan Kendo Federation, which also promotes the related arts of iaido and jodo.

Practice and equipment

Training comprises basic cutting practice (suburi), set forms (kata, performed with wooden bokutō), technique drills (keiko) and free sparring (jigeiko). A valid point (yūkō-datotsu) requires the correct part of the shinai to strike the correct target with proper posture, spirit and follow-through (zanshin). Practitioners wear a heavy cotton jacket (keikogi) and pleated trousers (hakama); ranks progress from kyū grades to dan grades up to 8th dan.

Philosophy

The stated purpose of kendo, as formulated by the All Japan Kendo Federation in 1975, is "to discipline the human character through the application of the principles of the katana". Etiquette (reigi) — bowing, correct handling of equipment, respect for opponents and teachers — is considered integral to the art.

International spread

Kendo is practised worldwide under the International Kendo Federation (FIK), founded in 1970; the World Kendo Championships have been held every three years since 1970. South Korea, where the art is known as kumdo, is Japan's principal rival at international level.
