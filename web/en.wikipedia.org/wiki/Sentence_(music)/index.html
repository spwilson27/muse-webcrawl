Sentence (music)

A musical sentence divides into a presentation phrase and a continuation phrase.

In music theory, a sentence is a type of musical theme or phrase structure, most fully described by Arnold Schoenberg in his treatise Fundamentals of Musical Composition and later elaborated by William Caplin in his theory of formal functions. A sentence consists of two main parts: a presentation phrase and a continuation phrase, which together typically span eight measures in the Classical style.

Structure

The presentation phrase itself divides into two parts: a basic idea (usually two measures) and its repetition, called the repetition of the basic idea. The repetition may be exact, varied, transposed, or sequential, but it confirms the basic idea as the thematic core of the passage. Together the basic idea and its repetition form a four-measure presentation.

The continuation phrase follows, also typically four measures. Its formal functions include fragmentation (breaking the basic idea into smaller motivic units), acceleration of harmonic rhythm, sequence, and liquidation of characteristic motives. The continuation drives toward a cadence—commonly a half cadence, an imperfect authentic cadence, or, in expanded sentences, a perfect authentic cadence—that closes the theme.

Sentence versus period

The sentence is one of the two principal "small theme" types in Caplin's theory, the other being the period. The crucial difference lies in the second phrase: a period's second phrase (the consequent) begins by restating the basic idea, whereas a sentence's continuation does not restate the opening but instead develops and fragments it. A hybrid type combines an antecedent phrase with a continuation phrase.

Examples

Famous examples of sentences include the opening theme of Beethoven's Piano Sonata Op. 13 ("Pathétique") slow movement's main theme and the opening of his Piano Sonata Op. 14 No. 1. Schoenberg analyzed numerous sentences from the works of Mozart, Beethoven, and Brahms, noting that sentences may be compressed or expanded well beyond the normative eight measures while retaining their formal functions.
