Spike fiddle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Spike fiddle
ClassificationBowed string instrument (chordophone)
Defining featureNeck continues as a spike through the resonator body
ExamplesErhu, rebab, masenqo, morin khuur

A spike fiddle, also called a spike lute in some classifications, is a bowed string instrument whose neck extends through the body of the resonator and protrudes at the bottom as a spike. The player rests the spike against the leg, the ground, or the body and plays the instrument held vertically or diagonally. Spike fiddles are among the most widespread traditional bowed instruments in the world, found across Asia, the Middle East, and Africa, and they are regarded by organologists as important ancestors in the development of bowed lutes.

Construction

The soundbox may be carved from wood, made from a gourd or coconut shell, or built up from staves, and is typically covered with a membrane of animal skin, though some types have wooden soundboards. The spike passes completely through the box, anchoring the strings, which run from pegs in the neck over a small bridge on the soundboard to the end of the spike. Most spike fiddles have one to three strings, historically of silk, gut, horsehair, or metal.

Types by region

East and Southeast Asia

The most famous spike fiddle is the Chinese erhu, a two-stringed instrument with a small snakeskin-covered soundbox, central to Chinese orchestral and solo music. Related instruments include the gaohu, zhonghu, and the Cambodian tro. In Mongolia the morin khuur (horsehead fiddle) is a larger spike fiddle with a trapezoid wooden box and a carved horse's head at the top of the neck.

Middle East and Indonesia

The rebab family, spread by Islamic cultures, includes the Arab and Turkish rababa and the Javanese and Malay rebab used in gamelan ensembles, with a heart-shaped or round coconut-shell body covered in skin.

Africa

African spike fiddles include the Ethiopian and Eritrean one-stringed masenqo, played by traditional bards, and various single-string fiddles of West Africa such as the Hausa goge.

History

Spike fiddles appear in medieval Islamic iconography and spread along trade routes across Eurasia and Africa. Scholars of organology group them in the Hornbostel–Sachs system among the spike lutes and spike fiddles, and they are frequently cited in discussions of the origin of the European violin family, though a direct line of descent remains debated.
