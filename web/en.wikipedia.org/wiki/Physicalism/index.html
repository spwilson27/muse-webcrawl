In philosophy, physicalism is the view that "everything is physical", that is, that there are no kinds of things other than physical things — or, in a common formulation, that everything supervenes on the physical. Physicalism is a form of ontological monism and is closely associated with, though not always identical to, materialism.

Definitions

The term was introduced by Otto Neurath and Rudolf Carnap in the 1930s, drawing on the "physical language" of the Vienna Circle. A standard modern definition: physicalism is true at a possible world if every positive fact at that world is necessitated by the physical facts. Debates turn on how "physical" is characterised — usually by reference to the entities and properties posited by physics.

Reductive and non-reductive physicalism

Reductive physicalism holds that higher-level facts (mental, biological, social) can be fully explained by, or reduced to, physical facts, as in type-identity theory of mind. Non-reductive physicalism accepts that everything depends on the physical but denies that higher-level properties are reducible; mental properties may be multiply realised by different physical states. Eliminative materialism goes further, holding that folk-psychological categories will be eliminated by mature neuroscience.

Hempel's dilemma

Carl Hempel posed a dilemma for defining physicalism: if "physical" means what current physics says, physicalism is almost certainly false because current physics is incomplete; if it means some ideal future physics, the thesis becomes vague or trivial. Responses include defining the physical negatively (non-mental) or via the theoretical commitments of a completed science.

Arguments for and against

Supporters cite the causal closure of the physical world, the success of physical explanation, and the dependence of mental states on brain states. Critics raise the knowledge argument (Frank Jackson's Mary), the conceivability of philosophical zombies (David Chalmers), and the explanatory gap concerning consciousness — the "hard problem" — as challenges that physicalism has yet to answer fully.
