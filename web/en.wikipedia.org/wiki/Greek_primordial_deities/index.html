Greek primordial deities

The first beings emerge from Chaos at the beginning of the world.

The Greek primordial deities (protogenoi, "first-born") are the first generation of gods in Greek mythology, personifying the fundamental elements of the universe. They appear at the very beginning of Hesiod's Theogony (c. 700 BCE), the earliest surviving Greek account of the origin of the gods, and precede the Titans and the Olympians. Unlike the anthropomorphic Olympians, the protogenoi are typically the physical elements they represent — Gaia is the earth itself, Pontus the sea, Tartarus the pit beneath the world.

Hesiod's account

The Theogony begins: "First of all came Chaos", then broad-breasted Gaia (Earth), dim Tartarus, and Eros (Desire), "fairest among the deathless gods". From Chaos came Erebus (Darkness) and black Nyx (Night); from the union of Erebus and Nyx came Aether (Brightness) and Hemera (Day). Gaia alone produced Uranus (Sky), the mountains, and Pontus (Sea).

Principal primordial deities

  • Chaos — the void or gap from which everything emerged.
  • Gaia — the Earth; mother of the sky, sea, mountains, Titans and giants.
  • Tartarus — the deep abyss below Hades, later a prison for the Titans.
  • Eros — desire, the procreative force (distinct from the later Cupid-like son of Aphrodite).
  • Erebus — darkness of the underworld.
  • Nyx — Night, mother of a grim brood including Thanatos (Death), Hypnos (Sleep), the Fates, Nemesis and Eris (Strife).
  • Aether — the bright upper air breathed by the gods.
  • Hemera — Day.
  • Uranus — the Sky, born of Gaia and later her consort; father of the Titans.
  • Pontus — the Sea, father of ancient sea gods such as Nereus and Thaumas.
  • Ourea — the mountains.

Other figures and later traditions

Other sources add or substitute other first beings: Ananke (Necessity), Chronos (Time — not to be confused with the Titan Cronus), Phanes the first-born creator of Orphic theogonies, Thesis, Hydros, and the Fates themselves. In the Orphic tradition, Chronos produced Aether and Chaos, and from the cosmic egg hatched Phanes. Homer, by contrast, calls Oceanus and Tethys the parents of the gods, and Alcman made the water-goddess Thetis the first principle — showing that Greek cosmogony was never a single fixed doctrine.

Family relationships

From Gaia and Uranus sprang the twelve Titans — Oceanus, Coeus, Crius, Hyperion, Iapetus, Theia, Rhea, Themis, Mnemosyne, Phoebe, Tethys and Cronus — as well as the Cyclopes and the hundred-handed Hecatonchires. The protogenoi thus stand at the root of the entire Greek divine genealogy: Chaos's line gives the powers of night and fate, Gaia's line gives the ruling dynasties of gods, successively Uranus, Cronus and Zeus.
