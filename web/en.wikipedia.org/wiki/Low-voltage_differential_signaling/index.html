Low-voltage differential signaling

Low-voltage differential signaling, or LVDS, also known as TIA/EIA-644, is a technical standard that specifies electrical characteristics of a differential, serial signaling standard. LVDS operates at low power and can run at very high speeds using inexpensive twisted-pair copper cables. It was introduced in 1994 by National Semiconductor and has become a widely used physical-layer standard in applications such as LCD panel interfaces, automotive infotainment, high-speed data links and scientific instruments.

Operation

An LVDS driver steers a constant current of about 3.5 mA through a pair of wires. At the receiver the current passes through a termination resistor of about 100 Ω, matched to the cable's characteristic impedance, producing a voltage difference of about 350 mV. Reversing the current direction reverses the polarity of the voltage across the resistor, encoding the logic state. Because the receiver responds only to the difference between the two wires, noise coupled equally into both conductors (common-mode noise) is rejected, and the small voltage swing minimises power consumption and electromagnetic emissions while allowing fast switching.

Applications

LVDS became ubiquitous in the Flat Panel Display Link (FPD-Link) used to drive laptop and monitor LCD panels, and in standards and systems including Camera Link, FireWire (IEEE 1394) physical layers, SCI, and the SpaceWire spacecraft bus. Related differential standards such as CML and LVPECL serve similar roles at higher speeds. Although newer interfaces such as eDP and MIPI DSI have displaced LVDS in many consumer displays, LVDS remains common in industrial and automotive equipment.

Advantages

The combination of low power, low EMI, good noise immunity and operation over inexpensive twisted-pair cabling made LVDS one of the most successful physical-layer standards of the digital era. The standard specifies only the electrical layer, leaving the protocol above it to the application.
