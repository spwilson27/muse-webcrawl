Triple cream

Triple cream
Country of originFrance
Source of milkCow
TextureSoft, creamy
Fat contentAt least 75% butterfat in dry matter

Triple cream (French: triple-crème) is a category of rich, soft cow's milk cheese from France in which extra cream is added to the milk before curdling, so that the finished cheese contains at least 75% butterfat in its dry matter (roughly 40% fat overall). Triple-cream cheeses are typically bloomy-rind cheeses, with a soft white rind formed by Penicillium candidum or similar moulds, and a smooth, buttery interior.

Characteristics

The addition of cream gives triple-cream cheeses their characteristic luxurious texture and mild, slightly tangy flavour. Young cheeses are firm and chalky at the centre; as they ripen the paste beneath the rind becomes runny and spreadable. Because of their high fat content, triple creams pair naturally with sparkling wines such as Champagne, whose acidity and bubbles cut through the richness of the paste.

Examples

  • Brillat-Savarin — created in the 1930s by cheesemaker Henri Androuët and named after the gastronome Jean Anthelme Brillat-Savarin; produced in Burgundy and Normandy.
  • Explorateur — a small cylindrical cheese from the Île-de-France, named in the space age after the first satellites.
  • Saint André — a widely exported triple cream made in Coutances, Normandy.
  • Délice de Bourgogne — made by Lincet in Burgundy from cow's milk enriched with crème fraîche.
  • Gratte-Paille and Pierre Robert — other well-known French triple creams.

Double cream cheeses

Double-cream cheeses follow the same principle but with a lower minimum fat content — at least 60% butterfat in dry matter. Classic examples include the original Brie and Camembert styles when made to richer recipes, and Petit-Suisse-style fresh cheeses. The category boundaries are matters of French dairy tradition and marketing rather than protected designations for most individual cheeses.
