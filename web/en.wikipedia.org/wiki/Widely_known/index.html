Widely known

This article discusses the general concept of something being widely known. For the related notion of fame, see Fame.

Something is described as widely known when it is recognised or understood by a large proportion of people within a relevant population. The phrase is used across journalism, scholarship, law, and everyday speech to signal that a fact, person, work, or event has achieved broad public awareness rather than being obscure or specialist knowledge.

Diffusion of information through a population leads to knowledge becoming widespread.

Usage

In encyclopedic and journalistic writing, "widely known for" is frequently used in the opening sentences of biographies to summarise a subject's notability. Style guides sometimes discourage the phrase as a form of "weasel wording" when it is used without attribution, since it asserts popularity without identifying who holds the knowledge. In legal contexts, courts may take judicial notice of facts that are widely known and not subject to reasonable dispute within a jurisdiction.

Related concepts

The concept overlaps with common knowledge, which in game theory and epistemology refers to information known by all members of a group, where each member also knows that the others know it. It is also related to public knowledge, collective memory, and cultural literacy — the body of references a culture assumes its members share.
