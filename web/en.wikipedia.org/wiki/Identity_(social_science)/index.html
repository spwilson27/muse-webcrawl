Identity (social science)

Identity
DisciplinesSociology, social psychology, anthropology, philosophy
Key theoristsErik Erikson, George H. Mead, Erving Goffman, Henri Tajfel, Stuart Hall
Key conceptsPersonal identity, social identity, role identity, identity politics
Social identity is built from group memberships, roles, and personal narratives.

In the social sciences, identity refers to a person's conception and expression of their individuality or group affiliations — the sense of who they are and how they are situated in relation to others. Identity is generally understood not as fixed and given but as constructed through social interaction, negotiated in everyday life, and liable to change over time. The concept spans individual ("personal identity"), relational, and collective ("social identity") levels.

Theoretical approaches

Erik Erikson made "identity" central to developmental psychology, describing adolescence as the crisis of identity versus role confusion and positing identity formation as a lifelong psychosocial task. George Herbert Mead distinguished the spontaneous "I" from the internalised social "me", locating the self in symbolic interaction. Erving Goffman analysed identity as performance, arguing that people manage the impressions they give in face-to-face interaction (The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life, 1959).

In social psychology, Henri Tajfel's social identity theory argues that part of a person's self-concept derives from membership in social groups, and that categorisation into in-groups and out-groups drives intergroup behaviour, including the minimal-group findings that mere assignment to groups produces favouritism. Identity theory (Stryker) complements this with a focus on roles: identities are tied to positions in the social structure, and the prominence and salience of a role identity shape behaviour across situations.

Collective and cultural identity

Cultural studies, following Stuart Hall, treats identities as hybrid, historically produced articulations rather than essences, particularly under conditions of migration and globalisation. The term identity politics denotes political mobilisation organised around shared social identities — race, gender, sexuality, ethnicity, religion — and has become one of the most contested concepts in contemporary political discourse, defended as a route to recognition and criticised for essentialism or fragmentation.

Identity in the digital age

Digital media have made identity performance more deliberate and persistent: profile pictures, curated feeds, avatars, and usernames are all identity work, while data brokers and algorithmic systems assemble identity from behavioural traces — the basis of "digital identity" and of debates over privacy, verification, and the right to be forgotten.
