The Bonfire of the Vanities


Tom Wolfe's 1987 novel of 1980s New York

The Bonfire of the Vanities is a 1987 novel by Tom Wolfe, his first work of fiction. The story is a drama about ambition, race, politics and greed in 1980s New York City, and its central metaphor — the "bonfire of the vanities" — recalls the 1497 Florentine burning of objectionable possessions under Savonarola. The novel was first serialized in Rolling Stone in 1984–85 before publication in book form by Farrar, Straus and Giroux.

Plot

Sherman McCoy, a wealthy Wall Street bond trader who privately regards himself as a "Master of the Universe", takes a wrong turn off the Triborough Bridge with his mistress Maria Ruskin and strikes Henry Lamb, a young Black man from the Bronx, in an apparent attempted robbery. The hit-and-run becomes a media and political sensation, propelling the careers of an alcoholic reporter, Peter Fallow; an ambitious assistant district attorney, Lawrence Kramer; and the demagogic Reverend Bacon, while McCoy is destroyed by the legal process and public humiliation.

Themes and reception

Written in the spirit of the nineteenth-century social novel — Wolfe cited Dickens and Thackeray as models — the book anatomized the status anxieties of Wall Street, the Park Avenue elite, the criminal justice system and the press. It was a bestseller and is frequently cited as the definitive novel of 1980s New York.

Film adaptation

Brian De Palma's 1990 film adaptation, starring Tom Hanks, Bruce Willis and Melanie Griffith, was a famous critical and commercial failure, chronicled in Julie Salamon's book The Devil's Candy.
