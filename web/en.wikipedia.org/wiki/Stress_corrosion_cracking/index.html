Stress corrosion cracking

Stress corrosion cracking (SCC) is the growth of crack formation in a corrosive environment. It can lead to unexpected and sudden failure of normally ductile metal alloys subjected to a tensile stress, especially at elevated temperature. SCC is highly chemically specific in that certain alloys are likely to undergo SCC only when exposed to a small number of chemical environments.

Mechanism and examples

SCC requires three simultaneous conditions: a susceptible material, a specific corrosive environment, and tensile stress (which may be residual stress from fabrication). Classic combinations include austenitic stainless steels in hot chloride solutions, carbon steel in caustic soda ("caustic embrittlement"), and brass in ammonia-containing environments ("season cracking"). Because cracks grow with little visible corrosion, SCC has caused major failures, including the collapse of the Silver Bridge in 1967 (in part attributed to stress corrosion of an eyebar) and leaks in chemical and nuclear plant piping.

Prevention

Prevention strategies include selecting resistant alloys, removing the critical chemical species, applying protective coatings or cathodic protection, and reducing residual stresses through annealing or shot peening.
