Tardigrades (from Latin tardus, "slow", and gradus, "step"), known colloquially as water bears or moss piglets, are a phylum of tiny eight-legged segmented micro-animals. First described by the German zoologist Johann August Ephraim Goeze in 1773, who called them kleiner Wasserbär ("little water bear"), they are typically about 0.5 mm long when fully grown and are found everywhere on Earth — from deep-sea sediments and tropical rainforests to Antarctica and the films of water on mosses and lichens.

Anatomy and life

Tardigrades have barrel-shaped bodies with four pairs of stubby legs ending in claws or suction discs, a complete digestive tract, and no respiratory or circulatory organs — gas exchange occurs across the body surface. Most feed by piercing plant cells or, in predatory species, other small invertebrates, with needle-like stylets. They reproduce sexually or, in some species, by parthenogenesis, and grow by ecdysis (moulting).

Survival extremes

Tardigrades are famed extremotolerants. Under harsh conditions many species enter cryptobiosis, contracting into a desiccated "tun" with metabolism reduced to near zero. In this state they have survived decades without water, temperatures from near absolute zero to about 150 °C, pressures far exceeding those in the deepest ocean trenches, ionising radiation hundreds of times the lethal human dose, and the vacuum of outer space — in 2007, tardigrades on a European Space Agency satellite survived ten days of direct exposure to space and solar radiation. Special proteins, including the tardigrade-specific damage suppressor (Dsup) protein, help protect their DNA. In 2019 an Israeli lunar lander, Beresheet, crashed on the Moon carrying dehydrated tardigrades among its cargo.

Evolution

Tardigrades belong to the moulting-animal superphylum Ecdysozoa and are grouped with arthropods and velvet worms in Panarthropoda. Their fossil record is sparse because of their size and soft bodies, but amber-preserved specimens are known from the Cretaceous, and Cambrian lobopodian-like fossils have been proposed as stem relatives.
