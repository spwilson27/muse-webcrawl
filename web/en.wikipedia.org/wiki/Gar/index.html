Gar

KingdomAnimalia
PhylumChordata
ClassActinopterygii
OrderLepisosteiformes
FamilyLepisosteidae

Gars are members of the family Lepisosteidae, a group of freshwater ray-finned fish in the order Lepisosteiformes. They are characterised by elongated, torpedo-shaped bodies, long jaws filled with sharp teeth, and heavy, diamond-shaped ganoid scales. Gars are sometimes called "living fossils" because their lineage extends back more than 100 million years with relatively little change.

Description

Gars have long, cylindrical bodies covered in an armour of interlocking ganoid scales. Their jaws are elongated and armed with numerous needle-like teeth suited to seizing fish. The dorsal and anal fins are set far back on the body, near the rounded tail. The largest species, the alligator gar (Atractosteus spatula), can exceed 2.5 metres in length and is one of the largest purely freshwater fishes in North America.

Gars possess a highly vascularised swim bladder connected to the throat, allowing them to gulp air and survive in warm, oxygen-poor waters.

Distribution and habitat

Living gars are found in fresh and brackish waters of eastern North America, Central America and Cuba. They prefer slow-moving rivers, lakes, bayous and backwaters with abundant vegetation, where they ambush prey near the surface.

Species

There are seven living species in two genera:

  • Atractosteus — alligator gar, Cuban gar, tropical gar
  • Lepisosteus — longnose gar, shortnose gar, spotted gar, Florida gar

Relation to humans

Gar flesh is edible, though their eggs are toxic to humans and many animals. Their tough scales were historically used by Native Americans for arrowheads and breastplates, and by early settlers for covering ploughshares. Gars are sometimes kept in very large aquaria and are popular as trophy sport fish.
