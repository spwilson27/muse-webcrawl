Sir Godfrey Copley, 2nd Baronet (c. 1653 – 9 April 1709) was a wealthy English landowner, art collector, and public figure from Sprotbrough, near Doncaster in South Yorkshire. He is best remembered for his bequest to the Royal Society in London, which funded the Copley Medal, one of the oldest and most prestigious awards in science.

Copley inherited the baronetcy and the family estates on his father's death. He served several terms as a Member of Parliament for Aldborough and for Thirsk, and was a Fellow of the Royal Society, elected in 1691. He was an avid collector of paintings and maintained a correspondence with figures of the early Enlightenment.

Legacy

In his will, Copley left £100 in trust to the Royal Society to be used for improving natural knowledge. The interest on the sum was initially used to fund experiments, and later to award an annual medal. The Copley Medal has since been awarded to many of the most eminent scientists in history, including Charles Darwin, Albert Einstein, and Francis Crick, and remains the Royal Society's premier award.
