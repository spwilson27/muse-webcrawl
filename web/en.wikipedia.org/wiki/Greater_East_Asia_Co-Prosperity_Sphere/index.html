The Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere (Japanese: 大東亜共栄圏, Dai Tōa Kyōeiken) was an imperialist concept and propaganda slogan devised by the Empire of Japan and proclaimed from 1940, envisioning a bloc of Asian nations led by Japan and free of Western colonial powers. In practice it served as the ideological banner for Japanese expansion and occupation across East and Southeast Asia during the Pacific War, in which nominally independent client states — Manchukuo, the Wang Jingwei regime in China, the State of Burma, the Second Philippine Republic, and others — were subordinated to Japanese military and economic control.

Origins and conference

Foreign Minister Yōsuke Matsuoka popularized the phrase in August 1940. The concept drew on Pan-Asianist ideas of Asian solidarity against Western imperialism, which attracted some genuine support among nationalists; figures such as Subhas Chandra Bose (Indian National Army) and Ba Maw of Burma cooperated with Japan in pursuit of independence from Britain. The Greater East Asia Conference in Tokyo (November 1943) assembled leaders of the member states and issued a joint declaration modelled partly on the Atlantic Charter.

Reality and legacy

Japanese occupation in practice involved resource extraction, forced labour (rōmusha), military requisitioning, and harsh repression, culminating in mass suffering in China, Southeast Asia, and elsewhere; the Sphere collapsed with Japan's defeat in 1945. Historians debate how far the project was pure propaganda versus a real, if coercive, regional order; its rhetoric of Asian liberation outlived it, echoing in post-war Asian regionalism even as its wartime record remains a source of regional tension and memory disputes.
