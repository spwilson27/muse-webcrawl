Second Battle of the Masurian Lakes

Date7–22 February 1915
LocationEast Prussia (Masurian Lakes region), German Empire
BelligerentsGerman Empire vs. Russian Empire
ResultGerman tactical victory; Russian Tenth Army mauled but not destroyed
The "Winter Battle" in Masuria, February 1915

The Second Battle of the Masurian Lakes, also known in Germany as the Winter Battle in Masuria (Winterschlacht in Masuren), was fought from 7 to 22 February 1915 on the Eastern Front of the First World War. German forces under Paul von Hindenburg and Erich Ludendorff attempted to encircle and destroy the Russian Tenth Army in the lake district of East Prussia, in bitter winter conditions.

Background

After the German victory at the First Battle of the Masurian Lakes in September 1914, Russian forces still held a salient projecting into East Prussia, anchored on the Tenth Army under General Thadeus von Sievers. Hindenburg, reinforced by the newly formed German Tenth Army under Hermann von Eichhorn, planned a double envelopment: Eighth Army (Otto von Below) would pin the Russians in front while Tenth Army swept around their northern flank through the lakes and forests toward Augustów.

The battle

The offensive opened on 7 February 1915 in a blizzard. The northern wing advanced rapidly, outflanking the Russian right, while the southern attacks pushed through the lake defiles. The Russian Tenth Army began a desperate retreat eastward; its XX Corps, under General Pavel Bulgakov, was encircled in the Augustów Forest, where it fought for several days to hold open an escape corridor. Although XX Corps was eventually destroyed and much of it captured, its sacrifice allowed the remainder of the Tenth Army to fall back to a new line along the Bobr and Narew rivers and into the fortresses of Grodno and Osowiec.

Outcome

German forces advanced up to 70 miles and inflicted heavy losses: Russian casualties are estimated at around 200,000 killed, wounded and captured, with the Germans claiming some 110,000 prisoners and hundreds of guns, at a cost of roughly 16,500 German casualties. Strategically, however, the operation fell short of a second Tannenberg: the Russians stabilised the front in Poland and East Prussia, and the territory gained was largely relinquished later in 1915. Like its predecessor at Tannenberg and the First Masurian Lakes, the battle contributed to the Hindenburg legend in Germany.
