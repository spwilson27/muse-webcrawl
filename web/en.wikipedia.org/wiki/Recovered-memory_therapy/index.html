Recovered-memory therapy

Recovered-memory therapy is a catch-all term for a controversial set of psychotherapeutic techniques, popular mainly in the 1980s and early 1990s, that aim to recover supposedly repressed memories of traumatic events — most often childhood sexual abuse. Techniques included hypnosis, guided imagery, age regression, dream interpretation and the use of "body memories".

Controversy

The approach became the centre of the "memory wars" of the 1990s. Some patients recovered memories that led to criminal prosecutions and the breakup of families; others later retracted their accounts. Critics, including memory researchers such as Elizabeth Loftus, argued that suggestive techniques can create vivid but entirely false memories, a phenomenon studied as false memory syndrome. Professional bodies including the American Psychological Association and the Royal College of Psychiatrists issued cautionary statements acknowledging both that abuse memories can be recovered and that false memories can be implanted.

Legal and professional consequences

High-profile malpractice suits against therapists, and cases in which convictions based on recovered memories were overturned, led to a sharp decline in the practice. Modern trauma therapies generally avoid suggestive memory-recovery techniques, and recovered-memory therapy is now widely regarded as discredited and potentially harmful.
