Five Ranks

The Five Ranks (Chinese: 五位, wǔwèi; Japanese: goi) is a teaching scheme of the Caodong (Japanese: Sōtō) school of Chan (Zen) Buddhism describing stages in the practitioner's realisation of the interplay between absolute reality and relative, phenomenal reality. The scheme is attributed to Dongshan Liangjie (807–869), the founder of the Caodong house, and was elaborated by his disciple Caoshan Benji.

The ranks

  1. The relative within the absolute. The practitioner glimpses the absolute (emptiness, the undifferentiated) within phenomena, like seeing one's reflection or the moon in water.
  2. The absolute within the relative. Having recognised emptiness, the practitioner returns to phenomena and finds the absolute expressed in each particular thing.
  3. Coming from within the absolute. Resting in the absolute alone, free of attachment to both poles — sometimes described as the rank of the solitary sage.
  4. Arrival at mutual integration. Absolute and relative interpenetrate without obstruction; the practitioner moves freely between them.
  5. Unity attained. Both absolute and relative are transcended in a unity that functions naturally in the everyday world, beyond any distinction between enlightenment and ordinary activity.

Interpretation

The five ranks are often presented with paired imagery of lord and vassal, or host and guest, drawn from classical Chinese political metaphor. Later teachers, especially Hakuin Ekaku in the Rinzai school, adopted the ranks as a framework for checking students' understanding after kensho. Because the ranks can be mapped onto one another in multiple orders and explained with many image sets, commentators have produced a large literature on their exact meaning. In modern presentations, notably by the Sanbo Kyodan lineage and its Western heirs, the five ranks serve as a map of deepening insight rather than a sequence of discrete attainments.
