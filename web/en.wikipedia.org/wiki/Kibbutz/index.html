A kibbutz (Hebrew: קיבוץ, "gathering" or "cluster"; plural kibbutzim) is a rural community in Israel traditionally based on agriculture and collective ownership. Founded by Zionist pioneers in Ottoman Palestine, kibbutzim became a defining institution of Israeli society, combining socialist and Zionist ideals in communal settlements governed by direct democracy.

History

The first kibbutz, Degania Alef, was founded in 1910 south of the Sea of Galilee by young Jewish immigrants, mostly from Eastern Europe, who sought to create a Jewish working class on nationally owned land. Larger "big kibbutzim" (kibbutz me'uchad) followed in the 1920s, and rival federations split the movement ideologically. Kibbutzim supplied a disproportionate share of Israel's military officers, pilots, and political leaders, and their communal dining halls, children's houses, and egalitarian ethos became iconic.

Classical structure

In the classic model, members owned no private property, received no wages, and were provided housing, food, clothing, healthcare, and education by the collective; work was assigned by committee and approved by the general assembly, which decided all major questions. Children were raised communally in children's houses in many kibbutzim, sleeping apart from their parents, though family sleeping arrangements returned from the 1970s. Alongside agriculture, most kibbutzim developed industries—plastics, optics, food processing, and tourism.

Change and privatisation

An economic crisis in the 1980s, driven by debt and inflation, and the wider decline of socialist ideology led most kibbutzim to adopt reforms: differential salaries, private budgets, home ownership, and hired outside managers. Only a minority—roughly a fifth—remain fully communal ("shitufi"); the majority are now "renewed" kibbutzim that retain a cooperative framework and safety net but permit private income. Interest in urban kibbutzim and new communal groups has grown in the 21st century.
