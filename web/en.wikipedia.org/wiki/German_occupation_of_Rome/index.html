German occupation of Rome

The German occupation of Rome lasted from 10 September 1943, when German forces seized the Italian capital after the announcement of the Italian armistice with the Allies, until 4 June 1944, when the city was liberated by American troops. The nine months of occupation were marked by deportations of Jews and military internees, reprisal massacres, the looting of the city's gold and food, and an organized armed Resistance involving thousands of Romans.

Seizure of the city

When Marshal Badoglio's government announced the Armistice of Cassibile on 8 September 1943, German forces executed Operation Achse, disarming the Italian military across the peninsula. Italian troops and armed civilians fought the Germans at points in Rome — notably at Porta San Paolo on 10 September — but the government and king had fled to Brindisi, and the city was surrendered. Rome's supposed status as an "open city" was never recognized in practice by either side.

Repression and deportations

The occupation regime under the Gestapo chief Herbert Kappler demanded 50 kg of gold from Rome's Jewish community in September 1943, which was delivered; nevertheless, on 16 October 1943 SS units rounded up over 1,200 Jews in the former ghetto and across the city. About 1,023 were deported to Auschwitz; only 16 survived. Thousands of other Romans were deported as forced labourers, and more than 700,000 Italian soldiers captured after the armistice were interned in Germany. Thousands of Jews and escaped Allied prisoners survived in hiding, sheltered by Roman families, convents, and Vatican-linked institutions — the extent of Pope Pius XII's personal role remaining the subject of historical debate.

Resistance and reprisals

A clandestine military front and communist, socialist, and action-party partisan groups (GAP) ran intelligence, sabotage, and attacks in the city. On 23 March 1944 partisans of the GAP detonated a bomb in the Via Rasella, killing 33 German military policemen. In reprisal, on Hitler's orders, Kappler organized the Ardeatine massacre: 335 prisoners — Jews, political prisoners, hostages, and bystanders — were shot in the Ardeatine caves on 24 March 1944, and the entrances were dynamited. After the war, Kesselring and Kappler were tried for the massacre; Kappler was sentenced to life imprisonment.

Liberation

After the Allied breakthrough at Anzio and Monte Cassino, German forces withdrew north, and on the evening of 4 June 1944 units of Mark Clark's US Fifth Army entered Rome — two days before the Normandy landings pushed the city's liberation off the front pages.
