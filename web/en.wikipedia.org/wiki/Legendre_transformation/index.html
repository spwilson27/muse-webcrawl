Legendre transformation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Legendre transformation
A convex function is fully described by its tangent lines

In mathematics, the Legendre transformation converts a function of a real variable into another function of the variable's derivative (slope). For a convex function f(x), its Legendre transform f*(p) is defined (in the common "max" convention) by

f*(p) = supx ( p·x − f(x) ),

where the point at which the supremum is attained satisfies p = f′(x). Geometrically, a convex curve can be described equivalently by the set of its points or by the set of its tangent lines; the Legendre transform passes between these descriptions, encoding the function by the intercepts of its tangents. The transformation is an involution: applying it twice returns the original (convex) function. It is named after Adrien-Marie Legendre.

Properties and generalisations

Derivatives transform reciprocally: if p = f′(x), then x = f*′(p). Strict convexity guarantees the transform is well defined and smooth. In several variables the definition uses the scalar product, f*(p) = supx ( ⟨p, x⟩ − f(x) ), and is also known as the convex conjugate or Fenchel conjugate (the Legendre–Fenchel transformation), central to convex analysis, convex optimisation and the theory of large deviations (where it produces rate functions, as in Cramér's theorem).

Applications in physics

Thermodynamics

The thermodynamic potentials are Legendre transforms of one another. Starting from the internal energy U(S, V), transforming with respect to entropy gives the Helmholtz free energy F = U − TS; transforming instead with respect to volume gives the enthalpy H = U + PV; transforming with respect to both gives the Gibbs free energy G = U + PV − TS. Each potential is "natural" for experiments holding its new variables (T, P) constant.

Classical mechanics

The Hamiltonian is the Legendre transform of the Lagrangian with respect to the velocities: H(q, p) = Σ pi q̇i − L(q, q̇), where the momenta pi = ∂L/∂q̇i. This substitution converts Lagrange's equations into Hamilton's equations and underlies Hamiltonian mechanics and, through it, quantum mechanics and statistical mechanics.
