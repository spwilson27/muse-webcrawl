Exchange rate

[Image: an airport bureau de change board listing rates for major currencies]

An exchange rate is the Price of one Currency in terms of another — for example around 1.05–1.10 US dollars per euro in recent years — determined in the foreign-exchange market, where daily turnover runs to several trillion dollars. A currency's rise is an appreciation (or revaluation under a fixed regime); a fall, a depreciation (devaluation).

Regimes and determinants

Under a floating regime (the dollar, euro, yen, pound), rates move with supply and demand driven by trade flows, interest-rate differentials, investment and expectations; under a fixed or pegged regime the Central bank defends a parity, as Hong Kong does against the dollar, while China manages the renminbi against a basket. Purchasing power parity predicts long-run rates from relative prices; in the short run rates are volatile and hard to forecast. Exchange-rate movements reshape competitiveness, import prices and Inflation, the value of foreign debts and assets, and the fortunes of exporters — making the choice of regime, and episodes such as sterling's 1992 ERM exit ('Black Wednesday'), central events in macroeconomic history.
