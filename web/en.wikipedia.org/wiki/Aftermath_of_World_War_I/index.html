The aftermath of World War I saw drastic political, cultural, economic and social change across Eurasia. Four empires — the German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman and Russian — collapsed, new nation-states were created from their territories, colonies changed hands under League of Nations mandates, and the peace settlement reshaped the map of Europe and the Middle East. The war's unresolved grievances, reparations disputes and economic instability contributed directly to the rise of fascism and, ultimately, to the Second World War.

Peace settlement and new states

The Paris Peace Conference of 1919 imposed the Treaty of Versailles on Germany, which lost territory, its colonies and its overseas assets, accepted a "war guilt" clause and was billed reparations. Austria-Hungary was broken up by the Treaties of Saint-Germain and Trianon; the Ottoman Empire was partitioned by Sèvres (1920), though Turkish resistance under Mustafa Kemal produced the revised Treaty of Lausanne (1923). Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Czechoslovakia and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (Yugoslavia) emerged or were re-established, while Ireland won independence from Britain after a war of independence.

Revolutions and civil wars

The Russian Revolution of 1917 led to the Russian Civil War and the creation of the Soviet Union in 1922. Germany experienced the Spartacist uprising and other revolutionary turmoil before the fragile Weimar Republic stabilised; Hungary passed through a short-lived Soviet republic. Across the former Ottoman lands, mandates in Syria, Iraq, Palestine and Transjordan laid the groundwork for modern Middle Eastern states — and enduring conflicts.

Social and economic consequences

Some 15–22 million people had died, with millions more wounded or displaced; the 1918 influenza pandemic killed tens of millions worldwide. Women's wartime work accelerated suffrage in several countries. Economically, war debts and reparations poisoned international relations, hyperinflation struck Germany in 1923, and the settlement's weaknesses — an absent United States in the League of Nations, resentful revisionist powers — left the post-war order precarious.
