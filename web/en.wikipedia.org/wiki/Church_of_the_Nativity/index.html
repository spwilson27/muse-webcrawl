The Church of the Nativity is a basilica in Bethlehem marking the traditional site of the birth of Jesus of Nazareth. One of the oldest continuously operating churches in the world, it is built over the Grotto of the Nativity, a cave venerated as the birthplace since at least the 2nd century AD. The church is a major destination of Christian pilgrimage and is jointly administered by the Greek Orthodox, Armenian Apostolic and Roman Catholic churches under the Status Quo arrangements.

History

Emperor Constantine and his mother Helena commissioned the first basilica over the grotto in about AD 327. After its destruction, probably by fire during the Samaritan revolts, Emperor Justinian rebuilt it by AD 565 in substantially the form surviving today, with its fortress-like exterior and low "Door of Humility" entrance. Unusually, the church survived the Persian invasion of 614 — legend says the invaders spared it on seeing a mosaic of the Magi in Persian dress — and it passed intact through Islamic and Crusader rule; the Crusaders redecorated it with mosaics and marble in the 12th century.

The Grotto

Beneath the altar, the Grotto of the Nativity contains a silver star set in the marble floor marking the traditional birthplace, inscribed in Latin "Here Jesus Christ was born of the Virgin Mary." Nearby is the Chapel of the Manger. Rivalry over the star's theft in 1847 was one of the sparks of the Crimean War.

Recent history

In 2002 the church was besieged for 39 days during the Second Intifada. A major restoration of the roof and medieval mosaics began in 2013. UNESCO inscribed the church and its pilgrimage route as a World Heritage Site in 2012.
