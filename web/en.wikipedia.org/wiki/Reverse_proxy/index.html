Reverse proxy

A reverse proxy is a server that accepts requests from clients on behalf of one or more origin servers and returns the responses as if they came from the proxy itself. To the client, the reverse proxy is the visible endpoint; the internal servers may remain private or change independently.

Client → Reverse proxy → Origin server(s)

Uses

Reverse proxies commonly provide load balancing, TLS termination, caching, compression, request routing, authentication and protection against malformed or abusive traffic. A large website can place many application servers behind one public name and distribute requests according to capacity, geography or URL path.

Comparison with a forward proxy

A forward proxy represents clients, often to control or anonymize their outbound access. A reverse proxy represents servers and is controlled by the service operator. The client ordinarily knows it is using a forward proxy but may be unaware that a reverse proxy is present.

Implementation details

The proxy may add headers such as X-Forwarded-For to preserve the original client address for logs and access control. It can terminate encrypted HTTPS connections and communicate with backends over a private network. Content delivery networks are, in effect, globally distributed reverse proxies with extensive caching.

Limitations

A reverse proxy introduces a hop and a potential bottleneck or single point of failure. Poor configuration can expose internal headers, create incorrect cache behavior or undermine client-address-based security. High availability therefore usually requires redundant proxies and health checks.
