Victor Grignard

FieldsOrganic chemistry

François Auguste Victor Grignard (6 May 1871 – 13 December 1935) was a French chemist who shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1912 with Paul Sabatier for his discovery of the organomagnesium compounds that bear his name. The Grignard reaction — the addition of an organomagnesium halide (a Grignard reagent) to a carbonyl group — became one of the most important methods for forming carbon–carbon bonds in organic synthesis.

The son of a sailmaker, Grignard initially studied mathematics at Lyon before switching to chemistry. Working under Philippe Barbier, he discovered in 1900 that alkyl halides react with magnesium in dry ether to give organomagnesium halides, which then react smoothly with aldehydes, ketones, esters, and carbon dioxide. His doctoral thesis on the subject (1901) established the method, which was far more general and easier to handle than the organozinc chemistry previously used.

Career and legacy

Grignard became professor at Nancy in 1910 and at Lyon in 1919. During the First World War he worked on chemical warfare agents, including the detection of mustard gas and the production of phosgene — an irony given the peaceful purposes of his most famous discovery. Grignard reagents remain a staple of organic synthesis laboratories worldwide, used in the preparation of alcohols, carboxylic acids, and countless pharmaceutical and industrial products.
