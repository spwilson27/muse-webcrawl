John Calvin (10 July 1509 – 27 May 1564) was a French theologian, pastor and reformer in Geneva during the Protestant Reformation, and the principal figure in the development of the Reformed tradition of Christianity ('Calvinism'). His Institutes of the Christian Religion (first edition 1536, expanded to 1559) is a landmark of systematic Protestant theology, emphasising the sovereignty of God and, in later formulations, predestination.

Calvin's Geneva — where he led the church from 1541 after an earlier expulsion — became a refuge for persecuted Protestants and a training ground for reformers who carried Reformed faith to France, the Netherlands, Scotland (via John Knox) and beyond. The city's strict moral discipline earned it the name 'Protestant Rome'; the trial and execution of Michael Servetus (1553) remains a stain debated by historians. Calvin also founded the Academy of Geneva (1559), forerunner of the university.

Influence

Reformed and Presbyterian churches, and strands of Congregationalism and Baptist thought, trace to Calvin's theology and Geneva's model. Max Weber famously linked the 'Protestant ethic' of disciplined work to Calvinist cultures, a thesis still debated.
