Germanic peoples

The Germanic peoples were a historical ethnolinguistic group of Northern European origin, identified by their use of the Germanic languages, which diversified out of Proto-Germanic during the 1st millennium BC. Roman authors such as Tacitus, in his Germania, described the peoples living beyond the Rhine and Danube frontiers of the Roman Empire.

History

Germanic tribes are generally thought to have originated in southern Scandinavia and northern Germany, associated archaeologically with the Nordic Bronze Age and the Jastorf culture. During the Migration Period (c. 300–700 AD), Germanic groups moved widely across Europe, founding successor kingdoms on former Roman territory: the Visigothic kingdom in Spain, the Ostrogothic and later Lombard kingdoms in Italy, the Frankish kingdom in Gaul, and the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms in Britain.

Society and culture

Early Germanic society was organised around kinship, chieftains, and assemblies known as things. Law was customary and compensatory, based on payments such as wergild. Religion centred on gods including Odin (Woden), Thor, and Freyr; most Germanic peoples converted to Christianity between the 4th and 11th centuries, some initially to Arianism.

Legacy

The Germanic languages today include English, German, Dutch, and the Scandinavian languages, spoken by hundreds of millions of people. Germanic legal customs, place names, and personal names left deep traces across Europe.
