Junk (ship)

A junk is a type of Chinese sailing ship with fully battened sails. There are two main types of junk in China: the northern junk, which developed from Chinese river boats, and the southern junk, which developed from Austronesian ship designs used in trade with the Eastern Han dynasty. They continued to evolve in later dynasties, and were predominantly used by Chinese traders throughout Southeast Asia.

Similar sails with fully battened configuration can also be found in other traditional Asian vessels, particularly the Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese variants. The English word "junk" derives from Portuguese junco, itself borrowed from a Malay or Javanese word for a large ship.

History

The junk rig and hull form developed over many centuries in China. By the Song dynasty (960–1279) junks were used extensively for maritime trade, and Chinese junks were among the largest and strongest wooden ships in the world. During the Ming dynasty, the treasure ships of the admiral Zheng He were great junks that sailed as far as East Africa in the early 15th century.

Marco Polo and the Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta both praised the seaworthiness of junks, noting their watertight bulkheads, which divide the hull into compartments and greatly improve survivability if the hull is breached — a feature European shipbuilding adopted only much later.

Design

Key features of the junk include:

  • Fully battened sails of cloth or matting stiffened by horizontal bamboo battens, which can be raised and lowered quickly and allow sailing close to the wind.
  • A stern-mounted rudder, often adjustable in depth, an invention found in China from about the 1st century AD.
  • Watertight compartments formed by transverse bulkheads.
  • A flat or lightly rockered bottom and high stern, with the hull planking fastened with iron nails or clamps.

Junks ranged from small fishing and river craft to ocean-going merchantmen of several hundred tons. Regional variants include the Fujian junk, the Guangdong junk and the northern "sand ship" (shachuan) adapted to shallow northern waters.

Modern use

Traditional junks have largely disappeared from commercial trade, but smaller junks remain in use for fishing, and replica and converted junks serve as tour boats, houseboats and pleasure craft, particularly in Hong Kong and along the Chinese coast. The junk rig is also occasionally used on modern cruising yachts for its ease of handling.
