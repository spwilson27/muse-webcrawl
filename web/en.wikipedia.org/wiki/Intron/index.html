Intron

Intron
Discovered1977 (split genes; Phillip Sharp and Richard Roberts, Nobel Prize 1993)
Removed byRNA splicing (spliceosome, or self-splicing)
Term coined byWalter Gilbert, 1978

An intron is any nucleotide sequence within a gene that is not expressed in the final RNA product. Introns are removed from the primary transcript by RNA splicing, while the remaining sequences — the exons — are joined together to form the mature messenger RNA (or functional non-coding RNA). Introns are found in the genes of most eukaryotes and many eukaryotic viruses, and their discovery in 1977 overturned the expectation that genes are continuous stretches of coding DNA.

Classes and splicing

The major class, spliceosomal introns, is removed by the spliceosome, a large RNA–protein complex, guided by conserved sequences at the intron's 5′ and 3′ ends and branch point. Other classes — group I and group II introns — are ribozymes that splice themselves out, and still others occur in transfer RNA genes. Because exons can be joined in different combinations, alternative splicing lets a single gene produce multiple distinct proteins, greatly expanding the proteome of complex organisms.

Evolution and function

Debate continues between "introns-early" theories, in which introns facilitated the assembly of primordial genes from exon modules, and "introns-late" theories, in which they invaded eukaryotic genomes later, plausibly descending from group II self-splicing elements. Whatever their origin, introns are not merely junk: they can contain regulatory elements and genes for small RNAs, and splicing errors that retain introns or skip exons are implicated in many genetic diseases and cancers.
