The Irish Transport and General Workers' Union (ITGWU) was a trade union representing workers in Ireland, founded by James Larkin in 1908–09 after his expulsion from the British-based National Union of Dock Labourers. Modelled on the "new unionism" of unskilled and general workers, it became the largest and most militant Irish union of its era, central to the Dublin Lock-out of 1913 and the parent organisation of the Irish Citizen Army. In 1990 it merged with the Workers' Union of Ireland to form SIPTU, Ireland's largest union.

Foundation and Larkinism

Larkin, a Liverpool-born organiser of Irish descent, arrived in Belfast in 1907 and led a landmark dock strike; moving to Dublin, he founded the ITGWU in December 1908 and registered it in January 1909, recruiting dockers, carters and other unskilled workers neglected by craft unions. The union's hallmarks — the sympathetic strike, the "tainted goods" boycott of employers in dispute, fiery oratory and its paper The Irish Worker — became known as Larkinism. By 1913 the union had some 10,000 members and its headquarters at Liberty Hall in Beresford Place.

The 1913 Lock-out

In August 1913, employers led by William Martin Murphy — owner of the Dublin United Tramways Company — resolved to break the ITGWU by requiring workers to sign a pledge renouncing the union. The resulting Lock-out drew in some 20,000 workers across the city and lasted into January 1914. Police violence on Bloody Sunday (31 August 1913), the attempted relief ship Hare, the controversial scheme to send strikers' children to English homes ("Kiddies' Scheme"), and the formation of the Irish Citizen Army all flowed from the dispute. Though the workers drifted back without formal victory, the union survived and the Lock-out became a founding myth of the Irish labour movement.

Connolly and after

When Larkin departed for the United States in 1914, James Connolly became acting general secretary, fusing the union with the independence struggle; his execution after the 1916 Easter Rising made him the union's martyr. Under Thomas Johnson and then William O'Brien, general secretary from 1924 to 1946, the ITGWU grew into a broad general union and a pillar of the Irish labour movement, though a bitter 1923 split saw Larkin found the rival Workers' Union of Ireland. The union's Liberty Hall was rebuilt in the 1960s as one of Dublin's first modern high-rise buildings.

Merger

On 1 January 1990 the ITGWU amalgamated with the Workers' Union of Ireland (with which it had reconciled) to form SIPTU (Services, Industrial, Professional and Technical Union), ending the ITGWU's eighty-one-year history while continuing its traditions and its Liberty Hall headquarters.
