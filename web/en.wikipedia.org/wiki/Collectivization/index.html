Collectivization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Collectivization is the process of organising individual farms, workshops or other means of production into collective enterprises, usually under state direction. The term is most closely associated with the agricultural policies of the Soviet Union under Joseph Stalin, and with similar campaigns in other communist states during the twentieth century.

Soviet Union

Beginning in 1928–1929, the Soviet government drove peasants into collective farms (kolkhozy) and state farms (sovkhozy), confiscating land, livestock and equipment. The campaign was combined with "dekulakization" — the deportation, imprisonment or execution of peasants labelled as kulaks (wealthier peasants). Resistance was widespread; many peasants slaughtered their livestock rather than surrender it. Forced grain requisitioning and the disruption of agriculture contributed to severe famines in 1932–1933, including the Holodomor in Ukraine and famine in Kazakhstan and southern Russia, which together killed millions of people. By the late 1930s, the great majority of Soviet farmland had been collectivised.

Other countries

Collectivization was later imposed, in varying forms, across Eastern Europe after the Second World War, in China during the 1950s (culminating in the people's communes of the Great Leap Forward, which were associated with a catastrophic famine), and in countries such as North Korea, Vietnam, Cuba and Ethiopia. Results generally included falling agricultural productivity, and many states later reversed or softened the policy; China dismantled its communes after 1978 under the household responsibility system, and Eastern European collectivised agriculture was largely privatised after 1989.
