GPS

The Global Positioning System (GPS), originally Navstar GPS, is a satellite-based radio-navigation system owned by the United States government and operated by the United States Space Force. It provides geolocation and time information to a receiver anywhere on Earth where there is an unobstructed line of sight to four or more GPS satellites. GPS is one of several global navigation satellite systems (GNSS), alongside Russia's GLONASS, China's BeiDou, and the European Union's Galileo, and is freely available for civilian use worldwide.

How it works

Each GPS satellite continuously broadcasts its position and the precise time from onboard atomic clocks. A receiver measures the signal travel times from at least four satellites and solves for its own three-dimensional position and the offset of its clock — a form of trilateration. Because light travels about 30 cm in a nanosecond, timing must be corrected to nanosecond precision; the system must also compensate for relativistic effects (satellite clocks gain about 38 microseconds per day relative to ground clocks from the combined effects of special and general relativity) and for atmospheric delays. Augmentation systems such as WAAS, and techniques such as differential GPS and real-time kinematic positioning, improve accuracy to the metre or even centimetre level.

History

GPS grew out of Cold War navigation programmes such as the Navy's Transit system. The project was approved in 1973 under Bradford Parkinson's leadership at the US Air Force, marrying the Timation and 621B programmes. The first Block I satellite launched in 1978. After Korean Air Lines Flight 007 was shot down in 1983 for straying into Soviet airspace, President Reagan announced GPS would be opened to civilian use. The constellation was declared fully operational in 1995. "Selective availability", the deliberate degradation of the civilian signal, was discontinued in 2000, improving civilian accuracy roughly tenfold overnight. Modernised GPS III satellites, first launched in 2018, add stronger signals and new civil codes.

Applications

Beyond navigation in aviation, shipping, road transport, and smartphones, GPS underpins surveying, precision agriculture, earthquake and tectonic monitoring, the timing synchronisation of power grids, telecommunications networks, and financial trading systems — making it a piece of critical global infrastructure whose vulnerability to jamming and spoofing is an increasing concern.
