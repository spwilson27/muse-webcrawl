Galatians (people)

The Galatians were a Celtic people who settled in the central highlands of Anatolia (in modern-day Turkey) in the 3rd century BC, giving their name to the region and later Roman province of Galatia. They were descended from the Gallic tribes — the Tectosages, Tolistobogii and Trocmi — who crossed from Thrace into Asia Minor in 278 BC, invited by King Nicomedes I of Bithynia to serve as mercenaries.

History

After ravaging western Anatolia, the Galatians were defeated by Antiochus I and by Attalus I of Pergamon — victories celebrated in monuments such as the Dying Gaul and the Great Altar of Pergamon — and settled the plateau around Ancyra (modern Ankara), Gordion and Tavium. Organised as three tribes, each divided into four cantons (tetrarchies), they governed themselves through an annual council of 300 meeting at a sacred oak grove, the Drynemetum. Galatian mercenaries served throughout the Hellenistic world, and their territory became a Roman client kingdom under kings such as Deiotarus before Augustus annexed it as a province in 25 BC.

Culture and legacy

The Galatians spoke a Celtic language closely related to Gaulish, survived as a distinct tongue into late antiquity — St Jerome, writing around 400 AD, remarked that they spoke a language like that of the Treveri of the Moselle valley. In the New Testament, the Apostle Paul addressed his Epistle to the Galatians to churches in the region. Gradually Hellenised and absorbed into the population of Roman and Byzantine Anatolia, they nevertheless preserved their name in the province of Galatia for centuries.
