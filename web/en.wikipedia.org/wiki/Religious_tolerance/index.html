Religious tolerance

Religious tolerance is the condition under which adherents of different religions or denominations are allowed to practise their beliefs without persecution by the dominant group or the state, and more broadly the acceptance of religious difference within a society. It differs from religious liberty in that tolerance can be a policy of forbearance granted by rulers, rather than a guaranteed right of conscience.

History

Notable historical episodes include the edicts of Ashoka in ancient India; the Millet system of the Ottoman Empire; the Edict of Torda (1568) in Transylvania; the Edict of Nantes (1598), which granted Huguenots rights in France before its revocation in 1685; and the English Toleration Act of 1689, which exempted Protestant dissenters from some penal laws. John Locke's A Letter Concerning Toleration (1689) gave influential philosophical expression to the idea, and Voltaire championed it after the Calas affair.

Tolerance and intolerance

Philosophers note a tension known as the paradox of tolerance: a society tolerant without limit may be undermined by intolerant movements, prompting debate about where the boundaries of toleration lie. In the modern era, tolerance is increasingly supplemented or replaced by legal guarantees of equal religious freedom.
