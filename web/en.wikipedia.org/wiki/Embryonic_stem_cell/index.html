Embryonic stem cell

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Embryonic stem cells (ESCs) are pluripotent stem cells derived from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst, an early-stage pre-implantation embryo. They can self-renew indefinitely in culture while retaining the ability to differentiate into cells of all three germ layers — endoderm, mesoderm, and ectoderm — and hence into virtually every cell type of the body. These properties make them a powerful tool for developmental biology, drug screening, and regenerative medicine.

History

Mouse embryonic stem cells were first isolated in 1981 by Martin Evans, Matthew Kaufman, and Gail Martin. In 1998, a team led by James Thomson at the University of Wisconsin derived the first human embryonic stem cell lines from surplus IVF blastocysts, opening the field of human stem cell research. Work on the transcription factors that maintain pluripotency — including Oct4, Sox2, and Nanog — later informed the creation of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) by Shinya Yamanaka in 2006.

Properties and culture

ESCs are characterised by rapid proliferation, normal karyotype, high telomerase activity, and expression of specific surface markers. Human ESCs traditionally require feeder cells or defined media containing growth factors such as FGF2. Their differentiation can be guided in vitro to produce, for example, cardiomyocytes, neurons, pancreatic beta-like cells, and retinal pigment epithelial cells, and their pluripotency is classically demonstrated by teratoma formation in immunodeficient mice.

Applications and controversy

Clinical trials have tested ESC-derived cells for spinal cord injury, macular degeneration, and type 1 diabetes. Because deriving human ESCs involves the destruction of an embryo, the research has been ethically and politically contested: several countries restrict it, and US federal funding policy has shifted repeatedly since 2001. The advent of iPSCs, which avoid embryo use, has provided an alternative, though ESCs remain a benchmark for pluripotency and continue in therapeutic development.
