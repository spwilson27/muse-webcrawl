The ocarina is a wind musical instrument — a type of vessel flute. Unlike a tube flute, its body is an enclosed chamber; air blown through a mouthpiece strikes a labium (edge) via a duct, and the pitch depends on the total area of the finger holes opened rather than on the length of a tube, so the instrument can take almost any shape. Traditional materials are clay and ceramic; modern ones include plastic, wood, glass and metal.

History

Vessel flutes are ancient and worldwide: the Chinese xun is thousands of years old, and Mesoamerican cultures made elaborate pottery flutes and whistles. The modern ocarina was created in 1853 by Giuseppe Donati of Budrio, near Bologna, who built the first tuned, ten-hole "little goose" (oca in Italian) and founded a workshop tradition that spread the instrument across Europe. It became a popular novelty and folk instrument, was carried by soldiers in both world wars, and gained a place in music education.

Types

The most common form is the transverse (or "sweet potato") ocarina, held horizontally with 10–12 holes. Others include pendant ocarinas (English 4–6 hole and Peruvian styles, worn on a cord), inline ocarinas, and multi-chamber "double" and "triple" instruments that extend the range to three octaves or more by adding extra chambers with their own mouthpieces.

In popular culture

The ocarina reached its widest audience through the 1998 video game The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, whose magical instrument and time-travel plot produced a lasting surge of interest and sales, especially among younger players; sheet-music and maker communities grew substantially in its wake. The instrument also features in the Studio Ghibli film Castle in the Sky and in classical arrangements by modern virtuoso players.
