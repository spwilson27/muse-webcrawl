Vorticism

Vorticism was a London-based modernist art movement formed in 1914 by the writer and artist Wyndham Lewis. The movement was partially inspired by Cubism and Futurism, and its members produced paintings and sculpture characterised by hard-edged, angular abstraction that celebrated the dynamism of the machine age.

History

Vorticism grew out of Lewis's break with Roger Fry's Omega Workshops in 1913 and his rivalry with the Italian Futurist Filippo Tommaso Marinetti. In the summer of 1914 Lewis and his associates launched the movement with the magazine BLAST, a striking large-format journal with a bright pink cover that published manifestos "blasting" Victorian sentimentality and "blessing" England's industrial energy. Contributors included the sculptor Henri Gaudier-Brzeska, the painters Edward Wadsworth, William Roberts, Helen Saunders and Jessica Dismorr, the photographer Alvin Langdon Coburn (who made abstract "Vortographs"), and the poets Ezra Pound and T. S. Eliot. Pound invented the name: the vortex, he wrote, is "the point of maximum energy".

Dissolution and legacy

The only Vorticist exhibition was held at the Doré Gallery in 1915. The First World War destroyed the movement: Gaudier-Brzeska was killed in 1915, and the other artists were scattered to the front or to war-art commissions. A second issue of BLAST appeared as a "war number" in 1915, but the movement never recovered after the war. Vorticism is now regarded as Britain's most distinctive contribution to early modernism.
