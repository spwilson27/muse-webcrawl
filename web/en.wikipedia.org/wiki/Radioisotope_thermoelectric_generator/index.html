Radioisotope thermoelectric generator

A radioisotope thermoelectric generator (RTG, RITEG), or radioisotope power system (RPS), is a type of nuclear battery that uses an array of thermocouples to convert the heat released by the decay of a suitable radioactive material into electricity by the Seebeck effect. Such generators have no moving parts and can operate reliably for decades.

Design

Most RTGs use plutonium-238, a non-weapons-grade isotope with a half-life of 87.7 years, in the form of plutonium dioxide ceramic pellets housed in heavily shielded, impact-resistant modules. Thermocouples of materials such as silicon-germanium or lead telluride convert the temperature difference between the hot fuel and radiator fins into electrical power, with typical conversion efficiencies of 5–8%.

Uses

RTGs have powered uncrewed spacecraft including Pioneer 10 and 11, Voyager 1 and 2, Galileo, Ulysses, Cassini–Huygens, New Horizons, and the Curiosity and Perseverance Mars rovers (the Multi-Mission RTG, MMRTG). The Soviet Union built over a thousand terrestrial RTGs, many for remote lighthouses and navigation beacons, some of which later required recovery as orphan sources.

Safety

RTGs are designed to survive launch failures and re-entry intact, containing their fuel in robust ceramic form. Accidents, such as the 1968 loss of Nimbus B and the 1964 SNAP-9A burn-up, informed stricter containment standards; Cassini's 1997 launch drew public protest over its plutonium inventory despite NASA safety assessments.
