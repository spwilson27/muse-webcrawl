Berkeley School of geography

Berkeley School
InstitutionUniversity of California, Berkeley
FounderCarl O. Sauer (chair from 1923)
FocusCultural geography, cultural landscape, historical geography, Latin America

The Berkeley School of geography was the dominant tradition in American cultural geography for much of the 20th century, centred on the Department of Geography at the University of California, Berkeley, under the leadership of Carl O. Sauer, who chaired the department from 1923 to 1954.

Approach

Sauer's programmatic essay "The Morphology of Landscape" (1925) defined geography as the study of the cultural landscape: the natural landscape as reshaped by the works of human cultures over time. Against the environmental determinism then prevalent, Sauer and his students emphasised culture as an active agent: "culture is the agent, the natural area is the medium, the cultural landscape is the result." Research stressed field observation, historical reconstruction, the domestication and diffusion of plants and animals, and the material traces of settlement — house types, field patterns, and agricultural systems.

People and regional focus

The Berkeley School drew on German landscape traditions and trained generations of geographers, many of whom worked on Latin America, where Sauer himself did extensive fieldwork in Mexico. Notable associated figures include Fred B. Kniffen, known for work on folk housing in Louisiana, and James J. Parsons, a pioneer of environmental history and Latin American studies.

Criticism and legacy

From the 1960s the school's descriptive, superorganic conception of culture was criticised by proponents of the quantitative revolution and later by the "new cultural geography", which recast culture as contested and symbolic rather than as a unified agent. Nevertheless, the Berkeley tradition's methods — archival work, field ethnography of landscapes, and attention to human–environment history — left a lasting mark on cultural and historical geography.
