Dionysius the Areopagite was an Athenian judge of the Areopagus court who, according to the Acts of the Apostles (17:34), became a follower of the apostle Paul after hearing him preach in Athens. Tradition made him the first bishop of Athens and a martyr.

The Pseudo-Dionysian writings

Dionysius is chiefly famous because of a body of theological writings attributed to him: the Corpus Dionysiacum, comprising The Celestial Hierarchy, The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy, The Divine Names and The Mystical Theology, together with ten letters. These works, written in Greek in a strongly Neoplatonic idiom, systematically describe the hierarchies of angels and the apophatic (negative) approach to God.

Scholarship since the Renaissance has shown that the writings are in fact the work of an unknown author of the late 5th or early 6th century, conventionally called Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, who depended on the Neoplatonist Proclus. Despite this, the corpus exercised enormous influence on both Eastern and Western Christian theology, shaping medieval angelology, mysticism and the thought of figures from Maximus the Confessor to Thomas Aquinas.

Confusion with St Denis

In the Middle Ages Dionysius was widely identified with St Denis, the third-century apostle and patron saint of France — a conflation promoted by the abbey of Saint-Denis near Paris.
