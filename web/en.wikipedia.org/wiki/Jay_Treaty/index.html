Jay Treaty

Title page of the Jay Treaty as printed in 1795

The Jay Treaty, officially the Treaty of Amity, Commerce, and Navigation, Between His Britannic Majesty and the United States of America, was an agreement signed on November 19, 1794, between the United States and Great Britain. Negotiated by Chief Justice John Jay, it averted war and resolved several issues left over from the American Revolutionary War.

Terms

Britain agreed to evacuate its forts in the Northwest Territory by 1796 and to submit disputes over pre-revolutionary debts and seizures of American shipping to arbitration commissions. The United States received limited trading rights with British colonies in the West Indies. The treaty failed, however, to compensate for enslaved people carried away by the British in 1783 or to end the impressment of American sailors — grievances that persisted into the War of 1812.

Controversy

The treaty provoked fierce opposition from Democratic-Republicans, who saw it as a betrayal of France and of republican principles; Jay was burned in effigy across the country. Federalists, led by Alexander Hamilton, defended it as necessary to preserve peace and trade. The Senate ratified it by exactly the required two-thirds margin (20–10) in June 1795, and President George Washington signed it after public debate, though a bitter fight over funding in the House of Representatives followed in 1796.

Significance

By securing a decade of peace and booming trade with Britain, the Jay Treaty gave the young republic time to strengthen its institutions. It also sharpened the emerging first party system and led indirectly to Pinckney's Treaty with Spain (1795). Its commercial provisions expired after ten years, and rising tensions over impressment and neutral rights eventually brought war in 1812.
