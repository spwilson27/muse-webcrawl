The Robinson projection is a map projection of a world map which shows the entire world at once. It was specifically designed in an attempt to find a good compromise to the problem of readily showing the whole globe as a flat image. It was developed in 1963 by Arthur H. Robinson at the request of the Rand McNally company, which used it in general-purpose world maps from that time onwards.

History and design

Unlike most other projections, the Robinson was not devised by mathematical construction but was developed through trial and error, using a table of interpolation coordinates for the parallels. It is classified as a pseudocylindrical projection because of its straight parallels, along each of which the meridians are spaced evenly, but it is neither equal-area nor conformal; indeed, Robinson himself described it as a compromise.

The projection gained widespread recognition in 1988 when the National Geographic Society adopted it as its standard for world maps, replacing the Van der Grinten projection, which had been in use since 1922. National Geographic used the Robinson projection for about a decade before moving to the Winkel Tripel projection in 1998.

Characteristics

The Robinson projection flattens the poles into lines 0.5322 times the length of the equator, which reduces the severe area distortion near the poles found in the Mercator projection. Distances along the equator and the central meridian are true to scale, as are distances along parallels at 38 degrees north and south. Shapes and areas are distorted, but generally less so than in many other projections intended for world maps.
