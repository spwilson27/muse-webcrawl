Gregory the Great

Pope Gregory I (Latin: Gregorius I; c. 540 – 12 March 604), commonly known as Saint Gregory the Great, was Pope from 3 September 590 until his death. He is famous for initiating the Gregorian Mission to convert the Anglo-Saxons of England to Christianity and for his extensive writings, including the Pastoral Rule. He is one of the Latin Fathers of the Church and a Doctor of the Church.

Early life

Gregory was born in Rome to a wealthy noble family; his father Gordianus was a senator. As a young man Gregory served as prefect of Rome, but after his father's death he converted the family palace on the Caelian Hill into the monastery of St. Andrew and became a monk. Pope Pelagius II sent him as apocrisiarius (ambassador) to Constantinople, where he spent six years before returning to Rome.

Papacy

Elected pope in 590 during plague and Lombard invasion, Gregory reorganized the administration of the Church's estates, fed Rome's poor, negotiated peace with the Lombards, and reformed the liturgy — the chant tradition of the Roman Church bears his name as Gregorian chant. In 596 he dispatched Augustine of Canterbury on the mission to Kent that began the conversion of Anglo-Saxon England.

Writings and legacy

His works include the Moralia in Job, the Dialogues (whose second book preserves the earliest life of Benedict of Nursia), over 850 surviving letters, and the Regula pastoralis on the duties of bishops. Gregory styled the pope "servant of the servants of God" (servus servorum Dei), a title still used. He is the patron saint of musicians, singers, students, and teachers.
