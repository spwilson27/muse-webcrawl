Filippo Juvarra (7 March 1678 – 31 January 1736) was an Italian architect, stage designer, and goldsmith's son from Messina, Sicily, who became one of the leading architects of the late Baroque in Europe. Working first in Rome, he spent his most productive two decades (1714–1735) as court architect to Victor Amadeus II of Savoy in Turin, reshaping the Piedmontese capital, and died in Madrid while designing royal palaces for Philip V of Spain.

Life and career

Juvarra was trained in his father's silversmith workshop and in the priesthood, then studied architecture in Rome under Carlo Fontana. His virtuoso drawings and stage designs for Cardinal Ottoboni brought him fame; he designed sets and was scenographer at the cardinal's private theatre, and in 1714 won the competition patronage that took him to Messina and then to Turin.

For the Savoy court he built the church and monastery of Superga (1717–1731) crowning the hill east of Turin — commissioned in fulfilment of a vow made during the siege of 1706 — the Palazzo Madama façade (1718–1721), the royal hunting palace at Stupinigi (1729–1733) with its celebrated elliptical central saloon, the church of Santa Cristina, the Palazzo Birago, and the expansion of the Venaria Reale. In 1735 Philip V summoned him to Spain; he produced designs for the Royal Palace of Madrid and for La Granja de San Ildefonso before his sudden death in Madrid in January 1736. His wooden model for the Madrid palace survives; the palace as built was executed by his pupil Giovanni Battista Sacchetti.

Style

Juvarra's work unites the Roman Baroque of Borromini and Guarino Guarini — whose Piedmontese buildings he studied — with a new lightness, clarity, and theatrical handling of light and space. His thousands of surviving drawings, many in Turin's Biblioteca Nazionale, include town-planning schemes that opened Turin westward and remain a major record of Baroque architectural imagination.
