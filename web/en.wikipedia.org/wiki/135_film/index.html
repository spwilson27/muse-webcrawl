135 film, more commonly called 35 mm film, is a format of photographic film stock supplied in individual cassettes for still photography. Introduced by Kodak in 1934 for use in its Retina camera, it uses the same 35 mm-wide film stock developed for motion pictures, perforated along both edges and wound on a spool inside a light-tight metal canister with a velvet-lined slit through which the leader protrudes.

Characteristics

The standard image format is 36 × 24 mm, known as "full frame", although half-frame cameras exposing 18 × 24 mm images were also popular. Film is sold in standard lengths of 12, 24 and 36 exposures. Since the 1980s most 135 cartridges have carried DX encoding — barcodes and electrical contacts by which cameras read the film speed, length and exposure tolerance.

History and decline

The format was popularised by the Leica and other rangefinder cameras and, later, by single-lens reflex cameras; by the 1960s it had become the dominant amateur and professional still-photography format. Although digital photography displaced it from the mass market in the 2000s, 135 film continues to be manufactured by Kodak, Fujifilm and Ilford and enjoys a sustained revival among enthusiasts.
