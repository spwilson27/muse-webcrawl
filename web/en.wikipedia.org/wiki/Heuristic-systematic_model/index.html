Heuristic-systematic model


The two routes of the HSM

The heuristic-systematic model (HSM) of persuasion is a widely recognized model in social psychology developed by Shelly Chaiken and colleagues. It proposes that people process persuasive messages in two ways: heuristic processing, in which individuals rely on simple decision rules or cues (such as the expertise or attractiveness of the source), and systematic processing, in which individuals carefully scrutinize the actual content and arguments of the message.

Background

Chaiken first outlined the model in 1980. The HSM is one of several dual-process models of persuasion, alongside the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) developed by Richard Petty and John Cacioppo. While the two models share many similarities, the HSM uniquely proposes that heuristic and systematic processing can occur simultaneously, and that heuristic cues can bias systematic processing when the two conflict (the "bias hypothesis").

Key principles

The model rests on several principles. The sufficiency principle holds that people balance a desire for confidence in their judgments against a desire to minimize cognitive effort: they process only as much as needed to reach a desired level of confidence (the "sufficiency threshold"). Motivation and ability determine which mode dominates: under high personal relevance and adequate ability, systematic processing tends to prevail; under low motivation or limited ability, heuristic cues exert greater influence.

Applications

The HSM has been applied in studies of advertising, health communication, legal decision-making, and political persuasion. Research using the model has examined how source credibility, mood, and message repetition interact with argument quality.
