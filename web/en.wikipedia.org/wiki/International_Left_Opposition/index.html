International Left Opposition

Leon Trotsky founded the International Left Opposition in 1930 after his expulsion from the Soviet Union.

The International Left Opposition (ILO) was an international organisation of followers of Leon Trotsky, founded in 1930 as a faction within the Communist International (Comintern) opposed to the policies of Joseph Stalin's leadership.

Origins

After Trotsky was expelled from the Communist Party of the Soviet Union in 1927 and deported to Turkey in 1929, his supporters in several countries — who had been expelled from their national communist parties as the Left Opposition — began coordinating their work. In April 1930, representatives of opposition groups met in Paris and formed the International Left Opposition, with an International Secretariat, a journal (the Bulletin of the Opposition, published in Russian), and national sections in countries including France, Germany, Spain, and the United States.

Development

The ILO initially sought to reform the Comintern from within, criticising the bureaucratisation of the Soviet state, the theory of "socialism in one country", and the Comintern's ultra-left "Third Period" line, which treated social democrats as "social fascists" and divided the German labour movement in the face of Nazism. After Hitler's accession to power in 1933, which Trotsky blamed on Stalinist policy, the ILO abandoned the strategy of reform and proclaimed the need for new parties and a new International. Renamed the International Communist League in 1933, the movement's sections pursued "entryism" into social democratic parties in some countries and in 1938 founded the Fourth International.
