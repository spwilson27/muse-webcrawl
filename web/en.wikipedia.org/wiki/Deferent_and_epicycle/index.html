Deferent and epicycle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Epicyclic motion

A planet (P) moves on an epicycle whose centre moves along the deferent centred near the Earth

In the geocentric model of ancient and medieval astronomy, a deferent was a large circle centred on or near the Earth around which the centre of a smaller circle, the epicycle, travelled. A planet was carried on the epicycle, so that its combined motion could reproduce the observed variations in speed and direction of the planets, including apparent retrograde motion, while preserving the principle that celestial motions are composed of uniform circular motions.

History

The epicyclic scheme is associated with Apollonius of Perga in the 3rd century BC and was developed by Hipparchus in the 2nd century BC, who used epicycles and eccentric circles to model the motions of the Sun and Moon. Claudius Ptolemy systematised the model in the Almagest (2nd century AD), adding the equant — a point about which the epicycle's centre moved with uniform angular speed — to improve agreement with observations. The Ptolemaic system, combining deferents, epicycles, eccentrics and equants, predicted planetary positions with useful accuracy and remained the standard astronomical framework in the Islamic world and Europe for over a millennium.

How it works

As the centre of the epicycle travels around the deferent in the same sense as the planet travels around the epicycle, the planet's path forms a series of loops. When the planet is on the inner part of its epicycle, its motion relative to the deferent's motion produces the appearance of moving backwards against the fixed stars — retrograde motion — and also explains why planets brighten at that time, since they are then closer to Earth.

Decline

The model was gradually displaced after Copernicus proposed a heliocentric system in 1543, and Kepler's elliptical orbits (1609) removed the need for epicycles altogether. The phrase "adding epicycles" survives as a metaphor for ad hoc complications added to rescue a failing theory, although historians note that the epicyclic model was a mathematically respectable and empirically successful tool in its time.
