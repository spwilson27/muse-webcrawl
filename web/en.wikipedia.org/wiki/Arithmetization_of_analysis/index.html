Arithmetization of analysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The arithmetization of analysis replaced geometric intuition with arithmetic rigour.

The arithmetization of analysis was a research program in the foundations of mathematics carried out in the second half of the 19th century, which aimed to remove all geometric intuition from the foundations of calculus, replacing it with purely arithmetical (number-based) concepts. Its principal figures were Karl Weierstrass, Richard Dedekind, Georg Cantor, and their contemporaries, following preparatory work by Bernard Bolzano and Augustin-Louis Cauchy.

Background

Early calculus, as developed by Newton and Leibniz, relied on intuitive notions of infinitesimals and continuous motion, and geometry provided both its objects and its standards of rigour. Paradoxes and counterintuitive examples—such as continuous functions that are nowhere differentiable, discovered by Weierstrass—showed that geometric intuition was unreliable, prompting a demand for foundations built solely on number.

Main developments

  • Limits: The ε–δ (epsilon–delta) definition of limit, formulated by Cauchy and made systematic by Weierstrass, reduced the notions of limit, continuity, derivative, and integral to statements about inequalities between numbers.
  • Real numbers: Dedekind's cuts (1872) and Cantor's construction of the reals as equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences of rationals defined the real numbers purely arithmetically, without reference to geometric magnitude.
  • Sequences and series: Convergence was defined via the Cauchy criterion, freeing analysis from appeals to "approaching" a value.

Significance

The program was largely successful: by about 1900 analysis had been rebuilt on the basis of the real number system, which in turn could be constructed from the natural numbers, prompting Kronecker's famous remark that God made the integers and all else is the work of man. The arithmetization opened the way to the further foundational programs of logicism, formalism, and set theory, and it set the modern standard of rigour in analysis. Ironically, in the 1960s Abraham Robinson's nonstandard analysis rehabilitated infinitesimals on a rigorous logical footing.
