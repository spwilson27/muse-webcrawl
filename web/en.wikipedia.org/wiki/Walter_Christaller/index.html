Walter Christaller

Walter Christaller (21 April 1893 – 9 March 1969) was a German geographer whose principal contribution to the discipline is central place theory, first published in 1933. The theory, concerning the size, number and distribution of human settlements in an urban system, was a foundational work of quantitative geography and urban economics.

Life

Christaller was born in Berneck (now part of Altensteig) in the Black Forest, the son of a Protestant pastor. He studied philosophy and economics, served in the First World War, and completed his doctorate at the University of Erlangen in 1932. His dissertation became his famous work Die zentralen Orte in Süddeutschland ("Central Places in Southern Germany", 1933).

During the Second World War, Christaller worked in the planning apparatus of Nazi Germany, applying his settlement theory to the reorganisation of annexed territories in Eastern Europe — a role that was controversial after the war. He later joined the Communist Party of Germany, then the Social Democratic Party, and spent his later years applying central place theory in regional planning and tourism research in West Germany, while his ideas gained wide influence abroad, particularly in the United States.

Central place theory

Central place theory seeks to explain the spatial arrangement, functionality and hierarchy of settlements. Assuming an isotropic plain with evenly distributed population and purchasing power, Christaller showed that settlements serving as market centres ("central places") for surrounding areas would tend to arrange themselves in a regular hexagonal lattice: larger, higher-order centres offering specialised goods and services spaced widely apart, surrounded by smaller, lower-order centres offering everyday goods. The theory introduced key concepts such as the range of a good (the maximum distance consumers will travel for it) and the threshold (the minimum market size needed to support it), and organised settlement hierarchies by the marketing (k=3), transport (k=4) and administrative (k=7) principles.

Legacy

Christaller's model became a cornerstone of the quantitative revolution in geography and influenced urban planning, retail location analysis and regional policy in many countries. The Walter Christaller Award is given in his honour for work in applied geography.
