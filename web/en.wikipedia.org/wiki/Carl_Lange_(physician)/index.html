Carl Lange (physician)

Carl Georg Lange (4 December 1834 – 29 May 1900) was a Danish physician and psychologist best known for his 1885 work on the physiology of the emotions, in which he independently proposed a theory very similar to that of William James. Together, the two accounts form the James–Lange theory of emotion, which holds that emotions are the mind's perception of physiological changes in the body. Lange also made early clinical contributions on depression, tabes dorsalis and the therapeutic use of lithium.

Life and career

Lange studied medicine at the University of Copenhagen, completing his degree in 1859 and his doctorate in 1866 with work on bulbar paralysis. After hospital posts in Copenhagen and further study abroad, he became a lecturer and later professor of pathological anatomy, while maintaining a large private practice in nervous and mental diseases. His clinical lectures on tabes dorsalis and on spinal cord disorders were influential in Danish neurology.

The theory of emotion

In Om Sindsbevægelser ("On Emotions", 1885), Lange argued that the vasomotor system is the key to emotional life: an emotional stimulus first produces bodily changes — blood vessels constricting or dilating, the heart racing, muscles tensing — and the feeling we call the emotion is simply our perception of those changes. Remove the bodily symptoms, he claimed, and nothing of the emotion remains. William James had published a nearly identical view in 1884, arriving at it independently; the paired credit is preserved in the name James–Lange theory. The theory dominated early emotion research until Walter Cannon's critiques in the 1920s, and it remains a reference point — revived in part by modern somatic-marker and interoception research.

Lithium and depression

Lange was also among the first to use lithium systematically in psychiatry: reasoning (incorrectly) that periodic depression reflected a uric-acid diathesis, he treated melancholic patients with lithium salts in the 1880s and reported favourable results, anticipating John Cade's famous 1949 rediscovery of lithium for mania by more than half a century. His brother Fritz Lange likewise used lithium in Denmark, keeping the treatment alive there into the 20th century.
