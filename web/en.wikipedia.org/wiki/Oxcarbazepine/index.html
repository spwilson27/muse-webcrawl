Oxcarbazepine

Oxcarbazepine, sold under the brand name Trileptal among others, is an anticonvulsant medication used primarily in the treatment of epilepsy, particularly focal (partial-onset) seizures, in adults and children. It is the 10-keto analogue of carbamazepine, to which it is closely related in structure and effect, and was developed in an attempt to retain carbamazepine's efficacy while improving its side-effect and drug-interaction profile. Oxcarbazepine is also sometimes used off-label for bipolar disorder and trigeminal neuralgia, though the evidence for these uses is weaker than for epilepsy.

Medical uses

Oxcarbazepine is approved as monotherapy and adjunctive therapy for focal seizures. In the United States it is approved for adults and for children aged four and above (and as adjunctive therapy from age two). An extended-release formulation (Oxtellar XR) allows once-daily dosing. Compared with older enzyme-inducing anticonvulsants it has fewer drug interactions and does not induce its own metabolism to the same extent as carbamazepine, and it avoids carbamazepine's toxic epoxide metabolite. It is generally not effective for generalised absence seizures and may worsen them.

Mechanism of action

Oxcarbazepine is a prodrug that is rapidly and almost completely converted in the liver to its active metabolite, the monohydroxy derivative licarbazepine (MHD). MHD stabilises hyperexcited neuronal membranes and suppresses repetitive neuronal firing primarily by blocking voltage-sensitive sodium channels. It may also modulate high-voltage activated calcium channels and increase potassium conductance, contributing to the inhibition of excitatory neurotransmission.

Side effects

Common side effects include dizziness, drowsiness, double vision (diplopia), nausea, vomiting, headache, fatigue and unsteadiness (ataxia). A clinically important adverse effect is hyponatraemia (low blood sodium), which is more frequent with oxcarbazepine than with carbamazepine and can occasionally be severe, especially in elderly patients. Serious skin reactions, including Stevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis, are rare but are associated with the HLA-B*1502 allele, common in people of Han Chinese and other Asian ancestries, for whom screening may be recommended. Like other antiepileptic drugs, oxcarbazepine carries a warning regarding a small increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviour.

Interactions and monitoring

Oxcarbazepine and MHD are moderate inhibitors of CYP2C19 and weak inducers of CYP3A4; at higher doses they can reduce the effectiveness of hormonal contraceptives. Serum sodium should be monitored, particularly in patients on diuretics or with renal impairment. The drug is generally avoided in pregnancy where possible because anticonvulsants carry teratogenic risk, and folate supplementation is advised for women of childbearing potential.
