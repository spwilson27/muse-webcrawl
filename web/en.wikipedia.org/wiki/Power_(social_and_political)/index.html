Power (social and political)

Power
FieldSociology, political science
Key thinkersMax Weber, Michel Foucault, Steven Lukes, Hannah Arendt
RelatedAuthority, legitimacy, domination, hegemony

In social science and politics, power is the capacity of an individual or group to influence the behaviour of others, to shape outcomes, or to control resources and agendas. Max Weber defined power (Macht) as the probability that an actor within a social relationship will be in a position to carry out his own will despite resistance. Power may rest on force, wealth, status, knowledge, law, or persuasion, and it may be exercised openly or operate invisibly through norms and institutions.

Power is exercised through states, organisations, and everyday social relations.

Theories

Robert Dahl's classic pluralist study asked "who governs?", treating power as observable in decision-making: A has power over B to the extent that A can get B to do something B would not otherwise do. Steven Lukes, in Power: A Radical View (1974), proposed three "faces" or dimensions of power: (1) power in decision-making; (2) power in agenda-setting — keeping issues off the table ("non-decisions"); and (3) power that shapes people's very wants and beliefs, so that conflict need never surface. Lukes drew partly on Antonio Gramsci's concept of cultural hegemony.

Michel Foucault argued against treating power as a possession held by rulers: power is relational, dispersed through institutions and discourses, productive as well as repressive, and bound up with knowledge ("power/knowledge"). Hannah Arendt distinguished power — the human capacity to act in concert, belonging to a group — from violence, strength and authority. John French and Bertram Raven identified bases of social power: legitimate, reward, coercive, expert and referent power (later adding informational power).

Forms and sources

  • Political power: exercised through the state, parties, and law; distinguished from authority, power recognised as legitimate.
  • Economic power: control of assets, employment, markets and capital.
  • Soft power: Joseph Nye's term for influence through attraction — culture, values and foreign policy legitimacy — as opposed to coercion ("hard power").
  • Structural power: advantages built into institutions and roles themselves.
