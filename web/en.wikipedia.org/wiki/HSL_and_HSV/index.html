HSL and HSV

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

HSL and HSV
TypeColour models (transformations of RGB)
ComponentsHue, saturation, lightness (HSL) or value (HSV)
Devised1970s (computer graphics)

HSL (hue, saturation, lightness) and HSV (hue, saturation, value) are alternative representations of the RGB colour model, designed in the 1970s by computer graphics researchers to more closely align with the way human vision perceives colour-making attributes. In these models, colours of each hue are arranged in a radial slice, around a central axis of neutral colours which ranges from black at the bottom to white at the top.

The HSV representation models how colours appear under light. The HSL representation models how different paints mix to create colour in the real world — its lightness dimension resembles varying amounts of black or white paint in a mixture.

Geometry

HSV is commonly pictured as a cone or a cylinder: hue is the angle around the central axis (0° for red, 120° for green, 240° for blue), saturation is the distance from the axis, and value is the distance along the axis, with black at the apex or base. HSL is pictured as a double cone or a cylinder in which pure hues sit at half lightness, with white at one end and black at the other. In both models, fully saturated colours lie on the outer surface and unsaturated greys toward the centre axis.

Conversion from RGB

Given RGB components in the range 0–1, let max and min be the largest and smallest components, and C = max − min the chroma. Hue is computed from which component is maximal, expressed in degrees from 0 to 360. For HSV, value V = max and saturation S = C / V. For HSL, lightness L = (max + min) / 2 and saturation S = C / (1 − |2L − 1|). If C = 0 the colour is achromatic and hue is undefined.

Uses

HSL and HSV are used in colour pickers in virtually all image editing and drawing software, because adjusting hue, saturation, and lightness is more intuitive than mixing red, green, and blue channels. HSL is a standard colour notation in web design via the CSS hsl() function. The models are also used in computer vision for colour-based segmentation, since they partly separate chromatic information from brightness. Their chief disadvantage is that neither is perceptually uniform: colours with the same numerical lightness or value may appear very different in brightness.
