Dynamic frequency scaling

Dynamic frequency scaling (also called CPU throttling) is a power management technique in computer architecture whereby the clock frequency of a processor is adjusted on the fly, either to conserve power and reduce heat or, in "boost" modes, to increase performance temporarily. It is closely related to dynamic voltage scaling; together they are known as DVFS, since power consumption rises steeply with both frequency and voltage.

How it works

Operating systems use governors — policies such as "performance", "powersave" and "ondemand" — to raise or lower the CPU's operating frequency in discrete steps (P-states) according to load. Modern processors from Intel (SpeedStep, Turbo Boost) and AMD (Cool'n'Quiet, Precision Boost) automate much of this in hardware, reacting within milliseconds.

Thermal throttling

Frequency scaling is also used defensively: if a chip approaches its temperature limit, firmware reduces its clock speed to prevent damage, a behaviour visible in laptops and phones under sustained load. Dynamic frequency scaling is a major reason battery-powered devices can combine long idle life with bursts of high performance.
