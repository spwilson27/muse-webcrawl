Cashmere wool

Cashmere wool, usually simply known as cashmere, is a fibre obtained from cashmere goats, pashmina goats, and some other breeds of goat. It has been used to make yarn, textiles and clothing for hundreds of years.

Cashmere wool
Source: Cashmere and pashmina goats
Main producers: China, Mongolia, Iran, Afghanistan, India
Uses: Sweaters, shawls, scarves

Characteristics

Cashmere is finer, softer, lighter and several times more insulating than sheep's wool, though also less durable. Fibres are typically 14–19 microns in diameter. A single goat produces only about 150 grams of usable fibre per year, contributing to cashmere's high price.

Production

The soft undercoat is combed or shorn in spring, then de-haired to separate fine down from coarse guard hairs. Major production centres include Inner Mongolia, Mongolia, Kashmir, Iran and Afghanistan. The name derives from Kashmir, famed for its pashmina shawls exported to Europe in the 18th and 19th centuries.

Environmental concerns

Growth in demand and larger goat herds have been linked to grassland degradation and desertification in Mongolia and northern China, prompting sustainability certification efforts.
