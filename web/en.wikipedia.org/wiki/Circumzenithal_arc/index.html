Circumzenithal arc

The circumzenithal arc, also called the circumzenith arc (CZA), the Bravais arc, or colloquially a "smile in the sky", is an optical phenomenon similar in appearance to a rainbow, but belonging to the family of halos arising from refraction of sunlight through ice crystals, generally in cirrus or cirrostratus clouds, rather than from raindrops. The arc is located at a considerable distance above the Sun and at most forms a quarter of a circle centred on the zenith. It has been called "a smile in the sky", its first impression being that of an upside-down rainbow.

A circumzenithal arc appears as a brightly coloured inverted arc near the zenith.

Formation

The light that forms the CZA enters an ice crystal through its flat top face and exits through a side prism face. The refraction of almost-parallel sunlight through what is essentially a 90-degree prism accounts for the wide colour separation and the purity of colour. The arc can only be observed when the Sun is at an altitude of less than 32.2 degrees. The arc is brightest when the Sun is 22 degrees above the horizon, which causes the sunlight to enter and exit the crystals at the minimum deviation angle; then it is also about 22 degrees in radius.

The CZA is noticeably bright and displays vivid rainbow colours, with red on the outside (toward the Sun) and violet on the inside. It does not require a relatively dark sky background, though when the Sun is low the phenomenon can be quite inconspicuous.

History

The phenomenon was first systematically described by Auguste Bravais in 1847, whose name is sometimes attached to the arc. Observers had noted it much earlier, however; it appears in meteorological records and optical treatises from the eighteenth century onward.
