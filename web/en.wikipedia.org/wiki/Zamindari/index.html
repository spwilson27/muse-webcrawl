Zamindari

Zamindari (from Persian zamīn, "land") was a system of land tenure and revenue collection in India under which a zamindar, a hereditary landlord or revenue farmer, held rights over land and collected rent or revenue from the peasants (ryots) who cultivated it. The system existed in various forms under the Mughal Empire and was greatly reshaped under British rule.

History

Under the Mughals, zamindars were local chiefs and landholders who collected land revenue on behalf of the state and maintained law and order in their territories, retaining a share of the collections. Their rights were often hereditary, though formally dependent on imperial recognition.

In 1793, Lord Cornwallis introduced the Permanent Settlement in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, which recognised zamindars as proprietors of the land on condition of paying a fixed revenue to the East India Company. The settlement created a class of landlords modelled loosely on English landed gentry, but the high fixed demand and rigid payment deadlines led many estates to be auctioned for arrears, especially in the early decades.

Features and effects

Zamindars stood between the state and the cultivating peasants, often through chains of intermediaries. Critics charged that the system encouraged absentee landlordism and rack-renting of tenants, while defenders argued it provided stability of revenue. Zamindari tenures were most prominent in Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, parts of the United Provinces and northern Madras Presidency, in contrast to the ryotwari and mahalwari systems elsewhere.

Abolition

After Indian independence, zamindari abolition was among the first major land reforms undertaken by the new states in the late 1940s and 1950s. Legislation such as the Bihar Land Reforms Act of 1950 and the Uttar Pradesh Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act of 1950 vested intermediary interests in the state, with compensation paid to former zamindars, and brought cultivators into a direct relationship with the government.
