Pope Pius VI (Italian: Pio VI; born Giovanni Angelo Braschi, 25 December 1717 – 29 August 1799) was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 15 February 1775 until his death in August 1799. His reign of more than 24 years was among the longest in papal history and coincided with the French Revolution, whose anticlerical measures and military campaigns stripped him of his temporal power; he died a prisoner in France.

Early life and election

Braschi was born at Cesena in the Romagna, studied law, and entered papal service as secretary to Cardinal Tommaso Ruffo. He served Benedict XIV and Clement XIII, became papal treasurer, and was created cardinal in 1773 by Clement XIV. Following Clement's death, the conclave of 1774–1775 — dominated by the question of the recently suppressed Society of Jesus and by the pressure of the Bourbon courts — elected Braschi, who took the name Pius VI.

Pontificate before the Revolution

Pius worked to strengthen the Papal States' finances, drained portions of the Pontine Marshes, improved the port of Ancona, and enlarged the Vatican Museums (the Museo Pio-Clementino bears his and his predecessor's names). He was criticised for lavish patronage of his Braschi nephews, one of whom he made a cardinal and another Duke of Nemi. His chief external struggle was with the Emperor Joseph II of Austria, whose "Josephinist" reforms subordinated the Church to the state, suppressed contemplative orders, and asserted state control over seminaries, marriage, and communication with Rome. In an unprecedented step, Pius travelled to Vienna in 1782 to negotiate in person; the visit was courteous but largely unsuccessful. He likewise condemned the reforming Synod of Pistoia (1786) in the bull Auctorem fidei (1794).

The French Revolution

The Revolution transformed Pius's reign. He condemned the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790), which made the French Church a department of the revolutionary state, and suspended clergy who took the required oath. Revolutionary France annexed Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin, long-standing papal enclaves. When war came, French forces under Napoleon Bonaparte invaded Italy; by the Treaty of Tolentino (1797) Pius was forced to cede the Legations and pay a heavy indemnity, and to surrender many works of art.

Deposition and death

In February 1798, following the killing of a French general in a Roman riot, French troops occupied Rome, proclaimed a Roman Republic, and declared Pius deposed as temporal ruler. The aged and ailing pope was arrested and carried into exile — first to Siena and Florence, then across the Alps to Grenoble and finally to the citadel of Valence in southeastern France. There he died on 29 August 1799, a prisoner of the Republic. His body remained unburied for months until Napoleon, by then First Consul, permitted interment; his remains were returned to Rome in 1801 and buried in St Peter's Basilica. The cardinals, scattered by war, assembled at Venice to elect his successor, Pius VII.
