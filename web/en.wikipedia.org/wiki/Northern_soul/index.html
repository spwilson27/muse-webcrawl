Northern soul

Northern soul dancers at an all-nighter

Northern soul is a music and dance movement that emerged in Northern England in the late 1960s, centred on obscure American soul records of the mid-1960s — particularly fast, Motown-style recordings by lesser-known artists — played at all-night club sessions. The name was coined by the journalist Dave Godin around 1970 to describe the records favoured by customers from the North visiting his London shop.

The scene

Key venues included the Twisted Wheel in Manchester, the Golden Torch in Stoke-on-Trent, the Blackpool Mecca and, most famously, Wigan Casino, whose all-nighters ran from 1973 until 1981 and attracted thousands of dancers each week. DJs such as Richard Searling and Kev Roberts competed to find ever-rarer American 45s, and "cover-ups" — records played with their labels concealed — protected prized discoveries. Original pressings of the rarest records, such as Frank Wilson's "Do I Love You (Indeed I Do)", became extremely valuable.

Dance and culture

Northern soul dancing is athletic and solo, featuring spins, kicks, splits and backdrops, performed on talc-dusted floors in loose clothing. The scene had its own fashions — wide trousers, bowling shirts, badges bearing the clenched-fist logo and the motto "Keep the Faith" — and its vocabulary of rare soul shaped later British club culture and the "rare groove" scene.

Legacy

The movement has enjoyed repeated revivals and continues with weekenders and all-nighters across Britain and abroad. It has been depicted in the films SoulBoy (2010) and Elaine Constantine's Northern Soul (2014).
