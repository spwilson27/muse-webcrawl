Scientific illustration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Scientific illustration is the practice of producing images that record, explain and communicate scientific information. Scientific illustrators combine artistic skill with detailed knowledge of a subject — anatomy, botany, zoology, geology, medicine, astronomy and other fields — to create images that are accurate, clear and informative. Unlike photography, an illustration can emphasise diagnostic features, reconstruct extinct organisms, show structures hidden inside a specimen, or combine several views and stages into a single figure.

History

The tradition reaches back to ancient herbal and anatomical manuscripts, but it flourished with print. Renaissance landmarks include the woodcuts of Leonhart Fuchs's De historia stirpium (1542) and the anatomical plates of Andreas Vesalius's De humani corporis fabrica (1543). In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, artists such as Maria Sibylla Merian documented the insects and plants of Suriname, while the great voyages of exploration employed artists — Sydney Parkinson on Cook's Endeavour voyage among them — to record newly encountered species. The nineteenth century produced masters of lithography and engraving: John James Audubon's Birds of America, the botanical paintings of Pierre-Joseph Redouté, and Ernst Haeckel's Kunstformen der Natur, whose radiolarian plates remain famous.

Techniques

Traditional media include pen and ink (often with stippling to render tone), watercolour and gouache, carbon dust, scratchboard, and coloured pencil. Illustrators typically work from specimens, dissections, microscope observations and photographs, verifying details with the scientists who commission the work. Since the late twentieth century, digital tools — vector drawing, raster painting, 3D modelling and rendering — have become standard, and medical and technical illustrators routinely build images from CT and MRI data.

Applications

  • Taxonomy: illustrations accompanying the description of new species, where drawings can highlight the characters that distinguish a species.
  • Medicine: anatomical and surgical illustration, from atlases such as Frank Netter's to patient-education material.
  • Publishing: figures for textbooks, field guides, journals, museum exhibits and documentaries.
  • Reconstruction: palaeoart and archaeological reconstruction visualising subjects that cannot be photographed.

Profession

Scientific illustration is a recognised profession with training programmes — notably in medical illustration — and professional bodies such as the Guild of Natural Science Illustrators (founded 1968) and the Association of Medical Illustrators. Although photography displaced illustration in some roles, illustrated figures remain preferred where clarity, selectivity and synthesis matter more than literal record.
