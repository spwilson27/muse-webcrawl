Cryptozoology is a pseudoscientific field that seeks evidence for the existence of animals that are legendary, otherwise extinct, or unrecognised by science — collectively called cryptids. It was systematised in the 1950s by zoologist Bernard Heuvelmans, author of On the Track of Unknown Animals (1955), and popularised by Ivan T. Sanderson.

Approach and criticism

Cryptozoologists typically rely on eyewitness testimony, folklore, footprint casts, and ambiguous photographs or film. Mainstream scientists criticise the field for disregarding the scientific method, for accepting anecdote as evidence, and for rarely producing specimens despite decades of searching. Some animals once dismissed as legend — the okapi, the giant squid, and the coelacanth (a rediscovered "living fossil") — were later scientifically documented, and cryptozoologists cite these as vindication, though their discovery proceeded through conventional zoological evidence.

Notable cryptids

  • Bigfoot / Sasquatch — a large ape-like figure reported in North American forests; the 1967 Patterson–Gimlin film remains its most famous "evidence".
  • Loch Ness Monster — a long-necked creature said to inhabit Loch Ness, Scotland; sonar and environmental-DNA surveys have found no unknown large animal.
  • Yeti — the "Abominable Snowman" of the Himalayas; DNA analyses of attributed samples have matched bears and other known animals.
  • Chupacabra — a livestock-attacking creature reported from Puerto Rico and the Americas from the 1990s, generally explained by coyotes with mange.

Cultural impact

Whatever its scientific standing, cryptozoology is a durable part of popular culture, sustaining museums, festivals, television series, and local tourism in places associated with famous cryptids.
