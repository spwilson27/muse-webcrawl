Archaeopteryx

Archaeopteryx ("ancient wing") is a genus of bird-like dinosaurs from the Late Jurassic of Germany, around 150 million years ago. Roughly the size of a raven, it combined feathers and flight-capable wings with teeth, clawed fingers, and a long bony tail inherited from its theropod ancestors. Discovered in the Solnhofen limestone just two years after Darwin published On the Origin of Species, it became famous as a transitional fossil — the "first bird" — demonstrating the evolutionary link between dinosaurs and birds.

Specimens and discovery

About twelve specimens are known, all from the lithographic limestone quarries around Solnhofen and Eichstätt in Bavaria. The first, a single feather, was described in 1861; the London specimen followed the same year and the celebrated Berlin specimen — the most complete, with a preserved skull — in 1877. The fossils were acquired by museums in London, Berlin, Munich, and elsewhere, and individual specimens have been reclassified among several proposed species.

Anatomy and flight

Archaeopteryx had asymmetrical flight feathers with curved central shafts like those of modern flying birds, suggesting some aerial ability, though its shoulder anatomy and small sternum imply it was at most a weak, burst flier or glider. Adults weighed an estimated 0.8–1 kg. Analysis of melanosomes in a fossil feather suggests at least part of the plumage was black. Its brain and inner ear show bird-like features associated with flight and balance.

Significance

Thomas Henry Huxley used Archaeopteryx in 1868 to argue for the dinosaurian origin of birds; a century of debate followed, but the discovery since the 1990s of many feathered dinosaurs in China has confirmed its position near the base of the bird lineage. It remains an icon of evolution.
