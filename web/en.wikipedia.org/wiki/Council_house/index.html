Council house

A council house is a form of British public housing built by local authorities. A council estate is a building complex containing a number of council houses and other amenities like schools and shops. Construction took place mainly from 1919 to 1980, with less construction in recent years. There were local design variations, but the designs generally followed specific guidelines made in the government's Housing Manuals.

History

Large-scale council house building began with the Housing, Town Planning, &c. Act 1919 (the "Addison Act"), which promised "homes fit for heroes" after the First World War. Under housing minister John Wheatley, the Housing Act 1924 boosted construction, and the Tudor Walters standards set generous space and amenity norms. Building peaked in the post-Second World War decades under both Labour and Conservative governments; by 1979 around a third of British households lived in council housing.

Right to Buy and decline

The Housing Act 1980 introduced the Right to Buy, allowing sitting tenants to purchase their homes at a discount. More than two million council homes were sold, and restrictions on the use of receipts prevented like-for-like replacement, so the council housing stock shrank dramatically. Most remaining stock was transferred to housing associations, though some councils continue to build new council houses in smaller numbers.
