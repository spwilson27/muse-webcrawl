The Middle Eastern theatre of World War I saw operations between 29 October 1914 and 30 October 1918 in the territories of the Ottoman Empire, which entered the war on the side of the Central Powers. Fighting ranged across Mesopotamia, the Sinai and Palestine, the Caucasus, Persia, Arabia and the Gallipoli peninsula, pitting Ottoman forces, aided by Germany, against the British Empire, Russia, France and Arab irregulars.

Major campaigns

The Caucasus campaign opened with the Ottoman disaster at Sarikamish (1914–15), after which Russian forces advanced into eastern Anatolia. At Gallipoli (1915–16), an Allied attempt to force the Dardanelles and capture Constantinople was repelled by Ottoman forces under Mustafa Kemal and Liman von Sanders. In Mesopotamia, a British Indian expedition advanced from Basra, suffered the surrender at Kut (1916), then recovered to take Baghdad in 1917. The Sinai and Palestine campaign saw the defence of the Suez Canal, the advance across Sinai, and General Allenby's victories at Gaza, Jerusalem (1917) and Megiddo (1918), which destroyed the Ottoman armies in Syria. In Arabia, the Arab Revolt (1916–1918), advised by T. E. Lawrence, harassed the Hejaz railway and took Aqaba and Damascus.

Home front and atrocities

The theatre also encompassed the Ottoman government's deportation and extermination of its Armenian population in the Armenian genocide (1915–1917), alongside atrocities against Assyrian and Greek Christians, and the Great Famine of Mount Lebanon. Disease and starvation killed large numbers of soldiers and civilians across the region.

Outcome

The Armistice of Mudros (30 October 1918) ended hostilities. The subsequent peace settlement — the Treaty of Sèvres (1920), superseded by the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) — dissolved the Ottoman Empire, created British and French mandates in Iraq, Palestine, Transjordan, Syria and Lebanon, and laid the political foundations of the modern Middle East, amid competing promises made in the McMahon–Hussein Correspondence, the Sykes–Picot Agreement and the Balfour Declaration.
