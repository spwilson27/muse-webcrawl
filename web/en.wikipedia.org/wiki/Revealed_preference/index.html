Revealed preference

Revealed preference theory
FieldMicroeconomics, consumer theory
Introduced byPaul Samuelson (1938, 1948)
Key axiomsWARP, SARP, GARP
Related conceptsUtility, indifference curves, demand theory

Revealed preference theory, pioneered by the American economist Paul Samuelson in 1938, is a method of analyzing the choices made by individuals, mostly used in consumer demand theory. It holds that the preferences of consumers can be inferred from what they actually choose to purchase under different income and price circumstances, rather than from an assumed underlying utility function. If a consumer chooses one bundle of goods over another affordable bundle, the chosen bundle is said to be "revealed preferred" to the alternative.

History

Samuelson first presented the idea in a 1938 paper, "A Note on the Pure Theory of Consumer's Behaviour", and expanded it in his 1947 book Foundations of Economic Analysis. The approach was developed further by Hendrik Houthakker, who showed in 1950 that the Strong Axiom of Revealed Preference characterizes rationalizable choice behaviour, and by Sidney Afriat, whose 1967 theorem linked the Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference (GARP) to the existence of a utility function consistent with observed choices.

Axioms

The Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP) states that if a bundle x is revealed preferred to y, then y can never be revealed preferred to x when both are affordable. The Strong Axiom (SARP) extends this to indirect (transitive) chains of revealed preference. The Generalized Axiom (GARP), introduced by Hal Varian, allows for the case where a consumer is indifferent between bundles and underpins modern nonparametric tests of utility maximization, such as Afriat's theorem.

Criticisms

Critics note that revealed preference assumes choices are stable, transitive and unaffected by framing, assumptions challenged by behavioural economics; experiments have documented intransitivities and preference reversals. Defenders respond that the theory is a tool for deriving testable restrictions on demand behaviour without committing to a psychological account of preference.
