Starlink in satellite imagery

Starlink, the satellite internet constellation operated by SpaceX, has a notable presence in and impact on satellite imagery and astronomical imaging. Since the first launch of 60 satellites in May 2019, thousands of Starlink spacecraft in low Earth orbit have appeared in telescope exposures, Earth-observation data and long-exposure night-sky photography, usually as bright streaks or distinctive "trains" of lights shortly after deployment.

Appearance in imagery

Freshly launched Starlink satellites travel in a tight line — the "Starlink train" — that is widely photographed and filmed before the satellites disperse to their operational orbits at about 550 km altitude. In scientific images they register as linear trails crossing the field of view; surveys such as the Zwicky Transient Facility reported a rapidly growing fraction of twilight images affected by satellite streaks as the constellation expanded.

Impact on astronomy

Astronomers raised concerns early in the programme about interference with ground-based optical and radio astronomy, including wide-field survey telescopes such as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory. Studies estimated that a few percent of some survey images could contain trails, most removable in processing but with some loss of data.

Mitigation

SpaceX has experimented with darker coatings ("DarkSat"), sun-shade visors ("VisorSat") and, on later Starlink v2 Mini satellites, dielectric mirror films and off-pointing of solar arrays to reduce brightness. The International Astronomical Union established a Centre for the Protection of the Dark and Quiet Sky (CPS) in 2022 to coordinate responses.

Earth observation

Starlink satellites also cross the fields of view of Earth-observing satellites and the International Space Station, and conjunction warnings between mega-constellation spacecraft are a routine part of space traffic management.
