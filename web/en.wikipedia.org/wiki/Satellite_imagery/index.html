Satellite imagery

Satellite imagery (also called Earth observation imagery) consists of images of Earth or other planets collected by imaging satellites operated by governments and commercial providers. Images are captured across visible and non-visible parts of the electromagnetic spectrum and are used in meteorology, agriculture, forestry, land-use planning, disaster response, intelligence and environmental monitoring.

History

The first image of Earth from space was taken in 1946 from a captured V-2 rocket; the first satellite photographs came from Explorer 6 in 1959 and the TIROS weather satellites of the 1960s. The American Corona spy-satellite programme returned film capsules through the 1960s. The Landsat programme, begun in 1972, established continuous civilian Earth observation at moderate resolution, and its archive was made free in 2008. Commercial high-resolution imaging began with IKONOS in 1999; today operators such as Maxar and Planet image the entire land surface daily at resolutions down to about 30 cm.

Characteristics

Images are described by spatial resolution (size of a pixel on the ground), spectral resolution (number and width of wavelength bands), radiometric resolution and revisit time. Multispectral and hyperspectral sensors record bands beyond visible light, allowing indices such as NDVI for vegetation health. Synthetic-aperture radar images are formed from reflected microwave pulses and work at night and through cloud.

Applications

Uses include weather forecasting, crop-yield estimation, deforestation and ice-sheet monitoring, urban growth analysis, mapping for services such as Google Earth, verifying arms-control treaties and documenting war crimes and natural disasters.
