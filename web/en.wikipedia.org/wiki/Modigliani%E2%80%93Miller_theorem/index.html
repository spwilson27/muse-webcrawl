Modigliani–Miller theorem

FieldCorporate finance
Developed byFranco Modigliani and Merton Miller
Published1958; extensions 1961, 1963
Core claimFirm value is independent of capital structure under idealised assumptions

The Modigliani–Miller theorem is a foundational result in corporate finance, developed by Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller, stating that under certain assumptions the market value of a firm is independent of its capital structure — that is, of how it finances its operations through debt or equity. First published in 1958, the theorem is often regarded as the starting point of modern capital structure theory.

Propositions

Proposition I states that, in perfect markets with no taxes, bankruptcy costs or asymmetric information, the value of a levered firm equals the value of an otherwise identical unlevered firm. Proposition II states that the cost of equity rises linearly with leverage, exactly offsetting the benefit of cheaper debt, so the weighted average cost of capital remains constant.

The argument rests on an arbitrage mechanism: investors who dislike a firm's leverage can undo it by borrowing or lending on their own account, so no value can be created or destroyed by repackaging claims on the same cash flows.

Extensions

A 1963 correction introduced corporate taxes, showing that because interest payments are tax-deductible, firm value rises with debt — implying, absurdly, nearly all-debt financing. Later work balanced this tax shield against the expected costs of financial distress, producing trade-off theories of capital structure.

A companion 1961 paper by Modigliani and Miller established dividend irrelevance: under the same idealised conditions, a firm's dividend policy does not affect its value. Both authors later received Nobel Memorial Prizes in part for this work.

Significance

Although its assumptions are unrealistic, the theorem is valuable as a benchmark: it identifies exactly which market frictions — taxes, bankruptcy costs, agency costs and information asymmetries — must be responsible for any real-world effect of financing choices on firm value.
