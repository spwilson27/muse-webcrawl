Cèilidh

A cèilidh (Scottish Gaelic: cèilidh; Irish: céilí) is a traditional Scottish and Irish social gathering. In its most basic form, it simply means a social visit. In contemporary usage, it usually involves playing Gaelic folk music and dancing — Scottish country dancing and folk dances such as the Gay Gordons, the Dashing White Sergeant and Strip the Willow — either at a house party, in a large venue such as a village hall, or at a wedding reception or concert.

History

The word derives from the Old Irish céle, "companion". Traditionally a cèilidh was a gathering — often in a crofter's house, especially in the winter months — at which neighbours recited stories and poems, sang songs, played music and exchanged news; the folklorist Alexander Carmichael and later collectors recorded a rich oral tradition from such gatherings in the Highlands and Hebrides. Over the 20th century the emphasis shifted to dancing: organised cèilidh dances with bands (typically fiddle, accordion, piano and drums) became central to weddings and community celebrations in Scotland and the Scottish diaspora, while in Ireland the céilí became a more formalised dance tradition governed by the Irish dancing commission.

Modern cèilidh

A modern cèilidh band usually includes a "caller" who teaches each dance before it begins and calls the figures during it, so no experience is needed. The atmosphere is deliberately informal compared with formal Scottish country dancing: dances are energetic, mistakes are part of the fun, and sets repeatedly mix partners. Cèilidhs are a fixture of student life at Scottish universities, of weddings, and of festivals such as Celtic Connections.
