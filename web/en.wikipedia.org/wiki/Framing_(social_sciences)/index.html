Framing (social sciences)

In the social sciences, framing comprises a set of concepts and theoretical perspectives on how individuals, groups and societies organise, perceive and communicate about reality. A frame selects some aspects of a perceived reality and makes them more salient in a communicating text, promoting a particular problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation or remedy.

Framing
Field: Sociology, communication, political science
Key scholars: Erving Goffman, Robert Entman, social-movement theorists

Origins and theory

Erving Goffman’s Frame Analysis (1974) treated frames as schemata of interpretation guiding everyday experience. Robert Entman’s influential 1993 definition emphasises selection and salience in media texts. In social-movement theory, framing processes — diagnostic, prognostic and motivational framing, and frame alignment — explain how movements mobilise support.

Applications and effects

Framing effects, studied by psychologists and political scientists such as Shanto Iyengar, show that equivalent information presented differently (e.g., gains vs losses, episodic vs thematic news frames) shifts public opinion and policy choices. Framing is central to media studies, rhetoric and strategic communication, and is criticised when used manipulatively.
