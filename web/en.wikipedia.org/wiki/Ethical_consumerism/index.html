Ethical consumerism

Ethical consumerism (or ethical consumption) is the practice of purchasing products and services in ways that take account of their ethical impact — on workers, animals, communities and the environment — and of deliberately avoiding goods produced under conditions the buyer considers harmful. It is exercised through "positive buying" of certified or favoured goods and through boycotts and "buycotts" of disfavoured ones.

Forms

Ethical consumption is supported by an infrastructure of labels and standards: Fairtrade certification for producer prices and conditions, organic certification, the Forest Stewardship Council for timber, the Marine Stewardship Council for seafood, cruelty-free marks for cosmetics, and directories and magazines (such as the UK's Ethical Consumer, founded 1989) that rate companies. Related practices include local purchasing, vegetarianism and veganism, reduced consumption and "degrowth", and shareholder activism.

History

Consumer boycotts have a long history — from the abolitionist sugar boycotts of the 1790s and the co-operative movement to campaigns against apartheid-era South Africa, the Nestlé infant-formula boycott, and anti-sweatshop campaigns of the 1990s targeting global clothing brands. Institutionalised labelling began with the Max Havelaar fair-trade mark in the Netherlands in 1988.

Criticism

Critics note the "attitude–behaviour gap": surveys show many consumers profess ethical concern but relatively few consistently act on it, with price and convenience dominating. Others question whether individual purchasing can substitute for regulation, warn that ethical premiums may burden poorer consumers, or criticise certification as greenwashing. Supporters reply that ethical markets have measurably grown, pushed companies to adopt standards, and complement political action.
