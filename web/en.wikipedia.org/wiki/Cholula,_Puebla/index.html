Cholula, Puebla


Placeholder image: the Great Pyramid and the church of Los Remedios

Cholula (Spanish: [tʃoˈlula]), officially Cholula de Rivadavia, is a city and district located in the metropolitan area of Puebla, in the Mexican state of Puebla. It is divided into two municipalities, San Pedro Cholula and San Andrés Cholula, which together form one of the oldest continuously inhabited settlements in the Americas.

Cholula is best known for the Great Pyramid of Cholula, the largest pyramid by volume in the world, topped by the colonial church of Nuestra Señora de los Remedios, and for its large number of churches — local tradition claims one for every day of the year.

History

Cholula was an important religious and commercial center in pre-Hispanic Mesoamerica, sacred to the god Quetzalcoatl. The Great Pyramid, known as Tlachihualtepetl ("hand-made mountain"), was enlarged in successive stages over more than a thousand years, beginning around the 3rd century BCE.

In October 1519, during the Spanish conquest, Hernán Cortés and his Tlaxcalan allies carried out the Cholula massacre, killing thousands of inhabitants after suspecting a plot against the Spanish. After the conquest, the Franciscans built numerous churches over the city's pre-Hispanic temples, including the church of Los Remedios on the summit of the pyramid, completed in 1594.

Geography

The city lies about 8 kilometres (5 miles) west of the state capital, Puebla, at an elevation of roughly 2,150 metres (7,050 ft) above sea level, in a plain overlooked by the volcanoes Popocatépetl and Iztaccíhuatl to the west.

Culture and education

San Andrés Cholula is home to the Universidad de las Américas Puebla (UDLAP), one of Mexico's most prominent private universities, founded in 1940 as Mexico City College. The city is also known for its nightlife and for the Regional Museum of Cholula, opened in 2017 in a former psychiatric hospital building near the pyramid archaeological zone.
