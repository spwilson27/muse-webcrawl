McGurk effect

The McGurk effect is a perceptual phenomenon that demonstrates an interaction between hearing and vision in speech perception. The illusion occurs when the auditory component of one sound is paired with the visual component of another sound, leading to the perception of a third sound. For example, when the sound /ba/ is dubbed onto video of lips articulating /ga/, many listeners report hearing the intermediate syllable /da/.

Discovery

The effect was described by Harry McGurk and John MacDonald in a 1976 Nature paper, "Hearing Lips and Seeing Voices". The discovery was serendipitous: while studying how infants perceive speech, a technician dubbed a recording of one syllable onto a film of a speaker mouthing a different syllable, and the researchers themselves heard a third, fused sound.

Explanation

The McGurk effect shows that speech perception is inherently multisensory: the brain integrates visual information about articulation with the acoustic signal, and when the two conflict it may settle on a compromise percept. The effect is remarkably robust — it persists even when subjects are told about the dubbing — although its strength varies between individuals and languages, and it is weaker in some populations, including many people with autism spectrum conditions or dyslexia. It is widely cited in research on audiovisual integration, phonetics, and the design of speech-recognition and hearing-aid systems.
