Albert Schweitzer (14 January 1875 – 4 September 1965) was an Alsatian polymath — theologian, organist, musicologist, philosopher and physician — who founded a mission hospital at Lambaréné in French Equatorial Africa (now Gabon) and devoted most of his life to medical work there. He received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1952.

Theology and music

Trained at Strasbourg, Schweitzer won early fame with The Quest of the Historical Jesus (1906), a landmark of New Testament scholarship arguing that Jesus's preaching must be understood through Jewish eschatology. He was simultaneously an authority on the organ music of Johann Sebastian Bach, whose works he edited, and on organ building, advocating the "Alsatian reform" of organ design.

Lambaréné and Reverence for Life

Having resolved at 21 to give himself to direct service from the age of 30, Schweitzer studied medicine and in 1913 sailed to Africa, establishing a hospital at Lambaréné. Interned during the First World War as an enemy alien, he developed in captivity the ethical philosophy he called Ehrfurcht vor dem Leben — "Reverence for Life" — the conviction that the will to live shared by all creatures commands respect and care. He spent most of his remaining decades at the hospital, travelling to Europe and America to give concerts and lectures that raised funds for its work.

Later years

In the 1950s Schweitzer campaigned against nuclear weapons testing, corresponding with Einstein and Russell. He died at Lambaréné in 1965 and is buried there. Critics have noted the paternalism of his colonial-era hospital, but his ethic of reverence for life has remained influential in environmental and medical ethics.
