Crypt

A crypt (from Latin crypta "vault") is a stone chamber beneath the floor of a church or other building. It typically contains coffins, sarcophagi, or religious relics. Originally, crypts were typically found below the main apse of a church, and were used as burial places for saints and martyrs, whose tombs attracted pilgrims.

History

The earliest crypts developed in early Christian churches, where the altar was placed above the tomb of a martyr (confessio). In the Carolingian and Romanesque periods, crypts grew into large vaulted halls beneath the chancel, sometimes extending under much of the church; famous examples include the crypts of Canterbury Cathedral, St. Peter's Basilica in Rome, and Speyer Cathedral, one of the largest Romanesque crypts in Europe.

Uses

Besides housing tombs and relics, crypts often contain chapels and altars of their own and are used for private prayer or smaller services. Royal and noble families frequently used church crypts as dynastic burial places, such as the Imperial Crypt in Vienna used by the Habsburgs. The term is also used for subterranean burial chambers in cemeteries and mausolea more generally.
