Managed democracy

Managed democracy (Russian: управляемая демократия, upravlyayemaya demokratiya) is a term used to describe the political system of the Russian Federation under Vladimir Putin, in which the formal institutions of democracy — elections, multiple parties, courts and media — continue to exist, but their outcomes are effectively predetermined by those in power. The term is closely associated with the Kremlin political technologist Gleb Pavlovsky and with the related concept of "sovereign democracy" promoted by Vladislav Surkov.

Features

Analysts describing managed democracy point to a set of characteristic mechanisms:

  • Registration barriers, disqualifications and administrative pressure that keep genuine opposition candidates off the ballot.
  • Dominance of national television by state-aligned owners, marginalising independent viewpoints.
  • "Systemic opposition" parties that criticise the government within limits but support it on key votes.
  • Elections whose winners are known in advance, used to legitimise rather than to choose.
  • Selective use of the law and the security apparatus against independent businesses, media and politicians.

History

The phrase circulated in the 1990s regarding the Yeltsin era but gained currency in the early 2000s as gubernatorial elections were abolished (2004), parliamentary thresholds were raised, and the ruling United Russia party consolidated control. The system deepened after the protest wave of 2011–12, with laws on "foreign agents" and undesirable organisations, and after 2022 with the near-total elimination of independent media.

Usage

The expression has been applied beyond Russia to other hybrid or competitive-authoritarian regimes in which democratic forms coexist with entrenched incumbent control.
