Narcissistic personality disorder (NPD) is a personality disorder characterised by a life-long pattern of exaggerated feelings of self-importance, an excessive need for admiration, and a diminished ability to empathise with other people's feelings. People with NPD often spend much time thinking about achieving power or success, and may take advantage of the people around them. The behaviour typically begins by early adulthood and occurs across a variety of social situations. The condition is a Cluster B personality disorder in the DSM-5.

The disorder takes its name from the Greek myth of Narcissus, who fell in love with his own reflection.

Signs and symptoms

The DSM-5 lists nine criteria, of which five must be present:

  • A grandiose sense of self-importance, exaggerating achievements and talents
  • Preoccupation with fantasies of unlimited success, power, brilliance, beauty, or ideal love
  • A belief that one is "special" and can only be understood by, or should associate with, other special or high-status people
  • A need for excessive admiration
  • A sense of entitlement
  • Interpersonally exploitative behaviour
  • Lack of empathy
  • Frequent envy of others, or a belief that others envy them
  • Arrogant, haughty behaviours or attitudes

Clinicians distinguish grandiose ("overt") presentations from a "vulnerable" or covert presentation marked by hypersensitivity to criticism, shame, and social withdrawal beneath a grandiose self-image. NPD frequently co-occurs with depression, substance use disorders, bipolar disorder, and other personality disorders; narcissistic injury — criticism or defeat — can precipitate rage, humiliation, or suicidal behaviour.

Causes

The causes are not fully understood. Twin studies indicate substantial heritability of narcissistic traits. Proposed environmental contributors include inconsistent or excessively admiring parenting, and neurobiological research has reported differences in brain regions involved in empathy and self-referential processing, such as the anterior insula. Cultural factors, including individualism, are hypothesised to play a role but the evidence is debated.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, based on history and interview, and requires that the pattern be inflexible, pervasive, and cause significant impairment or distress. Structured interviews and self-report inventories (such as the Narcissistic Personality Inventory, which measures traits rather than the disorder) aid assessment. Differential diagnoses include bipolar mania, antisocial and histrionic personality disorders, and substance intoxication. The DSM-5's alternative model for personality disorders additionally describes NPD in terms of impairments in identity, self-direction, empathy, and intimacy, with grandiosity and attention-seeking as traits.

Treatment

No medication is approved specifically for NPD; treatment is psychotherapy, and people with NPD often seek help only for comorbid problems or after major setbacks. Approaches with some supporting evidence include transference-focused psychotherapy, mentalisation-based treatment, schema therapy, and metacognitive interpersonal therapy; these aim at the underlying self-esteem regulation and empathy deficits. Prognosis is guarded because limited insight and difficulty forming a therapeutic alliance are themselves features of the disorder, though outcomes improve with sustained treatment.

Epidemiology and history

The US National Epidemiologic Survey (NESARC, 2001–02) estimated lifetime prevalence at 6.2%, higher in men (7.7%) than women (4.8%). The term derives from Havelock Ellis's and Paul Näcke's work in the 1890s on "narcissism-like" self-love, and Sigmund Freud's essay "On Narcissism" (1914). Heinz Kohut's self psychology and Otto Kernberg's object-relations account offered competing theories of pathological narcissism in the 1960s–70s, and NPD entered official diagnosis with the DSM-III in 1980.
