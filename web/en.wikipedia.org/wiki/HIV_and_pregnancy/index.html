HIV and pregnancy

HIV and pregnancy concerns the management of human immunodeficiency virus infection during pregnancy and the prevention of mother-to-child transmission (MTCT), also called vertical transmission. Without intervention, the risk of transmission from a mother living with HIV to her infant during pregnancy, labour and delivery, or breastfeeding ranges from about 15% to 45%. With effective antiretroviral therapy and appropriate care, the risk falls below 1–2%.

Prevention of vertical transmission

The cornerstone of prevention is antiretroviral therapy for the mother, ideally achieving an undetectable viral load before delivery. Infants born to mothers with HIV receive prophylactic antiretroviral medication for the first weeks of life, with the regimen and duration determined by the estimated risk. The landmark ACTG 076 trial in 1994 showed that zidovudine given to mother and infant cut transmission by about two-thirds, and modern combination therapy has improved outcomes further.

Delivery and infant feeding

Where the mother's viral load is suppressed, vaginal delivery is generally considered safe; caesarean section may be recommended when the viral load near delivery is high or unknown. Advice on breastfeeding depends on setting: in high-income countries, formula feeding is usually recommended because it eliminates postnatal transmission, whereas in many low- and middle-income countries, breastfeeding while the mother remains on ART is encouraged because the risks of malnutrition and other infections from formula feeding outweigh the residual transmission risk.
