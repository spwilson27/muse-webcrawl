Palaeognathae

Palaeognathae is an infraclass of birds within the class Aves of the clade Archosauria. It is one of the two extant infraclasses of birds, the other being Neognathae, both distinguished by the anatomy of the bony palate. The Palaeognathae contains five extant orders: the flightless ratites (ostriches, rheas, cassowaries, emus and kiwis) and the flying tinamous of Central and South America.

Evolution and distribution

Palaeognaths are distributed almost entirely in the Southern Hemisphere, a pattern once attributed to the breakup of Gondwana carrying flightless ancestors apart. Molecular studies suggest instead that palaeognaths dispersed by flight and lost flight independently several times, with tinamous — which can fly — nested within the group, making the ratites paraphyletic as traditionally defined.

Extinct forms

Extinct palaeognaths include the moas of New Zealand, the elephant birds of Madagascar — among the largest birds ever to live — and the early flying lithornithids of the Paleogene Northern Hemisphere.

Characteristics

The palaeognathous palate retains a structure considered ancestral among birds, with a large vomer and complex connections between the pterygoid and palatine bones. Ratites additionally share a flat sternum without a keel and reduced wings, convergent adaptations to flightlessness.
