Census in the United Kingdom

A census of the population of the United Kingdom has been taken every ten years since 1801, with the sole exception of 1941, when it was cancelled because of the Second World War (a National Registration count was held in 1939 instead). The census records every person resident in the country on census night and collects data on age, sex, household composition, occupation, housing, and — in modern censuses — ethnicity, religion, health, and qualifications.

History

The first censuses (1801–1831) were simple headcounts organised by overseers of the poor. From 1841, under the newly created General Register Office, named schedules for each household were collected, producing the detailed individual returns that survive and are released publicly after 100 years. William Farr and later statisticians expanded the questions asked. The 1911 census was marked by suffragette boycotts and protests. No census was held in Ireland in 1921 owing to the Irish War of Independence, and the 1931 returns for England and Wales were destroyed by fire in 1942.

Organisation

The census is administered separately in each part of the UK: by the Office for National Statistics in England and Wales, National Records of Scotland, and the Northern Ireland Statistics and Research Agency. Completion is compulsory, with fines for refusal. The 2021 census was the first designed to be completed primarily online; it took place on 21 March 2021 in England, Wales, and Northern Ireland, while Scotland's census was delayed to 2022 by the COVID-19 pandemic and suffered low initial response rates.

Future

Statistical agencies have been exploring replacing or supplementing the traditional census with administrative data, following the "Beyond 2011" programme; the 2021 census may prove to be the last of its traditional kind.
