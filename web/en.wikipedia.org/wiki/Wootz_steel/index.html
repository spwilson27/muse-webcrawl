Wootz steel is a crucible steel characterised by a pattern of bands and high carbon content, originally produced in South India and Sri Lanka. The word wootz is a corruption of the Tamil word urukku or the Telugu/Kannada word ukku, meaning steel. Wootz ingots were exported across Asia and the Middle East, where they were forged into the celebrated blades known in Europe as Damascus steel.

Production

Wootz was made by sealing pieces of iron with carbonaceous material (wood, leaves, or charcoal) in small clay crucibles and heating them in a furnace for many hours, allowing the iron to absorb carbon and melt partially into a cake of high-carbon steel. Production centres in present-day Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka (notably at Konasamudram), and Sri Lanka supplied ingots from antiquity through the early 19th century. The process was described by travellers and investigated scientifically by Michael Faraday in 1819–1824, among others.

Characteristics

Wootz is a hypereutectoid steel containing roughly 1–1.6% carbon. Its microstructure consists of bands (sheets) of clustered cementite (iron carbide) particles in a ferrite/pearlite matrix. When a wootz cake is forged at modest temperatures and etched, these bands produce the flowing, watered-silk pattern (damask) prized in fine blades. Properly worked, wootz blades combine a hard, keen edge with toughness and flexibility. Modern research by J. D. Verhoeven and A. H. Pendray demonstrated that trace elements such as vanadium in the original ores helped the carbide banding form during thermal cycling.

History and decline

South Indian steel was famous in the ancient world; Indian ingots reached Persia and the Near East in antiquity and were praised by writers from the classical period onward. European attempts to analyse and reproduce wootz in the 18th and 19th centuries contributed to the development of modern metallurgy. Production declined in the 19th century owing to changes in trade, depletion or changing sources of ore, and competition from industrial European steels; by about 1900 the traditional craft had effectively died out.

Legacy

The study of wootz helped found the science of metallography. Contemporary bladesmiths have revived crucible-steel techniques and can produce steels closely resembling historical wootz. The word itself entered English through 18th-century reports of the Indian process.
