Level design

Level design, or environment design, is a discipline of game development involving the creation of video game levels — locales, stages, or missions. This is commonly done using a level editor, a specially designed program for building game levels; however, some games feature built-in level editing tools. Level design is both an artistic and technical process, shaping the player's experience through the layout of spaces, the placement of characters, items, and challenges, and the unfolding of scripted events.

Overview

The level designer lays out the challenges and geometry of a game world. Duties typically include documenting the level's goals, building the space in an editor ("greyboxing" or "blockout" first with primitive shapes), placing enemies, items, and interactive objects, scripting encounters, and iterating through playtesting to adjust difficulty, pacing, and flow. Good level design guides the player intuitively — teaching mechanics safely before testing them, using lighting, landmarks, and composition to signal where to go, and controlling the rhythm of tension and rest.

History

In the earliest arcade and home games, a single programmer or designer typically created all aspects of a game, including its stages. As games grew in complexity through the 1990s — with 3D shooters such as Doom and Quake, whose editors spawned a large modding community — level design became a distinct profession. Designers such as John Romero and American McGee exemplified the specialist role, and community level-making became a recognised path into the industry. The discipline broadened further with open-world games, where "world design" spans vast continuous environments, and with procedurally generated content.

Process and tools

Design usually proceeds from a paper or digital plan, to a blockout playable with placeholder geometry, to successive passes adding art, lighting, audio, and polish, with playtests at every stage. Designers work in editors such as Unreal Editor, Unity, Hammer (for the Source engine), or game-specific toolkits. Many games ship their editors to players, enabling user-generated levels, an early famous example being the pinball and racing construction sets of the 1980s and later the community scenes around Doom, LittleBigPlanet, and Super Mario Maker.

Key concepts

Level design theory discusses flow and pacing, affordances and "funelling" of players, environmental storytelling, difficulty curves, and the distinction between the game's critical path and optional exploratory space. In multiplayer games, designers must additionally ensure balance between spawn points, sightlines, and competing routes.
