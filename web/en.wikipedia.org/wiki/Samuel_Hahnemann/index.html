Christian Friedrich Samuel Hahnemann (10 April 1755 – 2 July 1843) was a German physician, chemist, and translator best known as the founder of homeopathy, the system of alternative medicine based on the principle that "like cures like" (similia similibus curentur) and on remedies administered in extremely diluted form.

Life and career

Born in Meissen, the son of a porcelain painter, Hahnemann showed a gift for languages — he eventually translated scientific works from English, French, Italian, and Latin — and studied medicine at Leipzig and Vienna, taking his MD at Erlangen in 1779. Practising in Saxony, he grew disillusioned with the heroic medicine of his day, with its bloodletting, purging, and toxic mineral drugs, and largely abandoned practice in the 1780s to support his growing family by translation and chemical research; his work on wine adulteration and mercury chemistry was respected by contemporaries.

Founding of homeopathy

In 1790, while translating William Cullen's materia medica, Hahnemann tested on himself the fever bark cinchona (quinine), and concluded from the symptoms it produced in him that medicines cure diseases whose symptoms they can themselves provoke in the healthy. He formalised this "law of similars" in an 1796 essay and in his magnum opus, the Organon of Rational Therapeutics (1810, later Organon of the Medical Art), which went through five editions in his lifetime and a posthumous sixth. To reduce side effects he developed serial dilution with vigorous shaking ("succussion" or "potentisation"), arguing that dilution increased a remedy's dynamic power even beyond the point — now known as Avogadro's limit — at which any molecule of the original substance remains. His Materia Medica Pura recorded "provings" of dozens of remedies on healthy volunteers.

Later years and legacy

Expelled from Leipzig in 1821 over disputes about dispensing his own remedies, Hahnemann practised under ducal protection at Köthen, and in 1835 moved to Paris, where he enjoyed a fashionable practice until his death in 1843. Homeopathy spread widely in the 19th century, particularly in the United States and India, and remains in use today; modern scientific assessments conclude that its remedies perform no better than placebo and that its principles conflict with established chemistry and pharmacology, though Hahnemann is also credited with early advocacy of humane treatment of the insane, hygiene, and careful clinical observation.
