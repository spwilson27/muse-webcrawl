Flying buttress

The flying buttress (French: arc-boutant) is a specific form of buttress composed of an arched structure — the "flyer" — that extends from the upper part of a wall to a pier standing away from the building, transmitting the lateral thrust of a vault or roof outward and down to the ground. It is a defining element of Gothic architecture, allowing walls to be pierced by large windows because the structural support is moved outside the building.

Flying buttresses around the apse of Amiens Cathedral.

History

Precursors existed in Romanesque construction as hidden quadrant arches under gallery roofs. The exposed flying buttress emerged in the late twelfth century in the Île-de-France: early examples appear in the apse of the Paris church of Saint-Germain-des-Prés and famously in the nave of Notre-Dame de Paris (c. 1180), where flyers countered the thrust of the high vaults. Through the thirteenth century, at Chartres, Reims, Amiens and Bourges, flyers became taller, slimmer and increasingly decorative, often doubled in tiers and crowned by pinnacles whose added weight steepened the line of thrust and improved stability.

Function

A stone vault pushes outward as well as downward. The flyer intercepts this thrust at the clerestory level and conveys it across the aisle roof to the outer pier. By relieving the main wall of structural duty, masons could open it into vast stained-glass windows and raise vault heights dramatically — reaching 48 metres at Beauvais, where ambition exceeded stability and part of the choir collapsed in 1284.

Later use

Flying buttresses persisted in Gothic Revival buildings of the nineteenth century and survive in modern guise wherever external arched supports carry lateral loads. They remain among the most recognisable silhouettes of the great cathedrals.
