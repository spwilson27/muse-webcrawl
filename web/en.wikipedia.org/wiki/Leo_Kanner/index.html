Leo Kanner (June 13, 1894 – April 3, 1981) was an Austrian-American psychiatrist, physician, and social activist best known for his work related to autism spectrum disorder. He is widely regarded as the founder of child psychiatry in the United States and, through his 1943 paper "Autistic Disturbances of Affective Contact", the first physician to systematically describe early infantile autism.

Early life and career

Kanner was born in Klekotów, in Galicia, Austria-Hungary (now in Ukraine), and studied medicine at the University of Berlin, earning his degree in 1921 after his studies were interrupted by service in the Austro-Hungarian army during the First World War. In 1924 he emigrated to the United States, working first at the state hospital in Yankton, South Dakota. In 1930, Adolf Meyer and Edward Park invited him to establish the first academic child psychiatry service in the United States at the Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore, where he remained for the rest of his career. His 1935 textbook Child Psychiatry was the first English-language textbook on the subject.

Description of autism

In 1943 Kanner published a landmark paper describing eleven children (eight boys and three girls) who shared a distinctive pattern: profound social aloofness from early life, an obsessive insistence on sameness, and language abnormalities such as echolalia and pronoun reversal. Kanner concluded that these children had "come into the world with an innate inability to form the usual, biologically provided affective contact with people", coining the term early infantile autism, from the Greek autos ("self").

Controversy and legacy

Kanner's early remarks about the apparent emotional detachment of the parents of autistic children were later taken up by others, most notoriously Bruno Bettelheim, into the damaging "refrigerator mother" theory, which Kanner himself ultimately repudiated; in 1969 he publicly declared that parents had been acquitted of blame. His original syndrome is now understood as part of the broader autism spectrum. The journal he effectively founded at Johns Hopkins and his training of generations of child psychiatrists secured his status as a central figure in the field.
