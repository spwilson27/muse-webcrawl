Internal waters

Internal waters lie on the landward side of the baseline from which the territorial sea is measured.

According to the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), a nation's internal waters include waters on the side of the baseline of a nation's territorial sea facing the land, except in archipelagic states. They include the waters of ports, rivers, lakes, and bays whose entrance is narrow enough to be closed by a baseline across the mouth, as well as waters enclosed by straight baselines drawn along an indented coast.

Legal regime

Internal waters are treated in international law much like land territory: the coastal state exercises full sovereignty over them, including the water column, seabed, subsoil, and the airspace above. Unlike the territorial sea, there is generally no right of innocent passage for foreign vessels in internal waters, and no right of overflight for foreign aircraft. Access to ports in internal waters is entirely at the discretion of the coastal state, subject to treaty obligations and customary exceptions such as vessels in distress.

Exception for straight baselines

Where the drawing of straight baselines encloses as internal waters areas that had not previously been considered as such, Article 8(2) of UNCLOS preserves a right of innocent passage through those waters. This exception applies, for example, where straight baselines are drawn along deeply indented coastlines or across the mouths of certain bays.

Disputes

Claims to treat large bays or historic waters as internal waters have generated disputes between states. The criteria for "historic bays" — long, open, and effective exercise of sovereignty with the acquiescence of other states — have been contested in cases involving, among others, the Gulf of Sidra and Hudson Bay.
