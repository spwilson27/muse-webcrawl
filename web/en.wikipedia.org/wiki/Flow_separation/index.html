Flow separation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In fluid dynamics, flow separation or boundary layer separation is the detachment of a boundary layer from a surface into a wake. It occurs when fluid travels in the direction of increasing pressure (an adverse pressure gradient): the slow-moving fluid near the wall loses momentum, comes to rest, and then reverses, forcing the outer flow to lift away from the surface. Downstream of the separation point a region of recirculating, often turbulent, flow forms.

Consequences

Separation greatly increases pressure (form) drag and destroys lift. On an aircraft wing at high angle of attack, separation spreading from the trailing edge toward the leading edge produces a stall. Bluff bodies such as spheres and cylinders shed vortices from their separation points (the von Kármán vortex street), and the onset of separation is sensitive to whether the boundary layer is laminar or turbulent — the basis of the drag crisis and of dimples on golf balls, which trip the boundary layer to turbulence and delay separation.

Control

Engineers delay or prevent separation by streamlining bodies, using vortex generators, boundary-layer suction or blowing, slats and flaps on wings, and by energising the boundary layer. Ludwig Prandtl introduced the boundary-layer concept and the modern explanation of separation in 1904.
