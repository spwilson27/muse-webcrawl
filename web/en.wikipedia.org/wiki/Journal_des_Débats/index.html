Journal des Débats

The Journal des Débats (French for "Journal of Debates"), full title Journal des Débats politiques et littéraires, was a French newspaper founded in 1789 during the French Revolution. One of the most influential papers of nineteenth-century France, it was published until 1944.

History

The paper began in 1789 as a record of the debates of the National Assembly. In 1799 it was bought by the Bertin brothers, Louis-François Bertin ("Bertin the Elder") and Pierre Louis Bertin, under whose ownership it became the leading newspaper of liberal-conservative, constitutional-monarchist opinion. Napoleon, suspicious of its influence, subjected it to strict control and renamed it the Journal de l'Empire (1805-1814); it resumed its original name at the Bourbon Restoration.

Influence

Under the Restoration and July Monarchy the Journal des Débats was effectively the semi-official voice of the governing elite. It was celebrated for its literary feuilleton: critics and writers such as Jules Janin, Sainte-Beuve (briefly), and the composer-critic Hector Berlioz, who wrote musical criticism for the paper for many years, contributed to it, and authors including Victor Hugo and Alexandre Dumas published in its pages. Serial novels (romans-feuilletons) helped it keep a large bourgeois readership.

Final years

The paper's influence declined in the Third Republic as mass-circulation dailies took over, though it remained a prestigious conservative paper. It continued publication through the Second World War under the Vichy regime and was shut down at the Liberation in 1944 for collaboration, ending a run of more than 150 years.
