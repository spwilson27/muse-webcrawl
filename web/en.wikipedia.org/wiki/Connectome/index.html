Connectome

A connectome is a comprehensive map of neural connections in the brain, and may be thought of as the brain's "wiring diagram". Connectomics is the production and study of connectomes, at scales from a complete synaptic map of a small nervous system to macro-scale descriptions of long-range tracts and functional connectivity in humans.

Scales

  • Microscale — individual neurons and synapses, requiring electron microscopy.
  • Mesoscale — populations and local circuits, traced with viral and optical methods.
  • Macroscale — large fibre tracts and region-to-region connectivity, imaged with diffusion MRI tractography and resting-state fMRI.

Milestones

The only complete synaptic connectome for decades was that of the nematode C. elegans (302 neurons), mapped by White and colleagues in 1986 and later completed for both sexes. In the 2020s, large electron-microscopy projects mapped the full adult Drosophila brain (FlyWire, announced 2024) and cubic-millimetre volumes of mouse and human cortex (MICrONS).

Human Connectome Project

The NIH-funded Human Connectome Project (launched 2009) collected diffusion and functional MRI from over a thousand healthy adults, providing reference maps of human structural and functional connectivity used in studies of cognition and mental illness.

Applications and limits

Connectomics informs network models of brain disorders, brain–computer interfaces, and artificial neural network design. Critics note a wiring map alone does not capture synaptic weights, dynamics, neuromodulation, or development.
