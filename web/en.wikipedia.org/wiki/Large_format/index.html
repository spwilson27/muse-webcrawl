Large format

Large format refers to photography using film (or a digital sensor) of 4 × 5 inches (about 102 × 127 mm) or larger. The classic large format camera is the view camera, a design in which the lens standard and film standard can be moved independently — rising, falling, tilting, swinging, and shifting — giving the photographer precise control over perspective and the plane of focus (the Scheimpflug principle).

Formats

Standard large format sheet-film sizes include 4 × 5 in, 5 × 7 in, and 8 × 10 in, with still larger "ultra large format" sizes such as 11 × 14 in and 20 × 24 in used by some photographers. Between medium format and large format sits the 6 × 17 cm panoramic format on roll film. Large format digital backs exist but are rare and extremely costly, so most large format work remains film-based.

Characteristics

The large image area yields very high resolution, smooth tonal gradation, and extremely shallow depth of field at a given aperture, and the ground-glass focusing image is viewed large and (without a reflex mirror) laterally reversed. The workflow is slow and deliberate: each sheet of film is loaded into a holder, exposed individually, and processed separately, and the camera is almost always used on a tripod. These qualities have made large format the traditional choice for architectural photography, landscapes, studio portraiture, and advertising product work.

Notable practitioners

Large format is strongly associated with the f/64 Group and photographers such as Ansel Adams, Edward Weston, and Imogen Cunningham, and with the large-scale colour work of contemporary artists such as Andreas Gursky and Gregory Crewdson. Wet plate collodion and other historic processes are also practised at large format sizes.
