Land mine

TypeExplosive weapon
Main classesAnti-personnel; anti-tank
ActivationPressure, tripwire, tilt-rod, command, sensors
Legal statusAnti-personnel mines banned by the Ottawa Treaty (1997)

A land mine is an explosive device concealed under or on the ground and designed to destroy or disable enemy targets, ranging from individuals (anti-personnel mines) to vehicles including tanks (anti-tank mines), as they pass over or near it. Mines are typically detonated automatically by the target's pressure or proximity, or deliberately by remote control.

History

Precursors include the fougasse and devices of the American Civil War. Modern mines proliferated in the First World War (against tanks) and massively in the Second World War and the Cold War, when tens of millions were laid worldwide. Cheap, persistent anti-personnel mines became notorious for killing and maiming civilians long after conflicts ended, in countries such as Cambodia, Angola, Afghanistan, and Bosnia.

The Ottawa Treaty

The 1997 Ottawa Treaty (Mine Ban Treaty) prohibits the use, stockpiling, production, and transfer of anti-personnel mines and requires destruction of stockpiles and clearance of mined areas; more than 160 states are parties. The International Campaign to Ban Landmines received the 1997 Nobel Peace Prize. Major military powers including the United States, Russia, and China are not signatories. Demining — by manual prodding and detectors, mine-detection dogs and rats, and armoured flails — remains slow and dangerous work, coordinated by organizations such as the HALO Trust and MAG.
