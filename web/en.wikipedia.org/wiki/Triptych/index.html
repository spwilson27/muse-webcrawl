Triptych

A triptych (from the Greek triptychos, "three-fold") is a work of art — usually a panel painting — that is divided into three sections, or three carved panels that are hinged together and can be folded shut or displayed open. The middle panel is typically the largest and flanked by two smaller related works, called the wings or shutters.

History

The triptych form arose in early Christian art and became a popular format for altarpieces in the Middle Ages, since the hinged wings could be closed to protect the images and opened on feast days. The format flourished in Gothic and Renaissance painting in both Northern Europe and Italy. Notable examples include Hieronymus Bosch's The Garden of Earthly Delights, Rogier van der Weyden's altarpieces, and the Isenheim Altarpiece by Matthias Grünewald, the latter a polyptych structure built around a triptych core.

Beyond painting, triptychs were made in ivory carving, enamel, and precious metals for private devotion; small portable triptychs allowed travellers to carry a devotional image. In Byzantine art, triptychs of ivory and steatite often depicted the Deesis or the Virgin and Child flanked by saints.

Modern usage

The format survived in later art and was revived by modern artists treating three panels as a single statement. Francis Bacon painted many triptychs, regarding the format as a way of presenting images in sequence without narrative. Triptychs are also common in photography, where three related images are printed and hung together, and the term is extended metaphorically to any three-part work — films, novels, or musical compositions conceived as a trilogy of linked parts.

Related forms

A work of two panels is a diptych; one of more than three panels is a polyptych. The term predella refers to the smaller scenes painted along the base of an altarpiece below the main panels.
