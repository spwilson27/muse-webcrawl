Garfield Blackman (8 October 1941 – 12 July 2000), better known as Lord Shorty and later as Ras Shorty I, was a Trinidadian calypsonian and musician credited as the inventor of soca music. He is also credited with inventing "jamoo", a style of music blending Afro-Caribbean and East Indian influences.

Career

Blackman grew up in Lengua Village, South Trinidad, and began performing calypso in the early 1960s, adopting the sobriquet "Lord Shorty" — an ironic reference to his height. He rose to prominence with hits such as "Cloak and Dagger" (1963). Frustrated that calypso was losing popularity to reggae and American soul and funk, he experimented in the early 1970s with fusing calypso with other genres. His 1973 recording "Indrani" added East Indian instrumentation, including the dholak, tabla and dhantal, to calypso rhythm. In 1974 he released the album Endless Vibrations and the song "Soul Calypso" — shortened in his term to "sokah" and then "soca" — the "soul of calypso", which became the dominant popular music of the English-speaking Caribbean.

Later life

In the 1980s Blackman underwent a spiritual transformation, converted to Rastafari, renamed himself Ras Shorty I, and moved with his family into the Piparo forest in Trinidad to live a communal life. There he developed "jamoo" music, a slower, uplifting style, recording with his family group, The Love Circle. His best-known later composition, "Watch Out My Children" (1997), an anti-drug anthem, became a standard in schools across the Caribbean. He died in 2000 of complications related to cancer.

Legacy

Lord Shorty is remembered as one of the most innovative figures in Caribbean music: soca, the genre he created, underpins the music of Trinidad Carnival and has spread throughout the Caribbean diaspora.
