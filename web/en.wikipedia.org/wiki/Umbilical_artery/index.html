The umbilical artery is a paired artery of the pelvis that, in the fetus, forms a major part of the fetal circulation. There are normally two umbilical arteries, coiling around the single umbilical vein within the umbilical cord.

Fetal circulation: the umbilical arteries (red/blue scheme inverted relative to adult anatomy) carry deoxygenated blood to the placenta.

Function in the fetus

Unusually for arteries, the umbilical arteries carry deoxygenated blood — from the fetus back to the placenta, where gas and nutrient exchange takes place. They arise from the internal iliac arteries, pass up the anterior abdominal wall to the umbilicus, and spiral through the cord. Pulsation in the umbilical arteries corresponds to the fetal heartbeat, and Doppler ultrasound waveforms of the umbilical artery are an important clinical tool for assessing placental resistance and fetal well-being in pregnancies complicated by growth restriction.

After birth

At birth, when the cord is clamped, flow ceases and the distal (intra-abdominal) portions of the umbilical arteries obliterate, becoming the fibrous medial umbilical ligaments. The proximal portions remain patent and give rise to the superior vesical arteries supplying the bladder and, in males, the artery of the ductus deferens.

Clinical significance

A single umbilical artery (two-vessel cord) is found in roughly 0.5–1 per cent of pregnancies and is associated with an increased risk of congenital anomalies and growth restriction, prompting further assessment. The umbilical arteries of newborns are also used clinically for umbilical arterial catheterisation in neonatal intensive care.
