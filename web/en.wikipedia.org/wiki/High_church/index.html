High church

High church refers to a tradition within Protestant Christianity, most prominently Anglicanism, that emphasises continuity with the historic Catholic Church, formal liturgy, ceremonial worship, and the sacraments. It stands in contrast to "low church" traditions, which place greater stress on Scripture, preaching, and simplicity of worship.

History

The terms "high" and "low" church arose in the late 17th and early 18th centuries in the Church of England, originally describing attitudes toward the authority of the church and its relationship to the state. In the 19th century, the Oxford Movement (Tractarianism) gave the high church tradition a renewed theological basis, stressing apostolic succession and the sacramental life; its ritualist heirs further restored ceremonial practices such as vestments, incense, and altar candles.

Characteristics

High church worship typically includes a sung or said Eucharist as the principal service, use of liturgical texts such as the Book of Common Prayer, observance of the church calendar and saints' days, and practices such as confession and prayers for the dead in its more Anglo-Catholic expressions. The term is also applied by analogy to "high church" Lutherans, especially in Scandinavia.
