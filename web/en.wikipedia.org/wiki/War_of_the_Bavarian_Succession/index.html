War of the Bavarian Succession

The War of the Bavarian Succession (German: Bayerischer Erbfolgekrieg; 3 July 1778 – 13 May 1779) was a dispute between the Austrian Habsburg monarchy and an alliance of Saxony and Prussia over the succession to the Electorate of Bavaria after the extinction of the Bavarian branch of the House of Wittelsbach. The war consisted largely of manoeuvring and raids on supply lines, with few battles; in Germany it is sometimes called the Kartoffelkrieg ("Potato War"), because much of the troops' activity centred on consuming or denying food supplies, particularly potatoes.

Background

When Maximilian III Joseph, Elector of Bavaria, died childless in December 1777, the Bavarian line of the Wittelsbachs ended and the inheritance passed to his distant cousin Charles Theodore, Elector Palatine. Emperor Joseph II of Austria, seeking to expand Habsburg territory, negotiated a convention (January 1778) by which Charles Theodore ceded Lower Bavaria to Austria in exchange for recognition and other concessions. Frederick the Great of Prussia, determined to prevent Austrian aggrandisement within the Holy Roman Empire, championed the claims of the rival heir, Charles II August, Duke of Zweibrücken, and formed an alliance with Saxony.

The war

Prussian and Saxon armies invaded Bohemia in July 1778, taking up strong defensive positions. The Austrians, commanded by Joseph II with Field Marshals Lacy and Laudon, likewise entrenched. Neither side was willing to risk a major battle; the campaign devolved into skirmishes, raids and a struggle over forage and provisions, while disease and desertion caused most of the losses. Diplomacy, pressed by France and Russia, overtook the military stalemate.

Treaty of Teschen

The Treaty of Teschen (13 May 1779), mediated by France and Russia, ended the war. Austria returned most of the occupied Bavarian territory but retained the Innviertel, a strip of land east of the Inn River. Charles Theodore was confirmed as Elector of Bavaria, the succession rights of Zweibrücken were recognised, and Prussia's claims to Ansbach and Bayreuth were acknowledged. The settlement reaffirmed the balance of power within the Empire and foreshadowed Frederick's formation of the Fürstenbund (League of Princes) in 1785 to resist further Austrian expansion.
