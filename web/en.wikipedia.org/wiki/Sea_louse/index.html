Sea lice (singular sea louse) are marine copepod crustaceans of the family Caligidae — roughly 600 species in genera including Lepeophtheirus and Caligus — that live as ectoparasites on the skin of marine fish. Despite the name, they are not related to true lice (insects). The best-studied species is the salmon louse (Lepeophtheirus salmonis), a parasite of Atlantic and Pacific salmon and a major economic problem for salmon aquaculture.

Life cycle

Sea lice have a direct life cycle of free-swimming nauplius stages, an infective copepodid stage that must find a fish host, and attached chalimus and mobile pre-adult/adult stages that graze on the host's mucus, skin and blood. Heavy infestations erode the skin, cause open lesions, stress and secondary infection, and can kill small fish such as out-migrating salmon smolts.

Aquaculture problem

Farmed salmon held at high density provide ideal conditions for lice, and farms can amplify lice numbers, raising concern that larvae drifting from farms infest wild juvenile salmon on migration routes — a debate especially prominent in British Columbia, Scotland and Norway. Control costs the industry hundreds of millions of dollars or euros annually and include bath and in-feed medicines (with growing resistance problems), cleaner fish such as lumpfish and wrasse, freshwater and thermal treatments, laser systems, skirts and snorkel cages, and fallowing. Norway's "traffic light" system regulates farm growth by measured lice impact on wild salmon.
