Judo (Japanese: 柔道, Hepburn: Jūdō, lit. "gentle way") is an unarmed modern Japanese martial art, combat sport, and Olympic discipline. Created in 1882 by Kanō Jigorō as a safer, more educational synthesis of jujutsu, judo emphasizes throwing an opponent to the ground and subduing them with pins, joint locks, and chokes, using the principle of maximum efficiency with minimum effort (seiryoku zen'yō). Practitioners, called judoka, train in a dojo wearing the white judogi, with colored belts (kyū and dan grades) marking rank.

History

Kanō, an educator, opened his first school, the Kodokan, in 1882 at the Eishō-ji temple in Tokyo with just nine students. He removed jujutsu's most dangerous techniques and built training around randori (free sparring) and kata (forms), framing judo as physical education and moral discipline as much as combat. Judo spread worldwide in the early 20th century; the International Judo Federation (IJF) was founded in 1951, world championships began in 1956, and judo became an Olympic sport at the Tokyo Games of 1964. Women's judo joined the Olympics at Barcelona in 1992. Today it is practiced in nearly 200 countries, and its throws and groundwork heavily influenced Brazilian jiu-jitsu and sambo.

Techniques and competition

Judo techniques divide into throwing techniques (nage-waza — including hand, hip, foot, and sacrifice throws such as seoi-nage, ō-goshi, deashi-harai, and tomoe-nage) and grappling techniques (katame-waza: pins, strangles, and armlocks). Contest judo, fought in weight categories on tatami mats, is won by scoring ippon — a clean throw onto the back with force and control, a 20-second pin, or a submission — or by accumulating the lesser score waza-ari. Penalties (shido) punish passivity and rule violations. Modern IJF rules ban leg grabs in standing throws, shaping a distinctive upright gripping game (kumikata).

Philosophy and grades

Kanō's two guiding maxims were "maximum efficient use of energy" and "mutual welfare and benefit" (jita kyōei). Students progress through kyū grades (colored belts, typically white through brown) to dan grades from 1st dan (black belt) upward to the 10th dan, historically marked by a red belt; the Kodokan has awarded the 10th dan only rarely. Belts and grades vary somewhat by national federation.

Notable judoka

Legendary competitors include Yasuhiro Yamashita, undefeated in 203 consecutive matches; Tadahiro Nomura, the only judoka to win three consecutive Olympic gold medals; Teddy Riner of France, multiple-time world and Olympic heavyweight champion; and Ryoko Tani, a seven-time world champion in the women's extra-lightweight division.
