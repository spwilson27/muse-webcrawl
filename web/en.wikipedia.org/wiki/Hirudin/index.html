Hirudin

Hirudin is a naturally occurring anticoagulant peptide secreted by the salivary glands of blood-sucking leeches, most famously the medicinal leech Hirudo medicinalis. It is the most potent natural inhibitor of thrombin known, preventing the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin and so stopping blood from clotting while the leech feeds — and keeping blood flowing in tissue after it has detached.

Structure and action

Hirudin is a polypeptide of 65 amino-acid residues with three disulfide bridges and a sulfated tyrosine near its C-terminus. It binds thrombin bivalently and with extraordinary tightness: the globular N-terminal domain blocks the enzyme's active site while the acidic tail occupies its fibrinogen-recognition exosite (exosite I). Unlike heparin, hirudin acts directly and does not require antithrombin, and it can inhibit clot-bound thrombin.

Discovery and medical use

The anticoagulant effect of leech saliva was identified in 1884 by the British physiologist John Berry Haycraft, and the substance was named hirudin in 1904. Pure natural hirudin was impractical to produce in quantity, but recombinant DNA technology yielded pharmaceutical versions — lepirudin and desirudin — used particularly in the management of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, a condition in which heparin itself is dangerous. The synthetic 20-amino-acid fragment bivalirudin, modelled on hirudin's binding, is used as an anticoagulant in percutaneous coronary intervention.
