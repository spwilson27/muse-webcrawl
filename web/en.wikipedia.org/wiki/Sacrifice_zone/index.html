Sacrifice zone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sacrifice zone or sacrifice area is a geographic area that has been permanently impaired by environmental damage or economic disinvestment, often through the concentrated siting of polluting industry, mining, or waste disposal. The term emerged during the Cold War for areas contaminated by uranium mining and nuclear weapons testing in the American West, and was later adopted by environmental justice movements for communities — frequently low-income or minority — that bear a disproportionate share of industrial pollution.

Usage and examples

Frequently cited examples include the petrochemical corridor of Louisiana known as "Cancer Alley" between Baton Rouge and New Orleans; mountaintop-removal coal mining districts in Appalachia; the oil-sands region of northern Alberta; and Indigenous lands affected by uranium mining on Navajo territory. Writers such as Chris Hedges and Joe Sacco popularised the term in their 2012 book Days of Destruction, Days of Revolt, describing economically abandoned regions of the United States.

Environmental justice

The concept is central to environmental justice scholarship, which documents how race and income predict proximity to hazardous facilities. Policies responding to sacrifice zones include cumulative-impact screening tools, buffer zones, community benefit agreements and targeted clean-up programmes.
