Montreal Convention

The Montreal Convention (formally the Convention for the Unification of Certain Rules for International Carriage by Air) is a multilateral treaty adopted in 1999 by the member states of the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO). It modernised and replaced the 1929 Warsaw Convention system governing the liability of airlines in international carriage. The Convention establishes rules on airline liability for passenger death or injury, baggage and cargo loss or damage, and delays. It entered into force on 4 November 2003 and has been ratified by well over 130 states, including all European Union member states, the United States, and most major aviation nations.

Key provisions

For passenger death or bodily injury, the Convention imposes a two-tier liability regime: carriers are strictly liable for damages up to a threshold originally set at 100,000 Special Drawing Rights (SDRs) — later revised upward, to 128,821 SDRs in 2009 and 151,880 SDRs in 2019 — and are liable without limit above that amount unless they can prove the damage was not due to their negligence or was caused solely by a third party. Carriers must maintain insurance and may be required to make advance payments to victims' families.

The Convention also sets limits for baggage (per passenger), cargo (per kilogram), and delay, each expressed in SDRs and periodically revised. Passengers have two years to bring claims, and actions may be brought in the passenger's principal place of residence in death or injury cases.

Application

The Convention applies to international carriage between states parties, and in the EU its rules are extended to domestic flights by regulation. It has been central to litigation arising from air disasters and to consumer claims for lost baggage and long delays, alongside regimes such as EU261.
