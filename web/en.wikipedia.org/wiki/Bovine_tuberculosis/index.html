Bovine tuberculosis

Bovine tuberculosis (bTB) is a chronic infectious disease of cattle caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium bovis, a member of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex. It spreads mainly by inhalation of infected droplets and can infect many mammals, including humans, in whom it causes a form of tuberculosis historically contracted from unpasteurised milk.

Bovine tuberculosis
Causative agent: Mycobacterium bovis
Host: Cattle and other mammals
Control: Testing, culling, pasteurisation

Signs and transmission

The disease develops slowly; infected cattle may show weight loss, coughing, and reduced milk yield, but many remain outwardly healthy while shedding bacteria. Tubercles — the characteristic nodular lesions — form in the lungs and lymph nodes. Wildlife reservoirs, such as badgers in Britain and Ireland, brushtail possums in New Zealand, and deer in parts of the United States, complicate eradication.

Control

Control programmes rely on the tuberculin skin test, slaughter of reactors, movement restrictions, and meat inspection; pasteurisation has nearly eliminated human infection in developed countries. Tens of thousands of cattle are culled annually in the United Kingdom, where the disease costs the taxpayer and industry over £100 million a year.

Controversy

The role of badgers in transmitting bTB to cattle, and whether badger culling reduces herd breakdowns, has been fiercely debated in Britain. A large randomised trial found culling produced modest reductions inside culled areas but increased incidence at their edges, and policy has shifted toward cattle measures and vaccination research.
