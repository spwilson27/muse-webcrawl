Ecotype

In evolutionary ecology, an ecotype, sometimes called an ecospecies, is a genetically distinct geographic variety, population, or race within a species that is adapted to specific environmental conditions. Ecotypes typically display phenotypic differences — in morphology, physiology, or phenology — that are genetically based rather than merely the result of developmental plasticity, yet members of different ecotypes of the same species can still interbreed and produce fertile offspring.

History of the concept

The term was introduced by the Swedish botanist Göte Turesson in 1922, based on his transplant experiments with plants collected from different habitats. Turesson grew plants from contrasting environments — such as coastal and inland sites — side by side in a common garden and found that many habitat-correlated differences persisted, demonstrating a hereditary basis. His work distinguished the ecotype (a genetically fixed local form) from the ecophene, a non-hereditary modification produced directly by the environment.

Formation

Ecotypes arise when populations of a widespread species experience divergent natural selection in different habitats — for example, heavy-metal-contaminated mine soils, serpentine soils, alpine versus lowland climates, or coastal salt spray. Classic examples include:

  • Metal-tolerant races of grasses such as Agrostis capillaris growing on old mine spoil heaps in Wales, studied by Antonovics and Bradshaw.
  • Ecotypes of Potentilla glandulosa along an elevation gradient in the Sierra Nevada, investigated in the famous transplant experiments of Clausen, Keck and Hiesey.
  • Marine and freshwater ecotypes of the three-spined stickleback fish.

Ecotypes and speciation

Because ecotypes remain interfertile, they are not separate species; however, strong divergent selection combined with reduced gene flow can lead to reproductive isolation, making ecotype formation a possible first stage of ecological speciation. The distinction between an ecotype, a subspecies and a variety is not always sharp and has been debated among taxonomists.

Terminology

In microbiology, related concepts include the biovar and pathovar. In zoology the term "ecotype" is used for habitat-associated forms of animals, such as the ecotypes of killer whales (resident, transient and offshore forms) that differ in diet, social structure and vocal behaviour.
