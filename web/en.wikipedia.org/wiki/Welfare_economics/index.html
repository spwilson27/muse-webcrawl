Welfare economics is a branch of economics that uses microeconomic techniques to evaluate well-being (welfare) at the aggregate level. It studies how the allocation of resources and goods affects social welfare, and provides a framework for assessing markets, policies and distributions.

Fundamental theorems

The first fundamental theorem states that competitive market equilibria are Pareto efficient under certain conditions: no one can be made better off without making someone worse off. The second theorem states that any Pareto-efficient allocation can be achieved as a market equilibrium given suitable lump-sum transfers — separating efficiency from equity.

Approaches

The older Pigouvian approach used cardinal utility and money as a measure of welfare, justifying intervention against externalities with "Pigouvian taxes". The newer Pareto-based approach, developed by Hicks, Kaldor and Samuelson, avoids interpersonal utility comparisons; the Kaldor–Hicks criterion deems a change an improvement if winners could compensate losers. Social welfare functions, notably in Amartya Sen's work, explicitly aggregate individual well-being.

Market failure

Welfare economics identifies circumstances where markets fail to be efficient: externalities, public goods, monopoly power, asymmetric information and incomplete markets. Analysis of deadweight loss, consumer surplus and compensating variation underpins cost–benefit analysis used by governments.

Criticisms and extensions

Arrow's impossibility theorem shows no voting rule can aggregate preferences into a consistent social ordering satisfying a set of reasonable fairness conditions. Sen's capability approach argues welfare should be judged by people's real freedoms, not only income or utility.
