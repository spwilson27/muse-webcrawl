Words of Institution

The Words of Institution (also called the Words of Consecration) are the words spoken by Jesus Christ at the Last Supper as recorded in the New Testament: over the bread, "This is my body", and over the cup, "This is my blood". In the liturgies of most Christian churches they are recited by the celebrant during the celebration of the Eucharist, recalling and (in many traditions) effecting the institution of the sacrament.

Biblical accounts

The institution narrative appears in the three Synoptic Gospels (Matthew 26:26–28; Mark 14:22–24; Luke 22:19–20) and in Paul's First Letter to the Corinthians (11:23–25), the earliest written account:

"For I received from the Lord what I also delivered to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it, and said, 'This is my body which is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.' In the same way also he took the cup, after supper, saying, 'This cup is the new covenant in my blood. Do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of me.'"

The four accounts differ in details — for example, Matthew and Mark have Jesus say "this is my blood", while Luke and Paul phrase the cup saying as "the new covenant in my blood" — and liturgies have historically harmonised them in various ways.

Liturgical use

In the Roman Rite of the Catholic Church, the Words of Institution are embedded in the institution narrative within the Eucharistic Prayer, and Catholic theology teaches that consecration is effected by the words of Christ spoken by the priest acting in persona Christi. Lutheran liturgies likewise give the Verba a central, consecratory role, sung or spoken over the elements. Eastern Orthodox liturgies place the words within the anaphora but traditionally emphasise the epiclesis — the invocation of the Holy Spirit — in the consecration. Churches in the memorialist tradition treat the words chiefly as a recitation of the gospel narrative and a proclamation of Christ's death.

Theological significance

The interpretation of "This is my body" was a central point of controversy in the Reformation. The dispute between Luther and Zwingli at the Marburg Colloquy of 1529 turned on whether est ("is") should be read literally or figuratively. Debates over whether the words consecrate, and over the exact "moment of consecration", continued between Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, and Reformed theologians into the modern ecumenical dialogues, some of which have reached substantial agreement that consecration occurs through the whole Eucharistic prayer.
