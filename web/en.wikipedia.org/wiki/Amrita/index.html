Amrita

Amrita (Sanskrit: अमृत, IAST: amṛta), literally "immortality" or "deathless", is the nectar of immortality in the dharmic religions, conceptually akin to the Greek ambrosia. The word is formed from a- ("not") and mṛta ("death"). In the Rigveda, amrita is frequently associated with soma, the sacred ritual drink, and with the gods' freedom from death.

The churning of the ocean

The most famous amrita myth is the Samudra Manthan, recounted in the Mahabharata, Ramayana and Puranas. The devas (gods) and asuras (demons), having lost their immortality, jointly churned the Ocean of Milk using Mount Mandara as the churning rod and the serpent Vasuki as the rope, with Vishnu in his tortoise (Kurma) form supporting the mountain. Among the treasures that emerged was the physician god Dhanvantari bearing the pot (kumbha) of amrita. The asuras seized the pot; Vishnu, taking the enchantress form Mohini, distributed the nectar to the devas alone. The demon Rahu managed to drink a sip before being beheaded — his immortal head is said to periodically swallow the sun and moon, causing eclipses. Drops of amrita that fell to earth sanctified the four sites of the Kumbh Mela festival.

In other traditions

In Buddhism, amrita (amata in Pali) is a metaphor for the deathless — nirvana itself — and in Vajrayana ritual it denotes consecrated nectar used in initiations and ganachakra feasts. In Jainism, abhisheka water and the nectar flowing from the Tirthankaras are associated with amrita. In Sikhism, amrit is the sweetened water stirred with a double-edged sword in the Amrit Sanskar initiation of the Khalsa, and "Amritsar" — the city of the Golden Temple — means "pool of the nectar of immortality". Sweetened water or panchamrita (a mixture of milk, curd, honey, sugar and ghee) is also distributed as amrita in Hindu worship.
