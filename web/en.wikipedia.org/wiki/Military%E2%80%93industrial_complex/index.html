Military–industrial complex

The military–industrial complex is a concept describing the relationship between a country's military and the defense industry that supplies it, seen together as a vested interest which influences public policy. The term is most often applied to the United States.

Origin of the term

The phrase was popularized by U.S. President Dwight D. Eisenhower in his farewell address on 17 January 1961, in which he warned that "the potential for the disastrous rise of misplaced power exists and will persist" and urged citizens to guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence by the military–industrial complex. Eisenhower noted that the permanent armaments industry was a new feature of American life, created by the demands of the Second World War and the Cold War.

The concept

Analysts describe a network of defense contractors, the armed services, Congress, and executive agencies mutually reinforcing one another: contractors depend on military budgets, services seek advanced weapons, and legislators protect defense jobs in their districts. A related "revolving door" sees retired officers and officials take positions with defense firms.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue the complex encourages excessive military spending and foreign intervention; defenders respond that a strong industrial base is essential for national security and that procurement is shaped by genuine strategic needs. The term has since been extended to analogous concepts such as the prison–industrial complex and the medical–industrial complex.
