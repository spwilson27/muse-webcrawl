Lumpectomy

Lumpectomy (also called wide local excision, breast-conserving surgery, or in the UK a wide excision) is a surgical procedure to remove a localised breast tumour together with a surrounding rim ("margin") of normal tissue, in contrast to mastectomy, which removes the entire breast. It is the central component of breast-conserving therapy for early-stage breast cancer, usually combined with radiation therapy.

Procedure and evidence

The surgeon excises the lump — localised beforehand by imaging, a wire or a seed if impalpable — aiming for microscopically clear margins ("no ink on tumour" for invasive cancer). Randomised trials, notably those of Bernard Fisher (NSABP B-06) and Umberto Veronesi, showed that for suitable early cancers lumpectomy followed by radiotherapy gives survival equivalent to mastectomy, though with a somewhat higher risk of local recurrence. Lumpectomy may also be used for benign lumps and ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS).

Aftercare and considerations

Radiotherapy to the remaining breast tissue typically follows and reduces recurrence substantially. Positive margins may require re-excision or mastectomy. Cosmetic outcome, tumour-to-breast size ratio, multicentric disease and the ability to receive radiotherapy all influence whether lumpectomy is appropriate. Oncoplastic techniques combine lumpectomy with plastic-surgery reshaping to preserve appearance.
