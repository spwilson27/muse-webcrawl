Fiscal theory of the price level

The fiscal theory of the price level is the idea that government fiscal policy, in particular the level of government debts and the government's budget constraint, affects the inflation rate and thus apparently monetary policy decisions as well. Under the theory, a government's liabilities — money and bonds — are valued like the equity of a firm: their real value equals the present value of the future primary surpluses (tax revenues minus spending, excluding interest) that back them. If bondholders come to doubt that future surpluses will back the outstanding nominal debt, the price level rises until the real value of the debt falls to match the expected surpluses.


In the fiscal theory, nominal government debt is valued against expected future primary surpluses.

The valuation equation

The core of the theory is an equilibrium condition on the real value of nominal government liabilities:

Bt−1 / Pt = Et Σs≥0 βs st+s

where B is nominal debt, P is the price level, and s denotes real primary surpluses. In standard ("Ricardian" or monetary-dominant) regimes the budget constraint adjusts surpluses to validate any price level the central bank chooses; in "non-Ricardian" or fiscally dominant regimes, surpluses are set independently and the price level must adjust to satisfy the equation — giving the fiscal authority the leading role in determining inflation.

History and proponents

Ideas trace back to work by Eric Leeper (1991), who distinguished "active" and "passive" fiscal and monetary policies, and to papers by Christopher Sims and Thomas Sargent and Neil Wallace's earlier "unpleasant monetarist arithmetic" (1981), which showed that tight money today could force inflation later if deficits continued. John H. Cochrane became the theory's most prominent modern advocate, notably in The Fiscal Theory of the Price Level (2023), applying it to episodes such as the post-2020 inflation. Other contributors include Michael Woodford and Harald Uhlig.

Criticism

Critics, including Willem Buiter, argue that the theory mistakes a budget constraint (which must hold at all prices) for an equilibrium valuation equation, and that its predictions depend on implausible commitments by fiscal authorities not to adjust taxes and spending. Defenders reply that the condition is no different in status from asset-pricing equations elsewhere in macroeconomics. The theory remains a minority but influential position in debates about the interaction of fiscal deficits and central banking.
