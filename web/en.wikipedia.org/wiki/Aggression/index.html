Aggression is behaviour aimed at causing harm or pain to another individual who is motivated to avoid that harm. Psychologists distinguish reactive (hostile) aggression — an angry response to provocation or frustration — from proactive (instrumental) aggression, which is calculated behaviour used as a means to an end, such as bullying for status or robbery. Aggression can be physical, verbal, or relational (damaging social relationships), and occurs across human societies and throughout the animal kingdom.

Causes

Research identifies interacting biological, psychological and social causes. Genetic influences and neurochemistry (notably low serotonergic functioning), hormones such as testosterone, brain structures including the amygdala and prefrontal cortex, frustration, pain, heat, alcohol, exposure to violence and social learning — famously demonstrated in Bandura's Bobo doll experiments — all contribute. The frustration–aggression hypothesis (Dollard et al., 1939) proposed that frustration always produces aggression, later revised to say frustration produces negative affect that may lead to aggression among other responses.

Evolutionary and social perspectives

Ethologists view aggression as an adaptive system for competing over resources, mates and status, usually regulated by threat displays and submission signals that limit actual fighting. In humans, gender differences in physical aggression appear across cultures, while rates of violence vary hugely between societies, suggesting strong cultural modulation. Aggression differs from assertiveness and from violence, which is aggression in its extreme physical form.

Control and treatment

Interventions range from anger-management and cognitive-behavioural therapy to school-based programmes, and, in persistent pathological cases, medication. Catharsis — "venting" anger — is generally found to increase rather than reduce subsequent aggression.
