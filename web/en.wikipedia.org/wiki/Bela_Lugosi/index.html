Bela Ferenc Dezső Blaskó (Hungarian: [ˈbeːlɒ ˈfɛrɛnt͡s ˈdɛʒøː ˈblɒʃkoː]; 20 October 1882 – 16 August 1956), known professionally as Bela Lugosi (/ləˈɡoʊsi/), was a Hungarian-American actor best remembered for portraying Count Dracula in the 1931 horror classic Dracula, Ygor in Son of Frankenstein (1939) and his roles in many other horror films from 1931 through 1956.

Early life and career

Lugosi was born in Lugos in the Kingdom of Hungary (now Lugoj, Romania), the youngest of four children of a banker. He began acting on the Hungarian stage in 1902, playing dozens of roles with provincial companies and later in Budapest and in Hungarian silent films under the name Arisztid Olt. After serving in the Austro-Hungarian army in the First World War and participating in the failed Hungarian Communist revolution of 1919, he fled to Germany and then emigrated to the United States in 1920, working as a labourer before joining Hungarian immigrant theatre companies in New York.

Dracula and stardom

In 1927 Lugosi was cast as Count Dracula in the Broadway adaptation of Bram Stoker's novel, a critical success that ran for 261 performances and toured for two years. Universal Pictures cast him in Tod Browning's film version, Dracula (1931), whose success — and Lugosi's aristocratic accent, hypnotic stare and cape-swirling menace — defined the screen image of the vampire and made him, alongside Boris Karloff, one of the great stars of the first horror cycle.

His notable films of the 1930s include Murders in the Rue Morgue (1932), The Black Cat (1934), The Raven (1935), The Invisible Ray (1936) and Son of Frankenstein (1939), in which his performance as the hunchbacked Ygor was widely praised. Refusing the role of Frankenstein's monster in 1931 (it went to Karloff) and being typecast as villains and mad scientists limited his later opportunities.

Decline and final years

By the 1940s Lugosi was reduced to low-budget productions for Monogram and other Poverty Row studios, and to reprising Dracula in touring stage productions and, once on film, in Abbott and Costello Meet Frankenstein (1948). Chronic sciatic pain led to dependence on morphine and Demerol; in 1955 he made public his addiction and underwent treatment, an unusually frank step for the era. In his final years he worked with director Edward D. Wood Jr. on Glen or Glenda (1953) and Bride of the Monster (1955); footage of Lugosi shot shortly before his death was used in Wood's Plan 9 from Outer Space (1959). He died of a heart attack in Los Angeles in 1956 and was buried in his Dracula cape at Holy Cross Cemetery, Culver City.

Legacy

Lugosi's Dracula remains one of cinema's most imitated performances; the Bauhaus song "Bela Lugosi's Dead" (1979) made him an icon of goth culture. Martin Landau won an Academy Award for portraying Lugosi in the biographical film Ed Wood (1994). In 1979 the US Supreme Court's Lugosi litigation over posthumous publicity rights led California to enact the Celebrities Rights Act.
