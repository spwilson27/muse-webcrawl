Pamela; or, Virtue Rewarded

Pamela; or, Virtue Rewarded is an epistolary novel by the English writer Samuel Richardson, first published in 1740. It tells the story of Pamela Andrews, a fifteen-year-old maidservant whose master, the wealthy Mr B, repeatedly attempts to seduce and abduct her; resisting him, she is eventually rewarded when he reforms and marries her. The novel was an immediate bestseller and is often credited with helping to establish the modern novel in English.

Origins and form

Richardson, a printer, was commissioned to write a volume of model letters for the semi-literate; one scenario — a servant girl writing home while resisting her master's advances — grew into the novel itself. The story is told entirely through Pamela's letters and journal, a technique that gives intense access to her consciousness and moral reasoning.

Reception and the Pamela controversy

Pamela sparked a cultural sensation and a fierce debate: admirers praised its moral teaching, while critics charged that Pamela was a calculating social climber trading virtue for status. Henry Fielding parodied it in Shamela (1741) and answered it with Joseph Andrews (1742), in which Pamela's brother resists seduction in turn. Richardson published a less successful sequel, Pamela in Her Exalted Condition, and returned to the epistolary form in Clarissa (1748).

Influence

The novel influenced the development of the novel of manners and sentimental fiction across Europe, and "Pamela media" — prints, fans, and merchandise — made it an early example of a literary franchise.
