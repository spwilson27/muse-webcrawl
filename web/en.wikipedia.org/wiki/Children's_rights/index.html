Children's rights are the human rights of children, recognised with particular attention to their rights to special care, protection, and development because of their vulnerability and dependence. They include civil and political rights (such as identity, expression, and freedom from torture), economic and social rights (such as education, health, and an adequate standard of living), and protection rights (against abuse, neglect, exploitation, and recruitment into armed conflict), together with the right of children to participate in decisions affecting them.

The Convention on the Rights of the Child

The central instrument is the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC), adopted in 1989 and ratified by every UN member state except the United States. The Convention defines a child as every human being below the age of eighteen (unless majority is attained earlier under applicable law) and is organised around four general principles: non-discrimination (Art. 2); the best interests of the child as a primary consideration (Art. 3); the right to life, survival and development (Art. 6); and respect for the views of the child in matters affecting them (Art. 12). Three Optional Protocols address children in armed conflict, the sale of children and child prostitution and pornography, and a communications (complaints) procedure.

History

Earlier milestones include the child labour and compulsory education laws of the nineteenth century, the Geneva Declaration of the Rights of the Child (1924), drafted by Eglantyne Jebb and adopted by the League of Nations, the UN Declaration of the Rights of the Child (1959), and the pioneering educational philosophy of Janusz Korczak. The children's rights movement has debated the balance between protection of children and their autonomy — sometimes framed as "child liberation" versus paternalism — and the related question of parents' rights, which the Convention addresses by recognising parents' responsibilities and the child's evolving capacities.

Implementation

Implementation is monitored by the Committee on the Rights of the Child, to which states report periodically; many states also maintain children's ombudsmen or commissioners. Persistent concerns in the Committee's reviews include child poverty, violence against children, juvenile justice standards, and child labour, estimated by the ILO and UNICEF to affect around 160 million children worldwide.
