American Federation of Labor

The American Federation of Labor (AFL or A. F. of L.) was a national federation of trade unions in the United States founded in Columbus, Ohio, on December 8, 1886, by an alliance of craft unions disaffected from the Knights of Labor. Under its long-serving president Samuel Gompers, the AFL became the largest union grouping in the United States for most of the first half of the 20th century. It merged with the Congress of Industrial Organizations in 1955 to form the AFL–CIO.

Philosophy

Gompers articulated a "pure and simple" unionism focused on the economic interests of skilled workers — higher wages, shorter hours, and better conditions ("more") — rather than on political transformation or organising the unskilled. Craft unions kept autonomy within the federation, and the AFL generally avoided permanent partisan alignment, rewarding political "friends" and punishing "enemies" at election time.

Growth and rivalry

By 1914 the AFL had about two million members. It faced competition from the Industrial Workers of the World and, in the 1930s, from within: industrial unions organising whole industries (auto, steel) demanded charters, and after suspension by the AFL executive they formed the rival Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) in 1938. The two federations competed until post-war pressures, anti-communist purges, and shared political interests drove reunion.

Merger

Under presidents George Meany (AFL) and Walter Reuther (CIO), the federations merged on 5 December 1955, creating the AFL–CIO with about 15 million members — the largest labour federation in American history.
