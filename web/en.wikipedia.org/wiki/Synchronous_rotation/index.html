Synchronous rotation

In astronomy, synchronous rotation (also called tidal locking in its result) is the state in which an astronomical body's orbital period matches its rotational period, so that the same hemisphere always faces the body it orbits. The best-known example is Earth's Moon, whose near side perpetually faces Earth.

Mechanism

Synchronous rotation arises from tidal forces: gravitational interaction raises tidal bulges on both bodies, and the resulting torque transfers angular momentum, gradually slowing (or speeding) the satellite's spin until its rotation period equals its orbital period. Most major moons of the Solar System are tidally locked to their planets; Pluto and Charon are mutually locked, each keeping the same face toward the other.

Consequences

A synchronously rotating body has a permanent day side and night side relative to its primary. For close-in exoplanets around red dwarf stars, tidal locking has major implications for climate and potential habitability, though atmospheric circulation can redistribute heat.
