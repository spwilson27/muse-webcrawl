The Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, adopted on 9 July 1868, is one of the Reconstruction Amendments enacted after the American Civil War. It addresses citizenship rights and equal protection under the law, and has been the foundation of many landmark Supreme Court decisions.

Section One

The amendment's first section declares that all persons born or naturalised in the United States are citizens — overturning the Supreme Court's ruling in Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857) that African Americans could not be citizens. It then forbids any state to abridge the "privileges or immunities" of citizens, to deprive "any person of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law", or to deny "the equal protection of the laws".

Other sections

  • Section 2 revises congressional apportionment, penalising states that deny the vote to adult male citizens (superseding the three-fifths compromise).
  • Section 3 bars former officials who engaged in insurrection from holding office, unless Congress removes the disqualification.
  • Section 4 validates the Union's public debt and repudiates Confederate debts and claims for the loss of enslaved people.
  • Section 5 gives Congress power to enforce the amendment by legislation.

Interpretation and legacy

After a narrow reading in the Slaughter-House Cases (1873), the amendment became central to twentieth-century jurisprudence. The Supreme Court used its Due Process Clause to apply ("incorporate") most of the Bill of Rights against the states, and its Equal Protection Clause underpinned decisions such as Brown v. Board of Education (1954), which struck down school segregation. Scholars including Akhil Amar have described the Fourteenth as completing a "second founding" of the Constitution. The citizenship and rights questions it framed were also central to the work of later civil-rights thinkers such as W. E. B. Du Bois.
