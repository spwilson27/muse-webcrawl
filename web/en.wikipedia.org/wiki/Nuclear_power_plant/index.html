Nuclear power plant

A nuclear power plant is a thermal power station in which the heat source is a nuclear reactor. As is typical of thermal power stations, heat is used to generate steam that drives a steam turbine connected to a generator that produces electricity. As of recent years, the world has over 400 operable nuclear power reactors in about 30 countries, supplying roughly a tenth of global electricity.

How it works

Inside the reactor core, controlled fission of uranium-235 or plutonium-239 releases energy that heats a coolant — usually water, but also gas or liquid metal depending on the design. In a pressurised water reactor (the most common type), the primary coolant loop transfers heat to a secondary loop in steam generators; the steam spins a turbine-generator. Spent heat is rejected through cooling towers, rivers, lakes or the sea. Control rods of neutron-absorbing material regulate the chain reaction.

Main components

  • Reactor core — fuel assemblies, moderator and control rods inside a pressure vessel.
  • Steam generator / boiler — transfers heat to the working fluid.
  • Turbine hall — steam turbines, generator and condenser.
  • Containment building — a robust structure designed to contain releases in an accident.
  • Spent fuel pool — water-filled storage for used fuel bundles.

History

The first plant to generate electricity was the experimental EBR-I in Idaho in 1951; the first grid-connected station was Obninsk in the Soviet Union (1954), and the first commercial-scale station was Calder Hall in the United Kingdom (1956). The industry expanded rapidly in the 1970s, slowed after the Three Mile Island and Chernobyl accidents, and has seen renewed interest in the 21st century as a low-carbon energy source, alongside continuing debate over cost, waste disposal and safety.

Safety and waste

Safety philosophy rests on defence in depth: multiple physical barriers, redundant and diverse cooling systems, and trained operators. High-level waste, principally spent fuel, remains radioactive for thousands of years; deep geological repositories such as Finland's Onkalo facility are the reference disposal solution.
