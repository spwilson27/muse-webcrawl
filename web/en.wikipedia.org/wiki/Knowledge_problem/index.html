Knowledge problem

The knowledge problem is the argument that the data needed for rational economic planning are distributed among individual actors and cannot be fully collected by any central authority. It was formulated most famously by the Austrian economist Friedrich Hayek in his 1945 essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society", and is closely related to the economic calculation problem raised by Ludwig von Mises.

The argument

Hayek observed that much of the knowledge relevant to economic decisions is not statistical or scientific knowledge that can be written down and transmitted, but "knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place" — local, often tacit know-how held by "the man on the spot". Because this knowledge is dispersed, fleeting and partly inarticulable, no planning board could assemble it in full. The price system, by contrast, acts as a mechanism for communicating information: a rise in the price of tin, for example, tells users to economise on tin without any of them needing to know why tin has become scarcer.

Context and debate

The argument was a central salvo in the socialist calculation debate. Defenders of market socialism, such as Oskar Lange, proposed that planners could mimic markets by trial-and-error price adjustment; Hayek replied that this underestimated the sheer volume and tacitness of the knowledge involved. Later writers have revisited the problem in light of computers and big data, asking whether modern information technology and machine learning weaken Hayek's claim; critics of that view answer that the core problem — incentives, tacit knowledge, and the generation of knowledge through rivalry — is not one of raw computing power.
