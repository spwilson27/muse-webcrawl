Narmer Palette

Narmer Palette
MaterialSiltstone (greywacke)
Size63 cm × 42 cm (25 in × 17 in)
Createdc. 3200–3000 BC
Discovered1897–98, Hierakonpolis (Nekhen)
Present locationEgyptian Museum, Cairo

The Narmer Palette, also known as the Great Hierakonpolis Palette, is a ceremonial Egyptian cosmetic palette carved from a single piece of siltstone, dated to about the 31st century BC. It bears some of the earliest known hieroglyphic inscriptions and depicts King Narmer wearing the crowns of both Upper and Lower Egypt — for which it is conventionally regarded as evidence of the unification of Egypt, though scholars debate whether it records a historical event or a ritual/ideological statement.

Discovery

The palette was discovered in 1897–98 by British archaeologists James Quibell and Frederick Green in the "Main Deposit" at the Temple of Horus at Nekhen (Hierakonpolis), the ancient royal centre of Upper Egypt. It is now displayed in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo.

Description

On one side, Narmer — identified by his name written in a serekh at the top between two human-faced bovine heads (the goddess Bat) — wears the White Crown of Upper Egypt and raises a mace to smite a kneeling captive, before a falcon (Horus) holding a rope through the nose of a human head emerging from papyrus, symbolising dominion over the north. Below lie two naked, dead enemies.

On the other side, Narmer wears the Red Crown of Lower Egypt and processes with standard-bearers to inspect rows of decapitated prisoners. At the centre, two serpopards (long-necked lionesses) with intertwined necks — controlled by attendants — surround the circular depression for grinding cosmetics. At the bottom, a bull (representing the king) tramples an enemy and attacks a fortified town.

Interpretation

The palette established conventions of Egyptian art — hierarchical scale, composite view of the human figure, and registers — that endured for three millennia. It remains one of the most famous artefacts of ancient Egypt and a key document for the origins of the pharaonic state.
