Digital cinema is the use of digital technology to capture, distribute and exhibit motion pictures, in place of the historical use of reels of 35 mm film. A movie can be distributed to cinemas via hard drives, optical discs or satellite, and projected using a digital video projector instead of a film projector. Digital cinema is distinct from consumer high-definition television: theatrical systems are governed by standards set by Digital Cinema Initiatives (DCI), a joint venture of the major studios formed in 2002.

Technology

Under the DCI specification, films are distributed as a Digital Cinema Package (DCP): picture compressed frame-by-frame with JPEG 2000 at up to 250 Mbit/s, at 2K or 4K resolution in the X'Y'Z' colour space, with up to 16 channels of uncompressed 24-bit audio. DCPs are encrypted, and each projector/server combination requires a Key Delivery Message (KDM) to unlock playback for a set window of time. Projectors use either Texas Instruments' DLP (Digital Micromirror Device) technology or Sony's SXRD reflective LCD system, with xenon lamps or, increasingly, laser light sources. The system also supports 3D (with add-on polarization or colour-separation systems), high frame rates and immersive audio formats.

History

Experimental digital screenings took place in the late 1990s; Star Wars: Episode I – The Phantom Menace (1999) was shown digitally in a handful of theatres, and Toy Story 2 (1999) was among the first features mastered entirely digitally for such projection. George Lucas's Star Wars: Episode II – Attack of the Clones (2002) was shot largely on digital cameras. Conversion of the world's cinemas accelerated after 2005, financed in large part by "virtual print fee" arrangements under which distributors subsidized exhibitors' equipment purchases from the savings on film prints; Avatar (2009) drove a major wave of 3D-capable installations. By the early 2010s the great majority of screens worldwide were digital, and major distributors ceased releasing 35 mm prints. Digital capture likewise displaced film origination for most productions, though a minority of filmmakers continue to shoot on film.

Advantages and criticism

Digital distribution eliminated the cost and degradation of physical prints — a 35 mm print cost well over a thousand dollars and wore with each showing — and allowed simultaneous worldwide releases and alternative content such as live opera broadcasts. Critics have raised concerns about long-term archiving (digital formats and media become obsolete faster than film stock), the loss of the aesthetic qualities of film projection, and the capital and upgrade costs imposed on small exhibitors.
