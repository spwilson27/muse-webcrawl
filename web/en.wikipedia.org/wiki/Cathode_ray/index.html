Cathode ray

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Crookes tube: the cathode ray casts a shadow of the Maltese cross

Cathode rays are streams of electrons observed in discharge tubes (vacuum tubes). If an evacuated glass tube is equipped with two electrodes and a voltage is applied, glass behind the positive electrode glows from the impact of electrons emitted from the cathode. They were first observed in 1859 by German physicists Julius Plücker and Johann Wilhelm Hittorf, and were named in 1876 by Eugen Goldstein as Kathodenstrahlen, or cathode rays. In 1897, the British physicist J. J. Thomson showed that cathode rays were composed of previously unknown negatively charged particles, which were later named electrons; he received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1906.

Properties

Cathode rays travel in straight lines in the absence of external fields, casting sharp shadows; they can be deflected by electric and magnetic fields in the manner expected for negatively charged particles; they carry momentum and can heat objects, drive small paddle wheels, and cause fluorescence in phosphors; and when stopped abruptly by matter they produce X-rays. Heinrich Hertz's early experiments seemed to show no electric deflection — an artefact of poor vacuum — which briefly misled Continental physicists into treating the rays as a wave phenomenon, while British researchers such as William Crookes championed the particle view vindicated by Thomson.

Applications

The cathode-ray tube (CRT), developed from the Crookes tube and Karl Ferdinand Braun's 1897 diode tube, became the standard display device of the 20th century: in oscilloscopes, radar screens, television sets and computer monitors. Finely focused electron beams are also the basis of the electron microscope (1931 onwards), electron-beam welding and lithography. CRTs have since been displaced in consumer displays by LCD and OLED technology.
