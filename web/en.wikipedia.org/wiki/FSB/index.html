The Federal Security Service of the Russian Federation (FSB; Russian: Федеральная служба безопасности Российской Федерации, ФСБ) is the principal security agency of Russia and the main successor agency to the Soviet KGB. Its headquarters are in the Lubyanka Building in central Moscow. The FSB's responsibilities include counter-intelligence, internal and border security, counter-terrorism, surveillance, and the investigation of serious crimes.

History

The FSB descends from the lineage of the Cheka, NKVD, and KGB. After the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, the KGB was broken up; its Russian domestic functions passed through a succession of bodies — the Ministry of Security, then the Federal Counterintelligence Service (FSK) — before President Boris Yeltsin reorganised the FSK as the Federal Security Service in April 1995, restoring some powers of criminal investigation. Vladimir Putin, a former KGB officer, directed the FSB in 1998–1999 before becoming prime minister and then president; under his presidency the service's budget, staff, and remit grew substantially, and in 2003 it absorbed the border guards service and parts of the government communications agency FAPSI.

Functions and structure

The FSB combines the roles that in many states are divided among a domestic intelligence service, a federal investigative bureau, and a border force. Its departments cover counterintelligence, counter-terrorism and protection of the constitutional order, economic security, military counterintelligence, and information security, and it operates the Border Service of Russia guarding the world's longest land frontier. Special forces under its command include the Alpha and Vympel units of its Special Purpose Centre, used in hostage rescues and counter-terrorist operations.

Estimates of its staff size vary widely, commonly from 200,000 to 350,000 including border troops. The director of the FSB reports directly to the President of Russia.

Controversies

The FSB has been accused by journalists and opposition figures of involvement in political repression, pervasive surveillance of Russian society, and assassinations at home and abroad — allegations the service and the Kremlin generally deny. It played central roles in the Chechen wars, in the investigations of the 1999 apartment bombings (which some critics have controversially attributed to the service itself), and, more recently, in the suppression of dissent and in intelligence operations connected with the Russo-Ukrainian war. Western governments have sanctioned the FSB and its leadership for, among other things, the poisoning of Alexei Navalny, which European laboratories attributed to a Novichok nerve agent.
