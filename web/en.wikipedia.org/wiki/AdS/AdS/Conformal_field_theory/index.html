Conformal field theory in AdS/CFT

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A conformal field theory (CFT) is a quantum field theory invariant under conformal transformations — transformations that preserve angles but not necessarily distances, including translations, rotations, dilations (scale changes) and special conformal transformations. CFTs describe the critical points of phase transitions (such as the Ising model at its Curie temperature), where a system becomes scale-invariant, and they are the boundary side of the AdS/CFT correspondence, in which a CFT in d dimensions is exactly equivalent to a theory of gravity in (d+1)-dimensional anti-de Sitter (AdS) space.

Role in the correspondence

The canonical example, proposed by Juan Maldacena in 1997, equates type IIB string theory on AdS5 × S5 with 𝒩 = 4 supersymmetric Yang–Mills theory, a four-dimensional CFT with gauge group SU(N), on the boundary of AdS5. Every bulk quantity has a boundary translation: the mass of a field in AdS determines the scaling dimension of the dual operator; black holes in the bulk correspond to thermal states of the CFT; and strongly coupled regimes of the CFT — where ordinary perturbation theory fails — correspond to weakly coupled, classical gravity in the bulk, making otherwise intractable CFT calculations doable with geometry.

Structure

A CFT is characterised by its spectrum of local operators, labelled by scaling dimension and spin, and by the operator product expansion (OPE), which expresses the product of two nearby operators as a sum over others with coefficients (the CFT data). Conformal symmetry fixes two- and three-point correlation functions almost completely. The conformal bootstrap programme exploits associativity of the OPE plus unitarity to constrain or solve CFTs non-perturbatively, achieving record precision on critical exponents of the 3D Ising model. In two dimensions the conformal algebra becomes infinite-dimensional (the Virasoro algebra), making 2D CFTs especially tractable and central to string theory and statistical mechanics.
