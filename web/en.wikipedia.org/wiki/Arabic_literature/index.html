Arabic literature

A manuscript of classical Arabic poetry.

Arabic literature is the writing, both prose and poetry, produced by writers in the Arabic language. It emerged in the 6th century CE from an older oral tradition, of which the pre-Islamic odes known as the Muʿallaqāt ("Suspended Odes") are the most famous survivals. The Qur'an, regarded by Muslims as the word of God and by literary historians as the supreme model of Arabic prose, profoundly shaped all later writing.

Classical period

Under the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates, literature flourished in Baghdad, Damascus, and later Córdoba and Cairo. Major figures include the prose stylist al-Jahiz, the poets al-Mutanabbi and Abu Nuwas, and the critic and anthologist Abu al-Faraj al-Isfahani. Popular narrative collections such as One Thousand and One Nights (Alf layla wa-layla) combined folk tales from Arabic, Persian, and Indian sources and later became famous worldwide through European translations. The philosophical and scientific literature of the Islamic Golden Age — by writers such as al-Farabi, Avicenna, and Averroes — was largely transmitted to Europe in translation.

Modern period

The 19th-century Nahda (awakening) revived Arabic letters under the influence of printing and contact with Europe, giving rise to the Arabic novel and short story. In the 20th century, figures such as Taha Hussein, Khalil Gibran, and the Egyptian novelist Naguib Mahfouz — winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1988 — brought Arabic literature to a global audience, alongside the Palestinian poet Mahmoud Darwish and many others.
