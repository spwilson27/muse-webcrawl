Global illumination (GI) is a group of algorithms used in 3D computer graphics to add more realistic lighting to scenes. Such algorithms take into account not only light arriving directly from a source (direct illumination) but also light that has been reflected off other surfaces in the scene (indirect illumination), producing effects such as color bleeding, soft shadows and caustics.

The rendering equation

Global illumination methods attempt to solve the rendering equation, introduced by James Kajiya in 1986, which expresses the light leaving a point as the sum of emitted light and light reflected from all other points in the scene. Because the equation is recursive, practical solutions rely on numerical integration and statistical sampling.

Algorithms

Radiosity subdivides surfaces into patches and computes diffuse energy exchange between them, producing soft, view-independent lighting. Path tracing traces random paths from the camera, accumulating light contributions and converging to a physically accurate image, though at great computational cost. Photon mapping emits photons from light sources to build a map of stored energy, which is especially effective for caustics. Hybrid approaches, irradiance caching and modern GPU-based real-time GI (such as voxel cone tracing and screen-space methods) trade accuracy for speed in video games.

Applications

Global illumination is standard in film visual effects and architectural visualization, where photorealism matters more than render time. Real-time approximations are increasingly common in games, where precomputed lightmaps and probes have long substituted for fully dynamic GI.
