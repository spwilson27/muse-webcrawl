Vinyl flooring

Vinyl flooring is a finished flooring material made primarily of polyvinyl chloride (PVC), used widely in residential and commercial buildings for its durability, water resistance, and low cost. It is produced as sheet vinyl, vinyl tiles, and planks, including luxury vinyl tile (LVT) and luxury vinyl plank (LVP), which convincingly imitate wood, stone, and ceramic surfaces.

Luxury vinyl plank flooring printed with a wood-grain design.

Composition

A typical vinyl floor consists of several layers: a backing or core layer, a printed design layer carrying the visual pattern, and a transparent wear layer of PVC or polyurethane that resists scratches and stains. Rigid-core products (stone plastic composite, SPC, and wood plastic composite, WPC) add a stiff waterproof core popular in modern installations. Plasticizers historically gave the material flexibility; concerns about phthalate plasticizers have led to alternative formulations.

History

Vinyl flooring was exhibited at the 1933 Century of Progress Exposition in Chicago and grew after World War II as an affordable alternative to linoleum, peaking in popularity in the 1950s–70s. Early sheet and tile products sometimes contained asbestos in backing layers, a consideration in renovation of older buildings. Photographic printing and embossing advances in the 2000s–2010s drove the luxury vinyl boom.

Installation and properties

Products are installed by full-spread adhesive, peel-and-stick backing, loose lay, or click-lock floating systems. Vinyl is resilient, quiet underfoot, and waterproof in sheet and rigid-core forms, though it can be damaged by sharp impacts, is sensitive to strong solvents, and is not biodegradable—raising end-of-life and recycling concerns addressed by industry take-back programs.
