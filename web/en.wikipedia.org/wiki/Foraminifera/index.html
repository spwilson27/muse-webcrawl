Foraminifera

DomainEukaryota
GroupRhizaria
PhylumForaminifera
HabitatMarine (benthic and planktonic); rare freshwater forms

Foraminifera ("hole bearers"), commonly called forams, are single-celled amoeboid protists characterised by streaming granular pseudopodia (reticulopodia) and, in most species, a shell or test divided into chambers. They are among the most abundant shelly organisms in the oceans, living both as plankton floating in surface waters and as benthos on the sea floor.

Tests and life cycle

Tests may be made of calcium carbonate, cemented sediment grains (agglutinated), or organic material, and range from under 0.1 mm to, in exceptional deep-sea xenophyophores, many centimetres. Forams alternate between sexual and asexual generations, and many host photosynthetic symbionts such as algae, which helps larger species thrive in nutrient-poor tropical seas.

Importance

Because their tests fossilise readily, foraminifera are key index fossils used in biostratigraphy — the petroleum industry relies on them to date and correlate sedimentary rocks — and in palaeoclimatology, since the oxygen-isotope and trace-element chemistry of fossil forams records past ocean temperatures. Accumulated foraminiferal ooze covers vast areas of the deep ocean floor, and white cliffs of chalk and limestone, including building stone of the Egyptian pyramids, are partly made of foraminiferal remains.
