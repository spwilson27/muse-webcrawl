The Testament of Beauty is a long philosophical poem by Robert Bridges, Poet Laureate of the United Kingdom, published in 1929, the year before his death. It was Bridges's last and most famous work, and an unexpected bestseller on both sides of the Atlantic.

Content and form

Written in four books in a loose, sprung form of the alexandrine, the poem is a wide-ranging meditation on beauty as a guiding principle of human life, encompassing nature, love, art, ethics, science, and religion. Bridges sets out his conviction that beauty is inseparable from truth and goodness, reviewing subjects as various as evolution, aviation, Greek philosophy, and the workings of memory and sense perception. The poem's orthography is also distinctive: Bridges used a semi-phonetic spelling intended to indicate pronunciation, a feature of his late work reflecting his interest in prosody and his founding role in the Society for Pure English.

Reception

The poem was received as the crowning achievement of Bridges's career and a summation of his thought; its commercial success surprised the publishers and made the elderly laureate famous with a general readership. Although its reputation has receded since the mid-20th century, it remains the work for which Bridges is best known.
