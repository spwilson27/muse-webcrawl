Shareholder value

Shareholder value is a business term, sometimes phrased as shareholder value maximization or as the shareholder value model, which implies that the ultimate measure of a company's success is the extent to which it enriches shareholders. It became popular during the 1980s, and is particularly associated with former General Electric chairman and CEO Jack Welch, who said in a 1981 speech that in "an age of slow growth" the winning companies would be those "that grow and generate cash."

Shareholder value creation is commonly measured by growth in share price, dividends, and earnings per share.

History

The idea that management's primary responsibility is to increase the value of the firm for its shareholders dates to the efficient market hypothesis and the work of economists such as Milton Friedman, whose 1970 essay "The Social Responsibility of Business is to Increase its Profits" argued that corporate executives are employees of the owners and must conduct the business in accordance with their desires. Alfred Rappaport's 1986 book Creating Shareholder Value gave the concept a systematic framework, presenting techniques such as shareholder value analysis (SVA) for calculating the value of strategic decisions.

During the 1980s and 1990s, shareholder value became the dominant corporate objective in Anglo-American management practice, displacing older managerial models that balanced the interests of employees, customers, suppliers, and the community. Executive compensation was increasingly tied to share performance through stock options.

Measurement

Shareholder value can be increased through several drivers identified by Rappaport: sales growth, operating profit margin, incremental fixed capital investment, incremental working capital investment, the cash tax rate, the cost of capital, and the value growth duration. Common metrics include:

  • Economic value added (EVA) — net operating profit after tax minus a charge for the cost of capital.
  • Total shareholder return (TSR) — share price appreciation plus dividends over a period.
  • Market value added (MVA) — the difference between a company's market value and the capital contributed by investors.
  • Return on invested capital (ROIC) relative to the weighted average cost of capital (WACC).

Criticism

Critics argue that an exclusive focus on shareholder value encourages short-termism, excessive financial engineering such as share buybacks, underinvestment in research and employees, and risky leverage. Legal scholars such as Lynn Stout, in The Shareholder Value Myth (2012), contend that corporate law does not actually require directors to maximize shareholder value in most circumstances. Following the 2008 financial crisis, and amid the rise of stakeholder capitalism and ESG investing, the Business Roundtable in 2019 issued a statement redefining the purpose of a corporation to promote an economy that serves all stakeholders, a notable departure from its previous endorsement of shareholder primacy.

Jack Welch himself later called shareholder value "the dumbest idea in the world" when treated as a strategy rather than an outcome, remarking that "shareholder value is a result, not a strategy."
