Game theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A payoff matrix for the prisoner's dilemma, the classic two-player game]

Game theory is the study of mathematical models of strategic interaction among rational agents. It analyses how the outcome for each participant depends on the choices of all, and is used across economics, political science, biology, and computer science.

The field was founded by John von Neumann, who proved the minimax theorem in 1928, and by von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern's 1944 book Theory of Games and Economic Behavior. John Nash's equilibrium concept (1950) generalised the theory to non-zero-sum games.

Key concepts

A game is specified by players, strategies, and payoffs. A Nash equilibrium is a profile of strategies from which no player can profitably deviate alone. Famous examples include the prisoner's dilemma, whose dominant-strategy equilibrium is worse for both players than cooperation, and coordination games such as the stag hunt.

Games may be cooperative or non-cooperative, sequential or simultaneous, and of perfect or imperfect information. Evolutionary game theory applies the framework to populations in biology.

Applications

Game theory informs auction design (including spectrum auctions), bargaining, voting theory, market competition, and international relations. Several of its pioneers have received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.
