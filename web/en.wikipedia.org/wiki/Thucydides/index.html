Thucydides

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Bust of Thucydides, Roman copy]

Thucydides (c. 460 – c. 400 BC) was an Athenian historian and general, author of the History of the Peloponnesian War, an austere, analytical account of the war between Athens and Sparta (431–404 BC) that breaks off unfinished in 411 BC. Exiled for twenty years after losing Amphipolis to Sparta (424 BC), he used his leisure and access to both sides to write what he called 'a possession for all time': the founding work of political realism, containing the Funeral Oration of Pericles, the plague narrative, the Melian Dialogue and an unsparing analysis of power, fear and civil breakdown.

Method

Thucydides announced his method in advance: eyewitness testimony cross-examined, no mythology, speeches reconstructed as 'what was called for' by the occasion — an admission modern historians weigh carefully. His archaeology of early Greece, his distinction between immediate causes and 'the truest cause' (Spartan fear of Athenian growth), and his clinical pathology of civil war at Corcyra set standards of evidence and analysis unmatched until modern times.

Legacy

The work survives incomplete — whether from his sudden death (some sources say assassination; plague has also been proposed) is unknown. Hobbes translated him and praised his judgement; international-relations theory still teaches the 'Thucydides trap' (a modern coinage, Graham Allison's) of rising-power rivalry. Where Herodotus charms, Thucydides dissects: together they define the two poles of historical writing.
