Languedoc-Roussillon

Languedoc-Roussillon (French pronunciation: [lɑ̃ɡdɔk ʁusijɔ̃]; Occitan: Lengadòc-Rosselhon) is a former administrative region of southern France, bordering the Mediterranean Sea, Spain and Andorra. From 1982 to 2015 it comprised five departments — Aude, Gard, Hérault, Lozère and Pyrénées-Orientales — with its capital and largest city at Montpellier; other major cities include Nîmes, Perpignan, Narbonne and Béziers. On 1 January 2016 it merged with Midi-Pyrénées to form the new region of Occitanie.

Geography and economy

The region stretches from the Rhône delta and the Camargue in the east to the Pyrenees in the south. It contains the coastal plain of Languedoc, long dominated by vineyards — the Languedoc wine region is one of the largest vineyard areas in the world — and the rugged Cévennes mountains inland, part of which are a UNESCO biosphere reserve and national park. Tourism along the Mediterranean resorts, many built under the 1960s "Mission Racine" programme, agriculture, and Montpellier's universities and research centres anchor the economy.

History and culture

The name refers to the langue d'oc, the Occitan language historically spoken across southern France, and to Roussillon, the Catalan-speaking county around Perpignan that France acquired from Spain by the Treaty of the Pyrenees (1659). The region was the heartland of the medieval Cathar movement, suppressed in the Albigensian Crusade, and preserves Roman monuments such as the Pont du Gard and the amphitheatres of Nîmes and Arles' neighbouring territory.
