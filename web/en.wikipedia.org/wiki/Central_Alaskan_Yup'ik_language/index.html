Central Alaskan Yup'ik language

Central Alaskan Yup'ik (also rendered Yup'ik, Yugtun in the language itself) is a language of the Eskimo–Aleut family spoken by the Yup'ik people of western and southwestern Alaska. It is the most widely spoken Indigenous language in Alaska, with roughly 10,000 speakers out of a population of about 25,000 Yup'ik people, concentrated along the lower Yukon and Kuskokwim rivers and on Nunivak Island and Nelson Island.

Classification

Yup'ik is the largest of the Yupik languages, a branch of the Eskimoan division of the Eskimo–Aleut family. Its close relatives include Alutiiq (Pacific Gulf Yupik), Central Siberian Yupik, and Naukan Yupik; it is more distantly related to the Inuit languages spoken across northern Alaska, Canada, and Greenland. The major dialect groupings are often listed as Lower Yukon, Lower Kuskokwim, Bristol Bay, Hooper Bay–Chevak, and Nunivak.

Phonology and grammar

Yup'ik has a relatively small inventory of vowels (a, i, u, and schwa) and a rich consonant system with contrasts of voicing and length. Like other Eskimoan languages, it is strongly polysynthetic: long words are built from a root followed by derivational suffixes ("postbases") and inflectional endings, so that a single word can express what English would require a whole sentence to say. Nouns inflect for number (singular, dual, plural) and case; verbs agree with both subject and object.

Writing system

The modern orthography, based on the Latin alphabet, was developed in the mid-20th century by linguists working with Yup'ik speakers at the University of Alaska Fairbanks, notably Irene Reed and Michael Krauss. Earlier writing used a script devised by the Moravian missionary work and, in the 19th century, by Russian Orthodox priests. The standard orthography uses digraphs and apostrophes to mark sounds not present in English, such as the voiceless lateral fricative ll and the uvular stop q.

Education and revitalization

Yup'ik is used as a medium of instruction in some village schools in the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta through programs associated with the Lower Kuskokwim School District, and the University of Alaska Fairbanks offers degree programs in the language through the Alaska Native Language Center. Although intergenerational transmission has weakened in many communities, Yup'ik remains one of the more vigorous Indigenous languages of North America.
