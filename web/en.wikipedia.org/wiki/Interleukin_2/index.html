Interleukin 2 (IL-2) is a type of cytokine signaling molecule in the immune system. It is a protein that regulates the activities of white blood cells (leukocytes, often lymphocytes) that are responsible for immunity. IL-2 is part of the body's natural response to microbial infection, and in discriminating between foreign ("non-self") and "self". IL-2 mediates its effects by binding to IL-2 receptors, which are expressed on lymphocytes.

IL-2 was one of the first cytokines to be characterized at the molecular level. It was discovered in 1976 as a factor that allowed the long-term growth of T lymphocytes in culture, and its gene was cloned in 1983.

Function

IL-2 is secreted mainly by activated CD4+ T cells shortly after antigen recognition. It drives the clonal proliferation of activated T cells, enhances the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells and natural killer cells, and promotes the differentiation of naive T cells into effector and memory subsets. Paradoxically, IL-2 is also indispensable for immune tolerance: it is required for the development and maintenance of regulatory T cells (Tregs), which constitutively express the high-affinity IL-2 receptor α chain (CD25).

Receptor and signaling

The IL-2 receptor exists in three forms of increasing affinity: the low-affinity α chain alone (CD25), the intermediate-affinity βγ dimer (CD122/CD132, the common gamma chain shared by several interleukin receptors), and the high-affinity αβγ trimer. Signaling proceeds through JAK1 and JAK3 to STAT5, with additional PI3K–AKT and MAPK pathway activation.

Medical use

Recombinant IL-2, marketed as aldesleukin, was approved in the United States for metastatic renal cell carcinoma (1992) and metastatic melanoma (1998). High-dose therapy produces durable complete responses in a small fraction of patients but is severely toxic, causing capillary leak syndrome; it is administered only in specialized centers. Low-dose IL-2, conversely, preferentially expands regulatory T cells and has been investigated in autoimmune diseases and graft-versus-host disease. Engineered IL-2 variants ("IL-2 muteins") that avoid CD25 binding are an active area of cancer drug development.
