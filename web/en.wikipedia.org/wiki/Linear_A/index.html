Linear A is a writing system that was used by the Minoans of Crete from approximately 1800 to 1450 BC to write the hypothesized Minoan language. Linear A was the primary script used in palace and religious writings of the Minoan civilization. It was succeeded by Linear B, which was used by the Mycenaean Greeks to write an early form of Greek. Linear A was discovered by archaeologist Sir Arthur Evans in 1900 and remains undeciphered.

Corpus

About 1,400 inscriptions in Linear A survive, totalling roughly 7,400 signs — principally on clay administrative tablets from sites such as Hagia Triada, Knossos, Phaistos, Zakros and Akrotiri on Thera, but also on libation tables, votive objects, seals and pottery. The corpus is far smaller than that of Linear B, which hampers decipherment. Most tablets are accounting records listing commodities, quantities and totals; sign groups recur in patterns suggesting words for goods, places and personal names.

Structure

The script has roughly 70 syllabic signs, most of which have counterparts in Linear B with known sound values, plus a set of logograms (ideograms) for commodities and a sophisticated system of fractions and numerals. Because many signs are shared, Linear A texts can be partly "read aloud" using Linear B values — but the resulting language is not Greek and remains unidentified, which is why the script is classed as undeciphered even though its approximate pronunciation is partly recoverable.

Decipherment attempts

Proposed identifications of the Minoan language have included Semitic, Anatolian (Luwian), and unique isolate hypotheses, none of which has won scholarly acceptance. Computational analyses continue to refine the sign list and word patterns.
