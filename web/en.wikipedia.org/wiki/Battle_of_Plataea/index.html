The Battle of Plataea, fought in August 479 BC near the city of Plataea in Boeotia, was the final land battle of the second Persian invasion of Greece. A coalition of Greek city-states commanded by the Spartan regent Pausanias destroyed the Persian army of Mardonius, ending Persia's attempt to conquer mainland Greece. It is traditionally paired with the naval Battle of Mycale, said to have been fought the same day across the Aegean.

Background

After the Greek victory at Salamis in 480 BC, Xerxes withdrew with much of his army, leaving his general Mardonius with a picked force to complete the conquest. In spring 479 the largest hoplite army the Greeks had ever assembled — Herodotus gives some 38,700 hoplites plus tens of thousands of light troops, including 5,000 Spartan citizens — marched into Boeotia to confront Mardonius' army, which Herodotus numbers (with Greek allies) at about 350,000, though modern estimates are far lower.

The battle

For over a week the armies faced each other across the Asopus river, the Persians harassing the Greeks with cavalry and fouling their water supply. When the Greeks attempted a night withdrawal to better ground, confusion split their line; at dawn Mardonius attacked, believing them in full retreat. The Spartans and Tegeans, pressed hard, finally advanced and killed Mardonius; his death broke Persian resistance, and the Greek centre and right stormed the fortified Persian camp. The medising Thebans fought on against Athens before yielding. Persian losses were catastrophic.

Aftermath

Plataea, with Mycale, reversed the momentum of the Greco-Persian Wars: Greek forces went on the offensive for the next decades. The victors dedicated famous thank-offerings, including the Serpent Column at Delphi, made from melted Persian spoils, and swore — in a text whose authenticity is debated — the so-called Oath of Plataea.
