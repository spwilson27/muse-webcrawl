Binary cycle

[Image: schematic of a binary cycle geothermal power plant showing two separate fluid loops joined by a heat exchanger]
Binary cycle power plant
Working fluidsTwo (e.g. hot water/brine and a secondary hydrocarbon such as isobutane or pentane)
ApplicationsGeothermal power; waste-heat recovery
Typical source temperatureBelow ~180 °C (moderate-temperature resources)

A binary cycle is a method for generating electrical power from hot fluids that are not hot enough to flash into steam. The heat of the primary fluid is transferred through a heat exchanger to a secondary ("binary") working fluid with a much lower boiling point, which vaporises and drives a turbine. The vapour is then condensed and recycled in a closed loop, while the primary fluid is commonly reinjected into the ground.

Geothermal application

Binary cycle plants are the most common type of geothermal power plant built today, because they can exploit moderate-temperature geothermal resources (roughly 100–180 °C) that flash steam plants cannot use. The geothermal water never contacts the turbine and is reinjected, so binary plants have very low emissions and minimal fluid loss. The first such plant was built at Paratunka in Kamchatka, Russia, in 1967, using Freon as the secondary fluid.

Working fluids

Typical secondary fluids include isobutane, isopentane and pentane, chosen to match the temperature of the resource so that the thermodynamic cycle (usually an organic Rankine cycle) is efficient. Because the secondary fluid may be flammable, plants are engineered with appropriate safeguards.
