Roman von Ungern-Sternberg

Baron Roman Fyodorovich von Ungern-Sternberg (10 January 1886 [O.S. 29 December 1885] – 15 September 1921), often called Ungern or the "Bloody Baron", was a Baltic German officer in the Imperial Russian Army and an anti-Bolshevik general in the Russian Civil War. In 1921 his Asiatic Cavalry Division expelled Chinese forces from Mongolia and restored the Bogd Khan, making Ungern the effective dictator of Mongolia for several months before he was captured and executed by the Red Army. His mixture of monarchism, extreme violence, and Buddhist mysticism has made him a legendary figure in both Russian and Mongolian memory.

Early life and career

Ungern was born in Graz to a Baltic German noble family with estates in Estonia and grew up partly on the island of Hiiumaa. Expelled from school for indiscipline, he served as a volunteer in the Russo-Japanese War and later attended the Pavlovsk Military School in St Petersburg. Posted to Cossack regiments in the Transbaikal, he developed a fascination with Buddhism and Mongolian culture. He fought in the First World War on the Eastern Front, where he was decorated but also repeatedly disciplined for drunken violence.

Civil War and Mongolia

After the October Revolution Ungern joined the White forces of Ataman Grigory Semyonov in the Russian Far East, where he gained notoriety for the brutal discipline of his command at Dauria. In 1920 he broke with Semyonov and led his Asiatic Cavalry Division — a polyglot force of Russians, Buryats, Mongols, Tatars, and others — into Mongolia, then under Chinese occupation. After two failed attacks, his troops captured the capital Urga in February 1921. Ungern restored the Bogd Khan to the throne and was rewarded with high Mongolian titles, while his regime in Urga carried out pogroms and executions of Bolsheviks, Jews, and Chinese.

Downfall and death

Ungern dreamed of a restored Mongol empire and a march against Bolshevism. In the summer of 1921 the Red Army and the Mongolian revolutionary forces of Damdin Sükhbaatar invaded; Ungern's offensive into Siberia failed, his troops mutinied, and his own officers attempted to kill him. He was captured by a Red Army patrol in August 1921, tried in Novonikolayevsk (now Novosibirsk) in a five-hour show trial — one of the first of the Soviet era — and shot on 15 September 1921.

Legacy

Ungern's reputation rests on memoir accounts such as Ferdinand Ossendowski's bestseller Beasts, Men and Gods (1922), which popularised the legend of the "Mad Baron" who believed himself a reincarnation of Genghis Khan. Historians note that the label "Mad Baron" owes much to embellished sources, though his documented cruelty is not in doubt. In modern Mongolia he is sometimes credited — ambiguously — with restoring the country's independence in 1921.
