Liberal arts education

Modern scopeHumanities, social sciences, natural sciences, formal sciences, fine arts

Liberal arts education is an approach to undergraduate study that emphasises breadth of knowledge across the humanities, sciences, and social sciences, together with critical thinking, writing, and intellectual adaptability, rather than narrow vocational or technical training. The name derives from the Latin artes liberales, the arts befitting a free person (liber), as distinguished in antiquity from the mechanical arts practised by slaves.

History

In classical and medieval education the liberal arts comprised seven subjects: the trivium of grammar, logic, and rhetoric, and the quadrivium of arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy. This scheme, codified by writers such as Martianus Capella and Boethius, formed the foundation of the medieval university. The ideal was revived in the Renaissance studia humanitatis and in the 19th century shaped the modern university and, especially in the United States, the small residential liberal arts college.

Modern practice

Today a liberal arts curriculum typically requires distribution courses across disciplines, a major field of study, and often a capstone or thesis. American liberal arts colleges — such as Williams, Amherst, Swarthmore, and Oberlin — are known for small classes and close faculty contact. Advocates argue the model prepares graduates for a changing economy by teaching transferable skills; critics point to cost, questions of employability, and declining enrolment in humanities subjects.
