Kalka–Shimla Railway

The Kalka–Shimla Railway is a 2 ft 6 in (762 mm) narrow-gauge railway in northwestern India that climbs from Kalka, in the foothills of the Himalayas, to Shimla, the former summer capital of British India. Completed in 1903, it is celebrated for its dramatic engineering and scenery, and in 2008 was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of the Mountain Railways of India.

Route and engineering

The line runs 96 km (60 miles) and rises from 658 m at Kalka to 2,076 m at Shimla. It passes through 102 tunnels, 864 bridges and viaducts, and 919 curves, the sharpest being 48 degrees. The longest tunnel, at Barog (No. 33), is about 1,144 m long; the story of its chief engineer, Colonel Barog, who is said to have taken his own life after the two ends of the tunnel failed to meet, is a famous piece of railway folklore. The multi-arched Kanoh viaduct, built with stone arches in tiers like a Roman aqueduct, is the line's best-known bridge.

History

Shimla became the summer capital of the Government of India in 1864, and a rail link was long demanded to replace the slow tonga road. Construction by the Delhi-Umballa-Kalka Railway Company began in 1898; the line opened on 9 November 1903, inaugurated by Viceroy Lord Curzon. It was taken over by the state in 1906 and later became part of the North Western Railway and, after independence, Indian Railways' Northern Railway zone.

Services

Passenger trains, including the Shivalik Deluxe Express and the Himalayan Queen, take about five hours for the journey. Small diesel locomotives haul the "toy train" services, and a rail motor car also operates. The line carried the young Mahatma Gandhi and generations of officials, schoolchildren, and tourists, and remains both a working railway and a major tourist attraction.
