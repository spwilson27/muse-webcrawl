Sitting Bull (Lakota: Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake, "Buffalo Bull Who Sits Down"; c. 1831 – 15 December 1890) was a Hunkpapa Lakota leader who led his people during years of resistance against United States government policies. He was a holy man and chief known for his prophetic vision before the Battle of the Little Bighorn, where the Sioux and Cheyenne defeated the U.S. 7th Cavalry.

Early life

Sitting Bull was born near the Grand River in present-day South Dakota, the son of Jumping Bull, a respected Hunkpapa chief. Nicknamed "Slow" (Húŋkešni) as a boy for his careful, deliberate manner, he earned his famous name at about fourteen after a display of bravery in battle against the Crow. He grew to be noted as much for spiritual leadership and generosity as for war deeds, becoming a wichasha wakan (holy man) and, by the late 1860s, a principal leader of the non-treaty Lakota who refused to settle on reservations.

Resistance and Little Bighorn

After the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie established the Great Sioux Reservation, gold was discovered in the Black Hills, and the U.S. demanded the land. When the Lakota and Cheyenne refused to sell, the government ordered all bands onto agencies by January 1876 and launched a military campaign. Sitting Bull's camp on the Little Bighorn River became the rallying point for thousands of "non-treaty" Indians.

During a Sun Dance in June 1876, Sitting Bull reported a vision of soldiers falling upside-down into his camp — interpreted as a coming victory. On 25 June 1876, Lt. Col. George Armstrong Custer attacked the village; his battalion of about 210 men was annihilated by warriors led in the field by Crazy Horse and Gall. Sitting Bull, by then middle-aged, did not fight as a front-line warrior but directed the camp's defence and protected the noncombatants.

Exile in Canada and surrender

Facing relentless military pressure and the collapse of the buffalo herds, Sitting Bull led his followers into Canada in May 1877, settling near Wood Mountain in present-day Saskatchewan. Canadian authorities, including Major James Walsh of the North-West Mounted Police, maintained peaceful relations but refused a reserve. Starvation eventually forced his return: on 19 July 1881 he surrendered at Fort Buford, handing his rifle to his young son to pass to the fort's commander.

Later life and death

After two years as a prisoner at Fort Randall, Sitting Bull settled at the Standing Rock Agency. In 1885 he toured with Buffalo Bill's Wild West show, where he was billed as the "slayer of Custer" and famously cursed audiences in Lakota while appearing to bless them. He supported the Ghost Dance movement's promise of a restored world, though his personal involvement may have been more political than religious. Fearing he would join the Ghost Dancers, Indian agent James McLaughlin ordered his arrest. On 15 December 1890, at dawn, Indian police attempted to seize him at his cabin; in the ensuing fight Sitting Bull was shot and killed, along with his son Crow Foot and several police and supporters — two weeks before the Wounded Knee Massacre.

Legacy

Sitting Bull remains an enduring symbol of Native American resistance and dignity. He is buried on the Standing Rock Reservation near Mobridge, South Dakota, beneath a granite bust, and Sitting Bull College on the reservation bears his name.
