Great Syrian Revolt

The Great Syrian Revolt (Arabic: al-Thawra as-Sūrīya al-Kubrā), also known as the Great Druze Revolt, was a general uprising across Syria and Lebanon against French rule during the French Mandate for Syria and the Lebanon. The revolt began in July 1925 in the Druze region of Jabal al-Druze (Hauran) under the leadership of Sultan al-Atrash, and spread to Damascus and much of the country before being suppressed by French forces in 1927.

Causes

Grievances included French administrative practices in the Druze state—forced labor, taxation, and interference by the French governor Captain Carbillet—and broader resentment of the mandate, the partition of Syria into separate statelets, and French economic and political domination. The arrest of Druze notables invited to negotiate in Damascus in July 1925 ignited open rebellion.

Course of the revolt

Druze forces won striking victories, destroying a French column at al-Kafr and winning at al-Musayfirah and Salkhad. The revolt spread to Damascus, where nationalist figures including Fawzi al-Qawuqji led an uprising in October 1925; the French responded with a heavy artillery bombardment of the capital in October 1925 and again in May 1926. Rebels operated across the Ghouta countryside, Homs, Hama, and southern Lebanon. France reinforced its Army of the Levant to some 100,000 troops at the peak of the fighting.

Aftermath

By 1927 the revolt was defeated militarily; estimates of Syrian deaths range from 6,000 to over 10,000, and Damascus and many villages suffered extensive destruction. Politically, however, the revolt forced changes: France adopted a more conciliatory policy, and negotiations led eventually to the Franco-Syrian Treaty of 1936, though it was never ratified. The revolt became a founding symbol of Syrian nationalism, and Sultan al-Atrash is commemorated as a national hero.
