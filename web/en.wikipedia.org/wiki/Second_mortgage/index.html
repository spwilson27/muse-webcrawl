Second mortgage

A second mortgage is a loan taken out on a property that is already mortgaged, in which the new loan is secured by the borrower's equity in the property but is subordinate to the first (senior) mortgage: if the borrower defaults and the property is sold in foreclosure, the first mortgage is repaid in full before the second mortgage receives anything. Because of this junior position, second mortgages carry higher interest rates than first mortgages, though typically lower rates than unsecured credit, since the loan is still backed by real estate.

Forms

Second mortgages take two principal forms. A home equity loan is a lump-sum loan, usually at a fixed rate, repaid in equal instalments over a term of 5 to 30 years. A home equity line of credit (HELOC) is a revolving facility with a draw period — during which the borrower may borrow, repay, and borrow again up to a limit — followed by a repayment period; HELOC rates are usually variable, tied to the prime rate. Historically, "piggyback" second mortgages (such as 80-10-10 loans, combining an 80% first mortgage, a 10% second, and a 10% down payment) were used to avoid private mortgage insurance on high loan-to-value purchases.

Lending criteria and risks

Lenders evaluate a second mortgage against the property's combined loan-to-value ratio (CLTV) — the sum of all mortgage balances as a fraction of appraised value — along with the borrower's income and credit history. The principal risk to the borrower is that a second mortgage is still secured by the home: default can lead to foreclosure by either lender, and the second-mortgage holder can foreclose even when the first mortgage is current. In bankruptcy, wholly unsecured junior liens may in some circumstances be stripped off, a point heavily litigated after the 2008 housing crisis, when falling prices left many second mortgages entirely underwater. Second liens were a significant channel of loss in that crisis and a focus of subsequent loan-modification programmes.

Uses

Common uses include home improvement, debt consolidation, education expenses, and supplementing a down payment. In the United States, interest on second mortgages historically enjoyed mortgage-interest tax deductibility; the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 narrowed the deduction to debt used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home.
