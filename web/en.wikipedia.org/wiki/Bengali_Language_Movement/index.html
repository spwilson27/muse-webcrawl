Bengali Language Movement

Bengali Language Movement
Date1947–1956; climax 21 February 1952
LocationEast Bengal (East Pakistan), present-day Bangladesh
GoalRecognition of Bengali as a state language of Pakistan
ResultBengali recognized as a state language in the 1956 constitution of Pakistan

The Bengali Language Movement (Bengali: বাংলা ভাষা আন্দোলন, Bānglā Bhāshā Āndōlon) was a political movement in former East Bengal (renamed East Pakistan in 1952) advocating the recognition of the Bengali language as a state language of the then-Dominion of Pakistan, in order to allow its use in government affairs, schools, media, currency, and stamps.

Background

After the partition of British India in 1947, the Dominion of Pakistan comprised two geographically separated wings; a majority of its citizens lived in East Bengal and spoke Bengali, while the governing elite in West Pakistan sought to establish Urdu — spoken by a small minority — as the sole national language. In March 1948, Governor-General Muhammad Ali Jinnah declared in Dhaka that "Urdu, and only Urdu" would be the state language, provoking protests among Bengali students and intellectuals.

21 February 1952

Tensions culminated when the government declared Urdu the sole state language and banned public assemblies in Dhaka. On 21 February 1952, students of the University of Dhaka and other institutions defied Section 144 prohibitions and took to the streets. Police opened fire, killing several students, including Abdus Salam, Rafiq Uddin Ahmed, Abul Barkat, Abdul Jabbar, and Shafiur Rahman. The killings sparked mass civil unrest across East Bengal. A makeshift monument, the Shaheed Minar (Martyrs' Monument), was erected within days and became the enduring symbol of the movement.

Aftermath and legacy

The movement forced the state to concede: in 1954 the provincial elections brought the United Front to power in East Bengal, and the 1956 constitution of Pakistan recognized Bengali as a state language alongside Urdu. The language movement is widely regarded as the seed of Bengali nationalism that ultimately led to the creation of Bangladesh in 1971. In 1999, UNESCO proclaimed 21 February as International Mother Language Day in recognition of the movement and the martyrs of 1952; the day is observed worldwide to promote linguistic and cultural diversity.
