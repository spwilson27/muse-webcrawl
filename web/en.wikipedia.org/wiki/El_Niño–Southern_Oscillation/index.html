The El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is an irregular periodic variation in winds and sea surface temperatures over the tropical eastern Pacific Ocean, affecting climate across much of the tropics and subtropics. The warming phase is known as El Niño and the cooling phase as La Niña. The atmospheric component, the Southern Oscillation, is the accompanying seesaw in surface air pressure between the eastern and western tropical Pacific.

Mechanism

In neutral conditions, trade winds push warm surface water westward, piling it up near Indonesia while cold water upwells off South America. In an El Niño, the trade winds weaken, warm water spreads eastward, upwelling is suppressed, and convection follows the warm water toward the central and eastern Pacific. In La Niña the normal pattern intensifies. The oscillation is sustained by feedbacks between the ocean and atmosphere, first described by Jacob Bjerknes, and by the propagation and reflection of oceanic Kelvin and Rossby waves.

Impacts

ENSO is the dominant driver of year-to-year climate variability worldwide. El Niño typically brings heavy rain and flooding to the Pacific coast of South America and the southern United States, drought to Indonesia, Australia and parts of India, and warmer global average temperatures. La Niña generally produces the opposite pattern, including more Atlantic hurricanes. The events affect fisheries — the term El Niño was coined by Peruvian fishermen who noticed a warm current around Christmas (El Niño, "the Christ Child") that collapsed anchoveta catches.

Monitoring and prediction

Agencies including NOAA and the Australian Bureau of Meteorology monitor ENSO using moored buoy arrays (TAO/TRITON), satellites and indices such as the Oceanic Niño Index, based on sea-surface temperatures in the Niño 3.4 region. Seasonal forecasts can predict events several months ahead, though a "spring predictability barrier" limits skill for forecasts made early in the year.

History of study

Gilbert Walker identified the Southern Oscillation in the 1920s while seeking predictors of the Indian monsoon; Bjerknes connected it to El Niño in the 1960s. The devastating 1982–83 event, which was not recognised until it was well advanced, spurred the creation of the tropical Pacific observing system. The 1997–98 El Niño caused an estimated tens of billions of dollars in damage and thousands of deaths worldwide.
