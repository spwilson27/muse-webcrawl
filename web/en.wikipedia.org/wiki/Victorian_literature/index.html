Victorian literature

Victorian literature is English literature during the reign of Queen Victoria (1837–1901), the Victorian era. It was preceded by Romanticism and followed by the modernist movement of the Edwardian era. The nineteenth century saw the novel become the leading literary genre in England, driven by rising literacy, cheap serial publication, and circulating libraries such as Mudie's.

The novel

Charles Dickens is regarded as the pre-eminent Victorian novelist, with works such as Great Expectations, Bleak House, A Tale of Two Cities, and David Copperfield combining social criticism with vivid characterization. William Makepeace Thackeray's Vanity Fair (1847–48) satirized early-century society. The Brontë sisters — Charlotte (Jane Eyre), Emily (Wuthering Heights), and Anne — produced intense, psychologically penetrating fiction, while George Eliot (Mary Ann Evans) brought philosophical depth to the form in Middlemarch (1871–72), often called the greatest English novel.

Later Victorians include Thomas Hardy, whose Wessex novels (Tess of the d'Urbervilles, Jude the Obscure) grew increasingly pessimistic; Robert Louis Stevenson (Treasure Island, Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde); Bram Stoker, whose Dracula (1897) defined Gothic horror; and Arthur Conan Doyle, creator of Sherlock Holmes. Sensation fiction by Wilkie Collins (The Woman in White, The Moonstone) and Mary Elizabeth Braddon flourished in the 1860s.

Poetry

Alfred, Lord Tennyson, Poet Laureate for much of the era, wrote In Memoriam A.H.H. (1850), a long meditation on grief and faith shaken by science. Robert Browning perfected the dramatic monologue, and his wife Elizabeth Barrett Browning was celebrated for Sonnets from the Portuguese and the verse-novel Aurora Leigh. Matthew Arnold, Gerard Manley Hopkins (published posthumously), Christina Rossetti, and the Pre-Raphaelite circle around Dante Gabriel Rossetti also shaped the period's poetry.

Drama and prose

Victorian drama was long dominated by melodrama and adaptations, but by the century's end Oscar Wilde revived the comedy of manners with The Importance of Being Earnest (1895), and the plays of Henrik Ibsen influenced British theatre profoundly. In non-fiction, Thomas Carlyle, John Ruskin, John Stuart Mill, Charles Darwin (On the Origin of Species, 1859), and Cardinal Newman defined intellectual debate; children's literature flourished with Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865).

Themes

Recurring concerns include industrialization and class, faith versus scientific doubt, empire, gender and the "Woman Question", and the tension between progress and moral anxiety. Serialization shaped plotting: cliffhangers and large casts suited monthly or weekly parts sold cheaply to a mass readership.
