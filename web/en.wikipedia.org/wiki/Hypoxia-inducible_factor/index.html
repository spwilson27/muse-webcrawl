Hypoxia-inducible factor

Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) are transcription factors that respond to decreases in available oxygen in the cellular environment. They coordinate the expression of hundreds of genes involved in angiogenesis, erythropoiesis, metabolism and cell survival, and are central to how animals sense and adapt to oxygen availability.

Structure and regulation

HIF is a heterodimer of an oxygen-labile alpha subunit (HIF-1α, HIF-2α or HIF-3α) and a constitutive beta subunit (ARNT). Under normal oxygen levels, prolyl hydroxylase domain enzymes (PHDs) hydroxylate HIF-α, marking it for recognition by the von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) ubiquitin ligase and rapid proteasomal degradation. When oxygen falls, hydroxylation ceases, HIF-α stabilises, enters the nucleus and activates target genes bearing hypoxia response elements, including VEGF and erythropoietin (EPO).

Discovery and Nobel Prize

The pathway was elucidated by Gregg L. Semenza, William G. Kaelin Jr. and Peter J. Ratcliffe, who shared the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for their discoveries of how cells sense and adapt to oxygen availability".

Clinical significance

HIF activation helps tissues survive ischaemia but also supports tumour growth and metastasis, making the pathway a cancer drug target; HIF-2α inhibitors such as belzutifan are used in VHL-associated tumours. Conversely, prolyl hydroxylase inhibitors such as roxadustat stabilise HIF and are used to treat anaemia of chronic kidney disease.
