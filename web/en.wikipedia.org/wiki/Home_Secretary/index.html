Home Secretary

The Secretary of State for the Home Department, more commonly known as the Home Secretary, is a senior minister of the Crown in the Government of the United Kingdom and head of the Home Office. The office is one of the four Great Offices of State, alongside the Prime Minister, Chancellor of the Exchequer, and Foreign Secretary. The incumbent is a member of the Cabinet and is responsible for the internal affairs of England and Wales, and for immigration and citizenship matters across the whole United Kingdom.

Responsibilities

The Home Secretary's remit includes policing in England and Wales, national security (including oversight of MI5, the Security Service), counter-terrorism, immigration, asylum and border control (through UK Visas and Immigration, Border Force, and HM Passport Office), fire and rescue services in England, and policy on drugs and crime. Many functions once held by the office — such as prisons and probation — were transferred in 2007 to the newly created Ministry of Justice, and the Home Secretary's historic powers over criminal justice were correspondingly reduced.

History

The office was created in 1782, when the former Southern and Northern Departments were reorganized into the Home Office and Foreign Office. The first Home Secretary was William Petty, 2nd Earl of Shelburne. Notable holders have included Robert Peel, who founded the Metropolitan Police in 1829; Winston Churchill; and Theresa May, the longest-serving Home Secretary of the modern era (2010–2016), before becoming Prime Minister. Because the role combines high public salience with responsibility for departments prone to operational failure, it has often been described as one of the most exposed and demanding jobs in British government.
