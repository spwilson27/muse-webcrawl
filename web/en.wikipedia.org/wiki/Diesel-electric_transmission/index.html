Diesel–electric transmission

A diesel–electric transmission, or diesel–electric powertrain, is a transmission system for vehicles powered by diesel engines in road, rail, and marine transport. Diesel–electric transmission is based on petrol–electric transmission, a similar transmission system used for petrol engines.

Diesel–electric transmission is used on railways by diesel–electric locomotives and diesel–electric multiple units, as electric motors are able to supply full torque at 0 RPM. Diesel–electric systems are also used in marine transport, including submarines, and on some other land vehicles.

Description

The defining characteristic of diesel–electric transmission is that it avoids the need for a gearbox by converting the mechanical force of the diesel engine into electrical energy (through a dynamo or alternator), and using the electrical energy to drive traction motors, which propel the vehicle. The traction motors may be powered directly or via a set of rechargeable batteries, making it a type of hybrid electric vehicle.

Advantages and disadvantages

Because no clutch or multi-ratio gearbox is required, diesel–electric drive is smooth and allows the diesel engine to run at an efficient speed regardless of road speed. It also simplifies the distribution of power to multiple axles. Disadvantages include the weight and cost of the electrical machinery and conversion losses of roughly 10–15%.

Applications

The system dominates mainline freight and passenger locomotive design in North America and much of the world, and is used in large mining trucks, buses in hybrid form, and most conventionally powered submarines when running on the surface or snorkelling.
