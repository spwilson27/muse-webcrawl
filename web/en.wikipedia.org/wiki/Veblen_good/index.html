Veblen good


Luxury goods are typical examples of goods whose prestige rises with price

A Veblen good is a good for which demand increases as its price increases, in apparent contradiction to the law of demand. The effect arises because a higher price confers greater status or prestige on the buyer — a form of conspicuous consumption. The concept is named after the American economist Thorstein Veblen, who described conspicuous consumption in The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899).

Explanation

For ordinary goods, a price rise reduces quantity demanded (apart from income effects, as with Giffen goods). For Veblen goods, the price itself is part of the product's appeal: an expensive watch, handbag, wine, or car signals wealth and taste. Raising the price can make the good more desirable as a status symbol, so that over some range the demand curve slopes upward. If the price falls too far, the good may lose its cachet and demand can actually decline among status-seeking buyers.

Examples and evidence

Luxury brands frequently cite the Veblen effect in pricing strategy. Studies of fine wine have found that consumers rate the same wine more highly when told it costs more; perfume, designer clothing, and premium automobiles are commonly given as examples. The effect depends on the good being visible, scarce, and socially recognised.

Related concepts

The Veblen effect is one of several "snob" and "bandwagon" effects in consumer theory analysed by Harvey Leibenstein (1950). It is distinct from the Giffen good, where the anomaly arises from strong income effects on an inferior staple good, not from prestige. Some economists treat recorded cases sceptically, noting that quality differences and information effects can mimic upward-sloping demand.
