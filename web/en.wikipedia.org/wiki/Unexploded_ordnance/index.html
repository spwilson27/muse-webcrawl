Unexploded ordnance

Unexploded ordnance (UXO, sometimes abbreviated as UO) is weapons such as bombs, shells, grenades, land mines, naval mines, and cluster munition remnants that did not explode when they were employed and still pose a risk of detonation, sometimes many decades after they were used or discarded. When unwanted munitions are found, they are sometimes destroyed in controlled explosions, but accidental detonation of even very old explosives also occurs, sometimes with fatal results.

An unexploded shell recovered from farmland in a former conflict zone.

Definition and scope

Unexploded ordnance does not include military stockpiles in their proper storage; rather, it refers to munitions that were prepared for action, fired, dropped, launched or otherwise projected, and remain unexploded through malfunction or by design. Some sources use the term explosive remnants of war (ERW) to cover both unexploded ordnance and abandoned explosive ordnance left behind after a conflict.

Risks

UXO can be found on land, under water, and in former battlefields, training ranges, and areas struck by air raids. Even decades old, such munitions may detonate if disturbed. Children are particularly at risk because shiny or unusual objects can attract curiosity. Farmers, construction workers, and scrap-metal collectors are also frequently victims. Cluster munitions are especially dangerous because their small bomblets are scattered over wide areas and have high failure rates.

Historical legacy

Parts of Europe remain contaminated by munitions from the First and Second World Wars. In France and Belgium, farmers regularly plough up shells in the so-called "iron harvest" along the former Western Front. Laos is among the most heavily bombed countries per capita in history, and large quantities of cluster submunitions dropped during the Vietnam War era continue to kill and injure people there. The Baltic Sea and North Sea contain dumped chemical and conventional munitions from both world wars.

Detection and disposal

Detection methods include metal detectors, ground-penetrating radar, and magnetometry. Disposal is carried out by explosive ordnance disposal (EOD) specialists, who may destroy items in place, move them to a demolition area, or render them safe by defusing. Because moving old ordnance can be dangerous, controlled detonation in place is often preferred where it is safe for nearby people and property.

International law

Protocol V of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, adopted in 2003, addresses explosive remnants of war and requires parties to clear, remove or destroy ERW in territory under their control after a conflict. The Convention on Cluster Munitions (2008) additionally requires states parties to clear areas contaminated by cluster munition remnants.
