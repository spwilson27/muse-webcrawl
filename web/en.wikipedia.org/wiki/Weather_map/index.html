Weather map

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A synoptic chart with isobars and fronts.

A weather map is a map that displays meteorological data for a region at a particular time, used to show the current and forecast state of the atmosphere. The most common type, the synoptic map, plots surface observations — temperature, pressure, wind, cloud, and precipitation — at many locations simultaneously, allowing forecasters to identify pressure systems, fronts, and other features.

History

The first weather maps appeared in the mid-19th century. The German meteorologist Heinrich Wilhelm Brandes drew the first synoptic maps in 1820 from the observations of the Mannheim Meteorological Society, and the development of the telegraph in the 1840s made near-simultaneous data collection possible. The British Admiralty's Robert FitzRoy, founder of the Met Office, began issuing weather forecasts in the 1860s based on such charts. The Norwegian Bergen School in the 1920s added the modern concepts of fronts and cyclones, giving synoptic maps their familiar structure.

Elements of a synoptic chart

Synoptic charts show isobars (lines of equal pressure), which reveal high- and low-pressure systems; fronts, drawn as coloured lines with symbols (triangles for cold fronts, semicircles for warm fronts); and station models that encode temperature, dew point, wind, cloud cover, and present weather at each station. Upper-air charts show conditions at standard pressure levels such as 500 hPa, with isotherms and height contours. Forecasters also use specialised charts: rainfall radar imagery, satellite imagery, and surface analyses combined with model output.

Modern use

Today, weather maps are produced by national weather services and broadcasters from a combination of surface stations, weather balloons, radar, satellites, buoys, and numerical weather prediction models. Although digital graphics have largely replaced hand-drawn analysis, the conventions of the synoptic chart remain the standard language of forecasting, and simplified versions are ubiquitous in television weather reports.
