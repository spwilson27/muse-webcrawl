William Blake

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Ancient of Days (1794), Blake's frontispiece to Europe a Prophecy: the white-bearded creator kneeling in a sun-disc, compass extended]

William Blake (28 November 1757 – 12 August 1827) was an English poet, painter and printmaker — largely unrecognised in his lifetime and now counted among the supreme figures of Romanticism. Working as a commercial engraver, he invented a relief-etching process to print his own 'illuminated books' in which poem and image fuse: Songs of Innocence (1789), Songs of Experience (1794, containing 'The Tyger'), The Marriage of Heaven and Hell (c. 1790–93) and the vast prophetic books of his private mythology.

Blake claimed visions from childhood — angels in a Peckham tree, conversations with the dead — and built from the Bible, Milton and Swedenborg a mythology of Urizen (reason), Los (imagination) and the Zoas, against the 'mind-forg'd manacles' of industrial, rationalist England. His lyrics entered the language: 'Jerusalem' (from the preface to Milton), set by Parry in 1916, became England's unofficial anthem.

Life and craft

Apprenticed to the engraver James Basire and trained in Westminster Abbey's monuments, Blake set up as an engraver-print-seller, failed commercially, and was sustained by patrons (Thomas Butts, John Linnell) and by his wife Catherine, who printed and coloured beside him. In 1803 he was tried for sedition after ejecting a soldier from his garden at Felpham — and acquitted. He died in 1827, singing, by the devoted accounts of the young artists (the 'Ancients') who had gathered round him.

Rediscovery

Gilchrist's biography (1863), Swinburne and the Pre-Raphaelites, then Yeats's edition, began the recovery; Northrop Frye's Fearful Symmetry (1947) decoded the mythology for modern readers. Blake is now the presiding spirit of British counter-culture — doors, in Aldous Huxley's phrase from Blake, of perception.
