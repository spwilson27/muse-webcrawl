Rhyming slang

Rhyming slang
OriginLondon, especially the East End, mid-19th century
Associated dialectCockney
MechanismWord replaced by a rhyming phrase, often shortened to its non-rhyming element
SpreadThroughout Britain; Australia; parts of the anglophone world

Rhyming slang is a form of slang in which a word is replaced by a phrase that rhymes with it, which is then often truncated to its first, non-rhyming element, making the resulting expression opaque to outsiders. It is strongly associated with Cockney speech in London's East End but has spread throughout the United Kingdom, to Australia, and beyond.

How it works

To say "stairs," a speaker uses the phrase "apples and pears" (which rhymes with stairs) and usually shortens it to "apples": "I'm going up the apples." Because the element actually spoken does not rhyme with the intended word, the slang functions as an in-group code—one reason for its popularity among market traders, and by some accounts among criminals wishing to baffle police or customers.

Examples

  • "apples and pears" = stairs
  • "dog and bone" = telephone ("dog")
  • "trouble and strife" = wife
  • "loaf of bread" = head; abbreviated "loaf," giving the expression "use your loaf"
  • "butcher's hook" = a look; abbreviated "butcher's": "have a butcher's"
  • "porky pies" (or "porkies") = lies
  • "raspberry tart" = fart; "raspberry" passed into general English and American usage for a derisive sound
  • "berk" — from "Berkeley Hunt," a once-vulgar term now mild, its rhyming origin forgotten by most users

History

Rhyming slang is first documented in the mid-nineteenth century in the costermonger (street-seller) communities of London; John Camden Hotten's slang dictionary of 1859 describes it as a new cant of the London markets. Whether it began as a deliberate secret language or as a linguistic game is debated. New coinages continue to appear, often built on celebrity names, though many traditional terms survive mainly as colourful stereotypes of Cockney speech in film and television (for example the deliberately absurd pseudo-rhyming slang of Lock, Stock and Two Smoking Barrels).

Australian rhyming slang

British migration carried rhyming slang to Australia, where local forms developed ("Captain Cook" = a look; "Joe Blake" = snake). Some terms from both traditions have entered international English.
