The leopard (Panthera pardus) is one of the five extant species in the genus Panthera, a member of the cat family, Felidae. It occurs in a wide range in sub-Saharan Africa, in some parts of Western and Central Asia, Southern Russia, and on the Indian subcontinent to Southeast and East Asia. It is listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List because leopard populations are threatened by habitat loss and fragmentation, and are declining in large parts of the global range.

Compared to other wild cats, the leopard has relatively short legs and a long body with a large skull. Its fur is marked with rosettes. It is similar in appearance to the jaguar (Panthera onca), but has a smaller, lighter physique, and its rosettes are generally smaller, more densely packed and without central spots. Both leopards and jaguars that are melanistic are known as black panthers.

Etymology

The English name "leopard" comes from Old French leupart or Middle Latin leupardus, derived from Latin leopardus, itself from Greek leopardos — a compound of leon (lion) and pardos (spotted), reflecting the ancient belief that the leopard was a hybrid of a lion and a panther.

Characteristics

The leopard's skin colour varies by climate and habitat from pale yellowish to dark golden, with dark spots grouped in rosettes. Its head is broad and its skull somewhat elongated. Males are typically larger than females, standing 60–70 cm (24–28 in) at the shoulder and weighing 37–90 kg (82–198 lb); females weigh 28–60 kg (62–132 lb). The leopard is sexually dimorphic, and males are up to 50% larger.

Leopards are renowned for their strength and ability to climb: they frequently haul kills weighing more than themselves into trees to keep them from lions, hyenas and other scavengers. They are powerful swimmers and highly adaptable, able to live in habitats ranging from rainforest to arid savanna and even mountain terrain up to 5,200 m on Mount Kilimanjaro.

Distribution and habitat

The leopard has the largest distribution of any wild cat, occurring widely in Africa and Asia, though populations are fragmented and declining. It is extinct in several parts of its former range, including much of North Africa west of Egypt and parts of the Middle East. The largest populations remain in southern and eastern Africa and in India, where the population was estimated at 12,000–14,000 in the 2010s.

Behaviour and ecology

Hunting and diet

The leopard is a solitary and territorial animal. It is an opportunistic hunter, preying mainly on ungulates such as impala, bushbuck and common duiker in Africa, and chital and wild boar in Asia, but it takes prey ranging from dung beetles to adult eland. Leopards hunt mainly at night, stalk their prey and kill with a bite to the throat or neck.

Reproduction

Females give birth to a litter of usually two to four cubs after a gestation of about 90–105 days. Cubs remain with their mother for 18–24 months before dispersing. In the wild, leopards live 12–17 years; in captivity they may live over 20 years.

Subspecies

Nine subspecies are generally recognised, including the African leopard (P. p. pardus), the Indian leopard (P. p. fusca), the Javan leopard (P. p. melas), the Arabian leopard (P. p. nimr), the Persian leopard (P. p. tulliana), the Amur leopard (P. p. orientalis), the Indochinese leopard (P. p. delacouri), the Sri Lankan leopard (P. p. kotiya) and the North-Chinese leopard (P. p. japonensis). The Amur leopard is among the rarest big cats, with only a few dozen individuals surviving in the Russian Far East and adjacent China in recent decades.

Threats and conservation

Primary threats include habitat fragmentation, depletion of prey, human–wildlife conflict, poaching for skins and body parts, and trophy hunting. The species is protected across much of its range and is listed in CITES Appendix I. Leopards appear widely in mythology, art and heraldry, and remain a popular subject of wildlife tourism.
