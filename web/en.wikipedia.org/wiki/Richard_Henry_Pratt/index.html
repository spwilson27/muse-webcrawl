Richard Henry Pratt

Richard Henry Pratt (December 6, 1840 – March 15, 1924) was a United States Army officer who founded the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania in 1879, the first federally supported off-reservation Indian boarding school in the United States.

Pratt is known for his assimilationist philosophy, summarized in his 1892 phrase "Kill the Indian in him, and save the man." He believed Native American children should be removed from tribal influences, immersed in Euro-American culture, taught English, Christianity, and vocational skills, and prepared for citizenship.

Early life and military career

Born in Rushford, New York, Pratt enlisted in the Union Army during the Civil War, serving in Indiana cavalry units. After the war he remained in the Army and served on the Southern Plains, where he commanded Buffalo Soldiers and Native American scouts. From 1875 to 1878 he supervised seventy-two Plains Indian prisoners held at Fort Marion in St. Augustine, Florida, an experience that shaped his educational experiment.

Carlisle Indian School

With support from the Secretary of the Interior, Pratt opened Carlisle Barracks as a boarding school. Students included children of Lakota leaders such as Spotted Tail. The school emphasized discipline, military drill, manual labor through the outing system, and suppression of Native languages and customs. Carlisle became a model for a system of Indian boarding schools across the United States and Canada.

Later life and legacy

Pratt was removed as superintendent in 1904 after conflicts with the Bureau of Indian Affairs. He died in San Francisco in 1924 and is buried at Arlington National Cemetery. His legacy is deeply contested: contemporaries praised his belief in Native equality of capacity, while later historians and Native communities have documented the cultural loss, abuse, and deaths associated with the boarding school system he pioneered.
