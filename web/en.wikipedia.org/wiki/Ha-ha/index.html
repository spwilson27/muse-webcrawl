Ha-ha

Ha-ha
TypeRecessed landscape barrier (sunken fence)
OriginFrance (term), popularised in England, early 18th century
PurposeUninterrupted view; excludes livestock from gardens
Noted designersCharles Bridgeman, William Kent, Capability Brown
A ha-ha: the ditch is invisible from the house until the visitor is close

A ha-ha is a recessed landscape design element that creates a vertical barrier — typically a deep, steep-sided ditch, one side of which may be faced with a retaining wall — while providing an uninterrupted view of the landscape beyond. The ha-ha separates a garden from the surrounding parkland or fields and keeps grazing animals out, without the visual interruption of a fence or hedge.

Origin and name

The term originates in French gardens, where the feature was called a saut de loup ("wolf's leap"). The English name is traditionally explained as the exclamation of surprise of someone walking in the landscape who suddenly comes upon the concealed ditch: "Ha! Ha!" The device was described in print by Dezallier d'Argenville in 1709 and was taken up in England by Charles Bridgeman, William Kent and Lancelot "Capability" Brown, becoming a hallmark of the English landscape garden.

Uses

Ha-has were built around country houses and estates across Britain, and later in North America and Australia — George Washington's Mount Vernon has one. The form was also adopted for the boundaries of institutions, notably nineteenth-century psychiatric asylums (such as St Bernard's Hospital in London), where the sunken wall gave patients an open view while maintaining a secure perimeter. Ha-has survive in many historic gardens and are still occasionally built in new landscape designs.
