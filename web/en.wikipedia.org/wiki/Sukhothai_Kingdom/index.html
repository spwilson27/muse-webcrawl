The Sukhothai Kingdom was a post-Angkorian Siamese kingdom in the north-central region of present-day Thailand, lasting from 1238 until 1438, when it was fully absorbed into the Ayutthaya Kingdom. Thai tradition regards Sukhothai as the first true Thai kingdom, and the period is celebrated as a golden age of Thai art, literature, and kingship. Its ruins survive today in the Sukhothai Historical Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Founding

Sukhothai had been an outpost of the Khmer Empire until about 1238, when two Thai chieftains, Pho Khun Bang Klang Hao and Pho Khun Pha Mueang, joined forces to defeat the Khmer governor. Bang Klang Hao was crowned king as Sri Indraditya, founding the Phra Ruang dynasty. The kingdom expanded gradually through a loose mandala system of allied city-states bound by kinship and tribute rather than direct administration.

Ramkhamhaeng

Under Sri Indraditya's son Ramkhamhaeng the Great (r. 1279–1298), Sukhothai reached its greatest extent, claiming suzerainty over a vast area from the Malay Peninsula to Laos. The famous Ramkhamhaeng Inscription of 1292, written in a script traditionally attributed to the king himself and regarded as the earliest surviving example of written Thai, portrays an idyllic, prosperous realm in which the king hung a bell at his palace gate for any subject to ring with a grievance, taxes on trade were abolished, and fish and rice were abundant — "nai nam mi pla, nai na mi khao" ("in the water there is fish, in the fields there is rice"). Modern scholars debate the inscription's authenticity and date, but its image of paternal, benevolent kingship remains central to Thai national identity.

Culture and religion

Sukhothai adopted Theravada Buddhism through contacts with Sri Lanka and the Mon kingdom of Hariphunchai, and the Sukhothai style produced some of the most admired images in Thai art — serene, elegant Buddhas with flame-like ushnishas, including the distinctive "walking Buddha" pose that was a Sukhothai innovation. Wat Mahathat was the spiritual centre of the capital.

Decline

After Ramkhamhaeng's death the kingdom contracted rapidly as tributary states broke away. The rise of Ayutthaya to the south overshadowed Sukhothai; in 1378 it accepted Ayutthayan suzerainty, and in 1438, on the death of its last king, it was formally annexed and administered as a northern province.
