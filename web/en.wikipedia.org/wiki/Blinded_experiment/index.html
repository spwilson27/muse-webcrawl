Blinded experiment

In a blinded experiment, information that could introduce bias is withheld from participants, experimenters, or assessors until the results are recorded. Blinding is a cornerstone of clinical trials and much experimental science: in a single-blind study the subjects do not know which treatment they receive; in a double-blind study neither subjects nor experimenters know; and in a triple-blind study the data analysts are also blinded.

Blinded experiment
PurposePrevent expectation and observer bias
TypesSingle-blind, double-blind, triple-blind
Common withRandomised controlled trials, placebos

Rationale

Knowledge of group assignment can unconsciously change behaviour and reporting — through the placebo effect in patients, differential care by clinicians, and biased outcome assessment. Blinding, usually combined with randomisation and identical-looking placebo or sham treatments, isolates the true effect of an intervention.

History

An early blinded test was the 1784 French royal commission on mesmerism, which included Benjamin Franklin, using blindfolds to show that "animal magnetism" effects followed belief rather than any physical fluid. Double-blind methods became standard in medicine after the Second World War, notably in the 1948 streptomycin trial.

Limitations

Blinding can be impossible (surgery, psychotherapy) or broken by side effects that reveal assignment; trials therefore sometimes test whether participants can guess their group. "Unblinding" procedures exist for emergencies. Reporting guidelines such as CONSORT ask authors to state exactly who was blinded rather than relying on ambiguous labels.
