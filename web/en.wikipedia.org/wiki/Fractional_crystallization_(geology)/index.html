Fractional crystallization (geology)

Fractional crystallization, or crystal fractionation, is one of the most important geochemical and physical processes operating within the Earth's crust and mantle. It is the removal and segregation of mineral precipitates from a melt; because different minerals crystallise at different temperatures, their removal changes the composition of the remaining liquid, driving magmatic differentiation from primitive basaltic magma toward evolved compositions such as andesite and rhyolite.

Process

As magma cools, the first minerals to crystallise — olivine and pyroxene in a basaltic melt, following Bowen's reaction series — are denser than the melt and may sink, or be left behind as the melt migrates. This depletes the liquid in magnesium and iron and enriches it in silica, alkalis and incompatible elements (which do not fit readily into the early crystals, and so concentrate in the residual melt). The complementary solid accumulations are called cumulates, and form layered intrusions such as the Skaergaard and Bushveld complexes, which host major deposits of chromium, platinum-group elements and vanadium.

Modelling

Idealised fractional crystallisation is described by the Rayleigh fractionation equation, which predicts how trace-element concentrations evolve as crystallisation proceeds. Together with partial melting, magma mixing and assimilation, it explains much of the chemical diversity of igneous rocks.
