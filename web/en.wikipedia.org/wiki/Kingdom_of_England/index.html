The Kingdom of England was a sovereign state on the island of Great Britain from its unification in the tenth century — traditionally dated to 12 July 927 under Æthelstan — until 1 May 1707, when the Acts of Union united it with the Kingdom of Scotland to form the Kingdom of Great Britain. At its height under the Angevin kings it formed part of a continental "empire" stretching from Scotland's borders to the Pyrenees, and its institutions — common law, Parliament, and a precociously centralised monarchy — shaped the later British state.

History

Formation and the Anglo-Saxon kingdom

England emerged from the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms (the traditional Heptarchy) under pressure from Viking invasion. Alfred the Great of Wessex (r. 871–899) preserved a Christian English kingdom south of the Danelaw; his children Edward the Elder and Æthelflæd reconquered the Danish-held Midlands, and his grandson Æthelstan was recognised as king of all the English in 927 and overlord of Britain after his victory at Brunanburh (937). The kingdom was completed by the annexation of Northumbria in 954, governed thereafter through shires, royal writs, and a national coinage and taxation (the Danegeld). A second age of Danish conquest brought the Danish king Cnut to the throne (1016–1035), whose North Sea Empire joined England to Denmark and Norway; the Anglo-Saxon line was restored under Edward the Confessor (1042–1066).

Norman and Angevin England

Edward's death without a clear heir produced the succession crisis of 1066, resolved at the Battle of Hastings by William, Duke of Normandy. The Norman Conquest replaced the English aristocracy with a French-speaking one and was documented in the Domesday Book (1086). After the civil war of Stephen's reign (the Anarchy), Henry II (r. 1154–1189) founded the Angevin empire of lands in France, systematised the common law and royal justice, and clashed with Thomas Becket. His son John's loss of Normandy (1204) and misgovernment provoked the barons to impose Magna Carta (1215).

Late medieval England

The thirteenth century saw the growth of Parliament under Henry III and Edward I, who conquered Wales (1282–83) and intervened in Scotland. The Hundred Years' War with France (1337–1453) began under Edward III and saw English victories at Crécy and Agincourt, but ended with the loss of all French possessions except Calais. Dynastic conflict between Lancaster and York (the Wars of the Roses, 1455–1485) ended with the Tudor Henry VII on the throne.

Tudor and Stuart England

Henry VIII broke with Rome (1534), dissolving the monasteries and founding the Church of England; under Elizabeth I England repelled the Spanish Armada (1588), and Wales was legally united with England (1535–1542). In 1603 James VI of Scotland inherited the English crown, joining the two kingdoms in personal union. Conflict between Charles I and Parliament produced the English Civil War (1642–1651), the execution of the king, and the republican Commonwealth under Oliver Cromwell, followed by the Restoration (1660), the Glorious Revolution (1688) and the Bill of Rights (1689) establishing parliamentary supremacy. Queen Anne was the last monarch of the separate kingdom: the Acts of Union 1707 merged England and Scotland into Great Britain, and the English Parliament became the Parliament of Great Britain at Westminster.
