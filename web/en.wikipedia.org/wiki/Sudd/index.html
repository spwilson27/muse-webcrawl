Sudd

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Sudd (from Arabic sadd, "barrier" or "blockage") is a vast swamp in South Sudan, formed by the White Nile (the Bahr al Jabal section) as it spreads out over a nearly flat floodplain. It is one of the world's largest wetlands and one of the largest freshwater ecosystems in Africa, with an area that fluctuates seasonally between roughly 30,000 and over 130,000 square kilometres as floodwaters rise and fall.

Geography and hydrology

The Sudd lies in the low-lying clay plains of the central Nile basin. The White Nile's gradient through the region is extremely gentle, so the river spills into channels, lagoons, and seasonally flooded grasslands choked with papyrus, reeds, and floating vegetation. Great rafts of vegetation — also called "sudd" — break loose and block channels, historically obstructing navigation; nineteenth-century expeditions spent months cutting through them. Roughly half of the water entering the Sudd is lost to evaporation and transpiration, a fact that motivated the Jonglei Canal project, begun in 1978 to bypass the swamp and increase downstream flow, but abandoned in 1984 during Sudan's civil war when the works were attacked.

Ecology

The Sudd supports enormous populations of wildlife, including some of the largest remaining herds of migratory antelopes such as white-eared kob and tiang, along with Nile lechwe, hippopotamuses, elephants, shoebill storks, and vast numbers of waterbirds. It is designated a Ramsar wetland of international importance. Fishing, grazing on flood-retreat grasslands, and papyrus harvesting sustain local Nilotic peoples — Dinka, Nuer, and Shilluk — whose livelihoods are adapted to the flood cycle.

Threats

Proposed revival of canal construction, upstream water development, oil extraction within and near the swamp, and climate change pose ongoing debates about the Sudd's future.
