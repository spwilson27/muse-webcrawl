Vector (epidemiology)

In epidemiology, a vector is any organism that carries and transmits an infectious pathogen to another living organism. Most vectors are arthropods — blood-feeding invertebrates such as mosquitoes, ticks, fleas, lice and biting flies — that ingest disease-producing microorganisms during a blood meal from an infected host and later inject them into a new host during a subsequent meal. Diseases spread this way are called vector-borne diseases, and they account for more than 17 per cent of all infectious diseases in humans, causing over 700,000 deaths annually according to the World Health Organization.

An Anopheles mosquito feeding. Female Anopheles mosquitoes transmit the Plasmodium parasites that cause malaria.

Types of transmission

Vectors may transmit pathogens mechanically or biologically. In mechanical transmission the vector acts as a passive carrier: the pathogen does not develop or multiply inside it, as when houseflies carry enteric bacteria on their feet and mouthparts from faeces to food. In biological transmission the pathogen must pass part of its life cycle inside the vector before it becomes infective — malaria parasites, for example, undergo sexual reproduction inside the mosquito — and there is an incubation period, the extrinsic incubation period, before the vector can transmit.

Vectorial capacity

The intensity with which a vector population transmits disease is summarised by its vectorial capacity, which depends on the density of vectors relative to humans, their biting rate, their daily survival probability, the extrinsic incubation period of the pathogen and the vector's competence — its innate ability to acquire, support and transmit the pathogen. Because survival enters exponentially, small reductions in mosquito longevity, such as those achieved by insecticide-treated bed nets and indoor residual spraying, can sharply reduce transmission.

Control

Vector control is a central element of public health. Methods include eliminating breeding sites and standing water; applying insecticides and larvicides; distributing insecticide-treated bed nets; wearing protective clothing and repellents; and biological control using natural predators or bacteria such as Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis. Newer approaches release mosquitoes infected with the bacterium Wolbachia, which blocks dengue transmission, or genetically modified sterile males to suppress wild populations. Climate change, insecticide resistance and urbanisation are expanding the range of several vectors, bringing diseases such as dengue into previously unaffected regions.
