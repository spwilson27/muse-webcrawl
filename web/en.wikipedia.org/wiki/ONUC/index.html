ONUC

The United Nations Operation in the Congo (French: Opération des Nations Unies au Congo, abbreviated ONUC) was a United Nations peacekeeping force deployed in the newly independent Republic of the Congo from July 1960 to June 1964. Created at the outbreak of the Congo Crisis, it became one of the largest and most consequential — and controversial — operations in UN history, evolving from a neutral interposition force into combat operations to end the secession of Katanga.

Background

The Congo became independent from Belgium on 30 June 1960; within days the army mutinied, Belgian troops intervened, and the mineral-rich province of Katanga seceded under Moïse Tshombe with Belgian support. The Security Council authorized ONUC to secure the withdrawal of Belgian forces and to assist the Congolese government of Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba in maintaining law and order.

Operations

ONUC initially declined to act against Katanga, a neutrality stance that contributed to the rupture between Lumumba and the UN. After Lumumba's dismissal and murder (January 1961), the Security Council authorized the use of force to prevent civil war; ONUC operations (including Rumpunch, Morthor, and Grandslam, 1961–1963) progressively dismantled the Katangese secession, which ended in January 1963. Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld died in a still-disputed plane crash at Ndola in September 1961 while travelling to negotiate a ceasefire with Tshombe. Some 234 UN personnel and many thousands of Congolese died during the operation, which wound down by 30 June 1964.

Legacy

ONUC reshaped peacekeeping doctrine — it was the UN's first large operation involving combat ("peace enforcement") and its first use of air power — and its enormous cost, financed over Soviet and French objections, triggered the Article 19 financing crisis and an advisory opinion of the International Court of Justice on UN expenses.
