Diapause

Diapause, when referencing animal dormancy, is the delay in development in response to regularly and recurring periods of adverse environmental conditions. It is a physiological state with very specific initiating and inhibiting conditions, considered a means of surviving predictable, unfavourable environmental conditions, such as temperature extremes, drought or reduced food availability. Diapause is most often observed in arthropods, especially insects, and in the embryos of many oviparous fish.

Characteristics

Unlike simple quiescence, diapause is hormonally controlled and typically begins before conditions deteriorate, triggered by predictive cues such as changing day length (photoperiod). It is characterised by suppressed metabolism, arrested development and enhanced resistance to cold, heat and desiccation. Diapause can occur at any life stage — egg, larva, pupa or adult — depending on the species, and termination requires specific cues, often a period of chilling followed by warming.

Examples

The monarch butterfly enters a reproductive diapause for its long migration and overwintering. Many temperate insects overwinter as diapausing pupae or eggs; the silkworm's egg diapause is controlled by a specific diapause hormone. Among vertebrates, some annual killifish undergo embryonic diapause to survive dry seasons, and mammals such as roe deer exhibit delayed implantation, a related phenomenon.
