Irish orthography — the spelling system of the Irish language — has a documented history in the Latin script stretching from the Primitive Irish Ogham inscriptions and the Old Irish manuscripts of the early medieval period to the standardised spelling of the modern official standard, An Caighdeán Oifigiúil. Although modern Irish spelling appears daunting to learners, it is considerably more regular than English spelling once its conventions are understood.

Broad and slender consonants

The central feature of Irish spelling is the distinction between "broad" (velarised) and "slender" (palatalised) consonants. Irish spelling signals the quality of a consonant by the vowels written next to it: the vowels e and i mark a neighbouring consonant as slender, while a, o and u mark it broad. This gives the spelling rule caol le caol agus leathan le leathan ("slender with slender and broad with broad"): the vowels on either side of a consonant (or consonant group) must agree in quality. Hence spellings such as bó "cow" versus buí "yellow", and "dummy" vowels written purely to satisfy the rule, as in olive-like sequences in words like fearg "anger".

The alphabet and sounds

The traditional Irish alphabet has 18 letters; j, k, q, v, w, x, y and z appear only in loanwords. Consonant letters are frequently modified by a following h, which in the older Gaelic script was written as a dot above the letter (the ponc séimhithe). These digraphs mark lenition (séimhiú), the grammatical softening of initial consonants: bh and mh are pronounced like v or w, fh is silent, sh and th like h, ch as a fricative, and dh/gh as a voiced velar or glide. Vowel length is marked by the acute accent, the síneadh fada ("long sign"): á é í ó ú.

History and reform

Early Irish was written in the Ogham alphabet along the edges of standing stones, and from the Christian era in Latin letters in manuscripts whose spelling, after the classical standard of Early Modern Irish (c. 1200–1650), broke down into regional variation as the language's literary institutions declined. The Gaelic revival of the late nineteenth century produced competing spellings; Peadar Ua Laoghaire and others wrote in a partly phonetic orthography, while Classical spellings persisted.

The Caighdeán

In the 1940s and 1950s the Irish state introduced an official standard: the spelling reforms published from 1945 simplified many words (e.g. Gaedhilge → Gaeilge, beatha-type vowel digraphs reduced), and the grammar and spelling manual Gramadach na Gaeilge agus Litriú na Gaeilge: An Caighdeán Oifigiúil (1958) codified the system used in official publications and schools. A revised Caighdeán was published in 2017. The reforms remain mildly controversial among some Gaeltacht speakers, whose dialects the standard does not exactly mirror, but they greatly reduced the gap between spelling and pronunciation.
