New Comedy

New Comedy is the final phase of ancient Greek comedy, flourishing roughly from 320 to 263 BC, characterised by domestic plots, stock characters and romantic intrigue rather than political satire.

New Comedy
Period: ~320–263 BC
Chief author: Menander
Stock figures: clever slave, young lovers, stern father, courtesan

Characteristics

Following Old Comedy (Aristophanes) and Middle Comedy, New Comedy turned to everyday Athenian life: love affairs, lost children recognised by tokens, scheming slaves. Plays were in five acts divided by choral interludes, with prologues and sententious maxims.

Menander and influence

Menander (c. 342–290 BC) wrote over 100 plays; Dyskolos survives complete (recovered 1957). Through Roman adaptations by Plautus and Terence, New Comedy shaped European comedy — Shakespeare, Molière and modern sitcoms inherit its plots. See Menander, Old Comedy.
