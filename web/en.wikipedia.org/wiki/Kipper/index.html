Kipper

Kippers are traditionally eaten for breakfast, grilled or poached.

A kipper is a whole herring — or occasionally another oily fish — that has been split in a butterfly fashion from head to tail along the dorsal ridge, gutted, salted or pickled, and cold-smoked over smouldering woodchips, typically oak. The result is a golden-brown, strongly flavoured fish that keeps far longer than fresh herring.

History

Smoking fish to preserve it is ancient, but the modern kipper is often credited to John Woodger of Seahouses in Northumberland in the 1840s, said to have accidentally left herring overnight in a smoky shed. Kippering became a major industry around the fishing ports of Great Yarmouth, the Isle of Man, and Craster in Northumberland, whose kippers remain famous. Manx kippers and Loch Fyne kippers are noted regional varieties.

Preparation

Kippers are usually grilled with butter, poached, or microwaved, and are traditionally served at breakfast with eggs or toast. The word "kipper" can also be used as a verb: fish that has been so treated is "kippered".
