Occitan (endonym: occitan), also known historically as lenga d'òc ("language of òc", from its word for "yes"), is a Romance language spoken in southern France, Monaco, Spain's Val d'Aran and parts of north-western Italy — the region called Occitania. In the Middle Ages it was the prestigious literary language of the troubadours, whose lyric poetry influenced all of European literature.

History

Occitan emerged from Vulgar Latin in the early Middle Ages; the earliest texts date from around the year 1000. In the 12th and 13th centuries troubadour courts made a standardised literary Occitan (koine) the vehicle of courtly love poetry, admired by Dante and Petrarch. The Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229) and the annexation of the south by the French crown began its political decline; the Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539) imposed French in administration, and compulsory French schooling in the 19th–20th centuries — when children were punished for speaking the patois — drove a steep fall in transmission. A 19th-century revival, the Félibrige, led by Frédéric Mistral (Nobel laureate, 1904), restored literary prestige but not daily use.

Dialects and status today

Occitan is usually divided into six dialect groups: Provençal, Languedocien (often taken as the reference variety for standardisation), Gascon (with Aranese in Spain), Limousin, Auvergnat and Vivaro-Alpine. Competing classical and Mistralian spelling norms exist. France gives the language no official status, and UNESCO classifies its varieties as definitely to severely endangered; estimates of fluent speakers range from under 100,000 to around 800,000, with many more understanding it in the south. In Spain, Aranese Occitan is co-official in Catalonia and used in local schooling. Revival efforts include bilingual Calandreta schools, media, and regional cultural policy in Occitanie.

Characteristics

Occitan is closely related to Catalan — the two are sometimes grouped as Occitano-Romance — and shares many features with other southern Romance languages: two-gender nouns, pro-drop verb inflection, retention of final vowels lost in French, and distinctive sounds such as the palatalised outcomes of Latin -ct- (e.g. lach/lèit, "milk", from lacte).
