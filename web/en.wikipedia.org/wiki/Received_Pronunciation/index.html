Received Pronunciation

Received Pronunciation (RP) is the accent traditionally regarded as the standard and most prestigious form of spoken British English. For over a century it has been the prestige accent associated with the British establishment, the BBC, and the public schools, and it is the accent most often taught to foreign learners of English. RP is an accent, not a dialect: it gives no indication of a speaker's regional origin, but traditionally a great deal of information about their social class and education.

The term was coined by the phonetician A. J. Ellis in 1869 ("received" in the sense of "generally accepted"), and was popularised by Daniel Jones, whose English Pronouncing Dictionary (1917) codified it. The BBC adopted RP as its broadcast standard in the 1920s under its first managing director, John Reith, leading to the label "BBC English".

History

RP evolved from the speech of the London court and the universities of Oxford and Cambridge, and was disseminated by the nineteenth-century public schools, the civil service, the armed forces and the Empire. In 1922 the BBC established an Advisory Committee on Spoken English, and announcers were expected to use RP. During and after the Second World War the accent's dominance in broadcasting gradually loosened; regional voices — famously the wartime newsreader Wilfred Pickles — were heard more often, and by the late twentieth century the BBC no longer required RP of its presenters.

Phonology

RP is non-rhotic: the sound /r/ is pronounced only before a vowel, so car is [kɑː] but car engine has an /r/. It maintains the trap–bath split, pronouncing words such as bath, grass and dance with the long vowel /ɑː/ rather than the short /æ/ of Northern English. The traditional vowel inventory counts 12 monophthongs (including /ɪ, e, æ, ʌ, ɒ, ʊ, ə/ and the long /iː, ɑː, ɔː, uː, ɜː/) and eight diphthongs (/eɪ, aɪ, ɔɪ, əʊ, aʊ, ɪə, eə, ʊə/), though the centring diphthongs have increasingly merged toward long monophthongs in modern RP. RP also preserves the distinction between /ʍ/ and /w/ for some speakers (whine vs wine) and traditionally avoids the glottal stop except before syllabic nasals.

Status and change

Phoneticians such as John Wells distinguish mainstream RP from "U-RP" (upper-class) and adoptive RP. Surveys since the 1960s have charted sound changes including t-glottalisation in pre-consonantal positions, l-vocalisation, and the "Estuary English" influence from the Thames Estuary. Although RP speakers are a small minority of the population, the accent continues to carry prestige and remains the usual reference accent in dictionaries and English-language teaching, while attitudes toward it have become more ambivalent and regional accents more widely accepted in public life.
