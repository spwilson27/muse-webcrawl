In classical music, a major third is a musical interval that encompasses three staff positions (see interval number) and spans four semitones (see interval size). The major third (M3) is one of two commonly occurring thirds (the other being the minor third). It is called major because it is the larger of the two: the major third spans four semitones, the minor third three. For example, the interval from C to E is a major third, as the note E lies four semitones above C, and there are three staff positions from C to E.

Harmony

The major third is a foundational interval in Western harmony: it is the interval between the root and the third of a major chord, and gives the major triad its characteristic bright sound as opposed to the minor triad. In just intonation, the major third corresponds to the simple frequency ratio 5:4, known as the just major third or Ptolemaic major third. In twelve-tone equal temperament the major third is 400 cents, about 14 cents wider than the just 5:4 third, a discrepancy historically addressed by meantone temperaments, which tuned thirds close to pure at the expense of fifths.

Melodic examples

Well-known melodies beginning with an ascending major third include "When the Saints Go Marching In" and the opening of Beethoven's spring themes; the descending major third is a common feature of children's songs and doorbell chimes.
