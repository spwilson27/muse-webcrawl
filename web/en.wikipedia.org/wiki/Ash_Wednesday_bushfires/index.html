The Ash Wednesday bushfires, known in South Australia as Ash Wednesday II, were a series of bushfires that occurred in south-eastern Australia on 16 February 1983, which was Ash Wednesday in the Christian calendar. Within twelve hours, more than 180 fires fanned by winds of up to 110 km/h (68 mph) caused widespread destruction across the states of South Australia and Victoria. It was one of Australia's worst bushfire disasters, surpassed in loss of life only by the Black Saturday bushfires of 2009.

Background

Much of south-eastern Australia had suffered a severe drought following a strong El Niño event, and by early 1983 rainfall had been far below average for months. On 16 February a powerful cold front approached, preceded by very hot, dry northerly winds, low humidity and temperatures above 40 °C — conditions that produced extreme fire danger across both states. Many of the fires were ignited by clashing power lines and other electrical faults, as well as by arson and accident.

The fires

In South Australia, fires swept through the Adelaide Hills, the Clare Valley, the south-east of the state and Kangaroo Island's fringes; the towns of Mount Schank and Macclesfield were among those devastated. In Victoria, major fires burned in the Otway Ranges, the Dandenong Ranges and near Cockatoo, Upper Beaconsfield and Belgrave Heights, where many of the Victorian deaths occurred, including firefighters caught by a sudden wind change when the cold front arrived in the evening.

Aftermath

The fires killed 75 people, injured hundreds more, destroyed more than 2,400 homes and killed tens of thousands of livestock. The disaster led to major reviews of fire-warning systems, power-line safety, building standards and firefighting communications in both states, and it remains a reference point in Australian fire-safety education.
