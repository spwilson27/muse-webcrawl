Diogenes Laërtius (Greek: Διογένης Λαέρτιος; fl. 3rd century AD) was a biographer of the Greek philosophers. Nothing is reliably known about his life; his surname may derive from the town of Laërte in Cilicia or from the Roman family name Laërtii. His sole surviving work, the Lives and Opinions of Eminent Philosophers, written in Greek, is the principal surviving source for the history of Greek philosophy from Thales to the Hellenistic schools, and preserves quotations and summaries from hundreds of otherwise lost works.

The Lives

The work is divided into ten books. It begins with the "Ionian" succession — Thales, the Presocratics, Socrates, Plato and the Academy, Aristotle and the Peripatetics — and proceeds in a second "Italian" line through Pythagoras to the Hellenistic schools, ending with the Stoics and a valuable Book 10 devoted entirely to Epicurus, which includes three of Epicurus's own letters and his Principal Doctrines. Each life typically combines biography, anecdotes (often apocryphal or scandalous), a summary of doctrines, lists of writings, and the philosopher's will where available.

Method and value

Diogenes is generally regarded as an uncritical compiler rather than an original thinker: he stitched together material from intermediate sources such as Diocles of Magnesia, Favorinus, Apollodorus's chronology and the anecdotes of Hermippus, frequently without reconciling contradictions. His own philosophical allegiance is debated — he has been claimed for Epicureanism, scepticism and simple eclecticism — and he intersperses his prose with his own slight epigrams on his subjects. Yet because nearly all of his sources are lost, the Lives are indispensable: they preserve, among much else, the fullest accounts of Stoic logic and physics, the doxography of the Presocratics, and the texts of Epicurus.

Reception

The work appears to have been composed in the first half of the third century AD, probably under the Severan dynasty, and addressed to an unnamed female admirer of Plato. It survived into the Byzantine world, was translated into Latin in the fifteenth century (the version of Ambrogio Traversari), and became a staple of Renaissance humanism — Montaigne remarked that he wished there had been a dozen Laërtiuses. Modern scholarship treats Diogenes as a necessary but treacherous witness whose every report ideally requires corroboration.
