Carroccio

A carroccio was a large four-wheeled wagon bearing the city signs (vexilla) around which the army of a medieval Italian city-state gathered and fought. It was particularly common among the armies of Lombard, Tuscan and other Italian cities between the 11th and 14th centuries.

Description

The carroccio was drawn by oxen and carried the city's banner on a tall mast, an altar at which Mass was celebrated before battle, and a bell (the martinella) used to signal commands. It was crewed by a chaplain and trumpeters, and defended by an picked company of soldiers, who were sworn to defend it to the death; its capture was a catastrophic disgrace.

History

The carroccio is traditionally credited to Archbishop Aribert of Milan around 1039. Its most famous appearance was at the Battle of Legnano in 1176, where the infantry of the Lombard League rallied around the Milanese carroccio to defeat the army of Emperor Frederick Barbarossa. A modern replica and the memory of the carroccio feature in the Palio of Legnano. Florence's carroccio was captured by Siena at the Battle of Montaperti in 1260.
