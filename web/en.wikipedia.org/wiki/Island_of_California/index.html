Island of California

The Island of California was a long-standing European cartographic misconception, dating from the 16th century, which depicted California as an island completely separated from the North American mainland by a strait. The error appeared on many maps during the 17th and early 18th centuries before being corrected.

A circa 1650 map depicting California as an island.

Origins

The idea drew partly on the 1510 Spanish novel Las sergas de Esplandián by Garci Rodríguez de Montalvo, which described an island called California ruled by Queen Calafia. Early explorers initially recognised California as a peninsula — Baja California was correctly shown as one on maps around 1600 — but reports associated with the Carmelite friar Antonio de la Ascensión, from the 1602 expedition of Sebastián Vizcaíno, helped spread the island theory.

Correction

The Dutch mapmaker Joan Sanson popularised the island depiction from 1657. Doubts grew after the overland journeys of the Jesuit Eusebio Francisco Kino (1698–1701), whose maps showed California as a peninsula. The error nonetheless persisted on some maps until King Ferdinand VI of Spain issued a decree in 1747 declaring that California was not an island.
