Goldbach's conjecture

Goldbach's conjecture is one of the oldest and best-known unsolved problems in number theory and all of mathematics. It states that every even natural number greater than 2 is the sum of two prime numbers.

The conjecture was proposed on 7 June 1742 in a letter from the Prussian mathematician Christian Goldbach to Leonhard Euler. The original formulation concerned odd integers as sums of three primes; the modern "strong" Goldbach conjecture, as reformulated by Euler, concerns even numbers as sums of two primes. It has been verified computationally for all even numbers up to at least 4 × 1018, but no general proof has been found.

Related results

The "weak" Goldbach conjecture — that every odd number greater than 5 is the sum of three odd primes — was proved by Harald Helfgott in 2013. Major partial results toward the strong conjecture include Chen Jingrun's theorem (1966), which shows that every sufficiently large even number is the sum of a prime and a number with at most two prime factors, and results showing that even numbers are sums of at most four primes (Olivier Ramaré, 1995).

Heuristic justification

Probabilistic arguments based on the prime number theorem suggest that the number of ways to write a large even number as a sum of two primes grows roughly like n / (log n)2, making the conjecture overwhelmingly plausible, though such counting heuristics do not constitute a proof.
