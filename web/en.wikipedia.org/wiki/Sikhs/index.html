Sikhs (Punjabi: ਸਿੱਖ, sikkh, "disciple" or "learner") are the adherents of Sikhism, a monotheistic religion founded in the Punjab region of South Asia in the late 15th century by Guru Nanak and developed by nine successor Gurus. There are roughly 25–30 million Sikhs worldwide, making Sikhism one of the world's largest religions; the great majority live in the Indian state of Punjab, with major diaspora communities in Canada, the United Kingdom, the United States and Australia.

Beliefs and practices

Sikhs worship the one timeless God (Ik Onkar) and follow the teachings recorded in the Guru Granth Sahib, the scripture regarded as the eternal Guru. Core practices include honest work (kirat karni), sharing with others (vand chhakna), remembrance of God (nam japna), and selfless service (seva), exemplified by the langar, the free community kitchen in every gurdwara. Initiated Sikhs of the Khalsa, founded by Guru Gobind Singh in 1699, keep the Five Ks, including uncut hair (kesh), often covered by a turban.

History

Sikhism arose amid the devotional bhakti movement and the rule of the Mughal Empire. The execution of Guru Arjan (1606) and Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675) by Mughal authorities pushed the community toward militarisation; Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa and the ideal of the saint-soldier. In the early 19th century Maharaja Ranjit Singh built a Sikh empire centred on Lahore, annexed by Britain in 1849. Sikhs served in large numbers in the British Indian Army. The Partition of 1947 divided Punjab between India and Pakistan amid mass violence and migration. In India, Sikhs formed the majority in the reorganised Punjab state of 1966.

Identity and diaspora

The turban and uncut hair make Sikh men visually distinctive, which has at times led to discrimination, especially after the events of 1984 in India and after 9/11 in the West. Sikh communities are known for disaster relief and charitable service worldwide through organisations such as Khalsa Aid.
