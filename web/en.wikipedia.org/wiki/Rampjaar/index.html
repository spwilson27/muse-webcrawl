Rampjaar

The Rampjaar (Dutch for "Disaster Year") was the year 1672 in Dutch history, when the Dutch Republic was simultaneously attacked by France, England, and the prince-bishops of Münster and Cologne during the Franco-Dutch War and the Third Anglo-Dutch War. The near-collapse of the Republic led to the fall and lynching of the leading statesman Johan de Witt and the rise of William III of Orange as stadtholder.

The French invasion of 1672 nearly destroyed the Dutch Republic.

Events

In the spring of 1672, Louis XIV of France invaded the Republic with an army of over 100,000 men, bypassing the Spanish Netherlands and rapidly overrunning the provinces of Gelderland, Overijssel and Utrecht. England declared war at sea, and the armies of Münster and Cologne attacked from the east. The Dutch defence collapsed; only the province of Holland, protected by the Dutch Waterline — a deliberate inundation of polders ordered by the States of Holland — held out, saving Amsterdam.

Panic gripped the Republic. The regime of Grand Pensionary Johan de Witt, who had governed during the "First Stadtholderless Period", was blamed for neglecting the army. De Witt resigned in August. On 20 August 1672, Johan de Witt and his brother Cornelis were murdered by an Orangist mob in The Hague and their bodies mutilated. William III of Orange, appointed stadtholder in July, took power; many regents were purged from city councils (the wetsverzetting).

Aftermath

Despite the disaster, the Republic survived. Michiel de Ruyter's fleet defeated the combined Anglo-French fleets, the Waterline held, and by 1673 French troops withdrew from most occupied territory. The Franco-Dutch War ended with the Treaties of Nijmegen (1678). The Rampjaar remains a byword in Dutch for national catastrophe; a contemporary saying described the Dutch people as redeloos (irrational), the government radeloos (desperate) and the country reddeloos (beyond saving).
