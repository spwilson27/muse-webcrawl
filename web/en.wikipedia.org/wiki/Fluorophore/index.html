Fluorophore

A fluorophore (or fluorochrome, from Greek phōs-phoros, "light-bearing") is a chemical compound or molecular group that can re-emit light upon excitation by light of a shorter wavelength — the phenomenon of fluorescence. Fluorophores absorb photons at a characteristic excitation wavelength, and after a brief interval in an excited electronic state (typically nanoseconds), emit photons at a longer, characteristic emission wavelength; the difference is known as the Stokes shift.

PhenomenonFluorescence
ExamplesFluorescein, rhodamine, GFP, cyanine dyes
ApplicationsMicroscopy, labeling, sensors

Properties

A fluorophore's usefulness is characterized by its molar extinction coefficient (how strongly it absorbs), its quantum yield (the fraction of absorbed photons re-emitted), its photostability, and its fluorescence lifetime. Most organic fluorophores contain conjugated aromatic ring systems; common families include fluoresceins, rhodamines, coumarins, BODIPY dyes, and cyanines (Cy3, Cy5). Fluorescent proteins such as green fluorescent protein (GFP) and its engineered color variants are genetically encoded fluorophores. Fluorescence can be switched off by quenching through contact with another molecule or by energy transfer (FRET) to an acceptor fluorophore.

Applications

Fluorophores are indispensable labels in biology and medicine: they are attached to antibodies for immunofluorescence, to probes for fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and to nucleotides for DNA sequencing. They underpin fluorescence microscopy, flow cytometry, and a wide range of biosensors and assays.
