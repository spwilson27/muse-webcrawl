Traditional African religions

The traditional African religions, or African indigenous religions, are the ethnic religions of Africans. They are a set of highly diverse beliefs that include various ethnic religions. Generally, these traditions are oral rather than scriptural and are passed down from one generation to another through stories, songs and festivals. They include belief in a supreme creator god, belief in spirits, veneration of the ancestors, use of magic and traditional African medicine.

Characteristics

Most African societies with a notion of a single creator god also believe in a pantheon of divinities or spirits, who between them cover various aspects of life. There are recurring similarities: a belief in ancestral spirits, sacred kingship, healing practices, divination systems such as Ifá among the Yoruba, and ritual specialists who mediate between the human and spiritual worlds.

Traditional African religions were suppressed in some areas during the colonial era, and are sometimes practised alongside Christianity and Islam; in many communities, syncretic combinations are common. Adherents are estimated to number in the tens of millions, although precise figures are difficult because of this layering of practice.

Regional traditions

Well-known systems include the Yoruba religion (with its orisha pantheon), the Vodun of Benin and Togo — the source of Haitian Vodou and related diaspora religions — Akan religion in Ghana, Odinani among the Igbo, and the Dinka and Nuer religions of South Sudan. In southern Africa, the ancestors, known as amadlozi among Zulu speakers, occupy a central place.

Role of ancestors

Ancestor veneration is a near-universal feature: the dead are believed to retain an interest in the affairs of their descendants, who offer food, drink and prayer at family shrines. Specialists — diviners, healers and priests — diagnose misfortune, often attributed to neglected ancestors or spiritual imbalance, and prescribe sacrifices or remedies.

Modern situation

Traditional religion remains influential across the continent and in the African diaspora, where it shaped Santería, Candomblé and Vodou. Some countries, notably Benin, officially recognise Vodun, which has a national holiday. Scholars stress that these traditions are living, adaptive systems rather than survivals of a static past.
