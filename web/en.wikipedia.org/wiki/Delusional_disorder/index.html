Delusional disorder

Delusional disorder is a mental disorder in which a person has one or more non-bizarre delusions — fixed false beliefs about situations that could occur in real life — for at least one month, while general functioning is otherwise largely unimpaired and behaviour is not obviously odd apart from the delusion's consequences. Unlike schizophrenia, there are no prominent hallucinations, disorganised speech, or negative symptoms.

Subtypes

The DSM-5 classifies delusional disorder by the predominant delusional theme: erotomanic, grandiose, jealous, persecutory, somatic, mixed, and unspecified. The somatic type includes delusional parasitosis, the belief of being infested with parasites.

Diagnosis and course

Diagnosis requires excluding other medical conditions, substance effects, and other psychotic disorders. Onset is typically in middle or later adult life. The condition is relatively uncommon, with an estimated lifetime prevalence of around 0.2%. Because insight is usually poor, sufferers rarely seek treatment voluntarily.

Treatment

Treatment usually involves antipsychotic medication, though evidence is largely from case series, and supportive psychotherapy or cognitive behavioural therapy aimed at reducing distress and testing beliefs. Establishing a therapeutic alliance is often the most difficult step.
