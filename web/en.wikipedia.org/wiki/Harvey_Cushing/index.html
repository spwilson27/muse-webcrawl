Harvey Cushing

Harvey Williams Cushing Born: 8 April 1869, Cleveland, Ohio
Died: 7 October 1939, New Haven, Connecticut
Known for: Founding neurosurgery; Cushing's disease

Harvey Williams Cushing (April 8, 1869 – October 7, 1939) was an American neurosurgeon, pathologist, writer, and draftsman. Widely regarded as the founder of modern neurosurgery, he pioneered brain surgery techniques that dramatically reduced its mortality, and was the first to describe the endocrine disorder now known as Cushing's disease.

Harvey Cushing, pioneer of modern brain surgery.

Career

Cushing studied at Yale University and Harvard Medical School, then trained in surgery at Massachusetts General Hospital and under William Stewart Halsted at Johns Hopkins Hospital. After study in Europe, he returned to Johns Hopkins, where he built a surgical practice devoted increasingly to the nervous system. In 1912 he became surgeon-in-chief at the Peter Bent Brigham Hospital in Boston and professor of surgery at Harvard. During the First World War he served with the American Expeditionary Forces in France, treating head wounds. He later moved to Yale, where he spent his final years assembling his famed brain tumour registry and library.

Contributions

Cushing developed meticulous operative techniques — careful haemostasis, the use of silver clips, electrosurgical methods with William Bovie, and rigorous blood-pressure monitoring — that cut the mortality of brain tumour surgery from over 50 percent to around 10 percent. In 1912 he described the syndrome of pituitary basophilism, the basis of Cushing's disease. His biography of William Osler won the Pulitzer Prize in 1926. He trained a generation of neurosurgeons and co-founded what became the Harvey Cushing/John Hay Whitney Medical Library at Yale.
