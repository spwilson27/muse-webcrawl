Molniya orbit

A Molniya orbit (Russian: Молния, "lightning") is a type of highly elliptical orbit designed to provide communications and remote sensing coverage over high latitudes. It has an inclination of 63.4 degrees, an argument of perigee of 270 degrees, and an orbital period of approximately half a sidereal day (about 11 hours 58 minutes).

Characteristics

The orbit's high eccentricity (about 0.72) gives it an apogee of roughly 39,700 km over the northern hemisphere and a perigee of only a few hundred kilometres over the southern hemisphere. By Kepler's second law, the satellite moves very slowly near apogee, spending about eight hours of each revolution within line of sight of high-latitude ground stations — long "dwell" periods ideal for serving regions such as Russia and Canada, which geostationary satellites above the equator serve poorly. The inclination of 63.4° is the "critical inclination" at which the argument of perigee does not drift under the perturbation caused by Earth's oblateness (J2), keeping the apogee fixed over the desired hemisphere.

History and use

The orbit is named after the Soviet Molniya communications satellites, first launched in 1965, which used it to relay television and military communications across the USSR. Typically a constellation of three satellites, phased eight hours apart, provides continuous coverage. Molniya orbits have also been used by Soviet/Russian early-warning satellites (Oko) and by the American SDS relay satellites. Disadvantages include long passages through the Van Allen radiation belts and the need for steerable ground antennas or satellite handovers.
