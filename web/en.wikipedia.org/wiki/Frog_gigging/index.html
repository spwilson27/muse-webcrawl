Frog gigging

Frog gigging is the practice of hunting frogs at night with a multiple-pronged spear called a gig. The technique is most commonly used to harvest the American bullfrog (Lithobates catesbeianus), whose large hind legs are eaten as a delicacy. Hunters typically move slowly along the banks of ponds, bayous, swamps or canals in a flat-bottomed boat or on foot, shining a bright light into the frogs' eyes, which both reveals their location — frog eyes reflect light — and holds them still long enough to be speared.

Equipment and technique

The gig is a small multi-tined fork, usually with three to five barbed prongs, mounted on a pole ranging from about eight to sixteen feet long. A successful thrust pins the frog, which is then pulled off into a sack or cooler. In some traditions frogs are grabbed by hand instead ("frog grabbing"), and airboats are used in parts of Florida and Louisiana. Frogs are most active on warm, humid nights, and the croaking of males helps hunters locate them.

Regulation and culture

Frog gigging is a longstanding rural tradition in the southern and midwestern United States, particularly in Louisiana, Arkansas, Missouri, Kentucky and Oklahoma. Most states regulate it as a form of hunting or fishing, requiring a licence and imposing seasons and bag limits; some jurisdictions prohibit taking frogs with firearms or during breeding season. Cooked frog legs are typically breaded and fried and are said to taste like a cross between chicken and fish.
