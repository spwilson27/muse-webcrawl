Royal National Theatre

The National Theatre on the South Bank, London

The Royal National Theatre, commonly known as the National Theatre, is a publicly funded performing arts venue and theatre company in London. It is based in a landmark concrete building on the South Bank of the River Thames, designed by Denys Lasdun and opened in 1976.

History

Proposals for a British national theatre date from the 19th century. The company was finally established in 1963, opening at the Old Vic under its first artistic director, Laurence Olivier, with a production of Hamlet starring Peter O'Toole. The company moved to its purpose-built South Bank home in 1976–77. In 1988 it received the title "Royal", which it uses formally but rarely in branding.

The building

The Lasdun building contains three auditoria: the Olivier Theatre (a large open-stage amphitheatre named after the first director), the Lyttelton Theatre (a conventional proscenium-arch stage) and the Dorfman Theatre (a flexible studio space, formerly the Cottesloe). The building's layered concrete terraces are a celebrated example of Brutalist architecture and are Grade II* listed.

Work

The National stages a wide repertoire of classics, new plays and musicals, and its productions frequently transfer to the West End and Broadway; successful examples include War Horse, The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-Time and One Man, Two Guvnors. The NT Live programme broadcasts productions to cinemas worldwide.
