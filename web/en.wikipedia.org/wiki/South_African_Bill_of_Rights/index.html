South African Bill of Rights

The Bill of Rights is a human rights charter contained in Chapter 2 of the Constitution of South Africa, which came into force on 4 February 1997. It is considered a cornerstone of democracy in South Africa and is sometimes described as one of the most progressive bills of rights in the world.

History

South Africa's rights tradition stretches back to the 1923 "Bill of Rights" drafted by the African National Congress and the Freedom Charter of 1955. During the negotiated transition from apartheid, an interim constitution (1994) introduced the first justiciable bill of rights; the Constitutional Assembly then drafted the final text, which the Constitutional Court certified in 1996.

Contents

The Bill of Rights (sections 7–39) guarantees equality before the law (s 9), human dignity (s 10), life (s 11), freedom and security of the person (s 12), privacy (s 14), freedom of religion, belief and opinion (s 15), freedom of expression (s 16), freedom of association (s 18), political rights (s 19), and citizenship (s 20). Unusually, it also entrenches socio-economic rights, including rights to housing (s 26), health care, food, water and social security (s 27), and education (s 29). Section 8 applies the Bill of Rights to private persons as well as the state in appropriate cases, and section 36 contains a general limitation clause.

Enforcement

The Constitutional Court is the apex court for constitutional matters. Landmark cases applying the Bill of Rights include S v Makwanyane (1995), which abolished the death penalty, and Government of the Republic of South Africa v Grootboom (2000) on the right to housing.
