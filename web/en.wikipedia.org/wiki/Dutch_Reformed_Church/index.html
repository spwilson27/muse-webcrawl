Dutch Reformed Church

The Dutch Reformed Church (Dutch: Nederlandse Hervormde Kerk, abbreviated NHK) was the largest Christian denomination in the Netherlands from the onset of the Protestant Reformation in the 16th century until 1930, when it was surpassed in membership by the Roman Catholic Church. It spread rapidly during the Dutch Revolt against Spanish rule and became the public church of the Dutch Republic, though membership was never compulsory.

The church developed according to the theological traditions developed by John Calvin in Geneva and inherited from Swiss Reformation figures such as Huldrych Zwingli and Heinrich Bullinger. Its confessional standards included the Belgic Confession (1561), the Heidelberg Catechism (1563), and the Canons of Dort (1619), often called the Three Forms of Unity.

History

Reformed congregations formed in the Low Countries under persecution in the 1550s and 1560s. The first national synod met at Emden in 1571. After the Dutch Revolt and the establishment of the Republic of the Seven United Netherlands, the Reformed Church became the privileged public church, and its ministers were paid by the state.

The Arminian controversy led to the Synod of Dort (1618–1619), which condemned Arminianism and expelled Remonstrant ministers. In the 19th century, secessions in 1834 (the Afscheiding) and 1886 (the Doleantie, led by Abraham Kuyper) created separate Reformed denominations.

Merger

On 1 May 2004, the Dutch Reformed Church merged with the Reformed Churches in the Netherlands and the Evangelical Lutheran Church in the Kingdom of the Netherlands to form the Protestant Church in the Netherlands. Some congregations that declined to join formed the Restored Reformed Church.
