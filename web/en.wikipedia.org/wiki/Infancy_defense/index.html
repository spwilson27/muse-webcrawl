Infancy defense

The infancy defense is a form of legal defense in which the defendant claims that, because of their young age, they are not subject to the criminal law, or that their contracts are not enforceable. The underlying rationale is that children lack the maturity and judgement to understand the consequences of their actions and agreements.

Age thresholds for responsibility vary between jurisdictions.

Criminal law

At common law, a child under seven was conclusively presumed incapable of forming criminal intent (doli incapax); between seven and fourteen the presumption was rebuttable, weakening as the child grew older. Modern statutes generally set a minimum age of criminal responsibility — ten in England and Wales, for example — below which a child cannot be charged with an offence at all, and channel older children into separate youth justice systems. Many jurisdictions have abolished the rebuttable presumption for older children, though developmental capacity may still be litigated.

Contract law

In contract law, minors generally lack capacity to contract: agreements they make are usually voidable at the minor's option (subject to exceptions for necessaries and, in some places, employment and education contracts), a doctrine also called the infancy doctrine. A minor who disaffirms a contract must generally return any remaining consideration.
