Isaac is one of the three patriarchs of the Israelites and an important figure in the Abrahamic religions, including Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Isaac first appears in the Torah, in which he is the son of Abraham and Sarah, the father of Jacob and Esau, and the grandfather of the twelve tribes of Israel.

Isaac's name means "he laughs", reflecting the laughter, in disbelief, of Abraham and Sarah, when told by God that they would have a child in old age. According to the narrative, God promised Abraham that through his offspring all the nations of the earth will come to be blessed, and Isaac is the first of that promised line.

Biblical narrative

Birth and childhood

Genesis recounts that Sarah, long barren, bore Isaac when Abraham was 100 years old. On the eighth day Isaac was circumcised, and at his weaning feast his half-brother Ishmael was sent away with Hagar. Some time later comes the Binding of Isaac (the Akedah): God commands Abraham to offer Isaac as a burnt offering on Mount Moriah. Abraham binds his son and raises the knife, but an angel stops him at the last moment, and a ram caught in a thicket is sacrificed instead.

Marriage and family

Abraham's servant is sent to Mesopotamia to find Isaac a wife from among his kin; he returns with Rebecca, whom Isaac marries. Rebecca, initially barren, bears twin sons, Esau and Jacob. Isaac, aged and blind, intends to bestow his blessing on the elder son Esau, but Jacob, aided by Rebecca, deceives his father and receives the blessing — a turning point in the patriarchal story.

Later life

Isaac lives in the Negev and around Beersheba and Gerar, re-digging Abraham's wells and contending with the Philistines over water. Genesis gives his age at death as 180 years; he is buried by Esau and Jacob in the Cave of Machpelah.

In Islam

In Islam, Isaac (Ishaq) is recognized as a prophet and the son of Ibrahim (Abraham) and father of Yaqub (Jacob). Islamic tradition differs from the biblical account in elements of the near-sacrifice narrative: many Muslim commentators hold that the son Abraham was commanded to sacrifice was Ishmael (Ismail), though others identify Isaac.

Interpretation

The Binding of Isaac has generated immense theological, philosophical, and artistic commentary — from rabbinic midrash and Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling to countless paintings by Caravaggio, Rembrandt, and others. Biblical scholars generally date the composition of the Genesis narratives to the first millennium BCE, drawing on earlier oral traditions.
