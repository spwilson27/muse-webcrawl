Rates (tax)

Rates are a form of property tax levied by local authorities, historically in the United Kingdom and in several Commonwealth countries. The charge was based on the notional rental value of a property — known as its rateable value — and funded local services such as roads, schools, policing, and poor relief.

History in the United Kingdom

The rates system in England and Wales originated in the Poor Relief Act of 1601, which empowered parishes to levy a rate on occupiers of property to support the poor. Over the following centuries the system expanded to fund a wide range of municipal services, administered by local councils and assessed on the annual rental value of land and buildings.

Abolition and replacement

Domestic rates were widely criticized as unfair, since they took little account of the number of occupants or ability to pay. The Conservative government of Margaret Thatcher replaced them in Scotland in 1989 and in England and Wales in 1990 with the Community Charge (the "poll tax"), whose unpopularity led in turn to its replacement by the Council Tax in 1993 — a banded property tax. Business rates (non-domestic rates) continue to be levied on commercial properties in the UK.

Elsewhere

Rates remain a standard feature of local government finance in countries including Australia, New Zealand, Ireland (commercial rates), and South Africa, where municipal rates on property owners fund local services.
