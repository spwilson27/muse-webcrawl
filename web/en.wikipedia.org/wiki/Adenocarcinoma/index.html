Adenocarcinoma is a type of cancerous tumour (a carcinoma) that arises from glandular epithelial tissue — tissue whose normal function is to secrete substances such as mucus, digestive juices or hormones. Adenocarcinomas can develop in many organs and are among the most common forms of cancer worldwide; they include the most common type of lung cancer in non-smokers, nearly all colorectal and pancreatic cancers, and large proportions of prostate, breast and gastric cancers.

Pathology

Under the microscope, adenocarcinoma cells form gland-like structures or produce mucin, features that distinguish them from squamous cell carcinomas and other tumour types. Pathologists grade tumours by how closely the glands resemble normal tissue (well, moderately or poorly differentiated) and stage them by the TNM system. Immunohistochemistry and molecular profiling — for example testing lung adenocarcinomas for mutations in EGFR, rearrangements in ALK and ROS1, and expression of PD-L1 — guide modern treatment.

Causes and risk factors

Risk factors depend on the site of origin. Tobacco smoking is the dominant cause of lung adenocarcinoma, the commonest lung cancer subtype overall. Colorectal adenocarcinoma usually develops from adenomatous polyps along the adenoma–carcinoma sequence and is associated with APC and KRAS mutations. Chronic gastro-oesophageal reflux and Barrett's oesophagus predispose to oesophageal adenocarcinoma, whose incidence has risen sharply in Western countries.

Treatment

Management typically combines surgery for localised disease with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy against driver mutations, and immunotherapy, tailored to the organ of origin and the tumour's molecular features.
