Financial Stability Board

The Financial Stability Board (FSB) is an international body that monitors and makes recommendations about the global financial system. It was established by the G20 in April 2009, in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, as the successor to the Financial Stability Forum, and is headquartered at the Bank for International Settlements in Basel, Switzerland. The FSB coordinates the work of national financial authorities and international standard-setting bodies to promote global financial stability.

History

The Financial Stability Forum was founded in 1999 by G7 finance ministers and central bank governors, on the recommendation of Hans Tietmeyer, to promote international financial stability after the Asian financial crisis. At the London G20 summit of April 2009, leaders expanded the forum's membership to all G20 countries, strengthened its mandate and renamed it the Financial Stability Board. In 2013 the FSB was constituted as a Swiss association with legal personality, giving it a more permanent footing.

Mandate and structure

The FSB assesses vulnerabilities in the financial system, promotes coordination and information exchange among authorities, monitors implementation of agreed reforms through peer reviews, and advises on regulatory policy. Its plenary of national authorities and international bodies is its decision-making organ; a chair — who is typically a sitting central bank governor — serves a three-year term. Past chairs include Mario Draghi (2009–2011), Mark Carney (2011–2018) and Randal Quarles (2018–2021), followed by Klaas Knot.

Key work

The FSB's best-known output is the framework for global systemically important banks (G-SIBs): it publishes the annual list of banks whose failure could threaten the system, which are subject to higher capital surcharges and resolution planning. Related work covers "too big to fail" reform, total loss-absorbing capacity, shadow banking (non-bank financial intermediation), over-the-counter derivatives reform, compensation principles, and, more recently, climate-related financial risk, crypto-assets and cross-border payments. The FSB has no binding legal authority; its recommendations take effect through members' domestic implementation and peer pressure.

Membership

Members include the central banks, treasuries and regulators of the G20 jurisdictions, plus additional financial centres such as Hong Kong, Singapore, Spain, the Netherlands and Switzerland, together with international organisations (IMF, World Bank, OECD, BIS) and standard setters such as the Basel Committee and IOSCO. The forum's expansion from the narrower G8-era grouping to the G20 reflected the crisis-era shift in global economic governance toward emerging markets, a shift also institutionalised in the New Development Bank.
