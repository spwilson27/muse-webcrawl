Étienne de La Boétie (1 November 1530 – 18 August 1563) was a French judge, writer, and political philosopher, best known for the Discourse on Voluntary Servitude (Discours de la servitude volontaire), written when he was about eighteen, and for his intimate friendship with Michel de Montaigne, celebrated in Montaigne's essay "Of Friendship".

Life

La Boétie was born at Sarlat in the Périgord and studied law at the University of Orléans, graduating in 1553. He became a counsellor at the Parlement of Bordeaux, served on royal diplomatic missions, and wrote poetry and translations from Plutarch and Xenophon. He died of dysentery in 1563, aged 32, with Montaigne at his bedside; Montaigne published La Boétie's poems, translations, and correspondence, though he withheld the Discourse from his planned memorial volume.

The Discourse on Voluntary Servitude

Written around 1548 and circulated in manuscript, the Discourse (published posthumously in 1576, in part by Huguenot editors) asks why millions obey a single tyrant when their power derives entirely from the consent of the governed: servitude is "voluntary", sustained by custom and habit, and would collapse if the people simply withdrew their support — "Resolve to serve no more, and you are at once freed." The essay has been claimed as a founding text of civil disobedience and anarchist thought, influencing later writers from Rousseau and Thoreau to Tolstoy and twentieth-century theorists of nonviolent resistance.

Friendship with Montaigne

La Boétie and Montaigne met at the Parlement of Bordeaux around 1557 and formed an intense friendship, which Montaigne described as a fusion of souls: "Because it was he, because it was I." Montaigne's essay "Of Friendship" (Essais, I:28) remains the most famous tribute to their bond and to La Boétie's memory.
