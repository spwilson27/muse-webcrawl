Drying oil

A drying oil is an oil that hardens to a tough, solid film after a period of exposure to air. The oil hardens through a chemical reaction in which the components crosslink (and hence, polymerize) by the action of oxygen. Drying oils are a key component of oil paint and some varnishes.

Chemistry

The "drying" process is in fact an exothermic free-radical polymerization: oxygen attacks the unsaturated fatty acids, particularly the doubly allylic positions of linolenic and linoleic acid, forming hydroperoxides that decompose and crosslink the triglyceride chains into a solid network. Oils are classified by iodine number: drying oils have iodine numbers above roughly 140, semi-drying oils between about 125 and 140, and non-drying oils below that.

Examples

Common drying oils include linseed (flaxseed) oil, tung oil, poppyseed oil, perilla oil and walnut oil. Metallic soaps of cobalt, manganese or iron ("driers" or siccatives) are added to paints and finishes to catalyse the process. Boiled linseed oil is linseed oil processed with driers so it cures faster.

Applications and hazards

Drying oils are used in oil paints, putty, wood finishes, linoleum and printing inks. Because oxidation is exothermic, rags soaked in drying oil can heat up and spontaneously combust if piled up; they should be stored in sealed metal containers or spread flat to dry.
