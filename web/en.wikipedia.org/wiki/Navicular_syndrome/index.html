SpeciesHorse
Affected areaNavicular bone and surrounding structures of the hoof
SignsIntermittent forelimb lameness, shortened stride

Navicular syndrome, also called navicular disease or podotrochlosis, is a common cause of chronic forelimb lameness in horses. The term encompasses a group of related conditions affecting the navicular bone (distal sesamoid bone) and the structures around it within the hoof, including the deep digital flexor tendon, the navicular bursa, and the supporting ligaments.

Signs and diagnosis

Affected horses typically show intermittent, low-grade lameness in one or both front feet, a short, choppy stride, and a tendency to land toe-first. Lameness often worsens on hard ground or when the horse works in circles. Diagnosis combines clinical examination, nerve blocks, radiography, and increasingly magnetic resonance imaging, which can reveal soft-tissue lesions invisible on radiographs.

Causes and treatment

The syndrome is multifactorial: conformation (such as upright pasterns and small feet), breed predisposition (Quarter Horses, Thoroughbreds, and Warmbloods are over-represented), and biomechanical stress all contribute. There is no cure, but management includes corrective shoeing to support the heel and ease breakover, controlled exercise, and medications such as bisphosphonates. In refractory cases, palmar digital neurectomy may be performed as a salvage procedure.
