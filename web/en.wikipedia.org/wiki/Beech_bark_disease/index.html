Beech bark disease

Beech bark disease is a disease complex affecting beech trees (Fagus species), caused by the interaction of an invasive scale insect and fungal pathogens. The beech scale insect (Cryptococcus fagisuga) feeds through the bark, creating wounds that allow fungi — principally species of Neonectria — to infect the tree, producing cankers, deforming the trunk, and often killing the tree.

History and spread

The scale insect, native to Europe, was accidentally introduced to Nova Scotia around 1890 with ornamental European beech. The disease was first noticed in North America in the 1920s and has since spread through the range of the American beech (Fagus grandifolia) in eastern Canada and the United States, moving steadily southward and westward. In Europe the disease is generally less destructive, as European beech and its associated fungi have a longer shared history.

Disease cycle and symptoms

Infested trees develop white, woolly wax secreted by feeding scales on the bark. Fungal infection follows, producing reddish-brown fruiting bodies, sunken cankers, and rough, pitted bark. Crowns thin as the tree declines, and trunks weakened by decay may snap in wind — a phenomenon known as "beech snap." Stands pass through recognised stages: an advancing front of heavy scale infestation, a killing front of peak mortality, and an aftermath forest of surviving, often deformed, trees. A small proportion of American beech — roughly one to five percent — shows natural resistance to the scale insect.

Management

Control measures include removing heavily infested trees, applying insecticides or horticultural oils to high-value trees, and scrubbing scale from ornamental specimens. Foresters increasingly retain and propagate resistant trees, and researchers are breeding and selecting for resistance to secure the future of beech as a source of timber, wildlife mast, and forest cover.
