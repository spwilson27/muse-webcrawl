Sleep tracking

Sleep tracking is the process of monitoring a person's sleep, usually by means of a wearable device or a smartphone application that estimates sleep duration and quality. Consumer sleep trackers use actigraphy — movement measured by an accelerometer — combined in newer devices with heart rate, heart-rate variability, blood oxygen, skin temperature, and respiratory rate to infer sleep stages (light, deep, and REM).

Dedicated markers of the field include early devices such as the Zeo headband, followed by mainstream wearables from Fitbit, Apple Watch, Garmin, Oura Ring, and Whoop. Mattress-based sensors and phone apps that use the microphone and sonar-like signals also exist.

Accuracy

The gold standard for measuring sleep is laboratory polysomnography (PSG). Validation studies generally find that consumer wearables detect sleep versus wake reasonably well but classify individual sleep stages only moderately, tending to overestimate total sleep time and deep sleep. Clinicians caution that tracker data is not diagnostic for sleep disorders.

Effects on users

Sleep tracking is part of the "quantified self" movement. While it can encourage regular bedtimes, sleep physicians have described orthosomnia — anxiety and insomnia driven by the pursuit of perfect tracker scores — in some users preoccupied with their data.
