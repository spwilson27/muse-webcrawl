Champion tree

A champion tree is the largest known specimen of a particular tree species, typically determined by a scoring system combining trunk circumference, height, and crown spread. Registers of champion trees are maintained by forestry and conservation organisations in many countries, both to celebrate remarkable individual trees and to record the maximum dimensions a species can attain.

Scoring systems

The best-known system is that of American Forests, the United States conservation organisation, whose National Register of Champion Trees dates to 1940. Its formula awards:

  • 1 point per inch of trunk circumference (measured at 4.5 feet / 1.37 m above the ground),
  • 1 point per foot of height,
  • ¼ point per foot of average crown spread.

The total points determine the champion for each species; trees within five points of the champion may be recognised as co-champions. State forestry agencies maintain parallel registers. Official measurers follow detailed protocols for sloping ground, multi-stemmed trees, and leaning trunks to keep results comparable.

Other registers

In Britain and Ireland, the Tree Register of the British Isles records champion trees for height and girth, the two measures often producing different "champions" for the same species. Similar registers exist in many European countries, Australia, New Zealand (through the New Zealand Tree Register), and South Africa, where the Department of Forestry maintains a list of champion trees with protected status.

Notable champion trees

Famous champions include General Sherman, a giant sequoia in California that is the national champion of its species and the largest known living single-stem tree on Earth by volume; Hyperion, a coast redwood measured at about 115.9 m (380 ft), the tallest known living tree; and various national champions of oak, ash, and other species that become local landmarks. Because champions die, are damaged, or are superseded by newly discovered giants — especially as laser rangefinders and LiDAR have improved measurement — registers are updated regularly.
