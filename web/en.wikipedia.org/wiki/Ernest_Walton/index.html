Ernest Thomas Sinton Walton (6 October 1903 – 25 June 1995) was an Irish physicist who, with John Cockcroft, performed the first artificially induced nuclear reaction, splitting lithium atoms with accelerated protons at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge in 1932.

The Cockcroft–Walton experiment

Walton and Cockcroft built a voltage-multiplier circuit, now called the Cockcroft–Walton generator, capable of producing several hundred kilovolts. In April 1932 they directed protons accelerated through about 400 kV at a lithium target and observed the alpha particles produced when lithium nuclei disintegrated — the first nuclear transmutation achieved with artificially accelerated particles, and an early experimental confirmation of Einstein's mass–energy equivalence.

Later life and Nobel Prize

Walton returned to Ireland in 1934, becoming Erasmus Smith's Professor of Natural and Experimental Philosophy at Trinity College Dublin, where he remained for four decades. He shared the 1951 Nobel Prize in Physics with Cockcroft "for their pioneer work on the transmutation of atomic nuclei by artificially accelerated atomic particles". He remains Ireland's only Nobel laureate in physics.
