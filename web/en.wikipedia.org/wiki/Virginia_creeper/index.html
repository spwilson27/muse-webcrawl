Virginia creeper (Parthenocissus quinquefolia), also known as five-leaved ivy or five-finger, is a species of flowering vine in the grape family, Vitaceae. It is native to eastern and central North America, from southeastern Canada and the eastern United States west to Manitoba and Utah, and south to Mexico and Guatemala.

Description

Virginia creeper is a vigorous deciduous climber that can reach heights of 20–30 m (66–98 ft) in the wild, attaching itself to surfaces by means of small branched tendrils tipped with adhesive pads. The leaves are palmately compound, composed of five leaflets (occasionally three or seven), each 3–20 cm long, joined from a central point on the leafstalk. Inconspicuous greenish flowers appear in late spring, maturing in late summer or early fall into small hard purple-black berries 5–7 mm in diameter. The foliage is celebrated for its brilliant scarlet to burgundy autumn colour.

Cultivation and uses

The species is widely grown as an ornamental ground cover and facade climber because of its rapid growth, tolerance of varied soils and spectacular fall colour. Unlike Boston ivy it grips by adhesive discs rather than aerial rootlets and does comparatively little damage to sound masonry. The berries are an important winter food for birds, although they contain oxalic acid and are toxic to humans; the sap can cause contact dermatitis in sensitive people.

Similar species

Virginia creeper is often confused with poison ivy, but its five leaflets (against poison ivy's three) and adhesive tendril pads distinguish it; the rhyme "leaves of five, let it thrive" is sometimes used as an aid. It also resembles its close relative thicket creeper (Parthenocissus vitacea), which lacks adhesive pads.
