Jean-Maurice-Émile Baudot (11 September 1845 – 28 March 1903) was a French telegraph engineer and the inventor of the Baudot code, one of the first binary codes for telegraphy and the ancestor of modern character encodings. The unit of signalling speed, the baud, is named after him.

Life and work

Born in Magneux in Haute-Marne, Baudot had only a primary school education and worked on his father's farm before joining the French Postes et Télégraphes as an apprentice operator in 1869. Largely self-taught in electricity and mechanics, he worked on the Hughes printing telegraph and sought to multiply the capacity of telegraph lines.

In 1874 he patented a multiplex printing telegraph that shares one wire among several operators by time-division multiplexing, using a five-unit code in which each character is represented by five binary elements. A keyboard of five piano-like keys, operated with the fingers of both hands, generated the combinations directly. The system was adopted by the French telegraph administration in 1877 on the Paris–Bordeaux line and spread across Europe and the world; Baudot was made a Knight of the Legion of Honour.

Legacy

The Baudot code, in its later form standardized as International Telegraph Alphabet No. 2, remained the basis of teleprinter networks well into the twentieth century and influenced the design of ASCII. The baud — symbols per second — was named in his honour by international agreement in the 1920s. Donald Murray's modifications to the code produced the "Baudot-Murray code" used in most teleprinter systems.
