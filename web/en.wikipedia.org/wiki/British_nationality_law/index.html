British nationality law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

British nationality law is the law of the United Kingdom which concerns citizenship and other categories of British nationality. It is principally contained in the British Nationality Act 1981 and its predecessors, and determines who is a British citizen and who holds one of the other, mostly residual, forms of British nationality. Modern law gives British citizens the right of abode — the right to live and work in the United Kingdom without immigration control.

Classes of British nationality

There are six classes of British nationality:

  • British citizens — persons closely connected with the United Kingdom, the Channel Islands and the Isle of Man, who have the right of abode and full civic rights;
  • British Overseas Territories citizens — persons connected with an overseas territory; most also hold British citizenship since 2002;
  • British Overseas citizens — a residual class, mostly former Citizens of the United Kingdom and Colonies without close connection to the UK or a territory, generally with no right of abode;
  • British subjects — a residual class dating from before 1949; some (mainly those connected with Ireland) retain a right to hold a British passport;
  • British Nationals (Overseas) — Hong Kong residents who registered before the 1997 transfer of sovereignty; the status cannot be acquired anew or transmitted;
  • British protected persons — persons connected with former British protectorates; the class is closed to new entrants except in rare cases.

History

English law originally treated nationality as allegiance owed to the Crown by those born within the sovereign's dominions, a doctrine confirmed in Calvin's Case (1608). The first statutes, including the British Nationality and Status of Aliens Act 1914, codified a common code across the British Empire, under which all subjects of the Crown shared the status of British subject. The British Nationality Act 1948 introduced the status of Citizen of the United Kingdom and Colonies alongside separate citizenships of the independent Commonwealth countries, while preserving a common British subject status. As the empire contracted and Commonwealth immigration became politically contentious, the Commonwealth Immigrants Acts of 1962 and 1968 and the Immigration Act 1971 progressively restricted entry, culminating in the 1981 Act, which redefined citizenship around connection with the United Kingdom itself and ended unconditional jus soli.

Acquisition of British citizenship

British citizenship is acquired automatically at birth in the UK (or a qualifying territory) to a parent who is a British citizen or who is "settled" in the UK; by birth abroad to a British citizen parent, subject to rules distinguishing citizenship "by descent" (which generally cannot be passed on to the next generation born abroad) from citizenship "otherwise than by descent"; by registration, notably of children born in the UK who live there for their first ten years, and of certain stateless persons and holders of other British statuses; and by naturalisation, which for adults generally requires five years' residence (three for spouses of citizens), indefinite leave to remain, good character, an intention to make the UK home, and passing the "Life in the UK" test and language requirements. Citizenship may be lost by renunciation, or removed by the Home Secretary on "conducive to the public good" grounds where it would not render the person stateless (with wider powers where citizenship was obtained by fraud).

Multiple nationality and rights

The United Kingdom places no general restriction on its citizens holding other nationalities. British citizens may vote in all UK elections, stand for Parliament, and hold public office; Irish citizens and qualifying Commonwealth citizens resident in the UK enjoy reciprocal electoral rights under separate provisions.
