Antiqua–Fraktur dispute

The Antiqua–Fraktur dispute was a long-running controversy in German typography and cultural politics over which typeface should be used for printed German text: the Latin-style Antiqua typefaces, used across most of Europe, or the blackletter Fraktur typefaces, regarded by many Germans as a national script. The dispute ran from the late 18th century until 1941, when the Nazi regime abruptly banned Fraktur.

Background

Since the Gutenberg era, German printers had used blackletter forms such as Textura, Schwabacher and Fraktur, while Latin and Romance-language texts in Germany were set in Antiqua. By the 19th century, German books, newspapers and official documents were overwhelmingly printed in Fraktur, but scientific and export publications increasingly favoured Antiqua.

The controversy

Supporters of Fraktur argued it embodied German character and was easier for German readers; supporters of Antiqua argued it was more legible, internationally compatible and practical for science and commerce. Debates reached the Reichstag in 1911, where a proposal to make Antiqua the official school and administrative script narrowly failed.

Resolution

On 3 January 1941, Martin Bormann issued a decree on behalf of Adolf Hitler declaring Fraktur to be "Jewish letters" (Judenschrift) and mandating Antiqua (renamed Normalschrift) for all official use. The switch ended the dispute overnight; after 1945 Fraktur survived only in decorative and nostalgic contexts.
