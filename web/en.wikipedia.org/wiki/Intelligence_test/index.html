Intelligence test

An intelligence test is a standardised assessment designed to measure a person's cognitive abilities and potential, producing scores such as an intelligence quotient (IQ). Modern testing began with the Binet–Simon scale in 1905, developed by Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon to identify Paris schoolchildren needing educational support. Widely used modern tests include the Wechsler scales (WAIS for adults and WISC for children), the Stanford–Binet Intelligence Scales, the Kaufman batteries, and nonverbal tests such as Raven's Progressive Matrices.

History

Binet's scale introduced the concept of mental age. Lewis Terman at Stanford University revised it as the Stanford–Binet test and popularised the intelligence quotient, originally computed as mental age divided by chronological age times 100. Mass testing in the First World War, through the US Army Alpha and Beta tests, greatly expanded group testing. David Wechsler's scales, introduced from 1939, replaced the ratio IQ with deviation IQ scores and reported separate verbal and performance indices; contemporary editions measure index scores for verbal comprehension, perceptual reasoning, working memory and processing speed.

What tests measure

Scores on diverse cognitive tests correlate positively with one another (the "positive manifold"), which led Charles Spearman to propose a general factor, g, alongside specific abilities. Hierarchical models such as the Cattell–Horn–Carroll theory organise abilities from g down through broad domains (fluid reasoning, crystallised knowledge, memory, processing speed) to narrow skills. Test scores predict educational attainment, job performance and various health outcomes moderately well, but critics dispute whether tests capture the full breadth of human intelligence and emphasise cultural loading, coaching effects and the Flynn effect — the large rise in average scores over the 20th century that forces periodic re-norming.

Uses and controversies

Intelligence tests are used in clinical and educational psychology to diagnose intellectual disability and learning disorders, identify giftedness, and assess cognitive impairment, as well as in personnel selection. Their use has been historically controversial, including early 20th-century associations with eugenics and immigration restriction, and continuing debates about group differences, test bias and the heritability of intelligence. Courts in some jurisdictions have restricted their use in specific contexts, such as capital sentencing for people with intellectual disability.
