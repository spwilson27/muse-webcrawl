CT scan

A computed tomography scan (CT scan), formerly called computed axial tomography scan (CAT scan), is a medical imaging technique used to obtain detailed internal images of the body. The personnel that perform CT scans are called radiographers or radiology technologists. A CT scanner uses a rotating X-ray tube and a row of detectors placed in a gantry to measure X-ray attenuations by different tissues inside the body; the multiple measurements are then processed by computer algorithms (tomographic reconstruction) to generate cross-sectional images, or "slices", of the body.

History

The first commercially viable CT scanner was invented by Sir Godfrey Hounsfield at EMI in England, building on the theoretical work of Allan MacLeod Cormack; both shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The first clinical scan, of a patient's brain, was performed on 1 October 1971 at Atkinson Morley Hospital in Wimbledon. CT spread rapidly through the 1970s and 1980s, and modern multi-detector helical scanners can image the whole body in seconds in three dimensions.

Uses

CT is widely used for diagnosing trauma injuries, staging and following cancers, guiding biopsies, imaging the brain after stroke or head injury, and evaluating chest, abdominal and cardiovascular disease. Iodinated contrast agents are often injected to enhance blood vessels and organs. Tissue densities are expressed in Hounsfield units (HU), with water defined as 0 and air as −1000.

Risks

CT uses ionising radiation at doses considerably higher than plain radiographs, which is a particular concern for children and for repeated examinations; dose-reduction techniques and judicious clinical indications are used to limit exposure. Contrast agents can cause allergic-like reactions and kidney injury in susceptible patients.
