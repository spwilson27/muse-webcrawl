Arnold tongues

Arnold tongues: mode-locking regions for the circle map, with rotation number shown by color

FieldDynamical systems, nonlinear dynamics
Named afterVladimir Arnold
Related modelCircle map

In mathematics, particularly in dynamical systems, Arnold tongues (named after Vladimir Arnold) are a pictorial phenomenon that occur when visualizing how a driven nonlinear oscillator synchronizes its oscillation frequency to an external periodic forcing. The tongues are the tongue-shaped regions in parameter space within which the oscillator is mode-locked to the driving signal, i.e., its rotation number stays constant at a rational value.

The circle map

The standard example is the circle map θn+1 = θn + Ω − (K/2π) sin(2πθn), where Ω is the bare frequency ratio and K the coupling strength. For K = 0 the map rotates by Ω; for increasing K, regions of parameter space with rational rotation number p/q widen into tongues emanating from the K = 0 axis at each rational point.

Mode locking and the devil's staircase

Inside an Arnold tongue, the system is phase-locked: the oscillator completes exactly p cycles for every q cycles of the forcing. Tongues corresponding to small denominators are widest. At fixed K, plotting the rotation number against Ω yields a "devil's staircase": a continuous, non-decreasing step function constant on the tongues and increasing only on a fractal set. At K = 1 the staircase becomes complete, occupying full measure.

Applications

Arnold tongues describe synchronization phenomena across physics and biology: phase-locked loops in electronics, Josephson junctions, cardiac pacemaker entrainment, circadian rhythms, and the firing patterns of neurons responding to periodic stimuli.
