Buyid dynasty

Placeholder image: the Buyid amirates at their greatest extent, c. 970.

The Buyid dynasty (Persian: آل بویه, Âl-e Buye), also known as the Buwayhids, was a Shia Muslim Iranian dynasty of Daylamite origin that ruled over western and southern Iran and Iraq from 934 to 1062. Founded by Ali ibn Buya, a soldier of fortune from Daylam south of the Caspian Sea, the dynasty is noted for reviving Iranian kingship traditions — its rulers took the ancient Persian title Shahanshah ("King of Kings") — while exercising real power over the Abbasid caliphs in Baghdad.

CapitalShiraz, Ray, Baghdad
Period934–1062
ReligionShia Islam (Zaydi, later Twelver)

History

Three brothers — Ali, Hasan and Ahmad ibn Buya — carved out principalities in Fars, Jibal and Kerman in the 930s, and in 945 Ahmad entered Baghdad, where the caliph conferred on the brothers honorific titles and effectively surrendered temporal power. Under 'Adud al-Dawla (r. 949–983) the Buyid lands were briefly united into a single empire stretching from Oman to the Byzantine frontier; he is remembered for building works in Baghdad and Shiraz and patronage of scholarship. After his death the confederation fragmented into competing branches seated at Shiraz, Ray and Baghdad, weakened by succession disputes and Turkish soldiery.

Decline

The Baghdad branch fell to the Seljuk Tughril Beg in 1055; the Fars branch survived until 1062. The Buyid century is often called an "Iranian intermezzo" between Arab and Turkic domination, marked by Persian cultural revival and relative religious tolerance in which Sunni and Shia institutions both received patronage.
