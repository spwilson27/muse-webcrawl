The Belmont Report is a report created by the National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research. Its full title is the Belmont Report: Ethical Principles and Guidelines for the Protection of Human Subjects of Research. The report, issued on 18 April 1979, takes its name from the Belmont Conference Center in Elkridge, Maryland, where the commission met to draft it.

Background

The National Research Act of 1974 created the commission in response to abuses in human experimentation, most notoriously the Tuskegee syphilis study (1932–1972), in which treatment was withheld from African American men with syphilis without their informed consent. The commission was charged with identifying basic ethical principles governing research involving human subjects and developing guidelines to assure such research is conducted according to those principles.

Three principles

The report articulates three core principles:

  • Respect for persons — individuals should be treated as autonomous agents, and those with diminished autonomy are entitled to protection; this grounds the requirement of informed consent.
  • Beneficence — researchers must not harm subjects and must maximise possible benefits while minimising possible harms, assessed through systematic risk–benefit analysis.
  • Justice — the benefits and burdens of research should be distributed fairly, so that particular groups are not unfairly burdened or excluded from benefits.

Influence

The Belmont principles underpin United States federal regulations for human-subjects research, including the Common Rule (45 CFR 46), and institutional review board (IRB) practice. The report remains a foundational document in research ethics worldwide.
