Norwegian cuisine

Norwegian cuisine in its traditional form is based on the ingredients available in a northern climate with short growing seasons: fish and game, lamb and mutton, potatoes and root vegetables, dairy products, and preserved foods produced to last through long winters. Norway's long coastline and farming history shape a cooking style that is plain, seasonal, and heavily reliant on curing, drying, smoking, and fermenting.

Salmon, flatbread, and preserved fish are staples of Norwegian food

Fish and seafood

Fish is central. Stockfish (unsalted dried cod) and klippfisk (salted, dried cod) were exported for centuries; lutefisk, dried fish reconstituted in an alkaline solution, remains a traditional Christmas dish. Rakfisk is fermented trout eaten raw with flatbread and sour cream. Salmon, fresh and smoked, and gravlaks (cured salmon with dill) are internationally known.

Meat and festive dishes

Traditional meat dishes include fårikål (lamb and cabbage stewed with black peppercorns), often called the national dish; pinnekjøtt, salted and dried lamb ribs steamed over birch sticks, served at Christmas in western Norway; ribbe, roasted pork belly, the Christmas standard in the east; and smalahove, a sheep's head. Kjøttkaker (meatballs in brown sauce) are an everyday classic. Game such as elk, reindeer, and grouse is eaten in season.

Breads, dairy, and sweets

Thin crisp flatbrød and soft potato lefse accompany meals. Brunost, the caramelised brown whey cheese (notably Gudbrandsdalsost), is a national icon, sliced thin on bread or waffles. Waffles with brown cheese and jam, cinnamon buns (kanelboller), and cloudberries with cream are favourite sweets. Aquavit, distilled from potatoes and flavoured with caraway, is the traditional festive spirit. See also Scandinavian cuisine, lutefisk, and brunost.
