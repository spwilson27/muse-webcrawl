Socialism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Socialism is a political philosophy and movement encompassing a range of economic and social systems characterised by social ownership of the means of production, as opposed to private ownership. It describes the economic, political, and social theories and movements associated with the implementation of such systems, including democratic socialism, social democracy, and forms of Marxist–Leninist state socialism. Social ownership can take forms including public, community, collective, cooperative or employee ownership.

History

Socialist ideas build on older traditions of common ownership, from Plato's guardians and early Christian communities to the Diggers of the English Civil War. Modern socialism arose in reaction to industrial capitalism: "utopian" thinkers such as Robert Owen, Charles Fourier and Henri de Saint-Simon proposed cooperative communities, while Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels developed "scientific socialism", foreseeing capitalism's supersession after working-class revolution. The First International (1864) and mass socialist parties spread the movement; the Russian Revolution of 1917 split it between revolutionary communists and reformist social democrats — a division shaping the twentieth century.

Variants

Democratic socialism seeks social ownership through democratic means; social democracy (as it evolved in post-war Europe) accepts a mixed economy with a welfare state; Marxism–Leninism ruled the Soviet Union and its allies with central planning; and market socialism, libertarian socialism and eco-socialism offer further variants. Parties of the socialist tradition built much of the European welfare state, public health services and labour law, while one-party socialist states achieved rapid industrialisation alongside political repression and economic stagnation in their later decades.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that comprehensive planning faces a knowledge or "economic calculation" problem (Mises, Hayek), that abolishing markets weakens innovation and liberty, and that historical socialist states produced authoritarian outcomes. Defenders respond that actually existing policies vary widely, that markets themselves generate inequality and instability, and that democratic-socialist programmes differ fundamentally from one-party models. Polls in several countries show the word retains sharply different connotations by generation and region.
