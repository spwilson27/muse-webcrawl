Substitution reaction

A substitution reaction is a chemical reaction in which one functional group or atom in a compound is replaced by another atom or group. Substitution reactions are fundamental across organic and inorganic chemistry and are classified according to the nature of the attacking reagent and the mechanism by which substitution occurs.

Types

  • Nucleophilic substitution — a nucleophile donates an electron pair to an electrophilic centre, displacing a leaving group. At saturated carbon this proceeds by the bimolecular SN2 mechanism (single concerted step, inversion of configuration) or the unimolecular SN1 mechanism (through a carbocation intermediate, with racemisation).
  • Electrophilic substitution — an electrophile replaces an atom, classically a hydrogen of an aromatic ring in electrophilic aromatic substitution, as in nitration, halogenation, sulfonation and Friedel–Crafts reactions.
  • Nucleophilic aromatic substitution — a nucleophile displaces a leaving group on an electron-poor aromatic ring, often via a Meisenheimer complex.
  • Radical substitution — free-radical chain reactions, such as the chlorination of methane under ultraviolet light.
  • Organometallic substitution — ligand substitution at metal centres, important in coordination chemistry and catalysis.

Conditions and selectivity

Which mechanism operates depends on the substrate structure (methyl, primary, secondary or tertiary), the strength of the nucleophile or electrophile, the leaving group, and the solvent. Polar protic solvents favour SN1 ionisation, while polar aprotic solvents accelerate SN2 attack. Substitution frequently competes with elimination reactions, and controlling temperature and reagent choice allows chemists to favour one pathway over the other.
