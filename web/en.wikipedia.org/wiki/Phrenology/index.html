Phrenology is a pseudoscience that involves the measurement of bumps on the skull to predict mental traits. It is based on the concept that the brain is the organ of the mind, and that certain brain areas have localised, specific functions or modules. It was founded by the German physician Franz Joseph Gall in 1796 and was very popular in the 19th century, especially from about 1810 until 1840.

History

Gall began his research in Vienna in the 1790s, proposing that character and mental abilities could be inferred from the external configuration of the skull (cranioscopy). His collaborator Johann Gaspar Spurzheim later popularised the system in Britain and the United States under the name "phrenology". In Britain, George Combe's The Constitution of Man (1828) became one of the best-selling books of the century, and phrenological societies flourished. In America, the Fowler brothers built a commercial empire of phrenological readings, charts and publishing.

Principles

Phrenology rested on five tenets: the brain is the organ of the mind; the mind is composed of distinct innate faculties; each faculty is seated in a distinct "organ" of the brain; the size of an organ indicates its power; and the shape of the skull conforms to the shape of the brain beneath it, so faculties can be read from cranial contours. Gall and his followers enumerated around 27 to 35 organs, including amativeness, philoprogenitiveness, combativeness, acquisitiveness, veneration and benevolence.

Method

Practitioners palpated the client's head or measured it with calipers and specially designed instruments, comparing the development of each region against the phrenological chart. Readings were sold as guides to self-improvement, career choice, education and even marriage compatibility.

Decline and legacy

By the 1840s phrenology was increasingly challenged. Anatomists such as Pierre Flourens showed experimentally that functions were not localised as Gall claimed, and the system failed empirical tests. Although discredited as science, phrenology contributed indirectly to later ideas of cerebral localisation and influenced criminology, psychiatry and popular psychology. It is now cited as a classic example of pseudoscience and of the dangers of confirmation bias.
