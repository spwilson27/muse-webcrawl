Albert Claude

Albert Claude (24 August 1899 – 22 May 1983) was a Belgian-American cell biologist who shared the 1974 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Christian de Duve and George Emil Palade "for their discoveries concerning the structural and functional organization of the cell". Working at the Rockefeller Institute in New York, Claude pioneered the technique of cell fractionation — separating cell components by differential centrifugation — and was among the first to apply the electron microscope to biology, producing the first detailed images of the cell's internal structure.

Career

Born in Longlier, Belgium, Claude studied medicine at the University of Liège and arrived at the Rockefeller Institute in 1929 to work on the Rous sarcoma virus. In the 1930s and 1940s he developed methods for isolating mitochondria and microsomes (fragments of endoplasmic reticulum) and in 1945 published the first electron micrograph of a cell, revealing the lace-like endoplasmic reticulum. He became a US citizen in 1941 and returned to Belgium in 1949 to direct the Institut Jules Bordet in Brussels, while keeping a Rockefeller professorship; his Brussels laboratory trained Christian de Duve. Claude's fractionation methods laid the foundation of modern cell biology. See also cell biology, George Emil Palade and Christian de Duve.
