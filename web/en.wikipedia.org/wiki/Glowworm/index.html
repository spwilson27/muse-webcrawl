Glowworm

Glowworm or glow-worm is the common name for various groups of insect larvae — and adult females of some species — that glow through bioluminescence. They include the larvae of the firefly family Lampyridae, the railroad worms of the family Phengodidae, the family Rhagophthalmidae, and, among flies, the fungus gnat genus Arachnocampa of Australia and New Zealand, whose cave-dwelling larvae are a famous tourist attraction. Glowworms are not worms, despite the name.

Biology

In lampyrid beetles such as the European common glow-worm (Lampyris noctiluca), the wingless adult female climbs vegetation at dusk and produces a steady greenish light from photic organs at the tip of her abdomen to attract flying males; the larvae also glow, apparently as a warning of distastefulness (aposematism). The light is generated by the oxidation of the pigment luciferin, catalysed by the enzyme luciferase in the presence of ATP and oxygen — a "cold light" that converts nearly all its energy into visible radiation with almost no heat.

Phengodid "railroad worms" carry paired rows of green lights along their segments plus red head lights, resembling a tiny train at night. The fly larvae of Arachnocampa use their glow for predation: hanging from cave ceilings, they dangle sticky silk threads, and their blue-green light attracts flying insects that blunder into the snares — the glow-worm caves of Waitomo in New Zealand draw hundreds of thousands of visitors a year.

Distribution and habitat

Glowworms occur on most continents, in habitats ranging from European meadows and hedgerows to tropical forests and limestone caves. Species with flightless females have limited dispersal and are vulnerable to habitat loss and artificial light at night, which masks females' signals; declines of the common glow-worm have been reported in Britain and elsewhere in Europe, prompting citizen-science surveys to monitor populations.
