Fishing trawler

A fishing trawler is a commercial fishing vessel designed to operate fishing trawls — nets dragged through the water behind one or more boats. Trawlers tow a cone-shaped net, held open horizontally by trawl doors (otter boards) and vertically by floats and weights, catching fish as the vessel moves forward. They range from small inshore boats to freezer factory trawlers over 100 metres long that can fish the most distant grounds.


A stern trawler recovering its trawl over the stern ramp.

Types

  • Side trawlers — the historical arrangement, hauling the net over the ship's side.
  • Stern trawlers — the modern standard, shooting and hauling the net over a stern ramp, allowing larger nets and safer working decks.
  • Wet-fish trawlers — keep the catch fresh on ice for short trips.
  • Freezer trawlers — freeze the catch at sea for long voyages.
  • Factory trawlers — process the catch on board (filleting, mincing, fishmeal and oil production) as well as freeze it.
  • Beam trawlers — tow nets held open by a rigid beam, mainly for flatfish and shrimp.
  • Outrigger trawlers — common in shrimp fisheries, towing nets from booms on both sides.
  • Pair trawlers — two vessels tow a single large net between them.

History

Trawling developed in the nineteenth century with sailing smacks from ports such as Brixham in England; steam trawlers from the 1880s and diesel vessels vastly increased power and range, expanding fisheries onto the continental shelves and distant grounds such as Iceland, Newfoundland, and the Barents Sea. The introduction of the stern ramp (pioneered experimentally in the 1940s–50s, e.g. the British Fairtry, a pioneering factory stern trawler of 1954) and powerful winches and net drums transformed the industry.

Equipment and operation

A trawler's gear includes the trawl net itself (bottom trawl or midwater/pelagic trawl), steel warps, trawl doors, sweeps and bridles, and monitoring electronics — headline transducers and net sounders — that show the net's shape and fish entering it. Echo sounders and sonar locate shoals. Modern vessels also carry refrigerated seawater tanks or plate freezers, and crews work in shifts around the clock in distant-water fleets.

Environmental issues

Bottom trawling is controversial for its impact on seabed habitats and its bycatch; management responses include mesh-size rules, bycatch-reduction devices, closed areas, and real-time closures. Pelagic trawls avoid seabed contact but can take large volumes of schooling fish.
