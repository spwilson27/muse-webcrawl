Carillon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A carillon (US: /ˈkærəlɒn/, UK: /kəˈrɪljən/) is a pitched percussion instrument that is played with a keyboard and consists of at least 23 bells. The bells are cast in bronze, hung in fixed suspension, and tuned in chromatic order so that they can be sounded harmoniously together. Housed in bell towers — usually of churches, universities or municipal buildings — the carillon is the heaviest of all musical instruments.

Playing

The carillonneur plays from a cabin beneath or among the bells, using a baton keyboard of wooden levers struck with loosely clenched fists and a pedalboard played with the feet. Levers connect by wires and trackers to clappers inside the bells; the force of the stroke controls dynamics, making the carillon a genuinely expressive instrument. Many towers also have a mechanical drum that plays automatic melodies, historically to mark the hours.

History and distribution

The carillon emerged in the Low Countries in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, where cities competed in bell-founding and tower music; the Hemony brothers' tuned bells of the 1640s–50s set the standard. The tradition remains strongest in the Netherlands, Belgium and northern France — the belfries of Belgium and France are UNESCO World Heritage Sites — and spread to North America, where large instruments stand at universities and cathedrals; the world's largest by bell weight is at the Cathedral of St. John the Divine's rival... the Riverside Church in New York, whose bourdon bell is the largest tuned bell in the world. The total weight of a large carillon can exceed 90 tonnes. A recognised carillon requires at least 23 bells (two chromatic octaves); smaller sets are called chimes.

Repertoire

Repertoire ranges from Baroque arrangements and folk songs to original works by composers such as Jef Denyn of Mechelen, whose school (the Royal Carillon School, founded 1922) trained generations of players. The Guild of Carillonneurs in North America and the World Carillon Federation coordinate the profession.
