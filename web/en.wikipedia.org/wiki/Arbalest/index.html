Arbalest

The arbalest (sometimes arbalist) was a large variation of the crossbow used in medieval warfare. Coming into common use in 12th-century Europe, it had a steel prod (bow) and was capable of a far greater draw weight than earlier wooden or composite-bowed crossbows, giving its bolts exceptional penetrating power — enough, in contemporary accounts, to pierce chain mail and plate armour.

The word comes through Old French arbaleste from the Latin arcuballista, "bow-shooter." The largest arbalests, approaching siege engines, were sometimes called great crossbows, and the weapon is closely related to the Greek gastraphetes and cheiroballistra of antiquity.

Construction and use

Because a steel prod could store far more energy but was much stiffer, an arbalest could not be spanned by hand. Shooters used mechanical aids: a windlass (a pair of cranks with cords and hooks), a cranequin (a rack-and-pinion device), or a goat's foot lever. These devices made loading slow — perhaps one or two shots per minute — but the resulting shot was powerful and accurate, and the weapon could be held loaded and ready for long periods.

History

Steel crossbows appeared in Europe around the 12th century and became the signature weapon of professional mercenary companies, especially Genoese crossbowmen. Richard I of England championed the crossbow; ironically, he was killed by a crossbow bolt at the siege of Châlus-Chabrol in 1199. The Second Lateran Council of 1139 had earlier condemned the use of bows and crossbows against Christians, testimony to the weapon's feared lethality. Arbalests remained in use into the 15th and 16th centuries, gradually displaced by firearms.
