French Revolution of 1848

The French Revolution of 1848 was a wave of revolutions in 1848 that ended the July Monarchy (1830–1848) and established the French Second Republic. Prompted by economic crisis, demands for electoral reform and the government's banning of political banquets, insurgent Parisians erected barricades on 22–24 February 1848, forcing King Louis Philippe I to abdicate and flee to England. A provisional government proclaimed the Republic, introducing universal male suffrage — a dramatic expansion of the electorate from about 250,000 to some 9 million voters.

June Days and the election of Louis-Napoléon

The provisional government, which included the socialist Louis Blanc, set up National Workshops to provide work for the unemployed. When the conservative-dominated Constituent Assembly closed the workshops in June 1848, Parisian workers rose in the June Days uprising (23–26 June), which General Cavaignac crushed with great bloodshed — thousands were killed and some 11,000 arrested or deported. In the presidential election of December 1848, Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte, nephew of Napoleon I, won a landslide victory.

Aftermath

The Second Republic lasted only until Louis-Napoléon's coup d'état of 2 December 1851, after which he proclaimed himself Emperor Napoleon III and founded the Second Empire. The 1848 revolution in France was part of the Europe-wide Revolutions of 1848 ("Springtime of the Peoples"), and its trajectory — from democratic hopes to authoritarian reaction — was famously analysed by Karl Marx in The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte.
