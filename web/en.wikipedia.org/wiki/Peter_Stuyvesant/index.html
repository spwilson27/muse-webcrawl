Peter Stuyvesant (in Dutch also Pieter and Petrus; c. 1592 – August 1672) was a Dutch colonial officer who served as the last Dutch director-general of the colony of New Netherland from 1647 until it was ceded provisionally to the English in 1664, after which it was renamed New York. He was a major figure in the early history of New York City, and his name is given to various landmarks in the city, including Stuyvesant Town, Stuyvesant High School and Bedford–Stuyvesant.

Early career

Stuyvesant was born in Friesland, the son of a minister, and joined the Dutch West India Company, serving in the Caribbean. As director of the colonies of Curaçao, Aruba and Bonaire he led an attack on Portuguese-held Saint Martin in 1644 in which a cannonball shattered his leg; the leg was amputated and he wore a wooden prosthesis, earning the nickname "Peg Leg Pete".

Director-general of New Netherland

Arriving in New Amsterdam in May 1647, Stuyvesant governed in an authoritarian style that often brought him into conflict with the colonists, whom he sought to discipline in matters of trade, taverns and religion. He enforced Dutch Reformed orthodoxy against Lutherans, Jews and Quakers — his attempt to expel the Jewish settlers who arrived in 1654 was overruled by the Company in Amsterdam, and his persecution of Quakers in Flushing prompted the Flushing Remonstrance of 1657, an early American plea for religious freedom. Under his rule the settlement grew; he ordered the building of the defensive wall on what became Wall Street, established a market and improved the fort and church. He also conquered the colony of New Sweden on the Delaware in 1655.

Surrender and later life

In 1664, during the Second Anglo-Dutch War's preliminaries, an English expedition under Richard Nicolls demanded the colony's surrender. Lacking support from the burghers, who preferred English terms to a hopeless fight, Stuyvesant capitulated in September 1664. He travelled to the Netherlands to defend his conduct, then returned to New York, living out his life on his farm, the Bouwerie — the origin of the name "Bowery". He died in 1672 and was buried in St. Mark's Church in-the-Bowery, on the site of his estate chapel.

Legacy

Stuyvesant is remembered as a capable but stubborn and intolerant ruler whose administration gave New Amsterdam much of its early urban structure. Statues of him stand in New York and in the Netherlands, and his wooden-legged silhouette remains an emblem of Dutch New York.
