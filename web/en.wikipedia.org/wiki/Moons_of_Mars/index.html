Moons of Mars

MoonsPhobos, Deimos
Discovered1877, by Asaph Hall

The two moons of Mars are Phobos and Deimos. They are small and irregularly shaped, resembling asteroids, and were discovered in August 1877 by American astronomer Asaph Hall at the United States Naval Observatory in Washington, D.C. The moons are named after the Greek gods Phobos (fear) and Deimos (terror), who in Greek mythology accompanied their father Ares, the god of war, into battle; Ares is identified with the Roman god Mars.

Phobos

Phobos is the larger and inner of the two moons, with a mean radius of about 11 km. It orbits Mars closer to the planet's surface than any other known moon, at about 6,000 km above the surface, completing an orbit in just 7 hours 39 minutes — faster than Mars rotates — so it rises in the west and sets in the east. Tidal forces are slowly lowering Phobos's orbit, and it is expected eventually to break apart into a ring or crash into Mars.

Deimos

Deimos is the smaller and outermost moon, with a mean radius of about 6.2 km. It orbits at about 23,460 km from Mars with a period of 30.3 hours, slightly longer than a Martian day.

Origin

The origin of the Martian moons remains debated. Their composition and orbits have suggested captured asteroids, accretion from material ejected by a giant impact on Mars, or the break-up of an earlier larger moon.
