Limes

In Roman history, a limes (Latin: [ˈliːmɛs]; plural limites) was a fortified boundary or border defence of the Roman Empire. The term originally denoted a path or balk marking the boundary between fields, and later the military roads that ran along the frontiers, before coming to refer to the entire frontier system of walls, ditches, watchtowers, forts, and fortresses.

Principal limites

The most famous limes is the Upper Germanic-Rhaetian Limes in modern Germany, a line of frontier works some 550 km long running from the Rhine to the Danube, built in stages from the first to the third century AD. In Britain the empire built Hadrian's Wall and, briefly, the Antonine Wall. Other limites included the Limes Arabicus along the desert frontier of Arabia, the Fossatum Africae in North Africa, and the fortified lines along the lower Danube and in Dacia.

Function

Scholars debate the purpose of the limites: rather than impregnable defensive walls, they are generally understood as demarcated zones for controlling movement, trade, and migration, providing observation and a tripwire against raids, and projecting imperial power. They were manned by auxiliary troops in small forts and watchtowers, with legionary fortresses stationed further behind the line.

Legacy

Sections of the limites in Germany, Britain, and along the Danube are inscribed collectively by UNESCO as the "Frontiers of the Roman Empire" World Heritage Site, and stretches of palisade, ditch, and reconstructed towers remain visible today.
