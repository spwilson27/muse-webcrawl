Stalking horse

A stalking horse is a figure used to test a concept or mount a challenge on behalf of an anonymous third party. The term originates in hunting, where a stalking horse is a horse (or a model of one) behind which a hunter conceals himself to approach game without alarming it. By extension, a stalking-horse challenge or bid conceals the identity or intentions of the real challenger until the strength of the opposition has been revealed.

In politics

In British politics, a stalking-horse candidate is one who challenges a party leader not in expectation of winning but to expose the leader's vulnerability, paving the way for a stronger candidate. The phrase is famously associated with Sir Anthony Meyer, whose challenge to Margaret Thatcher for the Conservative leadership in 1989 revealed significant discontent and foreshadowed her resignation the following year.

In business and bankruptcy

In United States bankruptcy practice, a stalking-horse bid is an initial, court-approved bid on the assets of a bankrupt company, negotiated before an auction. The stalking-horse bidder sets a floor price that encourages other bids, and in exchange receives protections such as a break-up fee and expense reimbursement if outbid. The arrangement benefits the estate by guaranteeing a minimum recovery while giving the first bidder compensation for the cost of valuing the assets and effectively doing the other bidders' due diligence. Stalking-horse agreements are central to sales under section 363 of the Bankruptcy Code.

General usage

More broadly, the term describes any proposal, lawsuit or candidate advanced to test the waters, draw out opposition, or mask the motives of the person who truly stands to benefit.
