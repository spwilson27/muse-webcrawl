Hurling (Irish: iománaíocht) is an outdoor team game of ancient Gaelic Irish origin, played by men and administered by the Gaelic Athletic Association (GAA). Players use a wooden stick called a hurley (Irish: camán) to hit a small ball, the sliotar, between the opponents' goalposts — over the crossbar for one point or under it into a net for a goal worth three points. It is widely described as the fastest field sport in the world, and shares a common heritage with the Scottish game of shinty, with which it forms the hybrid sport of shinty-hurling.

History

Stick-and-ball games in Ireland are recorded in mythology — most famously the boyhood feats of Cú Chulainn — and in medieval texts and statutes; the Statutes of Galway (1527) and other early modern references attest to the game's long history. The modern rules were codified with the founding of the GAA in 1884, and the first All-Ireland championship was played in 1887. Kilkenny, Tipperary, and Cork have historically been the dominant counties.

Rules and equipment

Teams of fifteen play on a rectangular pitch about 130–145 metres long. The sliotar may be caught, carried for up to four steps in the hand, struck in the air, or struck along the ground; it may not be thrown or carried more than four steps. Players wear helmets with faceguards, mandatory since 2010. The hurley is made of ash wood with a flattened, curved blade (the bas). Women's hurling, played under slightly modified rules, is known as camogie.

Competitions

The premier competition is the All-Ireland Senior Hurling Championship, whose final at Croke Park in Dublin is one of Ireland's major sporting events. Provincial championships in Munster and Leinster feed into the All-Ireland series, and the National Hurling League runs in spring. Despite its amateur ethos, elite hurling draws large crowds and is central to Irish sporting culture; UNESCO added hurling to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2018.
