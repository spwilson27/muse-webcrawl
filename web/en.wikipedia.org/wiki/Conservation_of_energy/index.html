Conservation of energy

The law of conservation of energy states that the total energy of an isolated system remains constant; energy can be transformed from one form to another but cannot be created or destroyed. It is the first law of thermodynamics applied generally and one of the most fundamental principles in physics.

History

The principle emerged in the 1840s through the work of Julius Robert von Mayer, James Prescott Joule (whose experiments measured the mechanical equivalent of heat), Hermann von Helmholtz and others, building on earlier ideas of Leibniz's vis viva. It ended hopes of perpetual motion machines.

Foundations

By Noether's theorem, energy conservation follows from the time-translation symmetry of physical laws. In relativity, mass and energy are unified (E = mc²) and the conserved quantity is mass–energy. In general relativity, global energy conservation becomes subtle in expanding spacetime, though local conservation of the stress–energy tensor holds.
