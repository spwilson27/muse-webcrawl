Uthman

Uthman ibn Affan (c. 573/576 – 17 June 656) was the third Rashidun caliph, ruling from 644 until his assassination in 656. A wealthy merchant of the Umayyad clan and early convert to Islam, he was a companion and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad, married successively to two of Muhammad's daughters, earning the title Dhu al-Nurayn, 'Possessor of Two Lights'.

Uthman ibn Affan
Caliph: 644–656 CE
Predecessor: Umar
Successor: Ali
Died: 656, Medina

Caliphate

Chosen by a council (shura) after Umar's assassination, Uthman oversaw continued expansion into Persia, the Caucasus, and North Africa, the building of an Arab navy, and major public works. He is most famous for commissioning the standardized recension of the Qur'an — the 'Uthmanic codex' — ordering variant codices burned to preserve unity of the text.

Death and legacy

Accusations of nepotism toward his Umayyad relatives fuelled revolt; Egyptian rebels besieged his house in Medina and killed him in 656, an event that precipitated the First Fitna (civil war). Sunni tradition reveres him as one of the four rightly-guided caliphs and among the ten promised paradise.
