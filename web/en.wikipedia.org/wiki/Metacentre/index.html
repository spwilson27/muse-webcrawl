Metacentre

Metacentre
Type: Hydrostatics concept
Key quantity: Metacentric height (GM)
Depends on: Centre of buoyancy, centre of gravity

In fluid mechanics, the metacentre is the point about which a floating body starts to oscillate when it is displaced by a small amount. When a vessel or other floating object is tilted (heeled) slightly, its centre of buoyancy shifts because the shape of the submerged volume changes. The line of action of the buoyant force through the new centre of buoyancy intersects the original vertical line of buoyancy at the metacentre.

Stability

The position of the metacentre relative to the centre of gravity determines the initial stability of the floating body. If the metacentre lies above the centre of gravity, the buoyant force and gravity create a righting moment that pushes the body back to its upright position, and the body is stable. If the metacentre lies below the centre of gravity, the overturning moment dominates and the vessel capsizes.

The distance between the centre of gravity (G) and the metacentre (M) is called the metacentric height, GM. A positive metacentric height indicates stability. Naval architects use GM as a practical measure: passenger ships typically have a metacentric height between 0.5 and 1.5 metres, balancing stability against a comfortable, slow roll. Too large a GM makes a vessel "stiff", rolling rapidly and violently; too small a GM makes it "tender" and at risk of capsizing.

Calculation

For small angles of heel, the metacentre can be found from the relationship GM = BM − BG, where BM is the distance from the centre of buoyancy to the metacentre, equal to the second moment of area of the waterline plane divided by the displaced volume of water, and BG is the distance between the centres of buoyancy and gravity.

History

The concept was developed in the 18th century, notably by the French mathematician and ship designer Pierre Bouguer, who presented the theory in his Traité du navire (1746). It remains a cornerstone of ship stability theory and is taught in naval architecture worldwide.
