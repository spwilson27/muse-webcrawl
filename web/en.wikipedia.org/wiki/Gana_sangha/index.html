Gana sangha


The gana-sanghas were oligarchic republics of ancient India.

Gana-sangha (Sanskrit: गणसङ्घ, "assembly of equals") was a type of oligarchic republican polity in ancient India, in contrast to the monarchies (samrajya) of the same period. The gana-sanghas were ruled by an assembly of aristocratic families rather than a single king, and their leaders held the title of raja only by courtesy; real decisions were taken collectively in the assembly hall (santhagara).

History

Gana-sanghas flourished in the foothills of the Himalayas and northern India from around the 6th century BCE. Notable examples include the Licchavis of Vaishali, the Sakyas of Kapilavastu (the clan of the Buddha), the Mallas, the Koliyas, and later the Yaudheyas, Malavas and Arjunayanas, who issued coins and resisted foreign invasions well into the Gupta period.

Governance

Citizenship in a gana-sangha was restricted to the heads of kshatriya clan families. The assembly met to debate policy, elect officials, administer justice and command the army. The Buddhist monastic order (sangha) is thought to have modelled its procedures — quorum, motions, ballots — on those of the gana-sanghas.

Decline

Most gana-sanghas were gradually absorbed by the expanding monarchies of Magadha and later empires. Kautilya's Arthashastra discusses strategies for subverting them, regarding their unity as a source of strength. A few survived as tributary states into the 4th century CE before disappearing under the Guptas.
