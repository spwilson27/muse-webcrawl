Child tax credit

A child tax credit (CTC) is a tax credit available in many countries, which depends on the taxpayer's number of dependent children and often on their income. It is intended to offset part of the cost of raising children and, in the United States, has been a central element of anti-poverty and family tax policy since 1997.

The United States child tax credit was created by the Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997, initially worth $500 per qualifying child under age 17. The Economic Growth and Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2001 raised it to $1,000, and the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 doubled the maximum credit to $2,000 per child, with up to $1,400 (later $1,700, indexed) refundable as the Additional Child Tax Credit.

For tax year 2021 only, the American Rescue Plan Act temporarily expanded the credit to $3,600 per child under 6 and $3,000 per child aged 6–17, made it fully refundable, and provided advance monthly payments. Studies by the U.S. Census Bureau and academic researchers credited the expansion with a substantial reduction in measured child poverty that year; after the expansion expired, the credit reverted to its previous form and child poverty rates rose again.

Other countries

Several other jurisdictions offer similar benefits. The United Kingdom's Child Tax Credit, introduced in 2003, was a means-tested benefit that has largely been replaced by Universal Credit. Canada provides the Canada Child Benefit, a monthly tax-free payment introduced in 2016. Germany's Kindergeld is a monthly child allowance rather than a credit.
