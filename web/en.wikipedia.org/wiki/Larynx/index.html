Larynx

The larynx, commonly called the voice box, is an organ in the top of the neck involved in breathing, producing sound and protecting the trachea against food aspiration. It sits between the pharynx above and the trachea below, at the level of the third to sixth cervical vertebrae in adults. The larynx houses the vocal folds, and manipulating the larynx is used in generating the human voice. The intrinsic muscles of the larynx adjust the position and tension of the vocal folds, thereby changing pitch and volume; the extrinsic muscles move the larynx as a whole, as in swallowing.

Structure

Cartilages

The skeleton of the larynx is made of nine cartilages connected by membranes and ligaments. The three unpaired cartilages are the thyroid cartilage (whose laryngeal prominence forms the "Adam's apple"), the ring-shaped cricoid cartilage, and the leaf-shaped epiglottis, which folds down during swallowing to cover the laryngeal inlet. The three paired cartilages are the arytenoids — which anchor the vocal ligaments and rotate/slide to open and close the glottis — and the small corniculate and cuneiform cartilages.

Vocal folds

The vocal apparatus consists of two pairs of mucosal folds. The upper, false vocal cords (vestibular folds) play little part in phonation but protect the airway; the lower true vocal folds vibrate as air passes between them, producing sound. The opening between the true folds is the rima glottidis, the narrowest part of the laryngeal cavity.

Function

The larynx serves three broad functions. First, it is a valve protecting the lower airway: during swallowing the larynx rises, the epiglottis folds back and the vocal folds close, directing food into the oesophagus. Second, it produces the voice: the myoelastic-aerodynamic theory describes how subglottic air pressure blows the elastic vocal folds apart and their recoil and the Bernoulli effect draw them together again, setting up a periodic vibration whose frequency — roughly 85–155 Hz in adult men and 165–255 Hz in adult women — determines pitch. Third, forceful closure of the glottis enables coughing and the Valsalva manoeuvre used in lifting and defecation.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the larynx is laryngitis, usually viral, causing hoarseness. In children, swelling of the subglottic region causes croup, while bacterial epiglottitis is an airway emergency. Benign lesions such as vocal cord nodules and polyps arise from phonotrauma; laryngeal cancer, strongly linked to smoking and alcohol, most often arises on the vocal folds. Surgical removal of the larynx (laryngectomy) requires a permanent tracheostomy for breathing and alternative voice rehabilitation. The recurrent laryngeal nerve, which supplies all intrinsic muscles except cricothyroid, is at risk during thyroid surgery; its injury causes vocal cord palsy.
