A police dog, also known as a K-9, is a dog specifically trained to assist members of law enforcement. Dogs have been used in policing since the Middle Ages; organised police dog units began in late 19th-century Europe, with Ghent, Belgium, establishing a pioneering programme in 1899, and bloodhounds were used by London's Metropolitan Police during the Jack the Ripper investigation in 1888.

A German Shepherd police dog working with its handler.

Roles

General-purpose dogs track suspects and missing persons, search buildings, and protect their handlers. Specialist detection dogs are trained to find drugs, explosives, firearms, currency, cadavers, accelerants, or contraband electronics, exploiting a sense of smell vastly more sensitive than a human's. Breeds are matched to roles: shepherds and Malinois for patrol work, Labradors and spaniels for detection.

Training and legal status

Training uses reward-based methods and scent-imprinting from a young age; dogs usually live with their handlers and retire to them after service. In many jurisdictions, injuring a police dog is a specific criminal offence, and some countries treat police dogs formally as officers for assault laws. Use of dogs for crowd control and suspect apprehension has also drawn criticism and legal scrutiny in some countries.
