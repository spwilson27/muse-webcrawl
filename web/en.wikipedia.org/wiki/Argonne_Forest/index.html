Argonne Forest

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The heavily wooded hills of the Argonne.

The Argonne Forest (French: Forêt d'Argonne) is a long strip of mountainous and wild woodland in north-eastern France, roughly 200 km (120 mi) east of Paris, in the departments of Meuse, Marne, and Ardennes. It is bounded by the rivers Aisne and Meuse and forms a natural barrier of wooded ridges, steep valleys, and marshy ground.

History

In 1792 Charles François Dumouriez outmaneuvered the invading forces of the Duke of Brunswick in the forest before the Battle of Valmy. The forest also played an important part in the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71.

World War I

During World War I, the forest formed part of the Western Front. The Meuse-Argonne Offensive, fought from 26 September 1918 until the Armistice on 11 November 1918, was the largest operation of the American Expeditionary Forces (AEF) and the largest battle in United States military history, involving 1.2 million American soldiers. It was also the second-deadliest battle in American history, resulting in over 350,000 casualties including 28,000 German lives, 26,277 American lives, and an unknown number of French lives. The offensive formed part of the Allied Hundred Days Offensive that ended the war. Among the episodes that made the Argonne famous are the actions of the "Lost Battalion" of the 77th Division and the exploits of Sergeant Alvin York.

The forest today

Today the Argonne is a region of quiet woodland, small villages, and war memorials, including the Meuse-Argonne American Cemetery at Romagne-sous-Montfaucon, the largest American military cemetery in Europe. The forest is managed for timber, hunting, and tourism, and its hills and ponds support notable wildlife.
