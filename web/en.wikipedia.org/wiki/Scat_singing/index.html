Scat singing

Scat singing treats the voice as a melodic instrument

Scat singing is a style of vocal improvisation in jazz in which the singer uses nonsense syllables and vocal sounds instead of words, treating the voice as an instrument for melodic and rhythmic invention. Syllables are chosen for their sound and percussive quality, often imitating the phrasing of horns or other instruments.

History

Although antecedents exist in ragtime and vaudeville, scat singing is conventionally dated to Louis Armstrong's 1926 recording of "Heebie Jeebies"; according to the famous story, Armstrong dropped the lyric sheet during the session and improvised syllables instead. Armstrong's earlier recording "Skid-Dat-De-Dat" with the Hot Five (1925) also features scat passages.

Notable practitioners

Ella Fitzgerald became the most celebrated scat singer of the swing and bebop eras, noted for her precision and harmonic imagination. Other major exponents include Cab Calloway, whose "Minnie the Moocher" (1931) involved audiences in call-and-response scat, Mel Tormé, Anita O'Day, Sarah Vaughan, Jon Hendricks, Bobby McFerrin, and Al Jarreau. In bebop, scat was used to navigate the style's fast, harmonically complex lines.

Technique

Effective scat singing requires a thorough command of harmony, since the improvised syllables outline chord changes much as an instrumental solo does. Singers often build phrases from characteristic bebop syllables and may quote melodies or imitate instrumental timbres.
