Parallel ATA

A 40-conductor IDE ribbon cable, with an 80-conductor variant used for higher Ultra DMA modes.

Parallel ATA (PATA), originally known as ATA, AT Attachment, and Integrated Drive Electronics (IDE), is a standard interface designed for IBM PC-compatible computers. It was first developed by Western Digital and Compaq in 1984 for use in the IBM PC/AT. PATA connects storage devices such as hard disk drives, floppy disk drives and optical disc drives inside personal computers.

History

The interface integrated the drive controller onto the drive itself, greatly simplifying the cabling and electronics required. The standard was maintained by the X3/INCITS committee and published as ATA-1 in 1994. Successive revisions added faster transfer modes, larger disk support through logical block addressing (LBA), and the ATAPI extension allowing devices other than hard disks, such as CD-ROM drives.

Later Ultra DMA modes required an 80-conductor cable while retaining the same 40-pin connector, the extra conductors serving as ground wires to reduce crosstalk.

Master/slave configuration

Each PATA channel supports up to two devices, designated device 0 (master) and device 1 (slave), traditionally selected with jumpers on the drive or by the cable-select (CSEL) signal. The two devices share the channel bandwidth.

Succession by SATA

With the introduction of Serial ATA (SATA) in 2003, the older interface was retroactively renamed Parallel ATA. SATA offered higher transfer rates, thinner cables and one device per channel. PATA was progressively phased out of new computers during the late 2000s.
