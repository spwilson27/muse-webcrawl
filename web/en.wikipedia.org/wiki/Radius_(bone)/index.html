Radius (bone)

The radius is one of the two large bones of the forearm, the other being the ulna. It extends from the lateral (thumb) side of the elbow to the wrist, where it forms the main articulation with the carpal bones of the hand. The radius rotates around the ulna to allow pronation and supination of the forearm — turning the palm down and up.

Structure

The radius has a small disc-shaped head proximally, which articulates with the capitulum of the humerus and the radial notch of the ulna. Below the head are the neck and the radial tuberosity, where the biceps tendon inserts. The shaft is triangular in cross-section and connected to the ulna by the interosseous membrane. The distal end is broad and features the ulnar notch, grooves for extensor tendons, and the styloid process on its lateral side.

Function

Together with the ulna, the radius transmits load from the hand to the arm; at the wrist it bears most of the axial force through the scaphoid and lunate bones. Its rotation around the relatively fixed ulna gives the forearm its twisting movements essential for manipulation.

Clinical significance

Fractures of the distal radius are among the most common fractures, classically the Colles' fracture sustained in a fall onto an outstretched hand, which displaces the distal fragment dorsally. Fractures of the radial head and shaft, and congenital conditions such as Madelung's deformity, also affect this bone.
