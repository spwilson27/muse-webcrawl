Violin concerto

A violin concerto is a concerto for solo violin and instrumental ensemble, typically an orchestra. The genre arose in the Baroque era and has produced some of the most beloved works in classical music, combining lyrical melody with virtuoso display. Most violin concertos follow a three-movement pattern of fast–slow–fast.

A solo violinist performing a concerto with orchestra.

History

Baroque composers such as Vivaldi—whose Four Seasons (1725) are among the most famous concertos ever written—established the solo concerto form. Bach's concertos and the Classical works of Mozart (five concertos) and Haydn followed. The Romantic era produced the canonical masterworks: Beethoven's Concerto in D major (1806), Mendelssohn's in E minor (1844), Bruch's No. 1 in G minor, and the concertos of Brahms, Tchaikovsky, and Dvořák. In the 20th century, Sibelius, Elgar, Prokofiev, Shostakovich, Bartók, and Berg expanded the genre's expressive range, and contemporary composers continue to write concertos for leading soloists.

Notable violin concertos

  • Vivaldi — The Four Seasons
  • Beethoven — Violin Concerto in D major, Op. 61
  • Mendelssohn — Violin Concerto in E minor, Op. 64
  • Bruch — Violin Concerto No. 1 in G minor, Op. 26
  • Brahms — Violin Concerto in D major, Op. 77
  • Tchaikovsky — Violin Concerto in D major, Op. 35
  • Sibelius — Violin Concerto in D minor, Op. 47
  • Berg — Violin Concerto ("To the Memory of an Angel")

Form

The first movement usually combines sonata form with ritornello elements and includes a cadenza—an unaccompanied virtuoso passage, historically improvised and later written out by composers or performers. The slow movement is lyrical, often in a contrasting key, and the finale is typically a lively rondo.
