A Thousand Plateaus: Capitalism and Schizophrenia (French: Mille plateaux: capitalisme et schizophrénie) is a 1980 book of philosophy by Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari, the second and final volume of their Capitalism and Schizophrenia project, following Anti-Oedipus (1972). Rather than chapters, the book is composed of fifteen "plateaus" — a term borrowed from Gregory Bateson — each dated like a geological stratum and able, in the authors' intention, to be read in almost any order.

Structure and concepts

The opening plateau, "Introduction: Rhizome", contrasts the rhizome — a horizontal, multiply-connected network with no centre or origin — with the arborescent (tree-like) structures of Western thought, genealogy, and the state. Other plateaus treat the geology of morals and stratification, the "body without organs", faciality, linguistics (arguing against Chomsky's universal grammar in favour of a pragmatics of language), becoming-animal, music and the refrain, nomadology and the war machine, and smooth versus striated space.

Key concepts developed across the book include:

  • Assemblage (agencement) — provisional arrangements of bodies, actions, and statements.
  • Deterritorialization and reterritorialization — the breaking and re-forming of codes and territories.
  • Lines of flight — vectors of escape or change within a system.
  • The war machine — a mode of organization exterior to the state apparatus.
  • Minor literature / minoritarian becomings — forms of expression that work from within a major language against it.

Reception and influence

Translated into English by Brian Massumi in 1987, the book became a foundational text for post-structuralist thought, media theory, geography, architecture, and cultural studies. Its notion of the rhizome was widely taken up as a model for the internet and network culture, and its vocabularies of assemblage and deterritorialization remain standard in several humanities disciplines. Critics have found the book by turns exhilarating and wilfully obscure; it remains one of the most cited works of late twentieth-century French philosophy.
