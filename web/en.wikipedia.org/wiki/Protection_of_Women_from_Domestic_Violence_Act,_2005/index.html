Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005

The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 is an Act of the Parliament of India enacted to protect women from domestic violence. It was brought into force by the Indian government and the Ministry of Women and Child Development on 26 October 2006. The Act provides a civil law remedy designed to protect women in a domestic relationship from violence by men, and was the first Indian legislation to give a broad legal definition of "domestic violence".

Definitions

The Act defines domestic violence expansively to include physical, sexual, verbal, emotional and economic abuse, as well as harassment connected with unlawful dowry demands. It covers not only wives but also women in live-in relationships, mothers, sisters, widows, and other women living in a shared household. A "shared household" is defined broadly, and the aggrieved woman has a right to reside in it regardless of title.

Reliefs available

  • Protection orders restraining the respondent from committing further acts of violence or contacting the aggrieved person.
  • Residence orders preventing the respondent from dispossessing the woman from the shared household.
  • Monetary relief for expenses and losses, including loss of earnings, medical expenses and maintenance.
  • Custody orders granting temporary custody of children.
  • Compensation orders for injuries, including mental torture and emotional distress.

Implementation

The Act provides for Protection Officers and registered service providers to assist aggrieved women, and requires magistrates to dispose of applications expeditiously — ordinarily within 60 days. Breach of a protection order is a criminal offence punishable with imprisonment of up to one year, a fine, or both. The Act operates alongside, and does not derogate from, criminal provisions such as Section 498A of the Indian Penal Code.

Criticism and case law

The Act has been praised for its victim-centred, civil-remedy approach, but criticised for uneven implementation, a shortage of Protection Officers, and low awareness. In Hiral P. Harsora v. Kusum Narottamdas Harsora (2016), the Supreme Court struck down the words "adult male" from the definition of "respondent", allowing complaints against female respondents as well.
