Christian apologetics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Christian apologetics (from Greek ἀπολογία, apologia, "a speech in defence") is a branch of Christian theology that defends Christianity against objections and presents reasoned arguments for the faith. Apologists have addressed challenges from philosophy, science, history and other religions across two millennia.

History

The apostle Paul is sometimes regarded as the first apologist, arguing from scripture in the synagogues and from natural philosophy at the Areopagus in Athens. The 2nd-century "Apologists" — Justin Martyr, Athenagoras and Tertullian — defended Christians against pagan charges. Augustine's City of God answered the charge that Christianity had caused the fall of Rome. In the Middle Ages Thomas Aquinas presented the "five ways" of proving God's existence, and Anselm of Canterbury formulated the ontological argument.

Modern schools

Modern apologetics includes several approaches: classical apologetics, which argues first for God's existence (e.g. via the cosmological and teleological arguments) and then for Christian truth; evidentialism, which appeals to historical evidence such as the resurrection; presuppositional apologetics, associated with Cornelius Van Til, which argues that Christian theism is the precondition of intelligible experience; and experiential approaches. Prominent 20th-century apologists include C. S. Lewis, G. K. Chesterton and, in popular form, writers such as William Lane Craig and Ravi Zacharias.
