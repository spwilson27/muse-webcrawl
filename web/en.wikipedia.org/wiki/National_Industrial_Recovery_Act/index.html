National Industrial Recovery Act

National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933
Enacted16 June 1933
Signed byPresident Franklin D. Roosevelt
CreatedNational Recovery Administration (NRA); Public Works Administration (PWA)
StatusDeclared unconstitutional in A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States (1935)

The National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA) was a United States labor law enacted on 16 June 1933, the last major statute of the First Hundred Days, and a centerpiece of the New Deal's first phase. It authorised the President to regulate industry through fair-competition codes intended to end deflationary price-cutting and raise wages, and it guaranteed workers the right to organise and bargain collectively.

Provisions

Title I created the National Recovery Administration (NRA), headed by General Hugh S. Johnson, which approved codes of fair competition for hundreds of industries, setting minimum wages, maximum hours and production rules; businesses displaying the NRA's Blue Eagle symbol pledged compliance, and Roosevelt urged consumers to patronise only them. Section 7(a) of the Act guaranteed employees the right to organise and bargain collectively through representatives of their own choosing — a provision that helped spur a wave of union organising. Title II created the Public Works Administration under Harold Ickes, which spent billions on dams, bridges, schools and other infrastructure; the act also funded the work that became the Tennessee Valley programme's complements.

Schechter and legacy

The NRA's codes were widely criticised as cumbersome, cartel-like and favouring big business, and industrial recovery remained weak. On 27 May 1935 — remembered as the New Deal's "Black Monday" — a unanimous Supreme Court in A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States invalidated the Act, holding that Congress had unconstitutionally delegated legislative power and exceeded the commerce power. Congress salvaged parts of the programme in new statutes: the labour guarantees of Section 7(a) were re-enacted, strengthened, in the National Labor Relations Act of 1935, and wage-and-hour standards returned in the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938.
