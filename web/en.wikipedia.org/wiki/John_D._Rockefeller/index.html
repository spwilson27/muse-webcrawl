John Davison Rockefeller Sr. (8 July 1839 – 23 May 1937) was an American business magnate and philanthropist. He was a co-founder of the Standard Oil Company, which dominated the oil industry and was the first great American business trust. Rockefeller revolutionised the petroleum industry; his fortune, built through relentless cost-cutting, vertical integration and controversial competitive tactics, made him the richest American of all time and is often cited as making him the richest person in modern history.

Early life and career

Born in upstate New York, Rockefeller moved with his family to Cleveland, Ohio, where he worked as a bookkeeper before entering the produce and then oil-refining businesses. In 1870 he and his partners incorporated the Standard Oil Company (Ohio). Through horizontal and vertical consolidation—absorbing competitors, building pipelines and negotiating favourable railroad rebates—Standard Oil controlled over 90 percent of American refineries and pipelines by the early 1880s.

Antitrust and breakup

Standard Oil's dominance attracted public criticism, notably from journalist Ida Tarbell, and government action. In 1911 the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States that the company was an illegal monopoly and ordered it broken into 34 separate companies, many of which—such as ExxonMobil, BP and Chevron—remain among the world's largest firms.

Philanthropy

After retiring from business in the late 1890s, Rockefeller devoted the last four decades of his life to philanthropy, founding the University of Chicago and Rockefeller University and establishing the Rockefeller Foundation in 1913, which funded major advances in medicine, public health and education.
