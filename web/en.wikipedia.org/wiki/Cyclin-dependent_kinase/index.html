Cyclin-dependent kinase

A CDK is active only when bound to a cyclin.

Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) are a family of protein kinases first discovered for their role in regulating the cell cycle. As their name indicates, their enzymatic activity depends on association with regulatory subunits called cyclins, whose levels rise and fall through the cycle. CDKs are also involved in regulating gene transcription, mRNA processing, and the differentiation of nerve cells.

Function in the cell cycle

Different CDK–cyclin complexes drive successive phases of the cell cycle. In animal cells, CDK4 and CDK6 paired with D-type cyclins act in early G1; CDK2 with cyclin E drives entry into S phase and with cyclin A acts through S phase; and CDK1 with cyclin B is the master regulator of mitosis, the successor of the maturation-promoting factor identified in early experiments. CDK activity is further controlled by activating and inhibitory phosphorylations, by CDK-activating kinase, and by inhibitor proteins such as the INK4 and Cip/Kip families, which are important at cell-cycle checkpoints.

Discovery

The genes encoding CDKs were identified through genetic studies of fission and budding yeasts — cdc2 in Schizosaccharomyces pombe and CDC28 in Saccharomyces cerevisiae — and the discovery that cyclins periodically accumulate and are destroyed. Leland Hartwell, Tim Hunt and Paul Nurse received the 2001 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for these discoveries.

Clinical relevance

Because uncontrolled CDK activity contributes to the proliferation of cancer cells, CDKs are targets for anticancer drugs. Inhibitors of CDK4 and CDK6 — palbociclib, ribociclib and abemaciclib — are approved for the treatment of hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer. Mutations in the transcription-associated kinase CDK13 and related genes cause developmental disorders, and CDK5 has an atypical role in the nervous system unrelated to the cell cycle.
