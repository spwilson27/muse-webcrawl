Hodge theory

Hodge theory is a branch of mathematics that studies differential forms on smooth manifolds using methods from partial differential equations. Developed by W. V. D. Hodge in the 1930s, its central result — the Hodge theorem — states that on a compact oriented Riemannian manifold, every de Rham cohomology class has a unique harmonic representative.

Hodge decomposition

Using the Hodge star operator and the Laplace–Beltrami operator on forms, the space of differential forms decomposes into harmonic, exact, and co-exact parts. A form is harmonic if it is annihilated by the Laplacian, and the Hodge theorem identifies the space of harmonic forms with the de Rham cohomology of the manifold, giving concrete analytic representatives for topological invariants such as Betti numbers.

Hodge structures

On a compact Kähler manifold, Hodge theory refines further: the complex cohomology decomposes into subspaces Hp,q of forms of type (p,q), producing a Hodge structure. This decomposition underlies much of modern algebraic geometry. The Hodge conjecture — one of the Millennium Prize Problems — asks whether, on a smooth projective variety, every rational cohomology class in Hp,p is a combination of classes of algebraic cycles.

Applications

Hodge theory informs the study of period mappings, moduli of algebraic varieties, mirror symmetry, and the topology of complex manifolds.
