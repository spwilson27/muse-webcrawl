Perry is an alcoholic beverage made from fermented pears, analogous to the way cider is made from apples. It has been produced for centuries in the west of England, especially Gloucestershire, Herefordshire and Worcestershire, and also in parts of France (where it is called poiré) and other pear-growing regions. Traditional perry is made from perry pears — small, hard, astringent varieties that are largely inedible raw.

Production

Perry pears are harvested in autumn, milled, and pressed; the juice is fermented, traditionally by wild yeasts, then matured. Because perry pears contain sorbitol (an unfermentable sugar), finished perry retains a natural sweetness even when fully fermented, and its high tannin and acidity levels give the best traditional perries a delicate, wine-like character. Sparkling perry, made by the champagne method, is a celebrated speciality; commercially, much "pear cider" sold under the name perry is instead made from imported pear concentrate, a practice that has drawn criticism from traditional producers and CAMRA.

History

Pear fermentation in Britain may date to the Roman period, and perry-making was well established by the seventeenth century in the Three Counties, whose perry pear orchards — with long-lived trees bearing names such as Blakeney Red, Gin and Hendre Huffcap — became a distinctive landscape feature. Production declined sharply in the twentieth century as orchards were grubbed up and tastes shifted, but a revival of interest in traditional orchard drinks since the 1990s has encouraged new planting and the protection of perry under regional food heritage schemes.
