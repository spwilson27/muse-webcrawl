Government shutdowns in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A closed national park entrance during the 2013 federal government shutdown.

In the United States, a government shutdown occurs when Congress fails to enact appropriations legislation or a continuing resolution to fund federal operations, and the president fails to sign such legislation into law. Under the Antideficiency Act, federal agencies must suspend all non-essential functions until funding is restored, and many federal employees are furloughed.

Mechanism

The modern shutdown regime dates to legal opinions issued by Attorney General Benjamin Civiletti in 1980 and 1981, which held that the Antideficiency Act prohibits agencies from spending or obligating funds in the absence of an appropriation, except for functions protecting life and property. Essential employees continue working without pay during a shutdown and receive back pay when funding resumes, as do furloughed employees under the Government Employee Fair Treatment Act of 2019.

Notable shutdowns

Funding gaps occurred several times in the 1970s, but the first true shutdowns under the Civiletti interpretation took place in the early 1980s. The 1995–96 shutdowns, arising from a budget dispute between President Bill Clinton and the Republican Congress, lasted 26 days in total. The 2013 shutdown, centred on the Affordable Care Act, lasted 16 days. The 2018–19 shutdown over funding for a border wall lasted 35 days, the longest in U.S. history, and affected some 800,000 federal employees.

Effects

Shutdowns close national parks and museums, delay passport and permit processing, and suspend many regulatory and scientific activities. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that the 2018–19 shutdown reduced economic output by about $11 billion, of which roughly $3 billion was not recovered.

Comparison

Government shutdowns of this kind are unusual among developed democracies, most of which either continue the previous budget or trigger elections when budgets fail; the phenomenon is largely a product of the U.S. separation of powers and annual appropriations process.
