Short-faced bear

Short-faced bears are members of the subfamily Tremarctinae characterised by a shortened snout relative to other bears. The group includes the living spectacled bear of South America and several extinct North and South American genera, most famously the giant short-faced bear Arctodus simus and the even larger South American Arctotherium angustidens, among the biggest terrestrial mammalian predators known.

Giant short-faced bear

Arctodus simus roamed North America from about 800,000 to 11,000 years ago. Standing perhaps 1.6 metres at the shoulder on all fours and rearing up to 3.4 metres, it weighed an estimated 700–950 kg. Its long limbs suggest it was adapted for covering large territories; researchers debate whether it was an active predator, a kleptoparasite that stole kills from other carnivores, or a more omnivorous forager, with isotope studies indicating a heavily meat-based diet in northern populations.

Evolution and extinction

Tremarctine bears evolved in North America and crossed into South America during the Great American Interchange. The giant forms disappeared at the end of the Pleistocene, in the wave of megafaunal extinctions that coincided with climate change and the arrival of humans in the Americas. Only the small, largely herbivorous spectacled bear (Tremarctos ornatus) survives today.
