Predynastic Egypt

Predynastic Egypt (also written Pre-dynastic Egypt) was the period of ancient Egyptian history before the establishment of the First Dynasty around 3100 BCE, spanning the Neolithic and Chalcolithic cultures of the Nile Valley from the first farming settlements (around 6000 BCE) to the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt under kings such as Narmer.

Cultures

In Lower Egypt, the sequence includes the Faiyum Neolithic, the Merimde culture, and the Maadi culture of the Delta region. In Upper Egypt the key sequence runs from the Badarian culture (c. 5500–4000 BCE), known for fine black-topped pottery and early copper working, through the Amratian (Naqada I) and Gerzean (Naqada II) phases. The Naqada II period saw rapid social stratification, craft specialisation, long-distance trade reaching Nubia and the Levant, and the spread of Upper Egyptian material culture throughout the valley.

Toward unification

In Naqada III (the "Protodynastic" period), rival Upper Egyptian centres at Naqada, Hierakonpolis and Abydos competed for dominance. The royal cemetery at Abydos (Umm el-Qa'ab), the earliest Egyptian writing (labels from tomb U-j), and ceremonial objects such as the Narmer Palette document the emergence of kingship and the state. The traditional account holds that Narmer (identified with Menes) unified the Two Lands, founding the dynastic civilisation that lasted three thousand years. See also ancient Egypt and Narmer Palette.
