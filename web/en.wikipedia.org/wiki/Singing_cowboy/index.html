Singing cowboy

A singing cowboy was a subtype of the archetypal cowboy hero of early Western films, popular in American popular culture from the 1930s through the 1950s. Singing cowboys were depicted wandering the American frontier while strumming a guitar and singing songs, often pausing to dispense folksy wisdom, rescue a damsel in distress, or foil the schemes of villains.

History

The figure emerged with the advent of sound films in the late 1920s. Ken Maynard was among the first to sing on screen in Westerns, but the archetype was perfected by Gene Autry, whose first starring role came in In Old Santa Fe (1934). Autry's enormous success as a recording artist and film star established the formula: a clean-cut, honest hero who relied as much on music and charm as on his six-shooter.

Roy Rogers, billed as the "King of the Cowboys," became Autry's chief rival and eventual successor, starring in a long series of films and later a television programme. Other notable singing cowboys included Tex Ritter, Rex Allen, Eddie Dean, and Monte Hale.

Characteristics

Singing cowboy films combined elements of the Western, the musical, and the morality play. Heroes typically wore elaborate, decorative Western wear, rode a named horse with near-human intelligence, and were accompanied by a comic sidekick. Their songs, drawn from country and Western swing traditions, helped popularise country music nationally.

Legacy

Although the genre faded with the decline of the B-Western in the 1950s, the singing cowboy left a lasting imprint on American country music and on the popular image of the cowboy. The archetype has since been parodied and affectionately revived in films and animation.
