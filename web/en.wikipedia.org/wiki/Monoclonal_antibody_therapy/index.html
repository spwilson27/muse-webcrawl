Monoclonal antibody therapy

Monoclonal antibody therapy is a form of immunotherapy that uses monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) to bind specifically to targeted cells or proteins, in order to stimulate the patient's immune system to attack those cells, block growth signals, or deliver a therapeutic payload. Monoclonal antibodies are identical copies of a single parent antibody, produced by a clone of a single B cell (or an engineered cell line), and are therefore specific to one epitope of an antigen.

History

The hybridoma technique for producing monoclonal antibodies was developed by Georges Köhler and César Milstein in 1975, earning them the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1984. The first therapeutic monoclonal antibody approved by the US FDA was muromonab-CD3 (OKT3) in 1986, used to prevent organ transplant rejection. Early mouse-derived antibodies often provoked human anti-mouse immune responses, which led to the development of chimeric, humanised, and finally fully human antibodies.

Types

Therapeutic mAbs are named according to their structure: murine (-omab), chimeric (-ximab), humanised (-zumab), and fully human (-umab). They may be "naked" (unconjugated) or conjugated to a chemotherapy drug, toxin, or radioactive particle (antibody–drug conjugates).

Applications

Cancer

Antibodies such as rituximab (anti-CD20, for B-cell lymphomas), trastuzumab (anti-HER2, for breast cancer), and cetuximab (anti-EGFR) are mainstays of oncology. Checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab and nivolumab (anti-PD-1) block proteins that restrain the immune response against tumours.

Autoimmune and inflammatory disease

Adalimumab and infliximab, which block tumour necrosis factor (TNF), are widely used for rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn's disease, and psoriasis. Omalizumab (anti-IgE) is used in allergic asthma.

Infectious disease

Palivizumab prevents respiratory syncytial virus infection in infants. During the COVID-19 pandemic, several anti-SARS-CoV-2 monoclonal antibodies received emergency authorisations, though their usefulness waned as new variants evolved.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include infusion reactions, fever, and increased susceptibility to infection where the immune system is suppressed. Rare but serious reactions include cytokine release syndrome and anaphylaxis.
