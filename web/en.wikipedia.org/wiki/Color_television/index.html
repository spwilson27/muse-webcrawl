Color television

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Early colour sets used shadow-mask cathode-ray tubes.

Color television is a television transmission technology that includes color information in the picture, so that video images can be displayed in color on a receiver. It is the successor to black-and-white television, which displays images in shades of gray (grayscale).

History

Inventors experimented with mechanical color systems from the 1920s — John Logie Baird demonstrated a mechanical colour system in 1928 — but practical colour awaited electronic television. In the United States, CBS promoted a field-sequential system approved in 1950, but it was incompatible with existing monochrome sets; RCA's compatible system, in which colour information was encoded in a subcarrier added to the standard signal, won out. The NTSC standard was adopted in 1953, and regular colour broadcasting began in 1954, though colour sets remained expensive luxuries until the mid-1960s, when networks converted prime-time schedules to colour and sales surged.

Standards

Three analogue colour systems dominated the world: NTSC in North America and Japan, PAL in most of Europe, China, and Australia, and SECAM in France, the Soviet Union and its sphere. All three encoded colour as chrominance modulating a subcarrier within the monochrome luminance channel, so that black-and-white sets could still display the picture. Colour receivers decoded the signal into red, green, and blue components displayed by a shadow-mask or aperture-grille cathode-ray tube.

Digital era

Digital television standards (ATSC, DVB, ISDB) transmit colour digitally, typically in YCbCr component form, and modern LCD, plasma, OLED, and LED sets have made the CRT obsolete. High-definition and ultra-high-definition services widened colour gamuts (Rec. 709, then Rec. 2020) and added high dynamic range, continuing the expansion of reproducible colour that began with the first colour broadcasts.
