Anti-realism

In analytic philosophy, anti-realism is a position that encompasses many views which reject realism, understood in various ways. The term was introduced by Michael Dummett, who used it for positions that deny that statements of a given class have objective truth values independent of our means of knowing them, or that reject the existence of the entities a theory appears to posit.

Dummett's formulation

Dummett characterised the dispute between realists and anti-realists as a dispute about the correct theory of meaning. For the realist, meaning is explained in terms of potentially evidence-transcendent truth conditions, and every well-formed statement is determinately true or false (the principle of bivalence). For the anti-realist, truth cannot outrun the possibility of verification; meaning must be explained in terms of the conditions under which a statement can be warranted or asserted. This motivated connections with intuitionistic logic, which rejects the law of excluded middle.

Domain-specific forms

Anti-realist positions are usually adopted for particular domains rather than globally: anti-realism about the past, about other minds, about mathematics (intuitionism and formalism), about morality (error theory or expressivism), and about science (constructive empiricism and instrumentalism). A common strategy across domains is to argue that we could not acquire or manifest the knowledge of recognition-transcendent truths that realism seems to require.
