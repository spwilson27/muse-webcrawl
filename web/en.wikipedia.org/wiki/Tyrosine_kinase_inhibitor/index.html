Tyrosine kinase inhibitor

A tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) is a pharmaceutical drug that inhibits tyrosine kinases, enzymes responsible for the activation of many proteins by signal transduction cascades. The proteins are activated by adding a phosphate group (phosphorylation); TKIs block this step, typically by competing with ATP for the enzyme's binding site. Many TKIs have proven to be effective anti-tumor agents and form a cornerstone of targeted cancer therapy.

Examples and uses

The prototypical TKI is imatinib (Gleevec), approved in 2001, which inhibits the BCR-ABL fusion kinase driving chronic myeloid leukemia, as well as KIT and PDGFR, and transformed CML from a fatal disease into a chronic, manageable condition. Other important TKIs include gefitinib and erlotinib (against EGFR, in lung cancer), osimertinib (active against the T790M resistance mutation), sunitinib and sorafenib (multi-kinase inhibitors used in kidney cancer and other tumors), lapatinib (EGFR/HER2), and ibrutinib (BTK, in B-cell malignancies), though the last targets a kinase via covalent binding.

Resistance and adverse effects

Tumor cells commonly develop resistance through secondary mutations in the kinase domain (such as the BCR-ABL T315I "gatekeeper" mutation), amplification of the target, or activation of bypass pathways, driving the development of second- and third-generation inhibitors. Adverse effects vary by drug and target and can include rash, diarrhea, edema, hypertension, cardiotoxicity, and myelosuppression.
