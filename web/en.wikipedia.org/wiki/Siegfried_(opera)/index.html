Siegfried (opera)

Siegfried
ComposerRichard Wagner
LibrettistRichard Wagner
LanguageGerman
Based onNorse and Germanic legend: the Völsunga saga and the Nibelungenlied

Part ofDer Ring des Nibelungen (third of the four operas)

Siegfried (WWV 86C) is the third of the four music dramas that constitute Richard Wagner's cycle Der Ring des Nibelungen (The Ring of the Nibelung). It premiered at the Bayreuth Festspielhaus on 16 August 1876 as part of the first complete performance of the Ring, conducted by Hans Richter. The title role was created by Georg Unger. Written over an exceptionally long gestation—Wagner laid the score aside after Act 2 in 1857 to compose Tristan und Isolde and Die Meistersinger von Nürnberg, resuming only in 1869—Siegfried is the cycle's scherzo: the most lyrical and overtly heroic of the four operas, telling how the fearless young hero forges his father's sword, kills the dragon Fafner, and awakens Brünnhilde.

Roles

  • Siegfried, son of Siegmund and Sieglinde — tenor
  • Mime, a Nibelung smith — tenor
  • The Wanderer (Wotan) — bass-baritone
  • Alberich, brother of Mime — baritone
  • Fafner, the giant turned dragon — bass
  • Erda, earth goddess — contralto
  • Brünnhilde, a Valkyrie — soprano
  • The Woodbird — soprano

Synopsis

Act 1

In a forest cave, the dwarf Mime tries in vain to forge a sword strong enough for his foster-son Siegfried, whom he hopes to use to win the dragon Fafner's gold. Siegfried, who despises Mime, brings home a bear and shatters another sword. When the Wanderer visits, Mime loses a riddling wager and learns that only one who has never known fear can reforge the broken sword Nothung. Siegfried, ignorant of fear, melts down the fragments, reforges Nothung himself, and splits the anvil in two.

Act 2

Alberich keeps watch outside Fafner's cave. Mime leads Siegfried there; the youth, idly blowing his horn, wakes the dragon and kills him with Nothung. Tasting the dragon's blood, Siegfried understands the Woodbird's song, which tells him of the Tarnhelm and the ring in the cave—and warns him of Mime's treachery. When Mime offers a poisoned drink, Siegfried kills him. Following the Woodbird, he sets off toward a mountain where a sleeping woman lies ringed by fire.

Act 3

The Wanderer summons Erda, whose wisdom is failing, then bars Siegfried's path; when the god taunts him, Siegfried shatters Wotan's spear with Nothung, and the god yields. Siegfried passes through the flames and finds Brünnhilde asleep. He wakes her with a kiss; after initial terror she hails the day, and the two unite in an ecstatic duet—love accepted, and the gods' end foreshadowed, in Götterdämmerung.

Music

The opera is famous for the lyrical "Forest Murmurs" of Act 2, the forging songs of Act 1, and its luminous orchestral palette dominated by horns. Siegfried is among the most punishing roles in the tenor repertory; notable interpreters include Lauritz Melchior, Wolfgang Windgassen and, in the modern era, Siegfried Jerusalem.
