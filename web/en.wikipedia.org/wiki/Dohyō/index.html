Dohyō

Used inSumo

A dohyō (Japanese: 土俵) is the ring in which sumo wrestling bouts are held. A modern dohyō is a circle of rice-straw bales (tawara) partially embedded in a platform of clay and sand. The circle is 4.55 metres (14.9 ft) in diameter. In professional sumo (ōzumō), the dohyō is mounted on a square clay platform and surmounted by a suspended roof resembling that of a Shinto shrine, called a tsuriyane.

Construction

The dohyō is rebuilt for each professional tournament by the yobidashi (ring attendants) from a special clay sifted and prepared in advance. The platform is roughly 66 cm high and 6.7 m square. Rice-straw bales are buried to mark the circular boundary; the two shikiri-sen starting lines are painted or inlaid at the centre. A thin layer of sand covers the surface inside the ring, so that the gyōji (referee) can judge whether a wrestler's foot has touched outside by the marks left in the sand. Dohyō used in training at stables (heya) are simpler practice rings built directly into a clay floor.

Sacred character

The dohyō is considered a sacred space. Before tournaments, a Shinto purification ceremony (dohyō-matsuri) is performed, in which offerings such as salt, rice, and dried squid are buried in the centre of the ring. Historically, the exclusion of women from the dohyō has been a long-standing tradition defended by the Japan Sumo Association on religious grounds, a practice that has periodically attracted public controversy when women have been asked to leave the ring at events.

Dohyō-iri

The dohyō-iri is the ring-entering ceremony performed by wrestlers of the top divisions before their day's bouts, featuring the traditional leg-stamping (shiko) movements intended to drive away demons and warm up the body.
