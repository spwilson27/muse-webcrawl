The House of Luxembourg (Czech: Lucemburkové) was a late-medieval European royal family, whose members became counts, then dukes, of Luxembourg, as well as kings of Bohemia and Hungary and emperors of the Holy Roman Empire.

Rise

The family traced its origins to Count Siegfried, who in 963 acquired the rock of Lucilinburhuc — modern Luxembourg City. Descended in the female line from the counts of Ardennes, the house rose to European prominence when Count Henry VII was elected Holy Roman Emperor in 1308. Henry married his son John to the Bohemian heiress Elizabeth in 1310, acquiring the crown of Bohemia.

The imperial line

John of Bohemia ("the Blind") became a byword for chivalry and died fighting for France at the Battle of Crécy (1346). His son Charles IV (r. 1346–1378), Holy Roman Emperor and King of Bohemia, made Prague an imperial capital, founded Charles University (1348), and issued the Golden Bull of 1356, fixing the Empire's electoral constitution. His sons Wenceslaus IV and Sigismund — King of Hungary and later Emperor, convener of the Council of Constance — continued the line; with Sigismund's death in 1437 the male line became extinct, and its inheritance passed through his daughter Elizabeth to the Habsburgs.

Cadet branches

French cadet lines of the house — Luxembourg-Ligny, Luxembourg-Brienne, and Luxembourg-Piney — played a prominent role in the French Wars of Religion and the Fronde, producing marshals and peers of France into the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Legacy

The dynasty gave its name to the modern Grand Duchy of Luxembourg (ruled since the 19th century by the House of Nassau-Weilburg, not the medieval Luxembourgs) and left landmarks such as Prague's Charles Bridge and Karlštejn Castle.
