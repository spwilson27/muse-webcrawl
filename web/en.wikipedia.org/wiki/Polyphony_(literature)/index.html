Polyphony (literature)

In literature, polyphony (Russian: полифония) is a concept introduced by the philosopher and literary theorist Mikhail Bakhtin in his study of Fyodor Dostoevsky, Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics (1929, revised 1963). Borrowing the term from music — where polyphony denotes multiple independent melodic voices — Bakhtin argued that Dostoevsky created a new kind of novel in which the characters' voices and worldviews are not subordinated to the author's own ideology.

Key features

  • Plurality of independent consciousnesses: heroes are subjects of their own discourse, not objects of the author's finalizing judgment; Raskolnikov, Ivan Karamazov, and the Grand Inquisitor argue positions the narrative never simply refutes.
  • Dialogism: meaning arises in the interaction of voices; every utterance is "dialogized," oriented toward an anticipated response.
  • Unfinalizability: characters remain open and unfinished, capable of surprising themselves and the reader; the polyphonic novel has no conclusive authorial verdict.
  • The idea as hero: ideas are tested by being embodied in persons who live them out in dialogue.

Contrast with the monologic novel

Bakhtin contrasted Dostoevsky with writers such as Tolstoy, whose novels he characterized as fundamentally monologic: a single authorial consciousness governs the represented world, and characters' speech serves the author's design. Bakhtin linked polyphony to broader features of Dostoevsky's art — carnival, the menippean satire tradition, and threshold scenes where opposing worldviews collide.

Influence

The concept has become central to narrative theory and to readings of modernist and postmodern fiction, and is often applied — sometimes loosely — to any novel with multiple narrators or unresolved competing viewpoints.
