Confidence and supply

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Confidence and supply is an arrangement in a parliamentary democracy where a political party or independent members of parliament grant support to the government in motions of confidence and appropriation or supply votes, by either voting in favour or abstaining — without joining the government or a formal coalition. In return the supporting party typically negotiates policy concessions, consultation rights and funding for its priorities, recorded in a written agreement.

Famous instances include the UK Conservative government's agreement with the Democratic Unionist Party (2017–19), which supplied the majority for confidence and Brexit legislation in exchange for £1 billion of additional spending for Northern Ireland; New Zealand's repeated post-MMP arrangements (2005, 2008, 2017) that normalised confidence-and-supply as a standard instrument; and Australian and Canadian minority-government pacts. The arrangement's appeal is flexibility — support on essentials, freedom on the rest — and its risk is ambiguity of accountability: voters and journalists must work out who is responsible for decisions the supporter enabled but did not take.
