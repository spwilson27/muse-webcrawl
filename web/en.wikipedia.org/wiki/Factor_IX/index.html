Factor IX

Factor IX, also known as Christmas factor, is one of the serine proteases of the coagulation system; it belongs to the peptidase family S1. Deficiency of this protein causes haemophilia B, which is why it was given the name Christmas factor after Stephen Christmas, the first patient described with the deficiency.

Factor IX is a vitamin K-dependent zymogen in the intrinsic coagulation pathway.

Physiology

Factor IX is produced in the liver as an inactive zymogen and requires vitamin K-dependent gamma-carboxylation of its glutamate residues for activity. In the intrinsic pathway of coagulation, it is activated by factor XIa (or by the tissue factor–factor VIIa complex), and its activated form, factor IXa, works with its cofactor factor VIIIa to activate factor X to factor Xa, leading to thrombin generation and clot formation.

Clinical significance

Inherited deficiency of factor IX causes haemophilia B, an X-linked bleeding disorder treated with plasma-derived or recombinant factor IX concentrates. Recombinant products and extended half-life versions are standard therapies, and factor IX gene therapy has been developed as a long-term treatment.
