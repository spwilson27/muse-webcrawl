Thomas precession

Thomas precession, named after Llewellyn Thomas, is a relativistic correction to the precession of the spin of an elementary particle or the rotation of the axes of a gyroscope moving along a curved trajectory, such as an orbit. It arises in special relativity from the non-commutativity of successive Lorentz boosts in different directions: the composition of two non-collinear boosts is equivalent to a boost combined with a spatial rotation, known as a Wigner rotation.

History

Thomas precession had been anticipated in relative-rotation effects discussed by Silberstein and others, but it was Llewellyn Thomas who in 1926 used it to resolve a factor-of-two discrepancy in the fine structure of atomic spectra. The spin–orbit coupling energy computed in the rest frame of the nucleus was twice the observed value; taking into account the precession of the electron's rest frame introduced the famous "Thomas half", bringing theory into agreement with experiment. Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit initially reacted with disbelief but the correction was quickly accepted.

Description

For a particle with velocity v and acceleration a, the Thomas precession angular velocity is proportional to the cross product of velocity and acceleration divided by c², with a Lorentz-factor dependence; for circular motion at low speed it reduces to a precession opposite in sign and half the magnitude of the naive spin–orbit precession. In general relativity the effect combines with geodetic (de Sitter) precession and frame-dragging for gyroscopes in orbit, contributions tested by the Gravity Probe B satellite mission.

Applications

  • Fine structure of atomic spectra (spin–orbit coupling)
  • Dynamics of relativistic particles in accelerators and storage rings
  • Interpretation of the Bargmann–Michel–Telegdi equation for spin motion
  • Tests of general relativity with orbiting gyroscopes
