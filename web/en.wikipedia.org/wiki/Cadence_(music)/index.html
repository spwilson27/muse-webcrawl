Cadence (music)

In Western musical theory, a cadence is the end of a phrase in which the melody or harmony creates a sense of full or partial resolution, especially in tonal music. A cadence is a melodic or harmonic configuration that creates a sense of resolution or finality.

Types

Cadences are classified by the chords or scale degrees involved and by their strength as points of arrival:

  • Perfect authentic cadence (PAC): dominant (V) to tonic (I), both in root position, with the tonic in the melody — the strongest form of closure.
  • Imperfect authentic cadence (IAC): V–I with some element inverted, substituted, or with a non-tonic melody note.
  • Half cadence (HC): a phrase ending on the dominant, sounding unfinished.
  • Plagal cadence: subdominant (IV) to tonic (I), the "Amen" cadence of hymns.
  • Deceptive cadence: V resolving to an unexpected chord, often vi.
  • Picardy third: a minor-mode piece ending on a major tonic chord.

History

The concept descends from the clausulae of medieval polyphony and the standard contrapuntal formulas codified in the Renaissance. In the Baroque and Classical periods cadential formulas structured phrases, periods and whole movements, and composers played with evaded and extended cadences for expressive effect.

Rhythmic and melodic cadences

Beyond harmony, the term also applies to rhythmic patterns (such as the stock patterns of dance music) and to the concluding flourish of a solo concerto movement, where the cadenza occurs near a final cadence.
