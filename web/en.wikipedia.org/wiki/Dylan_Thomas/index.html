Dylan Thomas

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Dylan Thomas in New York in the early 1950s: the poet heavy-eyed at a café table during his American reading tours]

Dylan Marlais Thomas (27 October 1914 – 9 November 1953) was a Welsh poet and writer whose incantatory, sound-drunk verse and radio plays made him the most famous British poet of his generation — and whose drinking and early death in New York fixed the myth of the doomed bard. His best-known works are the poems 'Do not go gentle into that good night' (1951, a villanelle for his dying father), 'And death shall have no dominion' (1933) and 'Fern Hill' (1945), the stories of Portrait of the Artist as a Young Dog (1940), and the 'play for voices' Under Milk Wood (published 1954).

Born in Swansea, the son of an English teacher, Thomas published most of his mature poems by his early twenties — 18 Poems (1934) announced a prodigy of compressed, biblical, bodily imagery worked, he said, for the sound before the sense.

Radio, America and Milk Wood

Wartime film scripts and BBC broadcasts — his reading voice, rich and booming, made him a radio star — led to the American reading tours of 1950–53, where college audiences swooned and Thomas drank catastrophically. Under Milk Wood, his dream-day in the fictional Welsh town of Llareggub, was performed in New York in May 1953; six months later, on the fourth tour, he collapsed at the Chelsea Hotel after a binge and died in St Vincent's Hospital, aged 39.

Legacy

The cause of death (alcohol, pneumonia, medical error) is still argued; the work is not. Bob Dylan took his stage surname in homage (he has given shifting accounts), and Thomas's Swansea is now a literary landscape of boathouse, statue and festival.
