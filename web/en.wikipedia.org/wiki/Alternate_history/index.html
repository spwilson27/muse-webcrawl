Alternate history

Alternate history (also called alternative history, or uchronia) is a genre of speculative fiction in which one or more historical events have different results than in real history. Its works explore "what if" questions: they posit a point of divergence (PoD) from the actual timeline and imagine how subsequent events would unfold differently.

Characteristics

Most alternate histories change a single consequential event — a battle won instead of lost, a leader assassinated or spared — and trace plausible consequences. Common subjects include a victory for Nazi Germany in the Second World War (as in Philip K. Dick's The Man in the High Castle), a Confederate victory in the American Civil War (Harry Turtledove's works), or the survival of the Roman Empire. The genre overlaps with science fiction, historical fiction, and counterfactual history, the latter being a tool used by some historians to test causal claims.

History of the genre

Early examples include Livy speculating about Alexander the Great attacking Rome, and Louis Geoffroy's 1836 Napoléon et la conquête du monde. The genre flourished in 20th-century pulp magazines and was recognised with its own Sidewise Award from 1995. Alternate history is also popular in video games and television, such as the series For All Mankind, which imagines the Soviet Union landing on the Moon first.
