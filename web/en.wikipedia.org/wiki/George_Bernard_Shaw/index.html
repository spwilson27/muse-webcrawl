George Bernard Shaw

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: George Bernard Shaw in old age: the playwright with white beard and piercing eyes, photographed by Karsh (1943)]

George Bernard Shaw (26 July 1856 – 2 November 1950) was an Irish playwright, critic and polemicist, the dominant British dramatist of his age and the only person to win both a Nobel Prize in Literature (1925) and an Academy Award (for the screenplay of Pygmalion, 1938). He wrote more than sixty plays, turned drama into a vehicle for ideas — socialism, feminism, eugenics (which dates badly), religion and war — and wrote prefaces as entertaining as the plays.

His major works include Arms and the Man (1894), Candida (1894), The Devil's Disciple (1897), Caesar and Cleopatra (1898), Man and Superman (1903), Major Barbara (1905), The Doctor's Dilemma (1906), Pygmalion (1913), Heartbreak House (1919) and Saint Joan (1923).

From critic to dramatist

Born in Dublin to a feckless father and a musical mother, Shaw arrived in London in 1876, failed as a novelist, and made himself through journalism, music and theatre criticism, and Fabian socialism — lecturing, pamphleteering (the Fabian Essays, 1889) and reviewing Ibsen into English consciousness with The Quintessence of Ibsenism (1891). His early 'Plays Pleasant and Unpleasant' (1898) announced the drama of discussion.

Celebrity and sage

By 1914 Shaw was world-famous and world-controversial: Pygmalion (the Cinderella of phonetics that became My Fair Lady) and Saint Joan sealed the Nobel. He lived to 94 at Shaw's Corner, Ayot St Lawrence, a vegetarian sage courted by dictators and film stars alike — his late political judgements, on Stalin among others, remain the blot on his record his admirers debate.
