American Renaissance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The American Renaissance, in American literature, was a period roughly from the 1830s to around the Civil War in which American literature came of age as an expression of a national spirit. The term shares its naming with the Renaissance, and is closely associated with the related literary movement known as transcendentalism.

Overview

Scholar F. O. Matthiessen coined the term in his influential 1941 book American Renaissance: Art and Expression in the Age of Emerson and Whitman, which focused on five writers: Ralph Waldo Emerson, Henry David Thoreau, Nathaniel Hawthorne, Herman Melville, and Walt Whitman. The period saw the publication of landmark works including Emerson's essays, Thoreau's Walden (1854), Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter (1850) and The House of the Seven Gables (1851), Melville's Moby-Dick (1851), and the first edition of Whitman's Leaves of Grass (1855). The poetry of Emily Dickinson, though largely unpublished in her lifetime, also belongs to this era.

Characteristics

American Renaissance writers drew on European romanticism while seeking distinctly American subjects: the frontier, democratic ideals, individualism, and the moral tensions of a slaveholding republic. Later scholarship expanded the canon beyond Matthiessen's five authors to include women writers such as Harriet Beecher Stowe, whose Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852) was the era's best-selling novel, along with slave narratives by Frederick Douglass and others, and sentimental and reform literature.
