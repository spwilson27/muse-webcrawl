A Proth number takes the form N = k·2n + 1, where k is an odd positive integer and n is a positive integer such that k < 2n. A Proth prime is a Proth number that is prime. They are named after the French farmer and amateur mathematician François Proth (1852–1879).

Proth's theorem

Proth's theorem gives an efficient primality test: if for some integer a, the congruence a(N−1)/2 ≡ −1 (mod N) holds, then N is prime. Conversely, if N is prime, such an a (a quadratic non-residue) exists. This makes Proth primes attractive targets in the search for very large primes, because testing them is much easier than general primality proving.

Examples and related forms

The first Proth primes are 3, 5, 13, 17, 41, 97, 113, 193, 241, 257, 337, 353, 401, 577, 641, 673, 769, 929, 1153… When k = 1 the numbers are Fermat numbers, so prime Fermat numbers are Proth primes; when k = 3 they include Cullen-like forms, and the case N = 3·2n + 1 arises in Sierpiński-type problems. Several of the largest known primes found by distributed computing projects (notably PrimeGrid) are Proth primes, including primes with millions of digits.

Proth numbers that are not prime

Sierpiński numbers are odd k for which every number k·2n + 1 is composite; whether 78,557 is the smallest such k is the subject of the Sierpiński problem, attacked by large-scale computer searches over the remaining candidate values of k.
