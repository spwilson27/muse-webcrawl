Isle of Jura

Jura

Gaelic name: Diùra

Location: Inner Hebrides, Argyll and Bute, Scotland

Main settlement: Craighouse

Population: around 200

Highest point: Beinn an Òir, 785 m

Known for: The Paps of Jura, Jura whisky, red deer

The Isle of Jura (Scottish Gaelic: Diùra) is an island in the Inner Hebrides of Scotland, lying northeast of Islay. It is one of the most sparsely populated Scottish islands, with roughly 200 inhabitants, famously outnumbered by some 5,000–6,500 red deer — the island's name is thought to derive from the Old Norse Dýrøy, "deer island". Much of the island is covered by large sporting estates and blanket bog.

Geography

Jura is dominated by three steep quartzite cones, the Paps of Jura: Beinn an Òir (785 m, a Corbett), Beinn Shiantaidh and Beinn a' Chaolais, visible from much of the southern Hebrides and the Kintyre peninsula. The west coast is wild and largely roadless, with raised beaches and natural arches; at the island's northern tip, the Gulf of Corryvreckan between Jura and Scarba contains the Corryvreckan whirlpool, one of the largest tidal whirlpools in the world. The only road of any length runs up the east coast from the ferry slip at Feolin to the village of Craighouse and onward toward Ardlussa.

History and culture

Jura was part of the medieval Lordship of the Isles and long associated with the Campbells. Craighouse, the island's only village, is home to the Jura distillery, founded in 1810 and rebuilt in 1963, producing single malt Scotch whisky.

The island is celebrated in literature: George Orwell lived at Barnhill, a remote farmhouse at the north end, from 1946 and there wrote most of Nineteen Eighty-Four, describing Jura in a letter as "a very ungetatable place". In 1994 the pop group The KLF burned a million pounds in cash at Ardfin boathouse on Jura, an act that became a notorious episode in British art and music history. Access is normally via Islay, using the short vehicle ferry from Port Askaig across the Sound of Islay.
