Chữ Nôm

Chữ Nôm (𡨸喃, literally "southern script") is a logographic writing system formerly used to write the Vietnamese language. It uses a mixture of borrowed Chinese characters (chữ Hán) and locally invented characters modelled on the same principles to represent Vietnamese sounds, tones and native words that had no Chinese equivalent.

History

Vietnamese scholars adapted Chinese characters to their own language from roughly the thirteenth century, after Vietnam regained independence from China. A much-cited early example is the use of Nôm characters in names and inscriptions under the Trần dynasty. The system never became the primary vehicle of state administration — that role belonged to classical Chinese — but it flourished in literature. The greatest work in chữ Nôm is The Tale of Kiều (Truyện Kiều), the epic poem by Nguyễn Du (1765–1820), regarded as Vietnam's national poem. The reforming emperor Hồ Quý Ly promoted Nôm in the early 1400s, and the Tây Sơn regime used it in official documents in the late eighteenth century.

Character formation

Nôm characters were formed in several ways: direct borrowing of a Chinese character for its sound or meaning, and, most characteristically, the creation of phono-semantic compounds in which one component hints at the pronunciation and another at the meaning of a native Vietnamese word. Because tones had to be encoded in the sound component, individual characters were often complex, and the total inventory ran to many thousands of graphs with little standardisation.

Decline

The Latin-based Vietnamese alphabet, developed by Catholic missionaries in the seventeenth century and known as chữ Quốc ngữ, gradually displaced both chữ Hán and chữ Nôm. Under French colonial rule and with early twentieth-century educational reforms, Quốc ngữ became the sole script of mass literacy, and Nôm fell out of general use. Today it is studied by scholars and used ceremonially; many Nôm characters were encoded in Unicode, and digitisation projects are preserving Nôm literature.
