Clan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A clan is a group of people united by actual or perceived kinship and descent. Even if lineage details are unknown, a clan may claim descent from a founding member or an apical ancestor, who may serve as a symbol of the group's unity. Clans can be matrilineal, patrilineal, or cognatic, and membership may be hereditary, adoptive, or achieved through ritual incorporation.

Scottish clans are among the most widely recognised examples of clan organisation.

Characteristics

Anthropologists distinguish a clan — a descent group whose members claim a common ancestor but cannot necessarily trace all genealogical links — from a lineage, whose members can. Clans are often exogamous (members must marry outside the clan), may be associated with a totem, a territory, a distinctive name, crest, or tartan, and may be headed by a chief. Groups of clans sharing a more remote ancestor form a phratry; clans within a society are commonly ranked and segment into sub-clans.

Examples

Well-known clan systems include the Scottish clans of the Highlands, organised around chiefs, tartans, and clan societies; Irish septs associated with a family name and territory; Japanese uji of the classical period; Korean bon-gwan clan seats tracked in jokbo genealogies; Somali clan-families; Pashtun tribal-clan structures; Arab clan ('ashira) organisation; and the matriclans of many Indigenous peoples of North America and Aboriginal Australian groups.

History and decline

Clans historically provided law, defence, land management, and welfare in stateless or weakly governed societies. Centralising states, colonisation, and land reform often suppressed or co-opted clan institutions — as with the suppression of the Scottish clan system after the Jacobite rising of 1745 — yet clan identities remain socially and politically significant in many regions, and diaspora clan associations flourish worldwide.
