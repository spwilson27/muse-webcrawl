Oberth effect

In astronautics, a powered flyby, or Oberth manoeuvre, is a manoeuvre in which a spacecraft falls into a gravitational well and then uses its engines to further accelerate as it is falling, thereby achieving additional change in velocity more efficiently than the same impulse applied outside the well. The increased efficiency is explained by the Oberth effect, in which the use of a reaction engine at higher speeds generates a greater change in mechanical energy than its use at lower speeds.

Explanation

Rocket engines produce the same force regardless of their velocity. A rocket acting on a fixed object produces no useful work, whereas a rocket acting while moving fast does more work because the exhaust is left with less kinetic energy and the vehicle gains more. The effect is named after Transylvanian Saxon physicist Hermann Oberth, who described it in 1927. In practice, Oberth manoeuvres are performed at periapsis — the point of closest approach and highest orbital velocity — for example during interplanetary injection burns and lunar or planetary capture burns.
