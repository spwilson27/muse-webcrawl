How Long Is the Coast of Britain?

"How Long Is the Coast of Britain? Statistical Self-Similarity and Fractional Dimension" is a paper by the mathematician Benoit Mandelbrot, first published in the journal Science in 1967. In it Mandelbrot discussed the paradox, earlier noted by Lewis Fry Richardson, that the measured length of a coastline depends on the length of the measuring ruler: the smaller the ruler, the more detail is captured and the longer the coastline appears, without converging on a single value.

The coastline paradox

Mandelbrot argued that a coastline has a fractional (Hausdorff) dimension between 1 and 2 — about 1.25 for the west coast of Britain — so that its "length" is properly described by a power law relating measured length to ruler size rather than by a single number. The paper drew on Richardson's empirical finding that measured lengths of frontiers and coasts increase systematically as the unit of measurement decreases.

Significance

The paper became one of the foundational texts of fractal geometry, a term Mandelbrot coined in 1975. It popularised the idea that natural forms exhibit self-similarity across scales and that fractional dimensions quantify their roughness. The "coastline paradox" remains a standard illustration in geography, cartography and mathematics teaching of why the length of a natural coastline has no unique, scale-independent answer.
