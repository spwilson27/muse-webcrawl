Interconnection network

An interconnection network is a programmable system of switches and links that connects processors, memories and other components so they can exchange data, used in settings from on-chip networks and multiprocessors to supercomputers and data centres. The design of the network — its topology, routing, switching and flow control — largely determines the performance, cost and scalability of the machine as a whole.

Topologies

  • Direct networks, where each node combines computation with a router: meshes and tori (popular in supercomputers), hypercubes, trees and rings.
  • Indirect networks, built from switching elements separate from the endpoints: crossbars (ideal performance, quadratic cost), and multistage networks such as the fat tree, Clos and butterfly networks.

Key metrics include diameter (the longest shortest path), bisection bandwidth, node degree and the cost in pins and cabling. Modern high-performance systems — Cray and IBM supercomputers, GPU clusters for machine learning, and cloud data-centre fabrics — use tori, fat trees and dragonfly topologies, with technologies such as InfiniBand, Slingshot and NVLink. Routing may be deterministic or adaptive, and wormhole switching, in which a packet streams through switches in flits, is common in low-latency designs.

Evaluation

Because the network often becomes the bottleneck long before processors do, interconnection design balances latency, throughput under adversarial traffic, fault tolerance and cost. The same mathematics underlies packet-switched networks generally, though the term is most often used for the tightly coupled fabrics inside machines and data centres.
