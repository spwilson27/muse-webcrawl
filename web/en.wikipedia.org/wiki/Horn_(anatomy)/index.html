Horn (anatomy)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A horn is a permanent pointed projection on the head of various animals, consisting of a covering of keratin and other proteins surrounding a core of living bone. Horns are distinct from antlers, which are made of solid bone, are shed annually, and lack a keratin sheath.

Animals with horns

True horns are found mainly in even-toed ungulates (artiodactyls), particularly the cattle family Bovidae — including cattle, sheep, goats, buffalo, and antelopes — in which both males and (usually smaller) females may bear them. Horns typically have a curved or spiral shape, in part because of the way they grow from the base.

Other structures commonly called horns include the single or paired keratinous horns of rhinoceroses (made entirely of compacted keratin, without a bony core), the ossicones of giraffes, the pronghorn's branched keratin sheath (which is shed annually), and horn-like projections in some reptiles, insects, and dinosaurs such as Triceratops.

Growth and structure

Horns begin growing soon after birth and continue throughout the animal's life. They grow from the base outward; damage to a horn sheath may partially regrow, but a horn lost entirely does not regenerate in bovids. The bony core contains blood vessels and nerves and is an extension of the skull's frontal bone.

Function

Horns serve in defence against predators, in combat between males during the breeding season, and in social display and dominance signalling. Humans have long used horn for tools, containers, musical instruments, and ornament, and domestic cattle are frequently dehorned or bred polled for safety.
