Norse colonization of North America

The Norse colonization of North America began in the late 10th century, when seafarers from Iceland and Greenland — descendants of the Vikings — explored and briefly settled the north-eastern coast of present-day Canada. Around the year 1000, Leif Erikson reached a land he called Vinland, almost five centuries before Christopher Columbus. Norse settlement was confined to a small base camp, identified with the archaeological site at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, and was abandoned within a decade or so, though Greenland Norse continued occasional voyages to Markland for timber for centuries thereafter.

The sagas

The voyages are described in two Icelandic sagas written down in the 13th century: the Saga of the Greenlanders and the Saga of Erik the Red. According to the accounts, Bjarni Herjólfsson was blown off course in 986 and sighted unknown lands to the west without landing. Around 1000 Leif Erikson retraced the route, naming Helluland ("stone land," probably Baffin Island), Markland ("forest land," probably Labrador), and Vinland ("wine land" or "pasture land"), where his party wintered. Later expeditions, notably that of Thorfinn Karlsefni, attempted settlement and encountered Indigenous peoples the Norse called Skrælings; conflict with them, together with the settlements' small size and distance from Greenland, contributed to the withdrawal. Snorri Thorfinnsson, born in Vinland, is regarded as the first child of European descent born in the Americas.

Archaeological evidence

For centuries the Vinland sagas were often dismissed as legend. In 1960 the Norwegian explorer Helge Ingstad and his wife, archaeologist Anne Stine Ingstad, located and excavated a Norse settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows at the northern tip of Newfoundland: eight turf-walled buildings, a smithy with iron slag, and artefacts including a bronze ringed pin and a spindle whorl proved a Norse presence around the year 1000. Butternuts found at the site — which do not grow in Newfoundland — indicate the Norse travelled further south, at least to the Gulf of St Lawrence region. The site was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1978. In 2021, a study of tree rings marked by the cosmic-ray event of 993 CE established that Norse were felling trees at L'Anse aux Meadows in exactly the year 1021 — the earliest precisely dated European presence in the Americas.

Aftermath

The Vinland settlement was short-lived and left no permanent colony; the Norse Greenland settlements themselves disappeared by the 15th century. Knowledge of the western voyages survived in Icelandic manuscripts and Adam of Bremen's account of Vinland, and the episodes were revived as national legend in the 19th and 20th centuries — including the celebration of Leif Erikson Day in the United States — but had no practical influence on the age of exploration that began in 1492.
