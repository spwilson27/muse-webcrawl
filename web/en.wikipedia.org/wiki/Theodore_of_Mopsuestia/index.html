Theodore of Mopsuestia (Greek: Θεόδωρος Μοψουεστίας; c. 350 – 428) was a Christian theologian and bishop of Mopsuestia in Cilicia from 392 until his death. He was the foremost biblical scholar of the School of Antioch and a major figure in the development of early Christology. In the Church of the East he is honoured as "Theodore the Interpreter" (Mpashqana), while in the Chalcedonian churches his writings were posthumously condemned in the Three-Chapter Controversy.

Life

Born in Antioch, Theodore studied rhetoric under Libanius alongside John Chrysostom and became a monk and pupil of Diodore of Tarsus. Ordained priest in 383, he was consecrated bishop of Mopsuestia in 392 and served for 36 years, dying in communion with the Church in 428—before the controversies that would attach his name to "Nestorianism", the doctrine associated with his pupil Nestorius.

Work and theology

Theodore championed a literal-historical method of biblical interpretation, resisting the allegorical excesses of the Alexandrian school, and wrote commentaries on most of the Bible, of which many survive in Syriac translation. His catechetical homilies, rediscovered in Syriac in the twentieth century, are a key source for early liturgy. In Christology he emphasised the full humanity of Christ and the distinction of his two natures, describing their union in terms of indwelling "by good pleasure"—a position later critics charged with dividing Christ into two persons.

Condemnation and rehabilitation

More than a century after his death, passages from Theodore's writings were condemned as part of the "Three Chapters" by the Second Council of Constantinople (553) under pressure from Emperor Justinian, making him one of the few figures anathematised posthumously by an ecumenical council. He remains, however, the supreme exegetical authority of the Assyrian Church of the East, which venerates him as a saint and doctor.
