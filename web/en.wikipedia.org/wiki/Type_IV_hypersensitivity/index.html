Type IV hypersensitivity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Type IV hypersensitivity, also called delayed-type hypersensitivity or cell-mediated hypersensitivity, is one of the four types of hypersensitivity reaction in the Gell and Coombs classification. Unlike types I–III, it is not antibody-mediated; instead it is caused by T lymphocytes and typically takes 24–72 hours to develop after exposure.

Mechanism

Sensitised CD4+ Th1 cells, on re-encountering antigen presented by macrophages, release cytokines such as interferon-gamma, recruiting and activating macrophages that cause local tissue damage. CD8+ cytotoxic T cells may also directly kill target cells.

Examples

Classic examples include the tuberculin skin test (Mantoux test), allergic contact dermatitis caused by poison ivy, nickel or latex, and the granulomatous inflammation seen in tuberculosis and sarcoidosis. Type IV reactions are also involved in transplant rejection and in some drug eruptions, including Stevens–Johnson syndrome.

Subtypes

The reaction is sometimes subdivided into IVa (Th1/macrophage), IVb (Th2/eosinophil), IVc (cytotoxic T cell) and IVd (T-cell driven neutrophilic) patterns, particularly in the classification of drug hypersensitivity reactions.
