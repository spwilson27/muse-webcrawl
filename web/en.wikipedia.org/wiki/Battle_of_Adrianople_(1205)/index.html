Battle of Adrianople (1205)

The Battle of Adrianople was fought on 14 April 1205 between the Bulgarians and Cuman allies under Tsar Kaloyan and the Crusader forces of the newly established Latin Empire led by Emperor Baldwin I. It was one of the most decisive defeats suffered by the Crusaders in the aftermath of the Fourth Crusade: the Latin heavy cavalry was drawn into an ambush by feigned retreat and destroyed, and Baldwin himself was captured, dying later in captivity in Tarnovo.

Background

After the Crusaders captured Constantinople in April 1204 and founded the Latin Empire, relations with Kaloyan's Bulgaria deteriorated. Greek cities in Thrace, resenting Latin rule, revolted in early 1205 and appealed to Kaloyan. When the Bulgarians seized Adrianople's environs, Baldwin marched from Constantinople to relieve the city, against the advice of the aged Venetian doge Enrico Dandolo and other veterans who counselled delay.

Battle

On 13 April, Cuman horse archers skirmished with the Crusader outposts and lured some knights into a rash pursuit. The following day, Good Friday, Kaloyan concealed the bulk of his army in broken ground and ravines. His Cumans again attacked and then feigned flight; the Latin knights, led by Count Louis of Blois and despite orders to hold formation, charged in pursuit and were enveloped. Repeated Cuman attacks wore down the Crusader cavalry throughout the day. Louis of Blois was killed, Baldwin was captured, and the survivors under Geoffrey of Villehardouin and the marshal of Champagne organised a disciplined night retreat to Rodosto.

Aftermath

The defeat shattered the military strength of the young Latin Empire and halted its expansion into Thrace. Baldwin died in captivity — the exact circumstances are unclear — and was succeeded by his brother Henry of Flanders, whose diplomacy stabilised the empire. Kaloyan went on to ravage Thrace and take Philippopolis, though his harsh treatment of Greek cities drove many back into alliance with the Latins. The battle is often cited as a classic example of steppe-style feigned retreat tactics defeating Western heavy cavalry.
