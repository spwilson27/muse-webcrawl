A homeobox is a DNA sequence of about 180 base pairs found within genes involved in regulating development (morphogenesis) in animals, plants and fungi. It encodes a protein domain, the homeodomain of about 60 amino acids, which binds DNA and acts as a transcription factor, switching other genes on or off. Homeobox genes — above all the Hox genes — are fundamental to laying out the body plan of embryos.

The homeodomain folds into a helix-turn-helix structure that grips the DNA double helix.

Discovery

The homeobox was discovered in 1984 by teams led by Walter Gehring in Basel and by Matthew Scott and Amy Weiner at Indiana University, who found a shared sequence in the homeotic genes of the fruit fly Drosophila — Antennapedia, Ultrabithorax and fushi tarazu — mutations of which transform one body part into another (for example, legs growing where antennae belong). To researchers' astonishment, the same sequence soon turned up in vertebrates and almost all other animals, revealing a deeply conserved genetic toolkit for development.

Hox genes and body plans

In most animals the principal homeobox genes are the Hox genes, arranged in clusters (four clusters, HOXA–D, in humans, containing 39 genes). They are expressed in sequence along the embryo's head-to-tail axis — in the same order as their position on the chromosome, a property called collinearity — and specify the identity of segments and limbs. Other homeobox families (such as Pax, including Pax6, which directs eye development across the animal kingdom) pattern organs and tissues.

Structure and significance

The homeodomain folds into three alpha helices; the third helix sits in the major groove of DNA, recognising specific sequences, often with cofactors that sharpen specificity. Because homeobox genes sit at the top of developmental gene cascades, mutations can cause congenital disorders — for example, HOXD13 mutations cause synpolydactyly — and their misregulation features in cancers such as leukemias. Their conservation across more than 500 million years of evolution is a cornerstone of evolutionary developmental biology ("evo-devo").
