Dash

The dash is a punctuation mark consisting of a long horizontal line. It is similar in appearance to the hyphen but longer and sometimes higher. The most common versions are the en dash (–), generally half the width of an em dash, and the em dash (—), roughly the width of the letter M. The use of the word "dash" for the em dash is sometimes argued to be a misnomer, but it is long established in printers' usage.

En dash

The en dash is used principally for ranges, such as "pages 23–29" or "the years 1939–45", where it stands for "to" or "through". It also links equal or opposing terms, as in "the Liberal–Labour pact" and "a north–south divide", and appears in compound adjectives formed from open compounds, as in "New York–based". Style guides differ on whether spaces surround it.

Em dash

The em dash marks an abrupt break, an aside, or a strong interruption in a sentence — often performing the work of commas, parentheses, or a colon with greater emphasis. It is common at the start of quoted dialogue in many European languages. In English typography it is usually set closed up to surrounding words, though some American publications surround it with spaces. Two or three em dashes in a row are used to indicate omitted words or redacted names.

History and encoding

Dashes evolved from the marks of early printers, who distinguished the shorter hyphen from longer rules used for omission and interruption. In Unicode, the hyphen-minus is U+002D, the en dash U+2013, and the em dash U+2014; their HTML entities are &ndash; and &mdash;. On typewriters, lacking dedicated keys, writers doubled the hyphen to represent an em dash, a convention that persists informally in plain text.
