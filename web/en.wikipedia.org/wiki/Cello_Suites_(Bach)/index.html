Cello Suites (Bach)

The six Cello Suites, BWV 1007–1012, are suites for unaccompanied cello by Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750). They are some of the most frequently performed and recognisable solo compositions ever written for the cello. Bach apparently composed them during his period (1717–1723) as Kapellmeister in Köthen, together with the solo violin sonatas and partitas. Each suite comprises six movements: a prelude, an allemande, a courante, a sarabande, a pair of galanteries (minuets, bourrées or gavottes) and a final gigue.

History and reception

No autograph survives; the principal source is a copy made by Bach's second wife, Anna Magdalena, around 1727–1731. The suites were little known until the Catalan cellist Pablo Casals, who bought a printed edition in a Barcelona thrift shop in 1890 as a thirteen-year-old, championed them and recorded all six in the 1930s, transforming them from obscure études into pillars of the repertoire. The Prelude to the First Suite in G major is especially famous and is widely heard in films and ceremonies.

The suites

Suite No. 1 in G major, BWV 1007; No. 2 in D minor, BWV 1008; No. 3 in C major, BWV 1009; No. 4 in E-flat major, BWV 1010; No. 5 in C minor, BWV 1011, which calls for scordatura tuning; and No. 6 in D major, BWV 1012, written for a five-string instrument. The music is remarkable for implying rich harmony and counterpoint on a single melodic line.
