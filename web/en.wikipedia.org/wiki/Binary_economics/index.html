Binary economics is an economic theory developed by the American lawyer and economist Louis O. Kelso, first set out with philosopher Mortimer J. Adler in The Capitalist Manifesto (1958). The theory holds that there are two — "binary" — fundamentally distinct factors of production, human labor and capital (tools, machines, structures, and land), and that capital is independently productive: it produces a growing share of output as technology advances, regardless of labor input.

Binary economics: income flows from both labor and capital ownership.

Core ideas

Kelso argued that conventional economics, by treating wages as the normal route to income, obscures the fact that in industrial economies capital does most of the physical work of production. Because ownership of capital is highly concentrated, most households depend on labor income while the productiveness of capital accrues to a minority — a structural cause of poverty amid plenty. The remedy, in the Kelsonian program, is not redistribution but the broadening of capital ownership: financing the acquisition of new capital with credit repaid from the capital's own future earnings, so that workers and citizens generally become capital owners without reducing existing owners' holdings.

Policy influence

The most widely adopted offshoot of binary economics is the employee stock ownership plan (ESOP), which Kelso devised — the first ESOP was implemented in 1956 for the employees of Peninsula Newspapers in California — and which was written into United States pension law in the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974, championed by Senator Russell Long. Thousands of American companies have since adopted ESOPs. Later Kelso-influenced proposals include "capital homesteading" plans that would extend capital-acquisition credit to every citizen.

Criticism

Mainstream economists have criticized binary economics for treating capital and labor as independent when standard theory regards their contributions as intertwined and substitutable, and for understating how much of capital's income is consumed, taxed, or reinvested. Supporters respond that the theory's central practical insight — that the distribution of capital ownership determines the distribution of a rising share of income — anticipates modern debates about automation and inequality.
