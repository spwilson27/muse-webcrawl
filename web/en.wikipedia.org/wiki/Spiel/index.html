Spiel

A spiel (pronounced "shpeel") is a lengthy, often rehearsed speech or sales pitch intended to persuade or entertain. The word entered English from German Spiel, meaning "play" or "game", through Yiddish, and became common in American show business and salesmanship in the early twentieth century.

Usage

Originally associated with carnival barkers and vaudeville performers, the term now applies to any polished, fast-talking promotional patter: a salesperson's product spiel, a politician's stump speech, or a tour guide's rehearsed commentary. The word often carries a slightly ironic or weary implication that the speech is well worn and delivered by rote — "he gave me the whole spiel".

Etymology

German Spiel refers to a game or play; Yiddish shpil similarly means a play or game and also a long, involved story or argument. English use of "spiel" as a verb ("to spiel") and noun consolidated in the 1890s–1930s in entertainment contexts.
