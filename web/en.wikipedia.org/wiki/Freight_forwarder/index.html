Freight forwarder

A freight forwarder (or forwarding agent) is a person or company that organizes shipments for individuals or corporations to get goods from the manufacturer or producer to a market, customer, or final point of distribution. Forwarders contract with one or more carriers — by ship, aircraft, rail, and truck — to move the goods, but typically do not carry the freight themselves.

Services

Freight forwarders act as intermediaries and logistics experts. Typical services include:

  • Negotiating freight rates and booking cargo space with carriers
  • Preparing shipping and export documentation, including bills of lading
  • Customs clearance and advice on tariffs and regulations
  • Consolidating small shipments (groupage) into full container loads
  • Warehousing, packing, and cargo insurance
  • Tracking shipments and managing supply-chain information

Legal status

A forwarder may act as an agent of the shipper or as a principal (carrier) in its own right; in the latter case it issues its own "house" bill of lading and assumes carrier liability for the leg it contracts. Many jurisdictions license ocean freight forwarders and non-vessel-operating common carriers (NVOCCs); in the United States these are regulated by the Federal Maritime Commission. The international federation of forwarders' associations is FIATA, founded in 1926, whose standard documents are used worldwide.

History

Forwarding originated with innkeepers and carriers' agents in the 18th and 19th centuries who arranged onward transport of goods and luggage; one early forwarding house, Thomas Meadows and Company of London, dates to the 1830s. The containerization revolution of the late 20th century transformed forwarders into global logistics integrators, and the largest firms — such as Kuehne + Nagel, DHL Global Forwarding, and DB Schenker — now manage entire supply chains.
