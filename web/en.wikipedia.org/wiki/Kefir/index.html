Kefir

Kefir (also kephir) is a fermented milk drink with a thin yogurt-like consistency and a mildly sour, slightly effervescent taste, originating among the peoples of the North Caucasus. It is made by inoculating cow's, goat's or sheep's milk with kefir grains — cauliflower-like clusters of bacteria and yeasts embedded in a matrix of proteins, lipids and the polysaccharide kefiran. Kefir is consumed throughout Eastern Europe, Russia, Central Asia, and increasingly in Western markets as a probiotic food.

Production

Traditional kefir is fermented at room temperature for 12–48 hours in skin bags or wooden vessels, regularly agitated. The grains — typically containing Lactobacillus, Lactococcus and Leuconostoc bacteria together with lactose-fermenting yeasts such as Kluyveromyces marxianus — are strained out and reused, growing slowly over successive batches. Commercial kefir usually replaces live grains with defined starter cultures, giving a milder, more uniform product. Fermentation converts most lactose to lactic acid and produces small amounts of CO₂, ethanol and flavour compounds.

History

Caucasus herders long guarded the grains — legend calls them "grains of the Prophet" — but in the early 20th century the All-Russian Physicians' Society obtained grains, reportedly through the agent Irina Sakharova's mission to the Karachay prince Bek-Mirza Barchorov, and industrial production began in the Soviet Union in the 1930s.

Water kefir

A related culture, water kefir (tibicos), ferments sugar water or fruit juice with a different consortium, producing a fizzy non-dairy drink.
