Scenario planning

Scenario planning, also called scenario thinking or scenario analysis, is a strategic planning method that some organizations use to make flexible long-term plans. It is in large part an adaptation and generalization of classic methods used by military intelligence.

Scenario planning typically explores multiple plausible futures.

The original method was that a group of analysts would generate simulation games for policy makers. In business applications, the emphasis was shifted towards understanding possible futures rather than predicting a single outcome. Scenario planning helps decision-makers test strategies against a range of plausible environments and avoid being blindsided by surprises.

History

Scenario planning grew out of post-World War II military planning. The United States Air Force, working with the RAND Corporation, developed methods for imagining what opponents might do. Herman Kahn, who founded the Hudson Institute in 1961, became a leading proponent of "thinking about the unthinkable" and applied scenario techniques to public policy.

The method entered mainstream corporate practice through Royal Dutch Shell, where planners such as Pierre Wack developed scenarios in the early 1970s that anticipated the possibility of an oil price shock. When the 1973 oil crisis occurred, Shell was better prepared than its competitors, which greatly enhanced the reputation of the technique.

Method

A typical scenario exercise identifies key driving forces and critical uncertainties, then combines them — often along two axes — to produce a small set (usually two to four) of divergent but plausible narratives about the future. Each scenario is given a memorable name and developed into a story describing how the world might evolve. Strategies are then "wind-tunnelled" against each scenario to check their robustness.

Criticism

Critics note that scenario planning can be resource-intensive, that the scenarios produced are only as good as the imagination of the participants, and that organizations sometimes fail to act on the insights generated because no single scenario is treated as a forecast.
