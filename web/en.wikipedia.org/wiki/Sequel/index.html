Sequel

A work and its continuation.

A sequel is a work of literature, film, theatre, television, music, or video games that continues the story of, or expands upon, an earlier work. Sequels typically portray events set after those of the preceding work, often with the same characters, settings, or themes, though a sequel may also tell a self-contained story within the same fictional world. The word derives from the Latin sequela, from sequi, "to follow."

Types and terminology

Sequels are part of a family of related terms describing how works relate chronologically and in order of release. A prequel depicts events earlier than the original work but is created afterward. An interquel is set between two previously released works; a midquel fills in events occurring during the timeframe of another work; and a work that runs parallel to the original's events is sometimes called a parallel or sidequel. A sequel that follows two predecessors is the third installment of a trilogy (the second sequel completing a three-work arc), and long-running sequences become series or franchises.

Other related forms include the spin-off, which focuses on a secondary character or element of the original; the standalone sequel, which shares a world but few characters; and the spiritual successor, which continues a creator's ideas without formal continuity. In a "legacy sequel," released decades later, original cast members return alongside new characters.

History

Continuation of successful stories is ancient — epic cycles expanded Homeric material, and sequels to romances were common in early modern literature: Miguel de Cervantes published a second part of Don Quixote in 1615, partly in response to an unauthorized continuation by another writer. In the modern era, the economics of publishing and especially of Hollywood filmmaking made sequels central to the industry. Film sequels became increasingly common from the 1970s onward with successes such as The Godfather Part II (1974) — often cited among the greatest sequels — and The Empire Strikes Back (1980). By the twenty-first century, sequels, prequels, reboots, and shared-universe installments dominated the highest-grossing releases worldwide.

Sequels by other hands

Sequels are frequently made without the original creator's involvement, by studios or publishers who own the rights, or posthumously by other authors: examples include continuations of Margaret Mitchell's Gone with the Wind, Ian Fleming's James Bond novels, and the Dune series. Unauthorized sequels have a long legal and literary history, and the boundary between sequel, adaptation, and fan fiction is policed by copyright and trademark law.

Reception

Critical discourse often treats sequels skeptically, assuming commercial motivation and diminishing returns, yet numerous sequels are judged equal or superior to their originals, and systematic analyses of ratings and box office show a more mixed picture: sequels reliably attract audiences but tend to score slightly lower with critics than originals. Long gaps between installments, recasting, and "sequelitis" — the perceived overproduction of follow-ups — are recurring themes in commentary.
