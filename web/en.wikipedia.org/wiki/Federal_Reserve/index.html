Federal Reserve

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Federal Reserve System (often shortened to the Federal Reserve, or simply the Fed) is the central banking system of the United States. It was created on 23 December 1913 with the enactment of the Federal Reserve Act, after a series of financial panics (particularly the panic of 1907) led to the desire for central control of the monetary system. The Federal Reserve System is composed of several layers: the presidentially appointed Board of Governors, the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks, numerous member banks and advisory councils.

Functions and independence

Congress gave the Fed a dual mandate — maximum employment and stable prices — plus lender-of-last-resort banking supervision, payment-system operation and financial-stability responsibilities. The FOMC steers monetary conditions chiefly through the federal funds rate target; since 2008 it has added large-scale asset purchases (quantitative easing) and novel credit facilities, most controversially in the 2008 crisis and the 2020 pandemic. The Fed is designed as an independent agency — governors serve long staggered terms and its budget is outside appropriations — a design defended as insulation from inflationary politics and attacked as unaccountable power, with recurring "Audit the Fed" and reform campaigns. Its decisions move markets worldwide, and its chair is customary shorthand for the most powerful economic official in the world.
