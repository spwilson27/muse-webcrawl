Nominal interest rate

In finance and economics, the nominal interest rate is the interest rate stated without adjustment for inflation or for the compounding of interest within a year. The term has two related uses: the rate before taking inflation into account (contrasted with the real interest rate), and the periodic rate multiplied by the number of periods per year (contrasted with the effective interest rate).

Nominal versus real

The Fisher equation relates the nominal rate i, the real rate r and expected inflation π: i ≈ r + π. A lender who charges 7% nominal interest when inflation is 3% earns a real return of roughly 4%. Central banks set nominal policy rates, but it is the real rate that influences spending and saving decisions.

Nominal versus effective

When interest is compounded more than once a year, a nominal annual rate r compounded m times per year corresponds to an effective annual rate of (1 + r/m)m − 1. For example, a nominal 12% compounded monthly yields an effective annual rate of about 12.68%. Consumer-credit law in many jurisdictions requires disclosure of an annual percentage rate (APR), which is a form of nominal rate, alongside or instead of the effective rate (APY in United States deposit advertising).
