Chartres Cathedral, also known as the Cathedral of Our Lady of Chartres (French: Cathédrale Notre-Dame de Chartres), is a Roman Catholic church in Chartres, France, about 80 km (50 miles) southwest of Paris. It is widely considered one of the finest and best-preserved examples of French Gothic architecture. The current cathedral, mostly built between 1194 and 1220 after a fire destroyed the earlier church, replaced a succession of churches on the site dating back to late antiquity.

History

Chartres was a major pilgrimage centre because of its relic, the Sancta Camisa, said to be the tunic worn by the Virgin Mary at the birth of Christ, given to the cathedral by Charles the Bald in 876. The fire of 1194 destroyed most of the previous Romanesque cathedral, but the relic was found unharmed, which was taken as a sign to rebuild on a grander scale. Remarkably, the new church was substantially complete within about 26 years, giving it an unusually unified design.

Architecture and art

The cathedral is famed for its sculpture — notably the Royal Portal of the west front, which survived from the earlier church — and for its stained glass: 176 windows, many dating from the 13th century, renowned for their deep "Chartres blue". The building pioneered the High Gothic combination of flying buttresses, a three-level elevation and large clerestory windows. The south tower's plain Romanesque spire contrasts with the elaborate Flamboyant Gothic north spire, completed in the early 16th century. A labyrinth inlaid in the nave floor survives intact.

Significance

Chartres was added to the UNESCO list of World Heritage Sites in 1979. It remains an active pilgrimage destination, especially for the Pentecost pilgrimage from Paris, and one of the most visited monuments in France.
