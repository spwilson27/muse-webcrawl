Lewis's trilemma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lewis's trilemma is an apologetic argument for the divinity of Jesus, popularized by the British writer and scholar C. S. Lewis. It is often summarized as "Lunatic, Liar, or Lord": because Jesus claimed to be God, Lewis argued, he cannot consistently be regarded merely as a great moral teacher — he must have been either telling the truth (Lord), deliberately deceiving (Liar), or sincerely deluded (Lunatic).

The argument

Lewis put the argument in a 1942 BBC radio talk and in his 1952 book Mere Christianity:

"I am trying here to prevent anyone saying the really foolish thing that people often say about Him: 'I'm ready to accept Jesus as a great moral teacher, but I don't accept His claim to be God.' That is the one thing we must not say. A man who was merely a man and said the sort of things Jesus said would not be a great moral teacher. He would either be a lunatic — on a level with the man who says he is a poached egg — or else he would be the Devil of Hell. You must make your choice. Either this man was, and is, the Son of God: or else a madman or something worse."

The argument rests on the premise that the Gospels accurately report Jesus claiming divine status, and that a merely human claimant to such status could not be a wise and honest teacher.

Criticism

Critics, including the philosopher John Beversluis and various biblical scholars, respond that the trilemma is a false trichotomy. Additional possibilities include that the Gospel accounts are legendary or mythologically developed (a fourth option sometimes rendered "Legend"), that Jesus's sayings were misunderstood or mistranslated, that he used "Son of God" in a non-literal or messianic sense, or that he was a guru-type figure outside the categories Lewis offers. Defenders reply that the argument was aimed at casual readers rather than specialists, and that Lewis himself addressed the reliability of the Gospels elsewhere in his writings. The trilemma remains one of the most widely cited arguments in popular Christian apologetics.
