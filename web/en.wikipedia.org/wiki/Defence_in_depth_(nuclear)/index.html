Defence in depth (nuclear)

Defence in depth is the fundamental safety philosophy of the nuclear power industry. It is the practice of deploying multiple, independent, and redundant layers of protection so that no single failure — technical, human, or organisational — can lead to harmful effects, and so that failures are detected and compensated for before they can cause harm. The concept is applied in the design, construction, and operation of nuclear power plants and other nuclear installations worldwide, and is promoted by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA).

Levels of defence

The IAEA formulation describes five levels of defence:

  1. Prevention of abnormal operation and failures — conservative design, high-quality construction, and good operating practice.
  2. Control of abnormal operation and detection of failures — control, limiting, and protection systems that return the plant to a safe state.
  3. Control of accidents within the design basis — engineered safety features such as emergency core cooling, designed to keep releases within authorised limits.
  4. Control of severe plant conditions — measures to prevent accident progression and mitigate the consequences of severe accidents, including containment protection.
  5. Mitigation of radiological consequences of significant releases — off-site emergency response.

Physical barriers

Defence in depth is embodied in a series of physical barriers between the radioactive material in the reactor core and the environment: the fuel pellets themselves, the fuel cladding, the reactor coolant pressure boundary, and the containment structure. The integrity of these barriers is protected by the successive levels of defence.

Principles

Associated principles include redundancy (multiple components for each function), diversity (different means of performing a function), physical separation and independence of safety systems, the single-failure criterion, and a strong safety culture. Major accidents have repeatedly been analysed in terms of defence in depth: at Fukushima Daiichi in 2011, for example, an extreme external event overwhelmed successive levels simultaneously, leading the IAEA and national regulators to strengthen requirements, particularly for external hazards and for the independence of protection layers.
