The Elizabethan Religious Settlement is the name given to the religious and political arrangements made for England during the reign of Elizabeth I (1558–1603) that brought the English Reformation to a lasting, if contested, conclusion. Enacted by the Parliament of 1559, it re-established the Church of England's independence from Rome, restored Protestant worship after the Catholic restoration of Mary I, and sought a via media — a middle way — that a broad majority of the nation could accept.

Background

Within three decades England had swung from Henry VIII's schism from Rome, through Edward VI's thoroughgoing Protestantism, to Mary's reconciliation with the papacy and burning of Protestants. Elizabeth, who succeeded in November 1558, was herself Protestant but cautious and politically pragmatic; her first Parliament, meeting in January 1559, faced a Commons eager for Protestant reform and a House of Lords still containing Mary's Catholic bishops.

The Acts of 1559

The Act of Supremacy repealed the Marian legislation restoring papal authority, revived the Henrician statutes, and declared Elizabeth "Supreme Governor" of the Church of England — a title softened from Henry's "Supreme Head", partly out of deference to objections to a woman bearing it. Clergy and officeholders were required to swear an oath recognising her supremacy. The Act of Uniformity imposed a single liturgy: the Book of Common Prayer of 1559, based on Edward VI's 1552 book but modified in a more traditional direction — notably combining the 1549 and 1552 words of administration at communion and retaining ornaments and vestments at their late-Edwardian (1549) standard. Attendance at church on Sundays was compulsory, on pain of a fine of twelve pence.

Character of the settlement

The settlement's studied ambiguity enabled broad conformity: the communion formula could be heard in either a Catholic or a Protestant sense, and the retention of bishops, cathedrals, church courts and much ceremony distinguished the English church from the reformed churches of the continent. The Thirty-nine Articles, adopted by Convocation in 1563 and given final form in 1571, supplied a moderately Calvinist confessional framework. Elizabeth's own religious conservatism — her liking for crucifixes, candles and choral music — acted as a brake on further reform throughout her reign.

Opposition and enforcement

Nearly all of Mary's bishops refused the oath and were deprived; their places went to returning exiles and Edwardian clergy, with Matthew Parker as Archbishop of Canterbury. Opposition came from two directions: Catholic "recusants" who refused to attend the established church — whose position hardened after Elizabeth's excommunication by Pius V in 1570 and the arrival of seminary priests and Jesuits, met by savage penal laws — and Puritans within the church who pressed for further reformation of worship and government in the Vestiarian controversy and beyond. The settlement nevertheless endured: it defined the Church of England's identity for centuries and underlies the Anglican tradition today.
