TypeCode-switching / hybrid speech
LanguagesSpanish and English

Spanglish is a popular name for any variety of language that results from the interaction of Spanish and English. It encompasses code-switching between the two languages within a sentence or conversation, loanwords adapted from English into Spanish (such as troca for truck, parquear for to park, and lonche for lunch), calques, and the English spoken by Spanish speakers under Spanish influence. It is most associated with Latino communities in the United States, but related phenomena occur in Puerto Rico, Spain, and Latin American cities with strong US cultural influence, as well as in Gibraltar (Llanito).

Terminology and history

The word "Spanglish" (in Spanish, espanglish) is usually credited to the Puerto Rican journalist Salvador Tió, who used it pejoratively in a 1948 newspaper column to lament the Anglicisation of Spanish. For decades the term carried a stigma of corruption and incomplete learning; since the 1990s, scholars such as Ilan Stavans — who published a Spanglish dictionary and a Spanglish translation of the first chapter of Don Quixote — and many Latino writers have reclaimed it as the legitimate voice of a bicultural identity. Linguists generally prefer the more precise term code-switching for the alternation itself, reserving debate for whether any stabilised hybrid variety exists.

Linguistic features

Research on code-switching, notably by Shana Poplack, has shown that fluent bilinguals switch languages at grammatically permissible points in both languages — a skill, not a deficit. Typical Spanglish features include:

  • Intra-sentential switching: "Voy a la tienda to buy some milk."
  • Loanwords and loan blends: rufo (roof), janguear (to hang out), textear (to text).
  • Calques: llamar para atrás ("to call back"), estar supuesto a ("to be supposed to").
  • Semantic extension: using aplicación for a job application, or carpeta influenced by English "carpet".

Cultural presence

Spanglish pervades US Latino literature (Junot Díaz, Giannina Braschi, whose 1998 novel Yo-Yo Boing! is written largely in Spanglish), film and television, reggaetón and Latin pop lyrics, advertising aimed at bilingual consumers, and political campaigning. The 2004 film Spanglish popularised the term for general audiences.

Debate

Critics, including the Royal Spanish Academy in earlier decades, have warned that Spanglish erodes standard Spanish, while advocates argue it is a creative, rule-governed register that expresses a dual identity and parallels earlier contact varieties. Sociolinguists emphasise that its use varies by generation, region, and social context, and that most speakers can also command standard registers of both languages.
