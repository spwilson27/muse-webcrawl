Industrial Revolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A steam-powered cotton mill interior in 19th-century Lancashire, rows of powered looms in a long factory hall]
A powered textile mill of the Industrial Revolution.

The Industrial Revolution was the transition from an agrarian and handicraft economy to one dominated by industry and machine manufacturing, beginning in Great Britain in the later 18th century and spreading through Europe and North America in the 19th. New chemical and iron production processes, the increasing use of water power and steam power, the factory system and rising population transformed economies and societies.

Origins in Britain

Britain industrialised first for reasons historians still debate: abundant coal, high wages encouraging labour-saving inventions, colonial trade and markets, agricultural improvement, finance and a culture of practical invention. Textiles led the way, with the spinning jenny, water frame and power loom mechanising cotton production.

Spread and second phase

Industrialisation spread to Belgium, France, Germany and the United States, and from the later 19th century a 'second industrial revolution' added steel, railways, electricity, chemicals and mass production. Living standards eventually rose for many, though early industrial cities brought pollution, dangerous work and harsh conditions that prompted labour movements and reform.

Effects

The Industrial Revolution shifted populations to cities, created industrial working and middle classes, raised global production enormously, and began the sustained rise in fossil-fuel use and carbon emissions that continues today.
