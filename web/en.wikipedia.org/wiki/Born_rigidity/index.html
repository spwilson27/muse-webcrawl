Born rigidity

Born rigidity is a concept in special relativity introduced by Max Born in 1909. A motion of an extended body is Born rigid if the distance between any two infinitesimally neighbouring points of the body, measured in the body's instantaneous rest frame, remains constant over time. Born rigidity is the relativistic replacement for the classical notion of a rigid body — and it is highly restrictive: most motions that are rigid in Newtonian mechanics cannot be Born rigid.

[ Image: Spacetime diagram contrasting a Born-rigid accelerating rod with a non-rigid one ]

Definition

Formally, a congruence of timelike worldlines has Born rigidity when its expansion tensor vanishes — equivalently, when the projection of the metric onto the local rest spaces is Lie-dragged along the flow. For motion in a straight line, Born rigidity requires a specific acceleration profile along the rod: the proper acceleration must vary as

a(X) = a0 / (1 + a0X / c²),

the condition underlying relativistic (Rindler) accelerated motion and related to Bell's spaceship paradox.

Restrictions and paradoxes

The Herglotz–Noether theorem shows that in flat spacetime a Born-rigid body has only three degrees of freedom: its motion is entirely determined by the worldline of a single point. In particular, a disc cannot be spun up from rest to uniform rotation while remaining Born rigid — the heart of the Ehrenfest paradox — and a Born-rigid body cannot be given arbitrary acceleration and rotation independently. Consequently truly rigid bodies cannot exist in relativity, and elasticity must be treated relativistically.

Applications

The concept remains a standard tool in relativistic kinematics: it defines the Rindler horizon for uniformly accelerated rods, clarifies paradoxes of length contraction, and provides the framework for analysing relativistic rotating frames such as the Sagnac effect.
