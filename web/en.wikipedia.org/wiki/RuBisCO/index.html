RuBisCO

RuBisCO, short for ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase, is the enzyme that catalyses the first major step of carbon fixation in photosynthesis: the attachment of carbon dioxide to ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP). It is almost certainly the most abundant enzyme—and by many accounts the most abundant protein—on Earth, because nearly all photosynthetic organisms depend on it, and it must be present in huge quantities to compensate for its inefficiency.

Structure

In plants, algae and cyanobacteria, RuBisCO is a large complex of about 540 kilodaltons made of eight large subunits (encoded in the chloroplast genome) and eight small subunits (encoded in the nuclear genome). The active sites, located on the large subunits, must be activated by a separate molecule of CO2 and a magnesium ion, a process assisted by the helper enzyme RuBisCO activase.

Carboxylase and oxygenase activity

RuBisCO can bind oxygen as well as carbon dioxide at the same active site. When it acts as an oxygenase, it initiates photorespiration, a wasteful pathway that releases previously fixed CO2 and consumes energy, significantly reducing the efficiency of photosynthesis in many plants, especially in hot, dry conditions. This dual activity is thought to be an evolutionary legacy: the enzyme evolved more than three billion years ago, when the atmosphere contained little oxygen, so there was no selection pressure to discriminate between the two gases.

Significance

RuBisCO is famously slow for an enzyme, fixing only a few molecules of CO2 per second, and plants compensate by making it in enormous amounts—it can constitute up to half of the soluble protein in a leaf. C4 and CAM plants evolved carbon-concentrating mechanisms to supply RuBisCO with CO2 and suppress photorespiration. Because of its central role in the global carbon cycle and in crop yields, engineering a faster or more selective RuBisCO is a long-standing goal of plant science.
