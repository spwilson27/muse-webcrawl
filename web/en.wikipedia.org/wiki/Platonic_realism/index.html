Platonic realism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Platonic realism is the philosophical position that abstract objects — including universals such as properties, numbers, and propositions — exist independently of the human mind and of any particular instances. It is named after the Greek philosopher Plato (c. 428–348 BC), whose theory of Forms held that the physical world is a shadow or imperfect copy of a higher realm of eternal, unchanging Ideas.

Theory of Forms

In dialogues such as the Phaedo, Republic and Parmenides, Plato argued that for every property shared by particular things — beauty, justice, equality — there exists a single perfect Form that those things imperfectly imitate. The Forms are not located in space or time and can be grasped only by reason, not by the senses. The famous Allegory of the cave dramatises the contrast between the perceptible world of becoming and the intelligible world of being.

Mathematical Platonism

In the philosophy of mathematics, a closely related view called mathematical Platonism holds that mathematical entities such as numbers, sets and functions exist objectively and independently of mathematicians. Supporters argue that the objectivity and apparent necessity of mathematical truth are best explained this way; critics, following objections raised since antiquity, ask how human beings could acquire knowledge of a causally inert abstract realm — a challenge sharpened in the twentieth century by Paul Benacerraf.

Criticism and alternatives

Aristotle, Plato's own student, rejected separately existing Forms, arguing instead that universals exist only in particulars (a position known as moderate or Aristotelian realism). Medieval and modern debates opposed realism to nominalism, which denies that universals exist at all, and to conceptualism, which locates them in the mind. Platonic realism remains a live position in contemporary metaphysics and philosophy of mathematics.
