Platonism and Platonic realism

Platonic realism is the view, named after the Greek philosopher Plato, that abstract objects — universals, numbers, sets, propositions, and in Plato's own philosophy the Forms — exist independently of the minds that think about them and of the particular things that instantiate them. Applied to Platonism generally, it is the claim that the objects of a theory are not mental constructions or mere names but real entities with an existence of their own. The doctrine is most often discussed today in two settings: the problem of universals, and the philosophy of mathematics.

Plato's Forms

In dialogues such as the Republic, Phaedo, and Timaeus, Plato argued that for every class of things sharing a name there is a single Form — the perfect, eternal, unchanging exemplar (the Beautiful itself, the Equal itself) — of which sensible things are imperfect copies or "participants". The Forms are grasped by reason, not the senses, and they explain both the possibility of knowledge and the stability of the world beneath the flux of appearances. Aristotle, Plato's pupil, criticised the separation of Forms from things and developed the moderate realism later taken up by medieval scholastics.

Universals

In the medieval problem of universals, realism — opposed by nominalism and conceptualism — held that universals such as redness or humanity exist in reality. Extreme realists followed Plato in placing them before things (ante rem); moderate realists, following Aristotle and Aquinas, placed them in things (in re). The debate continues in analytic metaphysics, where David Armstrong defended an immanent realism of universals against nominalists such as W. V. O. Quine in his earlier work.

Mathematical Platonism

In the philosophy of mathematics, Platonism holds that numbers, sets, and other mathematical objects exist abstractly — outside space and time, causally inert — and that mathematical truths are discovered, not invented. Kurt Gödel endorsed a form of this view, and it draws support from the apparent objectivity of mathematics and from Quine and Hilary Putnam's indispensability argument: since mathematics is indispensable to our best science, we should believe in the entities it quantifies over. Critics, notably Paul Benacerraf, press the epistemological problem: if mathematical objects are causally isolated from us, how can we know anything about them? Alternatives include formalism, intuitionism, structuralism, and fictionalism.
