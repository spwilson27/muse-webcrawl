Earl of Somerset is a title that has been created several times in the Peerage of England. It is most closely associated with the Beaufort family, the legitimised descendants of John of Gaunt, whose members played a leading role in the Wars of the Roses.

Early creations

The title was first granted around 1141 to William de Mohun of Dunster during the reign of King Stephen, though this creation soon lapsed. It was later held briefly by other magnates in the fourteenth century before its most famous creation.

The Beaufort earls

In 1397 John Beaufort, eldest legitimised son of John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster, by Katherine Swynford, was created Earl of Somerset by Richard II; he was also briefly Marquess of Somerset. His descendants became one of the great families of Lancastrian England. His son John Beaufort, 1st Duke of Somerset (died 1444), and grandson Edmund Beaufort, 2nd Duke of Somerset, who was killed at the First Battle of St Albans in 1455, led the Lancastrian cause in the early Wars of the Roses. Henry Beaufort, 3rd Duke of Somerset, was executed after the Battle of Hexham in 1464, and his brother Edmund was executed after the Battle of Tewkesbury in 1471, ending the legitimate male Beaufort line. Margaret Beaufort, daughter of the 1st Duke, was the mother of King Henry VII.

Later creations

The earldom was later created for royal favourites and courtiers, including Robert Carr, favourite of James I, in 1613. The dukedom of Somerset, by contrast, passed to the Seymour family, whose head is the present Duke of Somerset; the earldom as a separate title is extinct.
