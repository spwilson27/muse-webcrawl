Angiosperm

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Angiosperms, the flowering plants, are the largest and most diverse group of land plants, with roughly 300,000 known species. Their defining features are flowers and seeds enclosed within a fruit. Appearing in the fossil record in the early Cretaceous (over 130 million years ago), they rose to dominate most terrestrial ecosystems and supply nearly all human plant food.

Diversity and reproduction

Angiosperms include grasses (wheat, rice, maize — the world's staple crops), legumes, roses, orchids (one of the largest families), oaks and sunflowers. Flowers evolve with pollinators — insects, birds, bats, wind — and fruits aid seed dispersal by animals. The group splits informally into monocots (one seed leaf: grasses, lilies, palms) and eudicots (most broad-leaved plants).

Importance

Flowering plants underpin agriculture, forestry, medicine (many drugs derive from them) and gardens, and they structure nearly every land ecosystem. Darwin called their rapid rise an 'abominable mystery'; research links it to genome duplications, efficient leaves and pollinator partnerships.
