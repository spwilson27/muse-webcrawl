André Le Nôtre (12 March 1613 – 15 September 1700), originally rendered as André Le Nostre, was a French landscape architect and the principal gardener of King Louis XIV of France. His masterpiece, the gardens of the Palace of Versailles, defined the French formal garden — the jardin à la française — whose geometry of grand axes, parterres, canals, and clipped greenery was imitated across Europe and remains the supreme expression of Baroque garden design.

Early life and Vaux-le-Vicomte

Le Nôtre was born in Paris into a family of royal gardeners: his father Jean Le Nôtre was gardener at the Tuileries under Louis XIII, and his godfather was Claude Mollet, author of an influential treatise on gardening. André studied painting under Simon Vouet, learned perspective and architecture, and in 1637 succeeded his father at the Tuileries. His reputation was made at Vaux-le-Vicomte (1656–1661), where, working for finance minister Nicolas Fouquet, he collaborated with architect Louis Le Vau and painter Charles Le Brun to create the first great integrated French formal garden — its daring use of a hidden canal (revealed only as one walks the axis) astonished contemporaries. Fouquet's fall weeks after his famous fête of August 1661 brought the whole team into royal service.

Versailles and the royal gardens

From 1661 Le Nôtre directed the creation of the gardens of Versailles for Louis XIV, a project that occupied him for decades: a three-kilometre central axis (the "royal axis") running from the palace across the Grand Canal to the horizon, flanked by parterres de broderie, bosquets (ornamental groves) with fountains and statuary, and great waterworks. He also remodelled or designed the gardens of the Tuileries, Saint-Germain-en-Laye, Saint-Cloud, Chantilly, Fontainebleau, and the Trianon, and extended the axis of the Tuileries westward, the future Champs-Élysées. Louis XIV valued him personally, ennobled him in 1675, and is said to have pushed the elderly gardener's wheelchair around the gardens himself.

Style and legacy

Le Nôtre's style subordinates nature to rational order: long sight-lines, terraces, rectangular basins and canals, topiary, and patterned flowerbeds, all scaled to demonstrate the monarch's dominion and the triumph of reason. Foreign work included the layout of Greenwich Park in London for Charles II. The French formal garden influenced Peterhof in Russia, Schönbrunn in Vienna, and countless parks worldwide; Le Nôtre's tercentenary in 2013 was marked by major exhibitions and restorations at Versailles.
