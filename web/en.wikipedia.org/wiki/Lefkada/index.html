Lefkada (Greek: Λευκάδα), also known as Leucas or Lefkas, is a Greek island in the Ionian Sea, off the west coast of mainland Greece. It is connected to the mainland by a causeway and floating bridge, making it one of the few Greek islands accessible by car without a ferry. The island is part of the Lefkada regional unit, which also includes nearby islands such as Meganisi, Kalamos and Kastos.

Geography

Lefkada is mountainous, covered in pine forests, olive groves and vineyards, with its highest peak, Mount Stavrota, rising to about 1,158 metres. The west coast is famed for dramatic white limestone cliffs and turquoise beaches such as Porto Katsiki, Egremni and Kathisma, among the most celebrated in Greece. The east coast is gentler and sheltered, dotted with islets including Skorpios, once owned by Aristotle Onassis. The capital, also called Lefkada, sits at the northern tip beside a lagoon noted for birdlife.

History

In antiquity the island was associated with the city of Leucas, a Corinthian colony. Excavations by Wilhelm Dörpfeld led him to propose Lefkada as the true location of Homer's Ithaca, a theory that remains disputed. The island passed through Roman, Byzantine, Venetian, Ottoman, French, Russian and British hands before joining the modern Greek state with the rest of the Ionian Islands in 1864. An earthquake in 1948 caused significant damage.

Tourism and culture

Tourism is the mainstay of the economy, with the resorts of Nidri, Vasiliki — a major windsurfing centre — and Lefkada town drawing visitors through the summer. The island hosts an international folklore festival founded in the 1960s. Lefkada was the birthplace of the poets Aristotelis Valaoritis and Angelos Sikelianos; the Lafcadio Hearn Historical Center honours the writer Lafcadio Hearn, born on the island in 1850.
