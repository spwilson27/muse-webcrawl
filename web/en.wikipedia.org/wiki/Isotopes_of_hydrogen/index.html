Isotopes of hydrogen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hydrogen (1H) has three naturally occurring isotopes — protium (1H), deuterium (2H or D), and tritium (3H or T) — together with a number of extremely short-lived synthetic isotopes. Protium, deuterium and tritium are distinctive enough to have been given individual names, which is unique among the chemical elements.

Deuterium

Deuterium, the "heavy" stable isotope, makes up about one in 6,500 hydrogen atoms. Heavy water (D2O) is used as a neutron moderator in CANDU and other heavy-water reactors; deuterated solvents are standard in NMR spectroscopy; and deuterium labelling is used in metabolic studies.

Tritium

Tritium is radioactive, decaying by beta emission to helium-3 with a half-life of 12.32 years. It is produced cosmogenically in the upper atmosphere and artificially in reactors. Uses include radioluminescent markers (e.g. in watches), self-powered lighting, and as a fuel in fusion research, since deuterium–tritium fusion is the easiest fusion reaction to sustain.

Heavier synthetic isotopes

Isotopes 4H through 7H have been created in particle accelerators and decay in fractions of a millisecond or less, chiefly by neutron emission.
