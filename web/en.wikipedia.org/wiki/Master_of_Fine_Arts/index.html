A Master of Fine Arts (MFA) is a graduate academic degree granted by universities and art schools in the visual, performing and literary arts, including creative writing, studio art, film production, theatre, and design. Unlike most master's degrees, the MFA is widely regarded as a terminal degree in its field — the highest qualification for practitioners — and usually requires two to three years of study beyond the bachelor's degree, culminating in a major creative project such as a thesis exhibition, film, production, or book-length manuscript.

Structure

Admission usually depends primarily on a portfolio, audition, or writing sample rather than on undergraduate grades. Coursework combines studio or workshop practice with seminars in theory, history and pedagogy. Low-residency MFA programmes, pioneered in creative writing, combine brief intensive residencies with distance mentorship. The MFA traditionally qualifies its holder to teach at university level; in creative writing it is offered in fiction, poetry and creative nonfiction.

History

Advanced studio degrees developed in American universities in the early 20th century; the University of Iowa's Writers' Workshop (founded 1936) became the model for creative writing MFAs. The number of programmes expanded greatly after the 1960s. The degree is chiefly a North American qualification, though similar programmes now exist elsewhere.

Debate

The proliferation of MFA programmes — especially in creative writing — has prompted debate over cost, debt, job prospects in academia, and whether the workshop model homogenises artistic style.
