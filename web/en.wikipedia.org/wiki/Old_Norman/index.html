Old Norman

Old Norman, also called Old Northern French or Old Norman French, was a langue d'oïl dialect spoken in Normandy in northern France during the Middle Ages, and the ancestor of modern Norman. It was a Romance language derived from the Gallo-Romance spoken in northern Gaul, with a significant admixture of vocabulary from Old Norse brought by the Scandinavian settlers who established Normandy in the tenth century.

Characteristics

Old Norman shared many features with the other northern French dialects but was distinguished by Norse loanwords — especially in nautical, agricultural and legal vocabulary — and by certain phonological traits shared with Picard, such as the preservation of /k/ before /a/ where central French palatalised (compare Norman cat with French chat, "cat").

Anglo-Norman

After the Norman Conquest of England in 1066, Old Norman developed in England into Anglo-Norman, the language of the court, law and administration for several centuries, which left a deep imprint on English vocabulary. In Normandy itself, Old Norman literature included works such as the chronicles of Wace, including the Roman de Rou, an account of the dukes of Normandy.

Legacy

The modern descendants of Old Norman are the Norman dialects spoken in mainland Normandy and the Channel Islands, including Jèrriais (Jersey), Guernésiais (Guernsey) and Sercquiais (Sark).
