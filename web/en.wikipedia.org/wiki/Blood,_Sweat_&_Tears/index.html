Blood, Sweat & Tears (also known as BS&T) is an American jazz rock music group founded in New York City in 1967, noted for combining brass and rock band instrumentation. Their name was taken from a phrase in Winston Churchill's famous 1940 speech. The group is best known for hit songs such as "You Made Me So Very Happy", "Spinning Wheel", and "And When I Die", all from their second, self-titled album.

History

Keyboardist Al Kooper, formerly of the Blues Project, conceived a band that would combine rock with a horn section. The original line-up recorded Child Is Father to the Man (1968), an acclaimed album that sold modestly. Kooper left shortly afterwards over creative differences, and Canadian singer David Clayton-Thomas joined as lead vocalist.

The second album, Blood, Sweat & Tears (1968), was a major commercial success, reaching number one on the US charts and winning the Grammy Award for Album of the Year in 1970, beating the Beatles' Abbey Road. It produced three top-five singles. The band played at Woodstock in August 1969, reportedly as one of the festival's highest-paid acts.

A 1970 tour of Eastern Europe sponsored by the US State Department damaged the group's standing with the counterculture audience. Subsequent albums sold progressively less well, though "Hi-De-Ho" and "Lucretia MacEvil" were minor hits. Clayton-Thomas left in 1972 and returned in 1974. After a series of line-up changes the group ceased recording in the early 1980s; a touring version under drummer Bobby Colomby's licence has continued to perform with rotating personnel, none of whom are original members.
