Nuclear safety

Nuclear safety is defined by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) as "the achievement of proper operating conditions, prevention of accidents or mitigation of accident consequences, resulting in protection of workers, the public and the environment from undue radiation hazards". It covers the design, operation and regulation of nuclear reactors and other facilities handling radioactive materials.

Principles and systems

The guiding design philosophy is defence in depth: multiple successive physical barriers (fuel matrix, cladding, reactor pressure vessel, containment building) combined with redundant and diverse safety systems so that no single failure can lead to a serious release. Fundamental safety functions are control of reactivity, removal of heat from the core, and confinement of radioactive material. Engineered features include emergency core cooling systems, passive safety systems that rely on gravity and natural circulation, and filtered containment venting. Safety assessment uses deterministic analysis and probabilistic risk assessment, and regulators such as the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission license and inspect facilities against these standards.

Major accidents

The discipline has been shaped by the accidents at Three Mile Island (1979), Chernobyl (1986) and Fukushima Daiichi (2011), each of which led to major reforms in design requirements, operator training, emergency preparedness and international cooperation. Safety culture — the attitudes and behaviours of operating organisations — is recognised as a key determinant of safety performance.
