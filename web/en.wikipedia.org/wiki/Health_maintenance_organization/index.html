Health maintenance organization

A health maintenance organization (HMO) is a type of health insurance organisation in the United States that provides managed care. An HMO typically requires its members to receive care from a defined network of doctors and hospitals, and to choose a primary care physician who coordinates their care and provides referrals to specialists. In return, members usually pay lower premiums and out-of-pocket costs than under less restrictive plans.

History

The concept traces to prepaid group practices of the early 20th century, such as the Ross-Loos Medical Group (1929) and the Kaiser Permanente health plan, which grew out of industrial healthcare programmes in the 1930s and 1940s. The term "health maintenance organization" was coined by physician Paul M. Ellwood Jr., and the HMO Act of 1973, passed under President Richard Nixon, provided federal funding and pre-emption of restrictive state laws to encourage the growth of HMOs as a means of controlling rising healthcare costs.

Models

  • Staff model — physicians are salaried employees of the HMO itself.
  • Group model — the HMO contracts with a single multi-specialty medical group, which is paid by capitation.
  • Network model — the HMO contracts with several medical groups and independent practice associations.
  • IPA model — the HMO contracts with an independent practice association whose physicians also see other patients.

Characteristics and criticism

HMOs are generally paid a fixed monthly fee per member (capitation) regardless of how much care is used, giving them an incentive to emphasise preventive care and cost control. Critics have argued that gatekeeping, prior authorisation requirements and restricted networks can delay or limit access to care, while supporters point to lower costs and coordinated, preventive care. During the 1990s, dissatisfaction with strict HMO restrictions led many enrollees toward the more flexible preferred provider organization (PPO) plans.
