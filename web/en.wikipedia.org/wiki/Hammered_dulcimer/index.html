Hammered dulcimer

The hammered dulcimer (also called the hammer dulcimer) is a percussion-stringed instrument consisting of strings stretched over a trapezoidal resonant sound board, which the player strikes with small hand-held beaters called hammers. It belongs to the family of instruments descended from the Persian santur, which spread across Asia and Europe and gave rise to relatives including the Hungarian cimbalom, the Chinese yangqin, the Indian santoor and the Romanian țambal.

Description

The instrument typically has a trapezoid-shaped wooden body with bridges dividing courses of two to five strings. Two main bridges split the string lengths into intervals of a fifth, so the layout of notes follows a repeating pattern based on the circle of fifths, allowing scales to be played by alternating left and right hands. A standard American instrument might have 12 courses on the treble bridge and 11 on the bass bridge, covering about three octaves; larger "chromatic" instruments add extra bridges and courses. The hammers are thin wooden beaters, sometimes with padded or double-sided heads for different timbres. Dampers are fitted on concert instruments such as the cimbalom but are absent on folk instruments, whose strings ring freely and resonate sympathetically.

History

Struck zithers are depicted in the Middle East from antiquity, and the santur family spread along trade routes east to China and west into Europe, where the instrument appears in medieval and Renaissance sources. It became firmly established in British and American folk music: in England it was especially popular in East Anglia and the industrial North, while in the United States it was played in Appalachian, Midwestern and lumber-camp traditions and in the social dance music of many immigrant communities. By the mid-20th century the instrument had nearly died out in the US, but the folk revival of the 1960s and 1970s — aided by performers and builders such as Sam Rizzetta — restored it to wide popularity; it is the state instrument of no state but a fixture of American folk festivals, and is the instrument from which the mountain (fretted) dulcimer borrowed its name, though the two are unrelated in construction.
