Solid of revolution

In mathematics, a solid of revolution is a solid figure obtained by rotating a plane figure around some straight line (the axis of revolution) that lies in the same plane. The surface generated by the rotation is a surface of revolution, and the solid is bounded by that surface together with planes perpendicular to the axis at its ends. Familiar examples include the sphere (a semicircular disc rotated about its diameter), the cone, the cylinder, and the torus (a circle rotated about an external axis).

Rotating a region under a curve about an axis sweeps out a solid of revolution.

Volume

The volume of a solid of revolution is computed in integral calculus by the disc method and the shell method. If a region under the curve y = f(x) from x = a to x = b is rotated about the x-axis, the volume is V = π ∫ab [f(x)]² dx. Using cylindrical shells for rotation about the y-axis gives V = 2π ∫ab x f(x) dx. The "washer method" generalises the disc method to hollow solids.

Surface area

By Pappus's centroid theorem (the second theorem of Pappus–Guldinus), the surface area generated by rotating a plane curve about an external axis equals the product of the arc length of the curve and the distance travelled by its centroid. Likewise the first theorem gives the volume of the solid as the area of the region times the distance travelled by its geometric centroid.

Representative examples

  • Sphere: rotating a semicircular disc about its diameter; volume 4/3 πr³.
  • Right circular cone: rotating a right triangle about one of its legs.
  • Torus: rotating a circular disc about an axis in its plane that does not intersect it.
  • Gabriel's horn: rotating 1/x (for x ≥ 1) about the x-axis yields a solid with finite volume (π) but infinite surface area — a classic paradox of calculus.
