Hail cannon

A hail cannon is a device claimed by its users to disrupt the formation of hailstones in storm clouds, protecting crops and property below. Modern hail cannons generate powerful shock waves by igniting a mixture of acetylene or butane gas with air in a combustion chamber at the base of a large inverted cone, directing repeated blasts upward every few seconds as a storm approaches.

History

Anti-hail devices have a long history, from the ringing of church bells and firing of ordinary cannons at storms in early modern Europe to the vortex cannons developed in the 1890s by the Austrian winegrower Albert Stiger, whose experiments prompted government-sponsored trials in Italy around 1900. Those trials failed to demonstrate any effect, and scientific interest waned, though modern gas cannons have been marketed to farmers and, in some cases, car manufacturers protecting outdoor stock since the 1970s.

Scientific assessment

Meteorologists are sceptical of hail cannons: the energy released is minute compared with that of a thunderstorm, and there is no accepted physical mechanism by which the shock waves could disrupt hail growth. No controlled study has shown them to be effective, and their use is generally classed with other unproven weather modification techniques such as cloud seeding, whose efficacy is also debated.
