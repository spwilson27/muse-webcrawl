Grokster was a peer-to-peer file-sharing software client and company best known as the defendant in the landmark United States Supreme Court case MGM Studios, Inc. v. Grokster, Ltd., decided in 2005.

History

Launched in 2001, Grokster distributed software based on the FastTrack protocol, the same network used by Kazaa. Like its contemporaries Morpheus and Kazaa, it allowed users to exchange music, video and software files directly with one another rather than through a central server, and the software was supported by advertising.

The Supreme Court case

A coalition of entertainment companies led by MGM sued Grokster and StreamCast Networks for inducing copyright infringement. Lower courts ruled for the defendants, citing the Sony v. Universal "substantial non-infringing uses" doctrine. On June 27, 2005, the Supreme Court unanimously reversed, holding that a distributor who promotes a product with the clear expression or affirmative steps to foster infringement can be liable for the resulting acts of third parties — the inducement theory of secondary liability.

Shutdown

Grokster settled with the plaintiffs in November 2005, agreeing to pay $50 million, and shut down its service. Visitors to its website were thereafter greeted by a notice warning that the illegal downloading of copyrighted material was prohibited and that their IP addresses had been logged.
