Fairlie locomotive

A Double Fairlie on the Ffestiniog Railway, Wales

A Fairlie locomotive is a type of articulated steam locomotive designed by the Scottish engineer Robert Francis Fairlie and patented in 1864. Its defining features are two powered bogies (trucks) that can swivel beneath the boiler, allowing a large locomotive to negotiate sharp curves, and — in the best-known "Double Fairlie" form — a double-ended boiler with a central cab, so the engine can work equally well in either direction.

Design

In a Double Fairlie, two boilers are joined back-to-back at a common central firebox, with a chimney at each end and the driving cab in the middle. Steam passes to cylinders on both bogies. Because all of the locomotive's weight rests on driven wheels, adhesive weight and tractive effort are maximised for a given axle load — an advantage on steep, sharply curved, lightly laid railways. A smaller number of Single Fairlies, with one conventional boiler, were also built.

Use

Fairlies were tested on the Neath and Brecon Railway in 1869–70 and famously demonstrated on the narrow-gauge Ffestiniog Railway in Wales, where engines such as Little Wonder proved the design in 1869–70 trials watched by international observers. Fairlies were subsequently exported to railways in Mexico, New Zealand, Russia, India, South America and elsewhere. The Ffestiniog Railway still operates Double Fairlies, including Earl of Merioneth and David Lloyd George, and built a new example in the 21st century. Related articulated designs include the Mason Bogie in the United States.
