Credit (finance)

Credit allows goods, services, or money to be received now in exchange for payment later

Credit (from Latin credit, "he/she/it believes") is the trust which allows one party to provide money or resources to another party wherein the second party does not reimburse the first party immediately, thereby generating a debt. The resources provided may be financial (e.g. granting a loan) or may consist of goods or services (e.g. consumer credit). Credit encompasses any form of deferred payment.

Types of credit

Credit takes many forms. Bank credit includes loans, overdrafts, and lines of credit extended by banks. Trade credit arises when suppliers allow buyers to pay for goods at a later date. Consumer credit includes credit cards, instalment loans, mortgages, and hire purchase agreements used by individuals. Bonds are a form of credit in which investors lend money to companies or governments for a set period at a fixed or variable interest rate. Public credit refers to the borrowing capacity of governments.

Creditworthiness and scoring

Before granting credit, lenders assess the borrower's creditworthiness — the likelihood that the debt will be repaid. Traditionally summarised by the "three Cs" (character, capacity, and capital), modern assessment relies heavily on credit history records maintained by credit bureaus and on numerical credit scores. Lenders may also require collateral, which can be seized if the borrower defaults.

Interest and cost of credit

The price of credit is usually expressed as interest, charged as a percentage of the amount owed, together with fees. The rate reflects the risk of default, the duration of the loan, inflation expectations, and monetary policy. Laws in many jurisdictions require lenders to disclose the total cost of credit, for example as an annual percentage rate (APR).

History

Credit predates coinage: records from ancient Mesopotamia document loans of grain and silver, with interest, more than three thousand years ago. Medieval merchant banking, bills of exchange, and the development of joint-stock companies expanded credit networks across Europe. In the twentieth century, mass consumer credit — charge cards, credit cards, and hire purchase — became a central feature of developed economies, while microfinance extended small loans to borrowers in developing countries.

Economic role

Credit enables investment and consumption to be smoothed over time, funds business expansion and home ownership, and is a principal mechanism by which banks create money. Excessive credit growth, however, has been associated with asset bubbles and financial crises, including the 2007–2008 financial crisis.
