Digital Cinema System Specification

The Digital Cinema System Specification (DCSS or DCI Specification) is a standard developed by Digital Cinema Initiatives (DCI), a joint venture of the major Hollywood studios, to define the technical performance, security, and quality requirements for digital cinema distribution and exhibition. Version 1.0 was published on 20 July 2005; subsequent versions have added provisions for high frame rates, immersive audio, and HDR.

Overview

Before the specification, digital projection systems were proprietary and incompatible. The DCSS established a common container and encoding: films are distributed as a Digital Cinema Package (DCP), with image encoded as JPEG 2000 intra-frame compressed essence in an MXF wrapper, using the DCI X′Y′Z′ colour space at 12 bits per component, at resolutions of 2K (2048×1080) or 4K (4096×2160) and a frame rate of 24 frames per second (with later amendments for 48 fps and other rates).

Audio and packaging

Audio is uncompressed PCM, up to 16 channels at 24-bit/48 kHz (or 96 kHz). Composition playlists, packing lists and asset maps — all in XML — describe how the package's files combine into a playable feature. Subtitles and captions are carried as timed text.

Security

The specification's security architecture was a central motivation: content may be encrypted with AES-128, and decryption keys are delivered separately as Key Delivery Messages (KDMs) tied to specific servers and time windows. Forensic marking (watermarking) of audio and video ties any pirated copy back to a particular screening. The specification defines requirements for media blocks, link encryption between server and projector, and secure logging.

Adoption

The DCSS underpinned the worldwide conversion from 35 mm film projection to digital cinema during the late 2000s and 2010s, financed in part through Virtual Print Fee arrangements. SMPTE later standardised much of the DCP ecosystem in the ST 429 family of standards.
