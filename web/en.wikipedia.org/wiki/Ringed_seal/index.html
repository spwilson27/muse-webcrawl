The ringed seal (Pusa hispida) is a small earless seal inhabiting the Arctic and subarctic regions of the Northern Hemisphere. It is the most abundant and wide-ranging ice seal in the Arctic and takes its name from the light, ring-shaped markings on its dark grey coat. Ringed seals are the principal prey of polar bears.

Description

Adults measure about 1.1–1.6 metres and weigh 40–90 kg, with weight varying strongly by season as blubber is laid down and spent. The coat is dark grey above with pale rings and a lighter belly. Ringed seals maintain breathing holes in land-fast ice using the claws of their foreflippers and can live 25–30 years or more.

Behaviour and reproduction

The species is largely solitary and feeds on fish such as Arctic cod and polar cod, as well as amphipods and other invertebrates. Females excavate birth lairs in snowdrifts over their breathing holes, where a single pup, born in a white lanugo coat, is nursed for five to seven weeks. Snow lairs protect pups from predators and cold but depend on adequate spring snowfall over stable ice.

Subspecies and freshwater populations

Several subspecies are recognised, including the Arctic ringed seal (P. h. hispida), the Baltic ringed seal (P. h. botnica), the Okhotsk ringed seal, and two freshwater forms: the Ladoga seal (P. h. ladogensis) and the critically endangered Saimaa ringed seal (P. h. saimensis) of Finland's Lake Saimaa.

Conservation

Globally the species is listed as Least Concern, but climate-driven loss of sea ice and snow cover is the chief long-term threat; the Arctic subspecies was listed as threatened under the US Endangered Species Act in 2012. Ringed seals have been hunted for millennia by Indigenous Arctic peoples for food, oil, and skins and remain an important subsistence resource.
