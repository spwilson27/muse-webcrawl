Davis wing

Davis wing
Type: Airfoil section / wing planform
Designer: David R. Davis
Best known use: Consolidated B-24 Liberator

The Davis wing is a World War II–era airfoil design, named after its inventor, David R. Davis, an American aeronautical engineer. The wing combined a distinctive teardrop-like cross-section with a high aspect ratio planform, and it became best known as the wing of the Consolidated B-24 Liberator heavy bomber, one of the most-produced Allied aircraft of the war.

Design

Davis developed the airfoil shape in the late 1930s using his own theoretical methods, reportedly refined with water-tank tests. The section had its maximum thickness relatively far forward and a highly cambered shape intended to produce high lift at low drag, particularly at the moderate angles of attack typical of cruising flight. Davis believed his design was significantly more efficient than the standard NACA profiles then in use, and he promoted it vigorously to aircraft manufacturers.

Application

Consolidated Aircraft adopted the wing for its Model 32, which became the B-24 Liberator. The long, thin, shoulder-mounted wing gave the Liberator greater range and a higher cruising speed than its contemporary, the B-17 Flying Fortress, though the wing's heavy loading also made the aircraft less forgiving at low speeds and the structure more vulnerable to battle damage. The Davis wing was also used on the Consolidated PB2Y Coronado flying boat and influenced the design of the B-32 Dominator.

Assessment

Wind-tunnel work at NACA suggested the real airfoil was good but not miraculous: its performance advantages were smaller than Davis claimed, and some of the B-24's efficiency came simply from its high aspect ratio. Nevertheless, the Davis wing remains a well-known example of an independently developed airfoil achieving mass production in a major combat aircraft.
