Rule of inference

Rule of inference
FieldLogic, mathematical logic
ExamplesModus ponens, modus tollens, hypothetical syllogism

In philosophy of logic and in formal logic, a rule of inference, inference rule, or transformation rule is a logical form consisting of a function which takes premises, analyses their syntax, and returns a conclusion (or multiple conclusions). A rule of inference is valid (truth-preserving) if, whenever all of its premises are true, its conclusion must also be true.

Form

Rules are commonly written in the form: premises listed above a horizontal line, with the conclusion below it. For example, the rule of modus ponens states: from P and P → Q, infer Q. Other standard rules include modus tollens (from P → Q and ¬Q, infer ¬P), hypothetical syllogism, disjunctive syllogism, and the introduction and elimination rules for connectives and quantifiers in natural deduction.

Role in formal systems

A formal system (deductive system) is built from axioms and rules of inference: a proof is a finite sequence of formulas, each of which is an axiom or follows from earlier formulas by a rule of inference. Following Hilbert's programme, systems are designed so that the rules can be applied purely mechanically, on the basis of the form of the expressions alone. In sequent calculus and natural deduction (developed by Gerhard Gentzen), most logical principles are expressed as rules rather than axioms.

Derived and admissible rules

A rule is derivable in a system if its conclusion can be obtained from its premises using the system's primitive rules; it is admissible if adding it does not enlarge the set of theorems. Cut elimination — showing that the cut rule is admissible — is a central result of proof theory.
