Neurofilament

Neurofilaments (NF) are class IV intermediate filaments found in axons of neurons of vertebrates. They are protein polymers measuring 10 nm in diameter and many micrometres in length. Together with microtubules (20–25 nm) and microfilaments (7 nm), they form the neuronal cytoskeleton. Their primary function is to provide structural support for axons and to regulate axonal diameter, which in turn influences how fast electrical signals are transmitted along the axon (saltatory conduction).

Subunits

Mammalian neurofilaments are heteropolymers built from several subunits:

  • Neurofilament light chain (NF-L) — about 68 kDa; forms the filament backbone.
  • Neurofilament medium chain (NF-M) — about 160 kDa.
  • Neurofilament heavy chain (NF-H) — about 200 kDa; its long, phosphorylated tail forms side arms that cross-link neighbouring filaments.
  • α-internexin — prominent in the developing nervous system.
  • Peripherin — expressed mainly in the peripheral nervous system.

The medium and heavy chains have long C-terminal tail domains rich in lysine-serine-proline (KSP) repeats that are heavily phosphorylated in mature axons. Phosphorylation controls filament spacing and hence axonal calibre: large, heavily myelinated axons have the most neurofilaments and the widest spacing between them.

Assembly and transport

Neurofilament proteins are synthesised in the neuronal cell body and transported down the axon in the slow component of axoplasmic transport, moving intermittently at rates averaging about a millimetre per day. Filaments are degraded at their destination by calcium-activated proteases such as calpain.

Clinical significance

When axons are injured, neurofilament proteins — especially NF-L — are released into the cerebrospinal fluid and blood. Neurofilament light chain measured in blood with ultrasensitive immunoassays has become an important biomarker of neuroaxonal injury in conditions including multiple sclerosis, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, frontotemporal dementia, and traumatic brain injury.

Mutations in the NEFL gene cause Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 2E/1F. Abnormal accumulations of neurofilaments are a hallmark of several neurodegenerative diseases, and neurofilament inclusions are found in ALS and some dementias.
