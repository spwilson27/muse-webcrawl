Brothers Grimm

The Brothers Grimm — Jacob Ludwig Carl Grimm (1785–1863) and Wilhelm Carl Grimm (1786–1859) — were German academics, philologists, cultural researchers, lexicographers and authors who together collected and published folklore. Their collection Kinder- und Hausmärchen (Children's and Household Tales, 1812), known in English as Grimms' Fairy Tales, popularised stories such as "Cinderella", "Hansel and Gretel", "Little Red Riding Hood", "Rapunzel", "Rumpelstiltskin", "Sleeping Beauty" and "Snow White".

Lives

The brothers were born in Hanau in Hesse-Kassel and studied law at the University of Marburg, where the lectures of Friedrich Carl von Savigny awakened their interest in history and philology. They worked as librarians in Kassel, later as professors at Göttingen, where in 1837 they were dismissed as two of the "Göttingen Seven" for protesting against the King of Hanover's abrogation of the constitution. Jacob later took a post in Berlin.

Fairy tales

The first volume of Children's and Household Tales appeared in 1812 with 86 stories; seven editions followed by 1857, growing to over 200 tales. Wilhelm progressively edited the tales toward a literary, child-friendly style, while retaining their folk character. Their sources were mostly oral storytellers of their acquaintance — notably Dorothea Viehmann and the Hassenpflug family — rather than peasants, as Romantic legend suggested.

Scholarship

Jacob's Deutsche Grammatik (1819–37) formulated Grimm's law, the systematic consonant shift separating Germanic from other Indo-European languages — a foundational result of historical linguistics. Together the brothers began the Deutsches Wörterbuch, a vast historical dictionary of German completed only in 1961. Jacob also wrote on Germanic mythology and law.
