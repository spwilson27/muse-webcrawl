Chimeric antigen receptor T cell

Other names: CAR T-cell therapy

Field: Immunotherapy, oncology

First approval: 2017 (United States)

Chimeric antigen receptor T cells (also known as CAR T cells) are T cells that have been genetically engineered to produce an artificial receptor for use in immunotherapy. Chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) are proteins that redirect the specificity and activity of T cells toward a chosen target, most commonly a protein expressed on cancer cells.

Structure

A CAR typically combines an extracellular antigen-binding domain — usually a single-chain variable fragment derived from a monoclonal antibody — with a hinge, a transmembrane domain, and intracellular signalling domains. The CD3ζ chain provides the primary activation signal. Second-generation CARs add a co-stimulatory domain such as CD28 or 4-1BB, improving expansion and persistence of the cells.

Therapy

In autologous CAR T-cell therapy, a patient's own T cells are collected by leukapheresis, genetically modified (often with a viral vector) to express the CAR, expanded in the laboratory, and infused back into the patient after lymphodepleting chemotherapy. The engineered cells then seek out and kill cells bearing the target antigen.

Approvals and uses

The first CAR T-cell therapy, tisagenlecleucel (Kymriah), was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2017 for certain children and young adults with relapsed or refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia. Subsequent products, most targeting the CD19 antigen, have been approved for several B-cell lymphomas and multiple myeloma (the latter targeting BCMA).

Adverse effects

The most significant toxicities are cytokine release syndrome (CRS), a systemic inflammatory response that can range from mild fever to life-threatening organ dysfunction, and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS). B-cell aplasia and resulting hypogammaglobulinaemia are expected on-target effects of CD19-directed therapy.
