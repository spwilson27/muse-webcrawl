Online shaming

Online shaming is a form of public shaming in which targets are harassed, mocked or stigmatised on the internet, typically via social media. It may be used to punish perceived offenders of social norms, to vent frustration, or as part of what participants frame as activism or accountability — frequently described by critics as "call-out culture" or "cancel culture". Tactics range from critical posts and viral mockery to doxing (publishing private information), review bombing and organised harassment campaigns.

Notable cases

Frequently cited incidents include the 2013 case of Justine Sacco, a public-relations executive whose ill-judged tweet, posted before an eleven-hour flight to Cape Town, became a worldwide trending topic and cost her job by the time she landed — an episode chronicled in Jon Ronson's 2015 book So You've Been Publicly Shamed. Other cases involve mistaken identity, out-of-context photographs, or private jokes made public, with targets reporting job loss, death threats and lasting psychological harm disproportionate to the original offence.

Discussion

Supporters argue that public shaming can hold powerful people and institutions accountable when formal channels fail, and can enforce norms against racism, abuse or cruelty. Critics respond that online mobs lack due process, proportionality and any mechanism for forgiveness, that shaming often falls hardest on private individuals rather than the powerful, and that decontextualised virality makes punishment effectively permanent. Scholars compare the phenomenon to pre-modern punishments such as the stocks and public penance, revived at global scale by networked media.
