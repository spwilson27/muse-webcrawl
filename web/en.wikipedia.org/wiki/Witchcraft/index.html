Witchcraft is the practice of magical skills, spells, and abilities. It is generally considered a subset of the broader concept of magic, the exercise of ritual activities intended to influence the natural or supernatural world. Historically, the term has been applied especially to harmful or malevolent magic, and persons accused of witchcraft were prosecuted in many cultures. Definitions of witchcraft vary widely between societies and over time, and anthropologists have used the term for a variety of magical and religious practices.

In early modern Europe, belief in witchcraft combined older folk ideas about harmful magic with Christian theology that portrayed witches as making a pact with the Devil. This produced the great witch hunts of the 15th to 18th centuries, in which an estimated 40,000 to 60,000 people—most of them women—were executed. Prosecutions peaked between roughly 1560 and 1630 and declined as legal standards, scepticism, and Enlightenment thinking spread.

History

Beliefs about harmful magic are ancient and widespread. The idea of the witch as a member of an organised, devil-worshipping sect developed in the 15th century, aided by treatises such as the Malleus Maleficarum (1487), attributed to Heinrich Kramer. Witch trials combined popular accusations of maleficium (harmful sorcery) with inquisitorial procedures and, often, torture. Famous episodes include the trials in Trier, Würzburg, and Bamberg in Germany, the North Berwick trials in Scotland, and the Salem witch trials of 1692–93 in colonial Massachusetts, in which 19 people were hanged.

By the mid-18th century, legal executions for witchcraft had effectively ended in Europe; England's Witchcraft Act of 1735 reclassified claims of magical power as fraud. In many parts of the world, however, accusations of witchcraft continue into the present day and can still lead to violence against accused persons.

Anthropological views

E. E. Evans-Pritchard's classic study Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic among the Azande (1937) described witchcraft as an explanatory system for misfortune. Anthropologists often distinguish sorcery (learned techniques for harming) from witchcraft as an innate quality, though usage varies. Many traditional cultures maintain healers and ritual specialists who counter harmful magic.

Modern witchcraft

In the mid-20th century, Gerald Gardner publicised Wicca, a neopagan religion centred on the worship of a Goddess and a Horned God, which he presented as the survival of an ancient witch-cult—an interpretation historians treat with scepticism. Wicca and related traditions, sometimes collectively called modern pagan witchcraft, now have practitioners worldwide. The pentacle, ritual tools such as the athame, and seasonal festivals (sabbats) are characteristic of many of these traditions.
