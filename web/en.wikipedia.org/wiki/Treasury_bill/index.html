Treasury bill

A Treasury bill (abbreviated T-bill) is a short-term debt obligation backed by the treasury department of a government, generally with a maturity of less than one year. In the United States, Treasury bills are issued by the Department of the Treasury in maturities of 4, 6, 8, 13, 17, 26 and 52 weeks, and are sold in denominations from $100.

How T-bills work

T-bills are zero-coupon securities: they pay no periodic interest but are sold at a discount to their face (par) value, and the investor receives the full face value at maturity. The difference between the purchase price and the face value represents the interest earned. Prices are quoted in terms of a discount yield, and bills are issued through regular auctions — competitive and non-competitive — conducted by the Treasury, with primary dealers obliged to participate.

Role in finance

Because they are backed by the full faith and credit of the issuing government and have very short maturities, T-bills are considered among the lowest-risk investments available; the three-month U.S. T-bill rate is commonly used as a proxy for the risk-free rate of return in financial models. Bills are a central tool of money markets, held in large quantities by money market funds, banks and foreign central banks, and serve the Treasury as a flexible means of managing the government's short-term cash needs. Longer-term U.S. government securities are classified separately as Treasury notes (2–10 years) and Treasury bonds (20–30 years).
