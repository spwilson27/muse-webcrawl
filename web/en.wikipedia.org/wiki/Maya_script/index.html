Maya script, also known as Maya glyphs, is the native writing system of the Maya civilization of Mesoamerica and is the only such system that has been substantially deciphered. The earliest inscriptions found identifiable as Maya date to the 3rd century BCE in San Bartolo, Guatemala. The script was used continuously until shortly after the arrival of the Spanish conquistadors in the 16th–17th centuries, after which it was suppressed and largely destroyed.

The script is logosyllabic: signs include logograms, which represent whole words or morphemes, and a syllabary of signs used to represent syllables and to provide phonetic complements. Roughly 800 distinct signs are known, of which perhaps 400–500 were in use at any one time. Glyphs were arranged in blocks in a grid pattern, read left to right and top to bottom in paired columns.

Structure

A typical glyph block contains a main sign, often a logogram, surrounded by smaller "affix" signs that modify its meaning or pronunciation. Many logograms could be substituted by syllabic spellings, and the same word could be written in multiple ways — a flexibility Maya scribes exploited for calligraphic and aesthetic effect. The known syllabary comprises roughly 150 signs of consonant-vowel form (e.g. ba, ka, ma, u), many with homophonous variants.

Maya writing recorded history (through the Long Count calendar and dynastic records on stelae), astronomy and calendrical calculations, ritual and prophecy in folded-screen codices, and ownership texts painted or incised on ceramics.

History

The script's mature form belongs to the Classic period (c. 250–900 CE), when kingdoms across the Maya lowlands erected carved monuments recording accessions, wars, marriages and period-ending ceremonies. A smaller number of inscriptions are Postclassic. By the Spanish conquest, professional scribes (aj tz'ib) maintained the tradition. Diego de Landa, a Franciscan friar in Yucatán, both preserved an invaluable (if flawed) account of the script in his Relación de las cosas de Yucatán (c. 1566) and participated in the suppression of Maya books, most notoriously at the auto-da-fé of Maní in 1562.

Only four Maya codices are generally accepted as surviving: the Dresden, Madrid, Paris and Grolier (Maya Codex of Mexico) codices, the last authenticated in 2018.

Decipherment

Although 19th-century scholars such as Ernst Förstemann deciphered the calendrical and astronomical content, the script's phonetic nature was widely denied until the 1950s, when Yuri Knorozov, working in the Soviet Union, argued on the basis of Landa's so-called "alphabet" that the signs combined logographic and syllabic values. Tatiana Proskouriakoff then demonstrated (1960) that the inscriptions recorded actual dynastic history. From the 1970s onward, epigraphers including Linda Schele, Peter Mathews and David Stuart achieved rapid progress, and today the great majority of surviving texts can be read.

Literature

Texts survive on stone monuments, lintels, ceramics, bone, and in the four codices. Colonial-period Maya literature, such as the Popol Vuh and the Books of Chilam Balam, was written in the Latin alphabet rather than in glyphs.
