Westminster Abbey

Westminster Abbey, formally the Collegiate Church of Saint Peter at Westminster, is a large, mainly Gothic abbey church in the City of Westminster, London, just west of the Palace of Westminster. It is one of the United Kingdom's most notable religious buildings and the traditional place of coronation and a burial site for English and, later, British monarchs. Since the coronation of William the Conqueror in 1066, all coronations of English and British monarchs have occurred here.

History

A Benedictine monastery stood on Thorney Island by the tenth century. Edward the Confessor built a great Romanesque church, consecrated in 1065 days before his death; Henry III rebuilt it in Gothic style from 1245, creating the present church with its shrine of St Edward. Henry VII added his magnificent Lady Chapel (1503–19). The western towers, by Nicholas Hawksmoor, were completed in 1745. Dissolved as a monastery in the Reformation, the abbey became a royal peculiar, governed by its Dean and Chapter directly under the monarch.

Coronations, weddings and burials

The Coronation Chair, holding the Stone of Scone beneath it, has been used since 1308. Royal weddings include those of Prince William and Catherine Middleton (2011). Over 3,300 people are buried or commemorated in the abbey: monarchs in the royal vaults, statesmen, and in Poets' Corner writers from Chaucer to Dickens; scientists Newton and Darwin rest in the nave, and the grave of the Unknown Warrior (1920) is among its most honoured memorials. The abbey receives over a million visitors a year and remains a working church with daily services.
