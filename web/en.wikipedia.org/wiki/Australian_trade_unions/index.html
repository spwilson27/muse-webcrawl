Australian trade unions

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Australian trade unions are organisations of workers in Australia formed to protect and advance their interests. From small beginnings in the 1850s, trade unionism grew to be a powerful force in Australian society and politics. Trade unions in Australia are organised into state and national bodies, with the Australian Council of Trade Unions (ACTU) acting as the peak national council.

Unions campaigned successfully for the eight-hour day in the 1850s.

History

Australian unionism traces its origins to early nineteenth-century craft societies. In 1856 stonemasons in Melbourne won the eight-hour day, among the first in the world. Great strikes of the 1890s — the maritime strike and the shearers' strikes — were defeated but spurred the formation of labour political parties, leading to the Australian Labor Party and the world's first Labor government in Queensland in 1899.

The federal system of compulsory conciliation and arbitration, established after Federation, registered unions and gave them legal status in exchange for regulated industrial action. Union density peaked in the mid-twentieth century at over 60 percent of employees, supported by arrangements such as union preference. From the 1980s, structural change, legislative reforms and enterprise bargaining contributed to a long decline; density fell to around 12–13 percent of employees by the early 2020s.

Structure

Australian unions include large general and industry unions such as the Australian Workers' Union, the Australian Manufacturing Workers' Union, United Workers Union, and professional associations such as the Australian Nursing and Midwifery Federation. Unions are regulated federally as registered organisations under the Fair Work (Registered Organisations) Act.
