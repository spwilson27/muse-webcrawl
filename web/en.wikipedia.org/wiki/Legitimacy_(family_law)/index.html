Legitimacy (family law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Legitimacy
Registers long recorded a child's legitimacy status

In family law, legitimacy is the status of a child born to parents who are legally married to one another, or born within a legally recognised relationship such as a common-law marriage. A child born outside marriage is termed illegitimate or — in older legal usage still found in statutes — a child "born out of wedlock." Historically, legitimacy determined rights of inheritance, succession to titles and property, parental custody, support obligations, nationality and social standing. Over the 20th and 21st centuries most legal systems have abolished or greatly reduced the legal disabilities attaching to birth outside marriage.

History

Roman law distinguished children born in lawful marriage (legitimi) from others, but allowed legitimation by the subsequent marriage of the parents or by imperial rescript. Canon law adopted legitimation by subsequent marriage; the English common law famously refused it — the barons at the Statute of Merton (1236) declared they would not change the laws of England — and illegitimate children were filius nullius, "nobody's child," unable to inherit. Until the 20th century the stigma was severe in much of Europe and the Americas, and an illegitimate child ordinarily could not inherit land, succeed to a peerage, or claim support from the father.

Reform

Reform proceeded piecemeal. In England and Wales, the Legitimacy Act 1926 introduced legitimation by subsequent marriage, and the Family Law Reform Act 1987 removed most remaining distinctions between children of married and unmarried parents. In the United States, a series of Supreme Court decisions beginning with Levy v. Louisiana (1968) struck down laws discriminating against nonmarital children under the Equal Protection Clause, and the Uniform Parentage Act regularised paternity regardless of marital status. Norway's Castberg Children's Laws of 1915 were pioneering in giving children born outside marriage the father's name and inheritance rights. France, Germany and most other European states similarly equalised status; the European Court of Human Rights held in Marckx v. Belgium (1979) that distinctions based on birth outside marriage could violate the Convention.

Legitimation

Legitimation is the legal process by which a child born outside marriage acquires legitimate status, classically by the parents' subsequent marriage (recognised in most civil law systems and many US states), by formal acknowledgment of paternity, or by court order. Legitimation matters today chiefly in the succession rules of monarchies and nobility, in some immigration and citizenship contexts, and in canon law, where legitimacy retains significance for certain purposes.

Modern position

In most Western jurisdictions the concept now has little operative legal significance for ordinary children: parentage, not marital status, governs support, custody and inheritance. The concept remains prominent in dynastic succession — for example, the British Act of Settlement and Royal Marriages rules — and historically in succession disputes over thrones and titles.
