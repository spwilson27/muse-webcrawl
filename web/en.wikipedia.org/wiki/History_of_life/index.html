History of life

This article outlines the evolution of life on Earth over more than four billion years.


The evolutionary history of life, from LUCA to the present.

The history of life on Earth traces the processes by which living and fossil organisms evolved, from the earliest emergence of life to the present day. Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago, and the earliest evidence of life dates to at least 3.8 billion years ago. All extant species descend from a last universal common ancestor (LUCA).

Origins

Life originated through abiogenesis — natural processes arising from simpler organic compounds. Early Earth had a reducing atmosphere rich in water, methane, ammonia and hydrogen. Experiments such as Miller–Urey (1953) showed amino acids can form under such conditions. Early replicators may have existed in an "RNA world" before DNA-based life emerged. Photosynthetic cyanobacteria later oxygenated the atmosphere in the Great Oxidation Event (c. 2.4 billion years ago), transforming the planet's chemistry and enabling aerobic life.

Prokaryotes and eukaryotes

For roughly two billion years, life consisted of single-celled prokaryotes (Bacteria and Archaea). Eukaryotic cells arose through endosymbiosis — an archaeal host engulfing bacteria that became mitochondria, and in plants, chloroplasts. Sexual reproduction accelerated genetic variation. Multicellular organisms evolved multiple times in algae, fungi, plants and animals.

The Cambrian explosion and colonisation of land

Around 541 million years ago the Cambrian explosion produced most major animal body plans, including trilobites, early chordates and anomalocaridid predators. Life subsequently colonised land: plants and fungi by the Ordovician–Silurian, then arthropods, and in the Devonian tetrapods evolved from lobe-finned fish. Forests of giant clubmosses formed coal deposits in the Carboniferous.

Mesozoic and Cenozoic

Amniotes split into synapsids (leading to mammals) and sauropsids (leading to reptiles and birds). Dinosaurs dominated the Mesozoic era until the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event 66 million years ago, caused by an asteroid impact, eliminated non-avian dinosaurs. Mammals and birds radiated into vacated niches. In the last few million years bipedal hominins evolved in Africa, culminating in Homo sapiens.

Mass extinctions

Five great mass extinctions punctuate the fossil record: the end-Ordovician, Late Devonian, end-Permian ("Great Dying", the largest of all), end-Triassic and end-Cretaceous events. Many scientists argue human activity is now driving a sixth mass extinction.
