Microfiction

Microfiction is a form of extremely short fiction, typically defined as a complete story told in 300 words or fewer. It overlaps with, and is sometimes treated as a subcategory of, flash fiction, which is usually capped at about 1,000 words. Related named lengths include the "drabble" (exactly 100 words), the 50-word "dribble" or minisaga, and the "hint fiction" of 25 words or fewer popularized by editor Robert Swartwood. The best-known example in the popular imagination is the six-word story "For sale: baby shoes, never worn", often attributed — probably apocryphally — to Ernest Hemingway.

Characteristics

Despite its brevity, microfiction aims to deliver a complete narrative arc or at least a resonant moment of implication, relying on suggestion, a final-line twist, and the reader's imagination to fill in the unwritten story. Writers typically begin in the middle of the action, use a single sharply drawn image or scene, and treat titles as part of the word count and the story itself. The form prizes compression: every word must work.

History and venues

Very short prose has antecedents in Aesop's fables, parables, and the vignettes of writers such as Ambrose Bierce and Franz Kafka. The modern form grew with literary magazines in the late twentieth century and found a natural home online, where journals devoted to short-shorts, social-media constraints such as Twitter fiction, and anthologies like Flash Fiction Forward and the Norton anthology Flash Fiction International have showcased it. Awards and contests for microfiction are now common, and the form is widely used in creative-writing teaching.
