Silbury Hill is a prehistoric artificial chalk mound near Avebury in the English county of Wiltshire. At around 39 metres (130 ft) high, it is the tallest prehistoric man-made mound in Europe and one of the largest in the world, comparable in volume to the contemporary Egyptian pyramids. Built during the Neolithic period, in several stages between about 2400 and 2300 BC, it forms part of the Avebury complex of monuments. Despite centuries of investigation, no burial has ever been found in it and its purpose remains unknown.

Construction

Archaeological excavation and coring have shown the hill was built in stages over perhaps a century: a small gravel mound was progressively enlarged with dumps of soil, chalk rubble and finally a stepped chalk cone, its ditches providing much of the material. Estimates suggest the work involved around 250,000 cubic metres of material moved with antler picks and shovels — plausibly millions of person-hours. The mound was built in the valley of the River Kennet near springs, within a landscape dense with monuments including Avebury's stone circles, West Kennet Long Barrow and the Sanctuary.

Investigations

Antiquarians dug into the hill repeatedly: a vertical shaft in 1776, a horizontal tunnel driven by the Duke of Northumberland in 1849, and further excavations by Professor Richard Atkinson in 1968–70 televised by the BBC — none found any burial or chamber. In 2000 a hole opened at the summit when Atkinson's shaft collapsed after heavy rain, prompting a major English Heritage conservation programme (2000–2007) that stabilised the mound with chalk fill. Because the hill is a Scheduled Monument and ecologically sensitive SSSI, climbing it is prohibited.
