Moral non-cognitivism

Non-cognitivism is the meta-ethical view that ethical sentences do not express propositions at all, and therefore can be neither true nor false (they are not truth-apt). A non-cognitivist denial of cognitivism holds that ethical judgements are not — and cannot be — objectively true or false, because they do not describe states of affairs in the world. Instead, moral utterances express attitudes, commands, or prescriptions.

Varieties

Emotivism

Emotivism, developed by A. J. Ayer in Language, Truth and Logic (1936) and by Charles Stevenson in Ethics and Language (1944), holds that moral statements primarily express the speaker's emotions and aim to influence the attitudes of others. "Murder is wrong" functions much like "Boo to murder!" Ayer rooted the view in logical positivism's verification principle: since ethical claims cannot be empirically verified, they are literally meaningless as factual statements, though they retain emotive meaning.

Prescriptivism

Prescriptivism, associated with R. M. Hare (The Language of Morals, 1952), holds that moral judgements are a form of universal prescription — imperatives that the speaker applies to all relevantly similar cases, including cases in which the speaker occupies the position of the victim. Unlike simple imperatives, moral judgements are universalisable, a feature Hare used to derive substantive constraints on moral reasoning.

Expressivism

Modern expressivism, developed by Allan Gibbard (Wise Choices, Apt Feelings, 1990) and Simon Blackburn, seeks to explain moral thought without moral facts by treating moral utterances as expressions of plans, norms, or attitude-like states. Blackburn's "quasi-realism" attempts to earn back, starting from an expressivist base, the right to talk of moral truth, knowledge, and error in a deflated sense.

The Frege–Geach problem

The principal technical objection to non-cognitivism is the Frege–Geach problem: moral sentences appear in unasserted contexts, such as the antecedent of a conditional ("If murder is wrong, then inviting murder is wrong"), where they plainly do not express the speaker's emotion or issue a prescription; yet the argument from "murder is wrong" and that conditional to "inviting murder is wrong" looks valid. A non-cognitivist must explain how a sentence can keep one meaning both when asserted and when embedded, without being truth-apt. Expressivists have responded with increasingly sophisticated accounts of moral logic, and the debate remains active.

Motivations

Non-cognitivism is motivated by several considerations: the close tie between moral judgement and motivation (which is hard to explain if moral judgements are mere beliefs); moral disagreement, which often persists after full agreement on the non-moral facts; and metaphysical discomfort with objective moral properties, famously pressed by J. L. Mackie — though Mackie himself defended a cognitivist error theory instead.
