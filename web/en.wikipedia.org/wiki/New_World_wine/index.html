New World wine

New World wines are wines produced outside the traditional winegrowing areas of Europe — the "Old World" — particularly in Argentina, Australia, Canada, Chile, Mexico, New Zealand, South Africa, and the United States. The term generally denotes both the wines and the wine-producing countries that developed viticulture after European colonization, largely from the 16th century onward and especially in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Characteristics

New World wines are typically labelled by grape variety rather than by region, reflecting a marketing approach centred on the grape and the producer's brand. Because many New World regions have warm, sunny climates, the wines are often described as riper, fruitier, fuller-bodied, and higher in alcohol than their Old World counterparts. New World producers historically adopted industrial techniques earlier and more enthusiastically — temperature-controlled stainless steel fermentation, irrigation, mechanical harvesting, cultured yeasts, and oak chips — and have been less bound by appellation rules governing permitted varieties and practices.

History

Vines were planted by Spanish missionaries in the Americas in the 1500s; winemaking reached South Africa with the Dutch in 1655 and Australia with the First Fleet in 1788. The modern New World wine industry took shape in the late 20th century, symbolized by the 1976 "Judgment of Paris," in which Californian wines outscored prestigious French wines in a blind tasting. Export-driven booms followed in Australia (led by brands such as Penfolds and Yellow Tail), Chile, and New Zealand, whose Marlborough Sauvignon blanc became a global phenomenon.

Major regions

  • United States: California (Napa Valley, Sonoma), Oregon (Pinot noir), Washington, New York
  • Australia: Barossa Valley, Margaret River, McLaren Vale, Hunter Valley
  • New Zealand: Marlborough, Central Otago, Hawke's Bay
  • Chile: Maipo, Colchagua, Casablanca valleys
  • Argentina: Mendoza (Malbec), Salta
  • South Africa: Stellenbosch, Franschhoek, Swartland
  • Canada: Okanagan Valley, Niagara Peninsula (noted for icewine)

The New World/Old World distinction has blurred as techniques and styles converge, but the terms remain common in wine writing and trade.
