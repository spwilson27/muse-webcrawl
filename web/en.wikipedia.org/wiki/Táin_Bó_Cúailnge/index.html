Táin Bó Cúailnge (modern Irish spelling Táin Bó Cuailnge), "The Driving-off of Cattle of Cooley", commonly called The Táin, is the central epic of the Ulster Cycle of Irish mythology and is often described as Ireland's national epic, comparable to the Iliad for Greece. It tells of a war waged by Queen Medb of Connacht to seize the great stud bull Donn Cúailnge of Ulster, and of the lone defence of the province by the young hero Cú Chulainn.

Plot

The tale opens with the famous "pillow talk" of Medb and her husband Ailill, who compare their possessions item by item and find them exactly equal — except that Ailill owns the magnificent white-horned bull Finnbhennach, born among Medb's herds but refusing to be owned by a woman. To surpass him, Medb resolves to obtain the Brown Bull of Cooley, Donn Cúailnge, the only bull to match Finnbhennach. When its owner Dáire mac Fiachna refuses the loan, Medb musters the men of Ireland to take it by force.

The invasion finds Ulster defenceless: its warriors are prostrated by the cess noínden, the nine-days' debility laid on them by the goddess Macha's curse. Only the seventeen-year-old Cú Chulainn, exempt from the curse, stands against the host. He harries the army with guerrilla attacks, then holds it at bay in a compact of single combats at the fords, defeating champion after champion — among them, to his grief, his beloved foster-brother Ferdia, in a duel lasting four days that is the emotional summit of the epic. When the Ulstermen finally recover and take the field, the Connacht army is routed; but the two great bulls meet in a final battle in which Donn Cúailnge kills Finnbhennach and then dies of his wounds, scattering his rival's flesh across Ireland — and so the war ends with neither side enriched.

Manuscripts and recensions

The Táin survives in three recensions. Recension I, in Lebor na hUidre (c. 1100) and the Yellow Book of Lecan, is incomplete and linguistically oldest. Recension II, in the Book of Leinster (c. 1160), is a more literary, harmonised version and breaks off near the end; its scribe famously appends a colophon in Latin half-blessing and half-doubting the tale. Recension III is a later, fragmentary version. The narrative was assembled from written sources of the 7th–9th centuries over a much older oral tradition.
