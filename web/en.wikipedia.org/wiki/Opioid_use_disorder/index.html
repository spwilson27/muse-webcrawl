Opioid use disorder

Opioid use disorder (OUD) is a substance use disorder characterized by cravings for opioids, continued use despite physical and/or psychological deterioration, increased tolerance, and withdrawal symptoms after discontinuing opioids. Opioid use disorder can cause long-term health effects, harm, and death. The signs and symptoms of opioid use disorder include drug-seeking behaviour, reluctance to discontinue, and frequent refilling of opioid prescriptions.

Signs and symptoms

Diagnosis is based on criteria in the DSM-5, including taking opioids in larger amounts than intended, unsuccessful efforts to cut down, great time spent obtaining or recovering from opioids, craving, tolerance, and withdrawal. Physical dependence, reflected in tolerance and withdrawal, is related to but distinct from addiction.

Causes and pathophysiology

Opioids such as morphine, heroin, fentanyl, and oxycodone bind to mu-opioid receptors, producing analgesia and euphoria. Repeated use alters the brain's reward circuitry and stress systems, driving compulsive use. Genetic factors account for a substantial share of vulnerability, alongside environmental and psychological factors.

Treatment

Medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) are the standard of care: the partial agonist buprenorphine, the full agonist methadone, and the antagonist naltrexone reduce illicit use, overdose, and mortality. The overdose-reversal agent naloxone is widely distributed as a harm-reduction measure. Psychosocial support is recommended alongside medication, though medication alone is effective for many patients.

Epidemiology

Opioid use disorder is a central component of the opioid epidemic, particularly in North America, where illicit fentanyl has driven overdose deaths to historic levels. In the United States, opioids were involved in roughly 80,000 deaths in 2023, though provisional figures showed a decline in 2024.
