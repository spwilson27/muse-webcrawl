Digital Revolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Digital Revolution, also known as the Third Industrial Revolution, is the shift from mechanical and analogue electronic technology to digital electronics which began in the latter half of the 20th century. Central to the revolution were the development and proliferation of digital logic circuits, the transistor and the integrated circuit, and derived technologies including the digital computer, the mobile phone, and the Internet. The term also describes the sweeping changes brought by digital computing and communications to the economy, society, and culture, comparable in scale to the Agricultural and Industrial Revolutions.

Origins

The theoretical groundwork was laid by Claude Shannon's 1937 master's thesis on Boolean logic and switching circuits and his 1948 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication". The transistor, demonstrated at Bell Labs in 1947, replaced bulky vacuum tubes, and the integrated circuit (1958–59, developed independently by Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce) placed many transistors on a single chip. Moore's law — the doubling of transistor counts roughly every two years — drove exponential improvements in cost and performance for decades.

Spread

Mainframe computers entered business and government in the 1950s and 1960s; minicomputers followed, and the microprocessor (Intel 4004, 1971) made personal computers practical. The IBM PC (1981) and Apple Macintosh (1984) brought computing to offices and homes. Telecommunications digitised in parallel: mobile phone networks spread from the 1980s, and the ARPANET's descendant, the Internet, opened to commerce in the early 1990s after Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web in 1989. By the 2010s, smartphones had put networked computing in the hands of billions.

Effects

The digital revolution transformed industries wholesale: music, photography, publishing, retail, banking, and film moved from physical media and face-to-face transactions to digital files and platforms. It enabled globalised supply chains and remote work, created entirely new sectors (software, e-commerce, social media), and displaced many clerical and manufacturing jobs through automation. Scholars debate its social consequences, from enhanced access to information and civic participation to concerns over privacy, surveillance, misinformation, and the digital divide. The ongoing spread of artificial intelligence and ubiquitous connected devices ("Industry 4.0", the Internet of things) is sometimes described as a continuation or a fourth phase of the same revolution.
