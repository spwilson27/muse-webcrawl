Lübeck law

The Lübeck law (German: Lübisches Recht) was a form of German municipal law developed in the city of Lübeck and adopted during the Middle Ages by more than a hundred towns around the Baltic Sea, making it one of the most influential town-law families in medieval northern Europe, alongside Magdeburg law.

History

After Lübeck was refounded by Henry the Lion in 1159 and received its privileges, the city's council (Rat) developed a body of law favourable to long-distance merchants, emphasising town autonomy, a governing council of merchants, freedom of trade, and inheritance rules suited to commercial life. Towns granted Lübeck law—including Rostock, Wismar, Stralsund, Greifswald, Kiel, Gdańsk (Danzig) in its early period, Elbląg, Tallinn (Reval), and Riga's surrounding region settlements—appealed in legal disputes to the Lübeck Oberhof, the city's council acting as a superior court, which kept the law relatively uniform.

Content and codification

The law was codified in manuscripts from the 13th century onward, in Low German and later Latin and High German versions; the 1586 revision (the Revidiertes Lübsches Recht) remained influential into the early modern era. Lübeck law governed council constitution, citizenship, market regulation, debt, property, and family and inheritance matters.

Decline

With the decline of the Hanseatic League and the rise of territorial states, Lübeck law gradually lost ground to territorial and Roman-derived law, though it survived in Lübeck itself until the introduction of the German Civil Code (BGB) in 1900.
