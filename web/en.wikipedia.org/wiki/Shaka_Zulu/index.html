Shaka Zulu

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Shaka standing with the long shield and stabbing spear of his regiments, from a later illustration]

Shaka (c. 1787 – 22 September 1828), also known as Shaka Zulu or Shaka kaSenzangakhona, was the founder of the Zulu Empire in south-eastern Africa. Rising from exile to chief of the small Zulu clan (1816), he revolutionised Ngoni warfare — close-order infantry with the short stabbing spear (iklwa), large cowhide shield and 'buffalo horns' envelopment — and by conquest and incorporation built a kingdom that transformed the region in the upheavals remembered as the Mfecane ('the crushing'). Assassinated by his half-brothers in 1828, he remains one of the most famous — and most mythologised — figures of African history.

The military revolution

Serving under Dingiswayo of the Mthethwa, Shaka absorbed and radicalised the age-set (ibutho) system: regiments quartered in military kraals, drilled to fight barefoot at the trot, forbidden marriage until discharged. His tactical system — chest to fix, horns to envelop, loins in reserve — and relentless campaigning absorbed clan after clan between the Drakensberg and the sea. Whether he was a state-builder unifying a shattered region or a destroyer whose wars depopulated it (the Mfecane's scale and causes are now debated — slave-raiding from Delagoa Bay and Griqua frontiers share the blame in revisionist accounts) remains historiographically alive.

Death and legacy

After the death of his mother Nandi (1827), grief — and in the sources, increasingly erratic tyranny — consumed the court; in September 1828 his half-brothers Dingane and Mhlangana, with his servant Mbopha, stabbed him at kwaDukuza. Dingane succeeded, to meet the Boers at Blood River (1838). Shaka's image has been endlessly remade — colonial demonology, Zulu nationalist hero, the 1986 television epic Shaka Zulu — over a documentary base of oral traditions and the accounts of traders who met him in the 1820s.
