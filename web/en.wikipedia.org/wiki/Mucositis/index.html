Mucositis

Mucositis is the painful inflammation and ulceration of the mucous membranes lining the digestive tract, usually as an adverse effect of chemotherapy and radiotherapy treatments for cancer. It can occur anywhere along the gastrointestinal tract but is most commonly described in the mouth, where it is called oral mucositis. Mucositis significantly affects quality of life, can limit a patient's ability to eat and drink, and may force dose reductions or interruptions in cancer therapy.

Pathophysiology

The condition develops through a multi-stage process: cytotoxic therapy damages the rapidly dividing basal cells of the mucosal epithelium and generates reactive oxygen species, triggering inflammatory signalling, thinning and ultimately ulceration of the mucosa. The ulcers are then colonised by bacteria, which amplify inflammation; healing follows as epithelial cells regenerate once treatment ceases.

Signs and grading

Symptoms begin with redness and soreness of the mouth and progress in severe cases to confluent ulcers, bleeding and severe pain. The World Health Organization scale grades oral mucositis from 0 (none) to 4 (ulceration so severe that oral intake is impossible). Gastrointestinal mucositis causes nausea, diarrhoea, cramping and malabsorption.

Prevention and treatment

Management is mainly supportive: meticulous oral hygiene, pain control (including topical anaesthetics and, when needed, opioids), nutritional support and treatment of secondary infection. Specific measures supported by guidelines include oral cryotherapy (ice chips) during certain chemotherapy infusions, the keratinocyte growth factor palifermin for patients undergoing haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and low-level laser therapy in some settings.
