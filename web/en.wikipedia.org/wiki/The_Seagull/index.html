The Seagull

The Seagull (Russian: Ча́йка, Chayka) is a play by Russian dramatist Anton Chekhov, written in 1895 and first produced in 1896. It is considered one of the director Konstantin Stanislavski's great achievements and a landmark of modern theatre.

Plot

At a lakeside estate, the ageing actress Irina Arkadina visits her brother Sorin with her lover, the successful writer Boris Trigorin. Her son Konstantin Treplev, an aspiring playwright, stages an experimental play starring Nina Zarechnaya, a neighbour's daughter he loves. When Arkadina mocks the performance, Konstantin halts it. Nina is drawn to Trigorin, follows him to Moscow, has his child, and is abandoned; her acting career fails. Konstantin, though published, remains unfulfilled. Two years later Nina returns briefly, comparing herself to the seagull Konstantin once shot and laid at her feet — destroyed by a man's idle cruelty — and Konstantin shoots himself.

Production history

The 1896 St Petersburg premiere was a notorious failure — Chekhov fled the theatre and vowed to stop writing plays. In 1898 Stanislavski and Nemirovich-Danchenko's Moscow Art Theatre production was a triumph that helped found the theatre: a seagull remains its emblem to this day. The play's subtext, ensemble acting and refusal of melodrama reshaped modern drama.

Themes

The play examines art versus fame, new forms versus old, love's asymmetries, and lives of quiet desperation. Its famous symbol — the seagull — is debated as standing for Nina, for Konstantin, for freedom, or for artistic innocence destroyed.
