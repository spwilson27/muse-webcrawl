Street photography, also sometimes called candid photography, is photography conducted for art or inquiry that features unmediated chance encounters and random incidents within public places. It usually, but not necessarily, works within the definition of the "street" literally; it might also be a rural scene or an object found in a public setting.

Presence and transience are key themes of street photography; the photographic interpretation of a street photographer's work may be influenced by those around them and by societal norms. Street photography does not necessitate the presence of a street or even the urban environment. People are usually in direct contact with the environment, though the subject can be absent of people and can be an object, an environment where an overriding impression is taken, or very straightforward ("sharp") photographic characteristics.

History

The genre emerged in the late nineteenth century as portable cameras became available. Early practitioners such as John Thomson documented London street life in the 1870s, and Eugène Atget photographed the streets of Paris in the decades around 1900. The invention of the small Leica rangefinder camera in the 1920s transformed the practice, allowing photographers to work quickly and unobtrusively.

Henri Cartier-Bresson, whose 1952 book Images à la sauvette (The Decisive Moment) became a manifesto for the genre, defined street photography as capturing a fleeting, decisive moment. In the post-war United States, Robert Frank's The Americans (1958) offered a raw, subjective view of American society, while Diane Arbus, Garry Winogrand and Lee Friedlander pushed the form toward confrontational and formally complex images.

Techniques

Street photographers typically use small, unobtrusive cameras—historically 35 mm rangefinders, now often mirrorless cameras or smartphones—and favour available light. Common techniques include:

  • Zone focusing – pre-setting focus and aperture so subjects at a given distance are sharp without autofocusing.
  • Shooting from the hip – photographing without raising the camera to the eye.
  • Layering – composing scenes with foreground, middle ground and background interest.
  • Juxtaposition – placing unrelated elements in a humorous or ironic relationship.

Legal and ethical issues

The legality of photographing strangers in public varies by jurisdiction. In many countries, photography in public places requires no consent, though commercial use may be restricted and some jurisdictions recognise a right to one's own image. Ethical debates centre on consent, dignity, and the photographer's responsibility toward vulnerable subjects.
