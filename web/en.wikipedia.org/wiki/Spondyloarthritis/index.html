Spondyloarthritis (also called spondyloarthropathy, abbreviated SpA) is a family of chronic inflammatory diseases of the joints that share distinctive clinical features and a strong association with the HLA-B27 genetic marker. Unlike rheumatoid arthritis, spondyloarthritis is typically seronegative (rheumatoid factor is absent) and characteristically involves the axial skeleton — the sacroiliac joints and spine — as well as entheses, the sites where tendons and ligaments attach to bone.

Classification

The spondyloarthritis family includes ankylosing spondylitis (radiographic axial spondyloarthritis), non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis, psoriatic arthritis, reactive arthritis, arthritis associated with inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis), and undifferentiated spondyloarthritis. The Assessment of SpondyloArthritis international Society (ASAS) classifies the conditions into predominantly axial and predominantly peripheral forms.

Signs and symptoms

The hallmark symptom is inflammatory back pain: onset before age 45, morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes, improvement with activity but not rest, and awakening in the second half of the night because of pain. Peripheral features include asymmetric oligoarthritis (often of the lower limbs), enthesitis (such as Achilles tendinitis and plantar fasciitis), and dactylitis ("sausage digits"). Extra-articular manifestations include anterior uveitis, psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease, and, less commonly, aortitis and cardiac conduction abnormalities. Over time, spinal inflammation can lead to syndesmophyte formation and ankylosis — the fused "bamboo spine" of advanced ankylosing spondylitis.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the clinical pattern, laboratory tests (HLA-B27, C-reactive protein), and imaging. Plain radiographs may show sacroiliitis and syndesmophytes; MRI can detect active sacroiliac inflammation (bone marrow oedema) years before structural changes appear on X-ray. No single test is diagnostic, and diagnostic delay of several years remains common.

Treatment

Regular exercise and physiotherapy are cornerstones of management. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are first-line pharmacotherapy. For axial disease that remains active, biologic agents — tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors and interleukin-17 inhibitors — and targeted synthetic drugs such as Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors are used. Conventional disease-modifying drugs such as sulfasalazine may help peripheral arthritis but are ineffective for axial symptoms. Smoking cessation is strongly advised, as smoking worsens disease activity and radiographic progression.
