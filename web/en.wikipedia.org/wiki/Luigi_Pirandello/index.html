Luigi Pirandello

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Pirandello in the 1930s, gaunt and white-bearded, the theorist of the mask receiving the Nobel medal]

Luigi Pirandello (28 June 1867 – 10 December 1936) was an Italian dramatist, novelist, poet and short story writer whose works count among the founding texts of modern theatre; he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1934 for his 'bold and ingenious revival of dramatic and scenic art' (in the Academy's phrasing).

His most famous play, Six Characters in Search of an Author (1921), exploded theatre's contract with reality: six characters, abandoned unfinished by their author, interrupt a rehearsal demanding to be played. His novels include The Late Mattia Pascal (1904), the dead man who restarts his life, and the hundreds of Short Stories for a Year.

Masks and faces

Born in Agrigento, Sicily, Pirandello's philosophy — worked out in his essay On Humour (1908) and his wife's madness, which shadowed his life — held that the self is a fiction others write: form against life, the mask society nails to the face. Enrico IV (1922), whose hero 'chooses' his madness as a role, and Right You Are (If You Think So) (1917) made the doctrine theatrical thunder; the 1921 Rome premiere of Six Characters was booed and cheered in the same breath, and within two years it was playing Paris, London and New York.

Politics and standing

Pirandello's fascist party membership (joined 1924, in the Matteotti crisis's shadow) and his donated Nobel medal (melted for the Ethiopian war effort, in the regime's famous collection) remain the blemish his admirers must carry; his influence — on existentialism, absurdism, and every metatheatrical work since — is not in doubt. 'Pirandellian' joined the language: the point where theatre and reality accuse each other of lying.
