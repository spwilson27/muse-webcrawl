The Palais Garnier (French: [palɛ ɡaʁnje]), also known as the Opéra Garnier, is a 1,979-seat opera house at the Place Jacques-Rouché in the 9th arrondissement of Paris. Designed by the young architect Charles Garnier and built between 1861 and 1875 under Napoleon III, it is the archetype of the Napoleon III style — an opulent fusion of Baroque, classical and Renaissance elements that became a template for the Beaux-Arts architecture of theatres worldwide. The URL title "Opéra Garnier" reflects the name by which the building is popularly known.

The building

The exterior is richly decorated with marble friezes, gilded statues — including the famous winged gold groups Harmony and Poetry — and busts of great composers. Inside, the Grand Foyer, modelled on the gallery of a château, and the marble Grand Staircase lead to the horseshoe-shaped auditorium, decorated in red and gold. The ceiling of the auditorium, originally by Jules-Eugène Lenepveu, was covered in 1964 by a new painting by Marc Chagall, and the seven-ton bronze and crystal chandelier is a celebrated feature. Construction was hampered by an underground lake beneath the site, which required a permanent cistern beneath the stage — a feature that fed the legend of the subterranean waters in Gaston Leroux's novel The Phantom of the Opera (1910), which made the building world-famous.

Use

The Palais Garnier was the home of the Paris Opera from its opening until 1989, when the Opéra Bastille became the company's principal opera venue. The Garnier is now used mainly by the Paris Opera Ballet and for symphony concerts, and is open daily for guided and self-guided tours.
