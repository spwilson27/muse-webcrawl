The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea 1972 (COLREGs) are published by the International Maritime Organization and set out, among other things, the "rules of the road" or navigation rules to be followed by ships and other vessels at sea to prevent collisions between two or more vessels. The convention was adopted in 1972 and entered into force in 1977, replacing the 1960 Collision Regulations. Nearly all maritime nations are parties, and the regulations apply to all vessels upon the high seas and connecting navigable waters.

Structure

The COLREGs comprise 41 rules divided into six parts: Part A – General (including Rule 2, responsibility, and Rule 3, definitions); Part B – Steering and Sailing Rules (look-out, safe speed, risk of collision, action to avoid collision, narrow channels, traffic separation schemes, and the conduct of vessels in sight of one another — head-on, crossing and overtaking situations, with "stand-on" and "give-way" vessels — and in restricted visibility); Part C – Lights and Shapes; Part D – Sound and Light Signals; Part E – Exemptions; and Part F – Verification of compliance (added 2013). Annexes cover positioning of lights, sound signalling appliances, additional signals for fishing vessels and distress signals.

Key provisions

Rule 5 requires a proper look-out by sight, hearing and all available means; Rule 6 lists factors for safe speed; Rule 7 covers risk of collision, noting that risk is deemed to exist if the compass bearing of an approaching vessel does not appreciably change. In a head-on situation (Rule 14) both vessels alter to starboard; in a crossing situation (Rule 15) the vessel which has the other on its starboard side gives way; an overtaking vessel (Rule 13) keeps out of the way. Power-driven vessels give way to vessels engaged in fishing, sailing vessels, vessels restricted in their ability to manoeuvre and vessels not under command, in a defined hierarchy. Nothing in the rules exonerates a vessel from the consequences of neglect, and Rule 2 allows departure from the rules where necessary to avoid immediate danger — the so-called "rule of good seamanship".
