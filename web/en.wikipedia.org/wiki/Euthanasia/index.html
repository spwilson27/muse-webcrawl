Euthanasia concerns the deliberate ending of life to relieve suffering.

Euthanasia (from Greek: εὐθανασία, "good death") is the practice of intentionally ending life to eliminate pain and suffering. Countries vary in how they define and regulate it. Euthanasia is usually distinguished from assisted suicide, in which the person performs the final act themselves with means provided by another.

Classification

  • Voluntary euthanasia — conducted with the patient's consent.
  • Non-voluntary euthanasia — conducted when the patient's consent is unavailable, e.g. in persistent vegetative states.
  • Involuntary euthanasia — conducted against the patient's will; generally regarded as murder.
  • Active vs. passive — active euthanasia involves administering lethal substances; passive euthanasia involves withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment.

History and law

The modern euthanasia debate dates to the late 19th century. The Netherlands became the first country to legalise voluntary euthanasia in 2002 (codifying a long-standing tolerance), followed by Belgium (2002), Luxembourg (2009), Colombia (2015), Spain (2021), and Canada (2016, under its medical assistance in dying, or MAID, framework). Assisted dying is legal in several US states beginning with Oregon's Death with Dignity Act (1997), and in Switzerland, where assisted suicide has long been permitted. The term is also historically associated with the Nazi "Aktion T4" programme, in which involuntary killing of people with disabilities was carried out under the label of euthanasia — a usage widely condemned as a perversion of the concept.

Debate

Supporters argue from autonomy and the relief of unbearable suffering; opponents argue from the sanctity of life, the risk of coercion of vulnerable patients ("slippery slope"), and the availability of palliative care. Major religious traditions are generally opposed, while opinion polls in many Western countries show majority public support for legal voluntary euthanasia under strict safeguards.
