Indian street food

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Indian street food encompasses the wide variety of snacks and light meals sold by hawkers and vendors from stalls and carts across India. Deeply regional in character, it ranges from the chaat of the north to the dosas and vada pav of the west and south. Street food is an integral part of Indian urban culture and a significant source of livelihood, and cities such as Mumbai, Delhi, Kolkata and Indore are especially celebrated for it.

Regional varieties

North India

Northern street food is dominated by chaat—a family of savoury snacks built on fried dough, potato, chickpeas, yogurt, tamarind and mint chutneys and chaat masala. Popular items include golgappa (also called pani puri or puchka), aloo tikki, samosa, papri chaat, dahi bhalla and chole bhature. Delhi's Chandni Chowk and Paranthe Wali Gali are historic centres of street food culture.

Western India

Mumbai is famous for vada pav (a spiced potato fritter in a bun, often called the "Indian burger"), pav bhaji, bhel puri and sev puri served at beachside stalls, and Bombay sandwiches. Gujarat contributes dhokla, khandvi, dabeli and fafda, while Indore's Sarafa Bazaar is a renowned night street-food market in Madhya Pradesh.

Eastern India

Kolkata is known for kathi rolls, puchka, jhal muri (spiced puffed rice), telebhaja (fried fritters) and egg devil. The city's street food preserves strong influences from its Mughlai and colonial past.

South India

Southern street stalls serve dosa, idli, vada, uttapam, bonda and punugulu, usually with coconut chutney and sambar, alongside filter coffee. Hyderabad is noted for its Irani chai and Osmania biscuits, and for biryani served in eateries near Charminar.

Organisation and regulation

Street vendors are typically informal-sector workers; India's Street Vendors (Protection of Livelihood and Regulation of Street Vending) Act, 2014 recognised their right to livelihood and provided for town vending committees and certificates of vending. Food safety licensing through FSSAI has gradually been extended to street food vendors, and organised street-food festivals and "khau gallis" (food streets) have been promoted in many cities.
