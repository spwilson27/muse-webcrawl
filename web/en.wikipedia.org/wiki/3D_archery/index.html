3D archery is a form of field archery in which archers shoot at life-size three-dimensional foam models of game animals placed at unmarked distances in natural terrain, typically along a walking course through woodland. It was developed as practice for bowhunting and grew into a competitive discipline in its own right, particularly in North America.

Format

A standard 3D course consists of 20, 28 or 30 targets, each a sculpted polyurethane foam animal — deer, bear, turkey, wild boar and exotic species — with scoring rings moulded or painted over the vital area of the animal. Archers shoot one or two arrows per target from a marked stake; stake colours indicate different maximum ranges for different classes (for example, compound, traditional recurve, and youth divisions). Because distances are usually unmarked, judging range accurately is the central skill of the discipline, though some events such as the World Archery 3D championships permit range estimation only without rangefinders, while certain professional circuits allow them.

Scoring

Scoring typically awards the highest points for a hit in the smallest ring centred on the heart/lung area — commonly 11, 12 or 14 points for the centre ring on IBO and ASA courses, 10 for the surrounding vital ring, 8 for the rest of the vital area, 5 for a body hit, and zero for a miss, horn, or hoof. World Archery 3D events use an 11/10/8/5 zone system scored over two rounds with elimination finals.

Organisations

The largest dedicated sanctioning bodies are the International Bowhunting Organization (IBO) and the Archery Shooters Association (ASA) in the United States, whose national tournament circuits draw thousands of competitors. World Archery has held 3D World Championships since 2003 using traditional, barebow, longbow and compound divisions.
