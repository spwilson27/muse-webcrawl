Anchor

FunctionMooring a vessel to the seabed
ComponentsShank, stock, flukes (arms), ring
Common typesAdmiralty pattern, plough, Danforth, mushroom

An anchor is a device, normally made of metal, used to secure a vessel to the bed of a body of water to prevent the craft from drifting due to wind or current. Anchors can be temporary, carried aboard the vessel, or permanent, as in fixed moorings.

How anchors work

Most anchors hold by engaging the seabed: hooks or flukes dig in under load and resist the pull of the anchor rode (the chain or rope connecting anchor to vessel). Proper anchoring depends on scope — the ratio of rode length to depth — which keeps the pull on the anchor nearly horizontal so it stays set. Weight-type anchors, such as mushroom anchors, hold mainly by mass and suction in soft bottoms.

History

Anchors of stone were used in antiquity; the ancient Greeks and Romans progressed to wooden and then iron anchors with flukes resembling modern Admiralty-pattern anchors. In the nineteenth century, improvements such as the stocked iron anchor and, later, stockless anchors that stow in a ship's hawsepipe became standard. Modern small-craft designs include the Danforth (fluke) and plough anchors, valued for holding power relative to weight.

Symbolism

The anchor is a traditional symbol of hope, stability and seafaring, appearing in heraldry, naval insignia and the Christian iconography of hope.
