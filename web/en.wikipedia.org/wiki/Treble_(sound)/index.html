Treble (sound)

Treble describes the high-frequency end of the audio spectrum—the tones at the top of the frequency range audible to humans, roughly above 2–5 kHz up to the limit of hearing near 20 kHz. It is the counterpart of bass (low frequencies), with midrange between them.

Use

On amplifiers, radios, and equalizers, a treble control adjusts the level of these high frequencies: boosting treble adds brightness, clarity, and presence to voices and instruments such as cymbals and strings, while excessive treble produces harshness or sibilance; cutting treble yields a warmer, darker sound. In sheet music, the treble clef (G clef) notates pitches in the upper range. The word also historically denotes the highest voice part in choral music, and a boy soprano singer.

Perception

High frequencies carry much of the information used to localize sound and to distinguish consonants in speech. Human sensitivity to treble declines with age (presbycusis), typically progressing from the highest frequencies downward, so younger listeners hear more treble detail than older ones.
