Kaykhusraw II

Silver coin (dirham) struck in the name of Kaykhusraw II

Ghiyath al-Din Kaykhusraw II ibn Kayqubad (Persian: غياث الدين كيخسرو بن كيقباد) was the sultan of the Seljuk Sultanate of Rûm from 1237 until his death in 1246. He was the son and successor of Ala al-Din Kayqubad I. His reign, which began amid the prosperity built by his father, ended in catastrophe: the Mongol invasions and the decisive Seljuk defeat at the Battle of Köse Dağ in 1243 reduced the sultanate to a Mongol vassal state.

Reign

Kaykhusraw succeeded his father Kayqubad I in 1237. The early years of his reign were marked by court intrigue; the powerful vizier Sa'd al-Din Köpek engineered the elimination of rivals and briefly dominated the sultan before being executed in 1239. The sultanate still appeared formidable, controlling much of Anatolia and ports on both the Mediterranean and the Black Sea.

In 1240–1241 the realm was shaken by the Babai revolt, an uprising of Turkmen followers of the preacher Baba Ishak in southeastern Anatolia. Although eventually suppressed with the help of Frankish mercenaries, the revolt weakened the Seljuk military at the very moment the Mongols under Baiju were advancing westward.

Mongol invasion and Köse Dağ

After the fall of Erzurum in 1242, Kaykhusraw assembled a large army, including Georgian, Trebizondine and mercenary contingents, and marched east to meet the Mongols. On 26 June 1243 the Seljuk army was routed at Köse Dağ. The sultan fled to Antalya. Under the subsequent settlement negotiated by the vizier Muhadhdhab al-Din, the sultanate agreed to pay a heavy annual tribute, and Rûm became a vassal of the Mongol Empire.

Death and succession

Kaykhusraw II died in 1246, reportedly in Antalya. He left three sons — Kaykaus II, Kilij Arslan IV and Kayqubad II — whose rival claims, manipulated by competing court factions and the Mongols, led to a period of divided rule and deepened the sultanate's decline.

Coinage

Kaykhusraw is notable in numismatics for his silver dirhams depicting a lion and a radiant sun — the famous "lion and sun" type struck at Konya and Sivas around 1240–1243, often interpreted as an astrological emblem or a reference to his Georgian wife.
