Thalassotherapy

Thalassotherapy (from the Greek thalassa, "sea", and therapeia, "treatment") is the medical and therapeutic use of seawater and marine products such as algae, seaweed, mud and marine climate. It is practised at seaside centres, particularly in France, where thalassotherapy resorts are numerous along the Brittany and Atlantic coasts, and treatments typically combine heated-seawater baths, hydro-massage, seaweed wraps and mud applications.

History

The therapeutic use of sea bathing was popularised in 18th- and 19th-century Britain by physicians such as Richard Russell, whose treatise on seawater in glandular diseases appeared in 1750. In France, the term was coined in the 19th century, and centres were founded at resorts including Dieppe, Roscoff and later Quiberon, where Louison Bobet founded a famous institute. French centres have traditionally emphasised medical supervision.

Practise

A genuine thalassotherapy centre is required, in the French tradition, to be located by the sea, to use seawater pumped directly from the ocean and heated, and to offer treatments under qualified supervision. Programmes typically last several days and combine balneotherapy, algotherapy (seaweed applications), physiotherapy and exercise.

Evidence

Some studies suggest benefits for certain skin, musculoskeletal and stress-related conditions, though the overall evidence base is limited, and thalassotherapy is generally regarded as complementary rather than a substitute for conventional medicine.
