Computational geometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Voronoi diagram, one of the classic structures of computational geometry

Computational geometry is a branch of computer science devoted to the study of algorithms which can be stated in terms of geometry. Some purely geometrical problems arise out of the study of computational geometric algorithms, and such problems are also considered to be part of computational geometry. While modern computational geometry is a recent development, it is one of the oldest fields of computing, with a history stretching back to antiquity.

Core problems

  • Convex hull — finding the smallest convex polygon (or polyhedron) enclosing a set of points; classic algorithms include Graham scan and the "gift wrapping" method.
  • Closest pair of points and nearest-neighbour search.
  • Line segment intersection — usually solved with sweep line algorithms such as the Bentley–Ottmann algorithm.
  • Triangulations — polygon triangulation and Delaunay triangulation of point sets.
  • Voronoi diagrams — partitioning the plane by proximity to given sites.
  • Point location and range searching — determining which region contains a query point, or reporting points inside a query region.
  • Motion planning — finding collision-free paths for robots among obstacles.

Techniques

Characteristic algorithmic paradigms of the field include the plane sweep, divide and conquer, randomized incremental construction, duality (mapping points to lines and vice versa), and the use of data structures such as kd-trees, range trees, and trapezoidal maps. Analyses focus on worst-case and output-sensitive running times, and on robustness in the face of floating-point precision problems — a major practical theme, addressed by exact geometric computation in libraries such as CGAL.

Applications

Computational geometry underpins computer graphics and ray tracing, geographic information systems (GIS), robotics, computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM), integrated-circuit layout, molecular biology (protein structure), and computer vision. The field emerged as a named discipline in the 1970s, largely through the work of Michael Ian Shamos, whose 1978 doctoral dissertation is often credited as its founding document; dedicated venues include the ACM Symposium on Computational Geometry (since 1985).
