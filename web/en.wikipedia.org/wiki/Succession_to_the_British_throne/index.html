Succession to the British throne

Succession to the British throne is determined by descent, sex (for those born before 28 October 2011), legitimacy, and religion. Under common law, the Crown is inherited by a sovereign's eldest child or by a childless sovereign's nearest collateral line. The Bill of Rights 1689 and the Act of Settlement 1701 restrict succession to the Protestant descendants of Sophia of Hanover who are in communion with the Church of England.

Rules

Succession follows primogeniture among eligible, legitimate, Protestant descendants of Sophia, Electress of Hanover. Roman Catholics are excluded, as are those who marry without the sovereign's consent (for the first six in line). The Succession to the Crown Act 2013, implementing the Perth Agreement among the Commonwealth realms, replaced male-preference primogeniture with absolute primogeniture for persons born after 28 October 2011, removed the bar on marrying a Catholic, and limited the consent requirement to the first six persons in line.

Current line

Following Charles III, the first positions in line are: (1) William, Prince of Wales; (2) Prince George of Wales; (3) Princess Charlotte of Wales; (4) Prince Louis of Wales; (5) Prince Harry, Duke of Sussex; (6) Prince Archie of Sussex; (7) Princess Lilibet of Sussex; (8) Prince Andrew, Duke of York; followed by his daughters Princess Beatrice and Princess Eugenie and their children, then Prince Edward, Duke of Edinburgh, and his children, then Anne, Princess Royal, and her descendants. Thousands of eligible descendants of Sophia of Hanover are in the full line.

Accession

Succession is immediate on the death of the reigning sovereign ("The King is dead, long live the King"); the Accession Council proclaims the new monarch, who later takes the coronation oath. Regency Acts govern the case of a minor or incapacitated sovereign, and Counsellors of State may deputise for the monarch during absence or illness.
