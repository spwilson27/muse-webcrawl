Company town

A company town is a place where practically all stores and housing are owned by the one company that is also the main employer. Company towns are often planned with a suite of amenities such as stores, houses of worship, schools, markets and recreation facilities.

Characteristics

Employers built towns where no existing settlement served a remote mine, mill or factory. Workers might be paid in company scrip redeemable only at the company store, a practice that, combined with company-owned housing, gave employers extensive control over workers' lives and could suppress labour organising.

Examples

Notable examples include Pullman, Chicago, built by the Pullman Company and site of the 1894 Pullman Strike; Hershey, Pennsylvania (chocolate); Saltaire and Bournville in England, model villages built by enlightened industrialists; mining towns across Appalachia and the American West; and Fordlândia in Brazil. In Britain, model villages reflected paternalistic reform as well as control.

Decline

Automobile commuting, labour law, antitrust pressure and the decline of extractive industries reduced company towns in the 20th century, though analogous arrangements persist around remote mines, oil camps and some modern corporate campuses.
