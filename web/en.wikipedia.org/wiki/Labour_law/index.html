Labour law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Labour law (also called employment law) mediates the relationship between workers, employing entities, trade unions and the government — the body of statutes, judge-made law and collective agreements governing individual employment rights (contracts, wages, hours, safety, discrimination, dismissal) and collective relations (union recognition, bargaining, industrial action).

Scope and debates

The field exists because the employment contract is not a contract like others: inequality of bargaining power led legislatures from factory acts onward to impose non-waivable floors (minimum wages, maximum hours, safety codes, anti-discrimination law, unfair-dismissal protection in most systems — US employment-at-will being the comparative outlier) and to charter collective self-help (protected concerted activity, strike immunities bounded by ballots, notice and essential-service limits). International labour law (ILO conventions, the European Social Charter) sets floors above. The field's boundary crisis is classification: dependent contractors, agency and platform workers test the employee/independent-contractor binary on which protections hang (the UK Supreme Court's Uber judgment, 2021; California's AB5 and Proposition 22 fight; the EU Platform Work Directive). Labour law's fortunes track politics directly — its expansion and retrenchment are among the clearest traces of post-war social democracy and its neoliberal revision.
