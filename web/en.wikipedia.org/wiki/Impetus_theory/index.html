Impetus theory

Buridan's impetus explained why projectiles continue to move after release

The theory of impetus is an auxiliary or secondary theory of Aristotelian dynamics, put forth initially to explain projectile motion against gravity. It was developed in the 14th century by Jean Buridan and his followers, building on earlier ideas of John Philoponus (6th century) and Avicenna (11th century), and was an important step towards the concept of inertia and momentum in classical mechanics.

The theory

Aristotle held that a projectile continued to move because the displaced air rushed behind it and pushed it forward, a doctrine called antiperistasis. Philoponus objected that instead the thrower implants an incorporeal motive force — an impetus — into the projectile itself. Buridan developed this into a quantitative theory: impetus is proportional to the speed of the body and the quantity of matter it contains, and it is corrupted only by external resistance and by gravity. A falling body, he argued, continually gains impetus from gravity, explaining its acceleration.

Significance

Buridan's impetus is strikingly similar to the modern concept of momentum, although it differs in that impetus was still thought of as a cause that maintains motion rather than a conserved state requiring no cause. The theory was used by Nicole Oresme and influenced early modern thinkers including Galileo Galilei, whose early writings used impetus concepts before he arrived at the principle of inertia that Newton formalised in the laws of motion.
