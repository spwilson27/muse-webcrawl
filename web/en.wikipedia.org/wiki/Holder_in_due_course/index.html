Holder in due course

In commercial law, a holder in due course (HDC) is a person who takes a negotiable instrument (such as a check, promissory note, or bill of exchange) for value, in good faith, and without notice of any claim, defense, or irregularity affecting the instrument. The doctrine, codified in Article 3 of the Uniform Commercial Code in the United States and in the Bills of Exchange Act 1882 in the United Kingdom, gives a holder in due course a privileged status: the holder takes the instrument free of most "personal" defenses that could have been raised against the original payee or prior holders.

The policy behind the doctrine is to promote the free transferability of negotiable instruments and thus the smooth functioning of commerce. Because a holder in due course can enforce the instrument largely free of defenses, instruments circulate more like money: transferees need not investigate the underlying transaction before accepting them.

Requirements

Under UCC § 3-302, to qualify as a holder in due course a person must first be a "holder" — in possession of an instrument drawn, issued, or endorsed to that person or to bearer — and must take the instrument:

  • For value — the holder must have given consideration, such as money, goods, services, or the taking of the instrument in payment of an antecedent debt;
  • In good faith — honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing;
  • Without notice — of any dishonor, default, unauthorized signature, alteration, claim of ownership, or defense.

In addition, the instrument must not bear apparent evidence of forgery or alteration and must not be so irregular or incomplete as to call its authenticity into question. A holder generally cannot be a holder in due course if the instrument was taken in a bulk transaction, as part of the purchase of a business, or after the instrument was overdue or dishonored.

Effect: freedom from defenses

A holder in due course takes the instrument free of personal defenses — such as failure of consideration, fraud in the inducement, non-delivery of goods, set-off, or breach of warranty — and free of claims in recoupment arising from the underlying transaction. The holder remains subject only to the so-called real defenses, which include infancy, duress, lack of legal capacity, illegality that nullifies the obligation, fraud in the factum (fraud as to the essential nature of the document signed), and discharge in insolvency proceedings. Subsequent holders may also benefit through the shelter rule, acquiring the rights of a prior holder in due course unless they themselves engaged in fraud or illegality affecting the instrument.

Criticism and consumer protection

The doctrine was criticized in consumer transactions, where buyers who signed installment notes for defective goods found themselves obliged to pay finance companies that had taken the notes as holders in due course. In response, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission promulgated the Holder Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 433) in 1975, requiring consumer credit contracts to include a notice preserving the consumer's claims and defenses against any subsequent holder, effectively abolishing holder-in-due-course status in most consumer credit sales.
