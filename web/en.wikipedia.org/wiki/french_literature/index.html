French literature

French literature comprises the body of written works in the French language, originating in medieval France and continuing to the present day. France has produced more Nobel laureates in literature than any other country, and French writers have been central to the development of the novel, theatre, and poetry in Europe.

Medieval literature

The earliest French literature includes the chansons de geste, epic poems such as The Song of Roland (c. 1100), the courtly romances of Chrétien de Troyes, and the lyric poetry of the troubadours and trouvères.

Renaissance and classicism

The sixteenth century saw Rabelais, Montaigne — inventor of the essay — and the Pléiade poets. In the seventeenth century, French classicism flourished under Louis XIV with Molière, Racine, Corneille, La Fontaine, and Pascal.

Enlightenment and the 19th century

Voltaire, Rousseau, Diderot, and Montesquieu led the Enlightenment. The nineteenth century produced Balzac, Flaubert, Stendhal, Hugo, Baudelaire, and Zola, spanning romanticism, realism, symbolism, and naturalism.

20th century and beyond

Proust, Gide, Camus, Sartre, and Beauvoir reshaped modern letters; movements such as surrealism and the nouveau roman emerged in France. Contemporary French-language literature also encompasses a rich francophone world beyond France.
