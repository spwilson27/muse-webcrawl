Berliner Weisse

Berliner Weisse is traditionally served in a broad bowl glass, often mit Schuss — with a shot of syrup.

Berliner Weisse (German: Berliner Weißbier) is a cloudy, sour wheat beer style native to Berlin. Low in alcohol (usually around 3% ABV) and refreshingly tart from lactic fermentation, it was once the most popular alcoholic drink in Berlin, and Napoleon's troops are said to have dubbed it the "Champagne of the North".

Production

The beer is brewed from a mixture of malted wheat and barley, usually lightly kilned so the finished beer is very pale. Its defining character comes from fermentation with both ale yeast and Lactobacillus bacteria, which produce lactic acid and a clean sourness; historically Brettanomyces yeasts also contributed complexity during long bottle conditioning. The wort is hopped very lightly and was traditionally not boiled.

History

Wheat beer brewing in and around Berlin is documented from the 17th century, possibly with Huguenot influence. By the 19th century Berliner Weisse dominated the capital's beer culture, produced by dozens of breweries; the Schultheiss brewery became its best-known producer in the 20th century. Sales declined sharply after the Second World War and through the later 20th century, when the style was often sold pre-mixed with syrup. Since the 21st-century craft beer revival, Berlin breweries and brewers worldwide have revived unsweetened and traditionally bottle-conditioned versions, and "Berliner Weisse" is a protected designation for beer brewed in Berlin.

Serving

The classic serve is mit Schuss — with a shot of raspberry syrup (red) or woodruff syrup (green) to soften the acidity — drunk through a straw from a wide bowl-shaped glass. Purists, and most modern craft interpretations, drink it straight.
