Discounted utility

In economics, discounted utility is the utility (desirability) of some future event, such as consuming a certain amount of a good, as perceived at the present time, discounted to account for the delay before it occurs. The discounted utility model, formulated by Paul Samuelson in 1937, is the standard framework for intertemporal choice: it represents an individual's total welfare from a stream of consumption as the sum of instantaneous utilities, each weighted by a discount function.

The model

For a consumption stream (c0, c1, …, cT), discounted utility takes the form

U = ∑t=0T D(t) u(ct),

where u is the instantaneous (cardinal) utility function and D(t) is the discount function. In the classical version D(t) = δt with a constant discount factor 0 < δ < 1, corresponding to exponential discounting at a constant rate of time preference.

Assumptions and criticism

The model assumes that the same utility function applies in every period, that utility is additive across periods, and that the rate at which future utility is discounted is constant — an assumption that guarantees dynamically consistent choices. A large experimental literature shows that people systematically violate these assumptions: they discount near-term delays more steeply than long ones (hyperbolic discounting), discount gains more than losses, and are sensitive to the magnitude and framing of outcomes. Behavioural economics has therefore developed alternatives, notably quasi-hyperbolic discounting, which retains much of the model's tractability while accommodating present bias.

Applications

Discounted utility is central to models of saving and consumption smoothing, asset pricing, optimal growth theory such as the Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans model, and cost–benefit analysis, where social discount rates for long-run projects remain contested.
