Modular synthesizer

TypeElectronic musical instrument
Invented1960s (Harald Bode; Robert Moog; Don Buchla)
Key formatsEurorack, 5U/Moog Unit, Buchla, Serge
ConnectionPatch cables carrying audio and control voltages

A modular synthesizer is an electronic musical instrument composed of separate, interchangeable modules — oscillators, filters, amplifiers, envelope generators, sequencers and effects — that the musician connects freely with patch cables to create sounds and musical structures. Unlike a fixed-architecture synthesizer, a modular system has no predetermined signal path: the same modules can be combined in indefinitely many configurations, so a patch exists only as long as the cables stay plugged in.

History

Modular synthesis began in the early 1960s. In Germany, Harald Bode demonstrated early modular devices; in the United States, Robert Moog on the East Coast and Don Buchla on the West Coast independently built voltage-controlled modular systems from 1963–64. Moog's systems, designed for studios, were popularised by Wendy Carlos's Switched-On Bach (1968) and used by the Beatles, Tangerine Dream and Keith Emerson. The cost and instability of modulars pushed the market toward portable instruments like the Minimoog (1970), and by the 1980s digital synthesis had eclipsed them. A revival began in the mid-1990s when Dieter Doepfer launched the Eurorack format (3U modules, 3.5 mm cables), which hundreds of manufacturers — including Make Noise, Mutable Instruments and Moog's semi-modular lines — adopted, making modular synthesis a mass hobby again.

Modules and signals

Modules communicate with standardised control voltages. Sound sources include voltage-controlled oscillators (VCOs) and noise generators; sound modifiers include voltage-controlled filters (VCFs) and amplifiers (VCAs); control sources include envelope generators (ADSR), low-frequency oscillators (LFOs), sequencers, random/uncertainty modules and quantizers. The classic "East Coast" patch sends oscillator → filter → amplifier, shaped by envelopes, while the "West Coast" (Buchla) approach emphasises waveshaping, low-pass gates and complex oscillators for additive, timbrally evolving sounds.

Formats

Systems are distinguished by module height and connector: Eurorack (3U, 3.5 mm), the large 5U format descended from Moog (1/4-inch), Buchla's 4U banana-cable format, and Serge 4U systems. Semi-modular instruments, such as the ARP 2600 and Moog Mother-32, have a pre-wired default path that patch cables can override.
