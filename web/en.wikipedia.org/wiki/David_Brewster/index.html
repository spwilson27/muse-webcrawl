Sir David Brewster KH PRSE FRS FSA Scot (11 December 1781 – 10 February 1868) was a Scottish scientist, inventor, author, and academic administrator. In science he is principally remembered for his experimental work in physical optics, mostly concerned with the study of the polarization of light. A major discovery, now known as Brewster's angle, describes the angle of incidence at which light reflected from a surface is perfectly polarized.

Brewster also invented the kaleidoscope (patented in 1817), which became an enormous popular success, and improved the stereoscope, applying the lens form for viewing three-dimensional images. He was a prolific science journalist and editor, founder of the Edinburgh Journal of Science, and served as principal of the University of St Andrews and later of the University of Edinburgh.

Life

Brewster was born at Jedburgh in the Scottish Borders, where his father was rector of the grammar school. Destined originally for the clergy, he studied divinity at the University of Edinburgh and was licensed to preach, but his interests turned decisively to science. From 1799 he was a close associate of leading Edinburgh men of science, and he contributed extensively to the Encyclopaedia Britannica, eventually editing its seventh and eighth editions' scientific articles and writing substantial entries on optics, electricity, and magnetism.

Work in optics

In 1815 Brewster discovered that light reflected from a transparent surface becomes completely plane-polarized when the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular to one another — the condition defining Brewster's angle, whose tangent equals the refractive index of the medium. His investigations of polarization by reflection, refraction, and absorption, of double refraction and of the optical properties of crystals, made him one of the founders of modern optical mineralogy. He received the Copley Medal of the Royal Society in 1815 for his work on polarization.

Inventions

The kaleidoscope, invented in 1816 during experiments on polarization, created a sensation when it reached the market in 1817; although pirated versions deprived Brewster of most of the profits, the device sold in the hundreds of thousands. Brewster also popularized the lenticular stereoscope, which used lenses rather than mirrors to present slightly different images to each eye, producing an illusion of depth. He advocated improvements in lighthouse optics, promoting the use of the Fresnel lens in British lighthouses, and experimented with photography, becoming a friend and correspondent of William Henry Fox Talbot.

Later years and legacy

Brewster was a founder of the British Association for the Advancement of Science (1831) and served as principal of the United College at St Andrews from 1838 and of the University of Edinburgh from 1859 until his death. He wrote well-received biographies, including a life of Isaac Newton, and the popular work The Martyrs of Science (1841) on Galileo, Tycho Brahe and Kepler. Brewster's angle, the Brewster crater on the Moon, and numerous scientific terms preserve his name in optics.
