Fermentation in food processing

In food processing, fermentation is the conversion of carbohydrates to alcohol or organic acids using microorganisms — yeasts or bacteria — under anaerobic conditions. Fermentation usually implies the action of microorganisms is desired. The science of fermentation is known as zymology. Humans have used fermentation to produce food and beverages since the Neolithic era; it preserves food, enriches it with protein, essential amino acids and vitamins, removes antinutrients, and creates distinctive flavours and textures.

Types of fermentation

  • Alcoholic fermentation — yeasts convert sugars to ethanol and carbon dioxide. Used for beer, wine, and spirits, and (via the CO2) for leavening bread.
  • Lactic acid fermentation — lactic acid bacteria convert sugars to lactic acid. Used for yogurt, cheese, sauerkraut, kimchi, pickles, sourdough, and fermented sausages such as salami.
  • Acetic acid fermentation — Acetobacter oxidizes ethanol to acetic acid, producing vinegar and contributing to kombucha.
  • Alkaline fermentation — as in Japanese nattō, fermented with Bacillus subtilis.

Fermented foods around the world

Nearly every food culture has signature ferments: kimchi and doubanjiang in East Asia; sauerkraut and kvass in Europe; yogurt and kefir from Central Asia and the Caucasus; idli, dosa, and pickles in India; injera in Ethiopia; miso, soy sauce, and natto in Japan; tempeh in Indonesia; and fermented fish sauces such as garum in the ancient Mediterranean and nước mắm in Vietnam. Microbial cultures — such as sourdough starter, kefir grains, and kombucha SCOBYs — are often maintained and shared over generations.

Health and safety

Live-culture fermented foods contain probiotic microorganisms that may benefit gut health, though the strength of clinical evidence varies by strain and condition. Fermentation also carries risks if done improperly: contamination can allow toxin-producing bacteria such as Clostridium botulinum to grow, so acidity, salt concentration, and hygiene are critical controls. Fermentation reduces some hazards as well — for example, fermenting cassava lowers its cyanide content.

History

Evidence of fermented beverages dates back thousands of years — a fermented drink of rice, honey, and fruit was made in Jiahu, China, around 7000 BCE. In 1856–1857 Louis Pasteur demonstrated that fermentation is caused by living microorganisms, identifying the yeasts responsible for alcoholic fermentation and the bacteria that sour wine, founding the modern science of microbiology in the process.
