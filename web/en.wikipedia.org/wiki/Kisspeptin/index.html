Kisspeptin

Kisspeptin Type: Neuropeptide
Gene: KISS1
Receptor: Kisspeptin receptor (KISS1R, formerly GPR54)
Key role: Initiation of puberty; regulation of GnRH secretion
Named after: Hershey's Kisses (Hershey, Pennsylvania)

Kisspeptin is a peptide hormone encoded by the KISS1 gene that acts as a central regulator of the reproductive system. It was first identified in 1996 in Hershey, Pennsylvania — and named, whimsically, after the town's famous Hershey's Kisses chocolates — as a suppressor of cancer metastasis. Its reproductive role came to light in 2003, when two groups showed that inactivating mutations in its receptor, KISS1R (then called GPR54), cause failure to enter puberty.

Function

Kisspeptin neurons in the hypothalamus — principally in the arcuate nucleus and the AVPV/preoptic area — stimulate gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) neurons. In the arcuate nucleus, kisspeptin is co-expressed with neurokinin B and dynorphin (the "KNDy" neurons), which together generate the pulsatile GnRH secretion that drives the pituitary hormones LH and FSH. Kisspeptin signalling is essential for the onset of puberty, for ovulation (the preovulatory LH surge in females), and for feedback regulation of fertility by sex steroids, nutrition and stress.

Clinical significance

Loss-of-function mutations in KISS1 or KISS1R cause hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, while activating mutations can cause precocious puberty. Kisspeptin and its analogues are under investigation as treatments for infertility — kisspeptin-54 has been used experimentally to trigger egg maturation in IVF with a lower risk of ovarian hyperstimulation — and for hypothalamic amenorrhoea and other reproductive disorders. Kisspeptin is also highly expressed in the placenta, and its original anti-metastatic role continues to be studied in oncology.
