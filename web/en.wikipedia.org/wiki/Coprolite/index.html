Coprolite

A coprolite (also known as a coprolith) is fossilized feces. Coprolites are classified as trace fossils as opposed to body fossils, as they give evidence for the animal's behaviour (in this case, diet) rather than morphology. The name is derived from the Greek words κόπρος (kopros, meaning "dung") and λίθος (lithos, meaning "stone"). They were first described by William Buckland in 1829.

Scientific value

Coprolites can preserve undigested residues such as bones, scales, plant fragments, pollen and parasites, providing direct evidence of ancient diets and food webs. Spiral coprolites are typically attributed to sharks and other fishes with spiral intestinal valves. Some of the largest known coprolites, over 60 cm long, are attributed to tyrannosaurid dinosaurs; a famous specimen attributed to Tyrannosaurus rex contains fragments of herbivorous dinosaur bone.

History of study

Mary Anning's observations of fossil "bezoar stones" containing fish remains from the Lyme Regis ichthyosaur sites led Buckland to recognize their origin as fossil dung. Coprolite mining for fertilizer became a significant industry in East Anglia in the mid-19th century after coprolite-rich beds were found to be rich in phosphate.

Preservation

Rapid burial and mineralization are usually required for feces to fossilize; coprolites are often phosphatic in composition. They range in size from a few millimetres to large masses.
