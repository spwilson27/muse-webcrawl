Dollar (reactivity)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The dollar is a unit of reactivity in nuclear reactor physics. It expresses the reactivity of a nuclear reactor relative to the fraction of neutrons that are delayed, rather than prompt. One dollar of reactivity is defined as reactivity equal to the effective delayed neutron fraction, usually denoted βeff.

Definition

Reactivity ρ is the fractional deviation of the neutron multiplication factor k from unity. Measured in dollars, reactivity is ρ divided by βeff, the effective fraction of fission neutrons emitted as delayed neutrons by fission product precursors. Because βeff is a small number — about 0.0065 for uranium-235 fuel — one dollar corresponds to a reactivity of roughly 0.65 percent.

Cents

The dollar is subdivided into 100 cents, in analogy with currency. Reactivity changes in operating reactors are commonly quoted in cents; for example, a control rod movement might be worth a few cents. The informal unit names reflect the historical practice at early reactor laboratories such as Los Alamos.

Prompt criticality

When inserted reactivity reaches exactly one dollar, the reactor becomes prompt critical: the chain reaction can be sustained by prompt neutrons alone, without waiting for delayed neutrons, and the reactor power rises on the very short prompt-neutron timescale. Operation at or above prompt criticality is therefore avoided in ordinary power reactors, though it is deliberately approached in pulsed research reactors. Reactivity between zero and one dollar is sometimes described in cents as the reactor being "cents short of a dollar" from prompt critical.
