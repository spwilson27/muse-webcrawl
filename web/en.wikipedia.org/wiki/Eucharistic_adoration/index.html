Eucharistic adoration is a devotional practice in the Roman Catholic Church — and, in modified forms, in some Anglo-Catholic and Lutheran traditions — of worshipping Christ present in the consecrated Eucharistic host. The host is typically displayed in a vessel called a monstrance and placed on an altar, while the faithful pray, meditate or simply remain in silent adoration before what they believe to be the real presence of Christ in the Blessed Sacrament.

Theology

The practice rests on the doctrine of transubstantiation and the real presence: because the consecrated host is, in Catholic teaching, truly the body of Christ, it is owed the worship of latria due to God alone. Adoration outside Mass developed as the reservation of the sacrament — originally for communion of the sick — became an occasion for devotion in its own right.

History

Elevation of the host during Mass became common in the thirteenth century, encouraged by the institution of the feast of Corpus Christi (1264) and eucharistic miracles such as that of Bolsena. Practices of exposition and benediction spread in the late medieval and Counter-Reformation periods. St Alphonsus Liguori promoted visits to the Blessed Sacrament, and in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries perpetual adoration — continuous exposition maintained by rotating adorers, day and night — was taken up by religious orders and parishes worldwide. Pope John Paul II strongly encouraged the practice and installed a perpetual adoration chapel in St Peter's Basilica.

Forms

Common forms include a scheduled holy hour of prayer before the exposed sacrament, solemn exposition concluding with benediction, Corpus Christi processions, and the Forty Hours' Devotion. Many parishes maintain adoration chapels open for extended or perpetual hours, staffed by lay volunteers in one-hour shifts.
