The Rio–Antirrio Bridge (Greek: Γέφυρα Ρίου–Αντιρρίου), officially the Charilaos Trikoupis Bridge, is one of the world's longest multi-span cable-stayed bridges. It crosses the Gulf of Corinth in Greece, linking the town of Rio on the Peloponnese peninsula with Antirrio on the mainland, just north of Patras. Opened in August 2004, shortly before the Athens Olympic Games, it forms part of the A5 motorway (Ionia Odos) and the European route E55.

Design and construction

The bridge's main span structure is 2,252 metres long, with four pylons and successive cable-stayed spans of 286, 560, 560, 560 and 286 metres; including approach viaducts the total length is about 2,880 metres. Construction presented exceptional challenges: the strait is up to 65 metres deep, the seabed consists of weak sediments with no bedrock within reach, and the region is seismically active, with the two shores slowly drifting apart. Engineers reinforced the seabed with hundreds of steel inclusions, rested the pylons on gravel beds so they can shift safely during an earthquake, and designed the continuous deck — among the longest fully suspended decks in the world — to accommodate tectonic movement.

History

The bridge is named after Charilaos Trikoupis, the 19th-century Greek prime minister who first proposed a crossing of the strait in 1885, an idea far beyond the technology of his era. The concession was awarded in the 1990s to the French-led Gefyra consortium; construction began in 1998 under chief engineer Berdj Mikaelian. The Olympic flame crossed the completed bridge on its way to Athens in 2004.

Use

The crossing replaced slow ferry services and cut the journey between the Peloponnese and western Greece to about five minutes. It carries road traffic and a pedestrian walkway, and is a prominent landmark visible across the western Gulf of Corinth.
