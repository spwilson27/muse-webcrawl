Morton Lyon Sahl (May 11, 1927 – October 26, 2021) was a Canadian-born American comedian, actor, and social satirist. He pioneered a style of social satire that skewered political and public figures of all stripes, performing in a conversational manner while wearing a sweater and holding a rolled-up newspaper — a sharp break from the tuxedo-and-mother-in-law jokes of earlier stand-up comics. His 1955 album Mort Sahl at Sunset was the first comedy album by a modern stand-up comedian to become widely popular.

Career

Sahl grew up in Los Angeles, served in the U.S. Air Force, and attended the University of California, Berkeley. His breakthrough came at Enrico Banducci's hungry i nightclub in San Francisco in 1953, where his off-the-cuff monologues about politics and current events found a young, educated audience. He appeared on The Tonight Show, hosted the first Grammy Awards in 1959, and became a favourite of John F. Kennedy, for whom he wrote jokes — though he joked relentlessly about Kennedy once he was in office as well.

After the Kennedy assassination in 1963, Sahl became consumed by the case, working with New Orleans district attorney Jim Garrison's investigation and reading the entire Warren Commission report on stage. The obsession stalled his mainstream career, but he continued performing for decades, teaching a course in critical thinking at Claremont McKenna College and staging a one-man Broadway show, Mort Sahl's America, in 1987.

Influence

Sahl's newspaper-in-hand, topical monologue format influenced a generation of comics including Lenny Bruce, George Carlin, Woody Allen, and later late-night hosts. In 2003, Comedy Central ranked him 40th in its list of the 100 greatest stand-ups of all time.
