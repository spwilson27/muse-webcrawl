Excretion

Excretion is the elimination of metabolic waste products and other non-useful materials from an organism. It is an essential process in all forms of life, distinct from defecation (the removal of undigested food) and from secretion (the release of useful substances). In vertebrates, excretion is carried out primarily by the kidneys, with important roles for the lungs, skin and liver.

Nitrogenous waste

The breakdown of proteins and nucleic acids produces ammonia, which is highly toxic. Animals dispose of nitrogen in different chemical forms depending largely on water availability:

  • Ammonotelic animals (most aquatic invertebrates and freshwater fish) excrete ammonia directly, diluted in abundant water.
  • Ureotelic animals (mammals, amphibians, sharks) convert ammonia to urea in the liver, excreting it in urine with moderate water loss.
  • Uricotelic animals (birds, reptiles, insects, land snails) convert ammonia to uric acid, which can be excreted as a nearly dry paste, conserving water — an adaptation essential to the shelled egg.

Excretory organs across organisms

Single-celled organisms expel waste by diffusion and contractile vacuoles. Flatworms use protonephridia (flame-cell systems); earthworms and other annelids use metanephridia; insects use Malpighian tubules that empty into the gut; and vertebrates filter blood in the kidneys, where nephrons regulate water, salts, pH and waste concentration. Plants excrete excess water by transpiration and guttation, store wastes in bark, leaves and vacuoles, and release oxygen as a by-product of photosynthesis.

In humans

The human urinary system filters about 180 litres of blood plasma daily, reabsorbing nearly all of it and producing roughly 1–2 litres of urine containing urea, creatinine, excess ions and water. The lungs excrete carbon dioxide and water vapour; the skin excretes salts, water and small amounts of urea in sweat; the liver detoxifies substances and excretes bile pigments (bilirubin) via the intestine. Failure of excretion, as in kidney failure, leads to dangerous accumulation of toxins and requires dialysis or transplantation.

Excretion vs. egestion

Biology texts distinguish excretion (removal of waste made by cells) from egestion (removal of material that never entered the body's cells, i.e. faeces). Confusing the two is a common error, since faeces consist mainly of undigested food and bacteria rather than metabolic waste, though bile pigments in faeces are genuinely excretory products.
