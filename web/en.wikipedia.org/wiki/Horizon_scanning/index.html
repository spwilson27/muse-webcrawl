Horizon scanning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Horizon scanning (also known as environmental scanning) is a technique used in futures studies and strategic foresight to systematically explore and detect early signs of potentially important developments — including emerging trends, new technologies, weak signals, wild cards, and other change factors that may affect policy, business, or society in the future.

Methods

Horizon scanning typically involves gathering information from a wide range of sources, such as scientific literature, news media, expert interviews, conferences, patents, and social media. Scanners look for signals that are novel, plausible, and potentially impactful, even if they are initially weak or ambiguous. The results are often assessed and prioritised through expert workshops, Delphi surveys, or cross-impact analysis.

Government use

Several governments operate formal horizon scanning programmes. In the United Kingdom, the Government Office for Science runs a Foresight programme and maintains horizon scanning activities to inform long-term policy. Singapore's Centre for Strategic Futures applies horizon scanning across government. The European Commission and OECD also use horizon scanning to support anticipatory governance.

Health technology assessment

In healthcare, horizon scanning systems such as the UK's NIHR Innovation Observatory identify new and emerging health technologies — medicines, devices, and diagnostics — before they reach the market, so that decision-makers can plan for their evaluation and introduction.

Conservation

Since 2008, an annual horizon scan of emerging issues in global conservation, led by researchers including William J. Sutherland, has identified topics such as microplastic pollution and new pathogen threats before they became widely recognised concerns.
