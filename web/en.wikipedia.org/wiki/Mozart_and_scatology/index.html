Mozart and scatology

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart displayed a fondness for scatological and sexual humour in his letters and in a number of recreational musical compositions. This material — long suppressed or bowdlerised by editors and biographers — attracted scholarly attention in the later 20th century, and explanations offered for it have included Mozart's upbringing, the social conventions of his family circle, and, controversially, diagnoses such as Tourette syndrome.

Letters

The most famous examples occur in Mozart's letters to his cousin Maria Anna Thekla Mozart, the "Bäsle," written in 1777–1778 when he was 21. The letters mix wordplay, nonsense rhymes and frank references to defecation. Scatological passages also appear, in milder form, in letters to his mother and sister. Such humour was not unusual in 18th-century German family correspondence, and Mozart's mother wrote to her husband in a similar vein; even so, early editors of Mozart's letters omitted or softened the passages, reinforcing an idealised image of the composer that the unexpurgated texts later complicated.

Music

Mozart set scatological texts in convivial canons for friends, including Leck mich im Arsch ("Lick me in the arse"), K. 231/382c, and Leck mir den Arsch fein recht schön sauber, K. 233/382d (whose attribution has been debated), as well as the canon Bei der Hitz im Sommer ess ich, K. 234/382e. These pieces were party music for private amusement. After Mozart's death, his widow Constanze had bowdlerised replacement texts fitted to some of them for publication.

Interpretations

Scholars have read the scatology variously: as ordinary period humour shared within the Mozart family; as evidence of Mozart's playful, punning verbal style; and, in an influential 1992 article in the British Medical Journal context of medical speculation, as a possible symptom of Tourette syndrome — a hypothesis most Mozart scholars and many clinicians consider unproven. The 1990s debate nonetheless helped bring the subject into mainstream biography.
