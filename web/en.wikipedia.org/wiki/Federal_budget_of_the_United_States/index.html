Federal budget of the United States

The United States federal budget comprises the spending and revenues of the US federal government. The budget is the financial representation of the priorities of the government, reflecting historical debates over the appropriate role of the federal government. The government primarily spends on healthcare, retirement benefits and defence: the largest categories are Social Security, Medicare and Medicaid, military spending, and interest on the national debt.

Overview

Federal spending in recent years has been on the order of $6 trillion annually (roughly 23–24% of gross domestic product), while revenues have been around $4.5–5 trillion, leaving annual deficits of more than $1 trillion. Revenue comes chiefly from individual income taxes and payroll taxes that fund social insurance programmes, with a smaller share from corporate income tax. When spending exceeds revenue, the Treasury borrows by issuing securities, adding to the national debt.

Process

Under the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, the process begins with the President's budget request, submitted to Congress on the first Monday in February. Congress then passes a budget resolution setting overall levels, followed by twelve annual appropriations bills funding discretionary programmes; "mandatory" spending, including Social Security and Medicare, follows automatically from authorising law. The federal fiscal year runs from 1 October to 30 September. Failure to pass appropriations on time leads to continuing resolutions or a government shutdown.
