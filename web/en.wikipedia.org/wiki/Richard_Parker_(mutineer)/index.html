Richard Parker (mutineer)

Richard Parker (16 April 1767 – 30 June 1797) was an English sailor executed for his role as leader of the Nore mutiny of 1797, one of two major mutinies in the Royal Navy during the Revolutionary Wars with France. The mutineers chose Parker as president of their committee of delegates — head of the so-called "Floating Republic" — and after the mutiny collapsed he was court-martialled and hanged from the yardarm of HMS Sandwich.

Background

Parker was the son of a successful Exeter baker and received a relatively good education. He served as a midshipman and master's mate in the navy but left under a cloud after a court martial, later working as a teacher and falling into debt. He re-enlisted in 1797 as a quota man — effectively as bounty labour supplied by his parish — and joined HMS Sandwich at the Nore anchorage in the Thames estuary.

The Nore mutiny

The mutiny began on 12 May 1797, inspired by the successful Spithead mutiny, with demands concerning pay, provisions, leave and the removal of unpopular officers. Parker, articulate and educated, was elected president of the delegates. The mutineers blockaded London's shipping lanes, but support ebbed, shore supplies were cut off, and the mutiny disintegrated by mid-June. Parker was arrested, tried aboard HMS Neptune, and hanged on 30 June 1797.

Legacy

Parker became a folk figure of radical politics; his wife Anne famously recovered his body from its initial burial ground and brought it through London crowds. His story is retold in the folk song "The Death of Parker" and referenced in Billy Bragg's song of the same theme.
