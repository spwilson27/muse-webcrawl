Degree distribution

In the study of graphs and networks, the degree distribution is the probability distribution of the degrees of the nodes over the whole network. If a network has n nodes and nk of them have degree k, then the degree distribution P(k) is the fraction P(k) = nk/n — equivalently, the probability that a randomly chosen node has degree k.

A power-law degree distribution appears as a straight line on a log–log plot

Types

In random graphs of the Erdős–Rényi type, degrees are Poisson distributed and concentrated around the mean, so most nodes have similar degree. Many real-world networks, by contrast — including the World Wide Web, citation networks and some biological networks — have degree distributions that approximately follow a power law, P(k) ∝ k−γ, with the exponent γ typically between 2 and 3. Such networks are called scale-free networks and are characterised by a small number of highly connected hubs alongside many low-degree nodes. Preferential attachment models, such as the Barabási–Albert model, generate scale-free degree distributions.

Directed networks

In directed networks one distinguishes the in-degree distribution (the distribution of incoming edge counts) from the out-degree distribution. Many directed networks, such as the web graph, show power-law behaviour in both, often with different exponents.

Significance

The degree distribution strongly influences network behaviour, including robustness to failures and attacks, the spread of epidemics and information, and synchronisation. Scale-free networks are typically robust to random node removal but vulnerable to targeted removal of hubs, and their epidemic threshold can vanish in the infinite-size limit for γ ≤ 3.
