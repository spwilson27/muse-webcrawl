National Labor Relations Act

National Labor Relations Act of 1935
Enacted5 July 1935
Sponsored bySenator Robert F. Wagner
CreatedNational Labor Relations Board (NLRB)
Also known asThe Wagner Act

The National Labor Relations Act of 1935 (NLRA or the Wagner Act) is a foundational statute of United States labor law that guarantees the right of private-sector employees to organise into trade unions, engage in collective bargaining, and take collective action such as strikes. It created the National Labor Relations Board to enforce the law. Along with the Fair Labor Standards Act, it remains a cornerstone of New Deal labour legislation.

Background and provisions

Section 7(a) of the National Industrial Recovery Act (1933) had proclaimed workers' organising rights but lacked enforcement machinery, and the Supreme Court voided that Act in 1935. Senator Robert F. Wagner of New York drafted a stronger replacement. Section 7 of the NLRA guarantees employees the right to self-organisation, to form, join or assist labour organisations, and to bargain collectively. Section 8 lists unfair labour practices by employers — interfering with organising, dominating company unions, discriminating against union members, and refusing to bargain. The NLRB conducts representation elections and adjudicates unfair labour practice charges.

Constitutionality and amendments

Employers widely defied the Act expecting it to be struck down, but in NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp. (1937) the Supreme Court upheld it under the Commerce Clause. Union membership surged: the act underpinned the great organising drives of the CIO and the growth of the American labour movement. In 1947 the Republican-led Congress passed the Taft–Hartley Act over President Truman's veto, adding union unfair labour practices, banning the closed shop, and allowing states to pass "right-to-work" laws. Further amendments came with the Landrum–Griffin Act of 1959. The NLRA excludes agricultural and domestic workers, supervisors, and public-sector employees.
