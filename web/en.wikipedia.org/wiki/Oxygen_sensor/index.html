Oxygen sensor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An oxygen sensor (or lambda sensor, where lambda refers to air–fuel equivalence ratio, usually denoted by λ) is an electronic device that measures the proportion of oxygen (O2) in the gas or liquid being analysed. It is most familiar from automotive exhaust systems, where it lets the engine control unit adjust the air–fuel mixture for efficient combustion and low emissions.

Automotive use

Most automotive sensors are zirconia (zirconium dioxide) devices that generate a voltage, acting as a galvanic cell whose output depends on the difference in oxygen concentration between the exhaust gas and outside air. A narrowband sensor switches sharply around the stoichiometric point (λ = 1, about 14.7:1 for petrol), while wideband sensors measure the ratio over a broad range. Sensors are usually heated to reach operating temperature quickly.

Other applications

Oxygen sensors are used in diving rebreathers to monitor the partial pressure of oxygen, in medical ventilators and anaesthesia machines, in industrial combustion control, and in laboratory instruments such as the Clark electrode for dissolved oxygen.
