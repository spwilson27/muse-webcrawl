Price cap regulation

Price cap regulation is a form of incentive regulation for monopolies, especially privatised utilities, in which the regulator limits the prices a firm may charge, typically by an indexation formula, and lets the firm keep the profit it earns by beating the cap. The best-known form is RPI − X, devised for British Telecom's privatisation in 1984 on the advice of economist Stephen Littlechild: average prices may rise each year by no more than the retail price index minus X, an expected efficiency factor set by periodic review.

How it works

Rather than regulating each price, the regulator caps a weighted basket of the firm's services. Every four or five years the regulator reviews the firm's costs, investment needs and expected productivity, and resets X for the next control period. Between reviews the firm has a strong incentive to cut costs, because savings translate directly into profit; consumers gain at the next review when tougher X factors pass accumulated efficiency gains into lower real prices.

Comparison with rate-of-return regulation

Price caps replaced the older American-style rate-of-return (cost-plus) regulation, under which a utility earned an allowed return on its capital base. Cost-plus schemes blunt cost-cutting incentives and can encourage gold-plated investment (the Averch–Johnson effect). Price caps sharpen efficiency incentives, but critics note they can also encourage firms to skimp on quality, under-invest in long-lived assets, or exploit informational advantages over the regulator, which knows less about true costs than the firm does. Modern regimes therefore combine price caps with quality standards, service guarantees and output targets.

Applications

The model spread from British Telecom to the UK's gas, electricity, water and rail sectors — water regulation in England and Wales uses variants such as RPI − X + K, where K allows price rises to fund capital investment — and was widely exported to telecommunications and energy regulation in Europe, Latin America and Australasia. In British water, the framework evolved into total-expenditure ("totex") and outcome-based controls under Ofwat's periodic reviews.
