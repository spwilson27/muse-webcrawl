Mughal gardens are a type of garden built by the Mughals in the Persian style of Islamic garden design. This style was heavily influenced by the charbagh — a quadrilateral garden divided by walkways and flowing water channels into four smaller parts — representing the four rivers of the Islamic paradise and the four elements. Gardens were built across the Mughal Empire from the reign of its founder, Babur (r. 1526–1530), through the empire's height under Shah Jahan in the 17th century.

The charbagh of the Taj Mahal at Agra, divided by water channels.

History

Babur, a Timurid prince from Central Asia, brought Persian garden traditions to India and laid out gardens at Agra and Kabul; his burial garden, the Bagh-e Babur in Kabul, survives. Under Akbar, tomb gardens such as that of Humayun in Delhi (c. 1570) set the monumental scale of the type. The style reached its peak under Jahangir and Shah Jahan, who created the celebrated pleasure gardens of Kashmir — Shalimar Bagh and Nishat Bagh on Dal Lake — and the Shalimar Gardens at Lahore (1641–42). The garden of the Taj Mahal (completed c. 1653) is the most famous Mughal charbagh.

Design elements

  • Charbagh layout — a square or rectangular plan quartered by axial water channels (nahrs) and raised walkways, often further subdivided.
  • Water — channels, pools, fountains, and cascades (chadar) supplied by aqueducts, wells, or rivers; water symbolises life and paradise.
  • Terraces — hillside gardens, especially in Kashmir, arranged in terraces, traditionally up to twelve representing the signs of the zodiac.
  • Pavilions and baradaris — open pillared pavilions, gateways, and tomb or palace buildings placed on the central axis.
  • Planting — fruit trees (mango, citrus, pomegranate), flowering plants, and shade trees in ordered beds.
  • Enclosure — high walls with corner towers, giving privacy and a sense of paradise enclosed from the world outside.

Symbolism

The Mughal garden was conceived as an earthly image of Jannah, the paradise described in the Quran, with its four rivers of water, milk, honey, and wine. Tomb gardens joined this symbolism to dynastic commemoration: the tomb at the centre or end of the charbagh awaited the resurrection in a paradisiacal setting.

Notable examples

  • Shalimar Gardens, Lahore
  • Shalimar Bagh and Nishat Bagh, Srinagar
  • Ram Bagh and Mehtab Bagh, Agra
