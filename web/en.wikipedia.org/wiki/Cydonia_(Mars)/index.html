Cydonia (Mars)

Cydonia is a region on Mars, in the planet's northern hemisphere near the boundary between the southern highlands and northern lowlands (the Acidalia Planitia), at roughly 40° north latitude. It is famous for its numerous mesas and for one formation popularly known as the "Face on Mars".

The "Face on Mars"

In 1976, the Viking 1 orbiter photographed a mesa in Cydonia that, under that day's lighting and resolution, resembled a human face. NASA released the image with a playful caption, but it was seized upon by enthusiasts as evidence of artificial construction by an ancient Martian civilisation, spawning books, films, and decades of speculation. The feature became a canonical example of pareidolia — the human tendency to perceive faces in random patterns.

Later imaging

Higher-resolution images from Mars Global Surveyor (1998 and 2001) and Mars Express showed the "face" to be a natural, heavily eroded mesa, with no trace of the smooth symmetry seen in the low-resolution Viking frame. Scientists regard the matter as settled, though the region remains geologically interesting for its mesas, possible debris aprons, and evidence bearing on the hypothesised ancient northern ocean of Mars.

Geology

Cydonia lies in a transition zone of "fretted terrain", where isolated mesas and knobs are interpreted as remnants of highland material eroded back, possibly by ice-rich processes. The area has been used as a test case for how image resolution and lighting affect geological interpretation.

In culture

The Face has featured in television series, films, and video games, and Cydonia lends its name to cultural works including the Muse song "Knights of Cydonia".
