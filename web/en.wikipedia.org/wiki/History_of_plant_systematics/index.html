History of plant systematics

The history of plant systematics — the scientific classification of plants — runs from the practical herbals of antiquity through the artificial sexual system of Linnaeus, the natural systems of the 19th century, evolutionary classifications after Darwin, to the molecular-phylogenetic systems (APG) used today.

Antiquity to the Renaissance

Theophrastus (c. 300 BCE), a pupil of Aristotle, classified about 500 plants by growth form and other characters in his Enquiry into Plants, earning him the title "father of botany". Dioscorides' De materia medica (1st century CE) organised plants by medicinal use and remained authoritative for over 1,500 years. In China, India and the Islamic world, pharmacopoeias and agricultural treatises developed parallel traditions. The printed herbals of Renaissance "German fathers of botany" such as Brunfels, Bock and Fuchs (1542) combined observation with classical texts.

Early scientific classification

Andrea Cesalpino (1583) classified by fruit and seed characters. John Ray's Historia Plantarum (1686–1704) defined species more rigorously and distinguished monocotyledons from dicotyledons. Joseph Pitton de Tournefort popularised the genus concept. Carl Linnaeus's Species Plantarum (1753) fixed the starting point of modern botanical nomenclature and introduced binomial names universally; his sexual system, based on stamens and pistils, was convenient but admittedly artificial, and later naturalists including Bernard and Antoine Laurent de Jussieu built "natural" systems grouping plants by overall resemblance.

Evolutionary systems

Darwin's theory of evolution (1859) recast classification as the reconstruction of genealogy. Eichler, and then Engler and Prantl's Die natürlichen Pflanzenfamilien, dominated German botany; in the English-speaking world the Bentham and Hooker system, used in Genera Plantarum, long prevailed. 20th-century systems by Cronquist, Takhtajan, Dahlgren and Thorne integrated anatomy, chemistry and palaeobotany.

Molecular era

From the 1990s, DNA sequence data — analysed cladistically — revolutionised plant classification. The Angiosperm Phylogeny Group's classifications (APG I 1998 through APG IV 2016) reorganised flowering-plant orders and families by molecular phylogeny, sometimes drastically (for example dissolving the old Scrophulariaceae and expanding Amaranthaceae). Fern classification was similarly revised by the Pteridophyte Phylogeny Group (PPG I, 2016). Modern systematics integrates genomics, morphology and fossils within an explicitly phylogenetic framework.
