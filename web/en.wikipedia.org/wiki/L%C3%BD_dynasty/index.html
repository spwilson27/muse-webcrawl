The Lý dynasty (Vietnamese: Nhà Lý, 1009–1225) was a Vietnamese dynasty that began when Lý Công Uẩn overthrew the Early Lê dynasty and ended when the eight-year-old empress regnant Lý Chiêu Hoàng was forced to abdicate in favour of her husband, Trần Cảnh. It was the first long-lived Vietnamese dynasty, ruling Đại Việt for over two centuries.

Founding and achievements

In 1010 Lý Thái Tổ moved the capital to the citadel of Đại La, renaming it Thăng Long ("Ascending Dragon") — the site of modern Hanoi. Under the Lý, the state consolidated a Confucian-style administration, promulgated the first written law code (the Hình thư of 1042), and held the first imperial civil service examination in 1075. The Temple of Literature (Quốc Tử Giám), founded in 1076, is regarded as Vietnam's first national university.

Religion and warfare

The dynasty strongly patronized Mahayana Buddhism, whose monks served as royal advisers. Militarily, the Lý defended against the Song dynasty in the Lý–Song War of 1075–1077, famous for the poem "Nam quốc sơn hà" recited on the Như Nguyệt River line, and expanded southward at Champa's expense. In the early 13th century, court decline allowed the Trần clan to take power peacefully.
