Ifriqiya (Arabic: إفريقية, Ifrīqiyya), also spelled Ifriqia, is the medieval Arab name for the region of North Africa corresponding roughly to the Roman province of Africa Proconsularis — that is, modern Tunisia, northeastern Algeria, and northwestern Libya. The name derives from the Latin Africa, which originally referred to this area before being extended to the whole continent.

History

Following the Muslim conquest of the Maghreb in the late 7th century, Ifriqiya was organized as a province governed from Kairouan, founded in 670 by Uqba ibn Nafi and destined to become one of the great cities of the Islamic world. Initially subordinate to Egypt and then to the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates in Damascus and Baghdad, Ifriqiya gained effective autonomy under the hereditary Aghlabid emirs (800–909), who embellished Kairouan and conquered Sicily.

In 909 the Fatimids, an Isma'ili Shi'a movement, overthrew the Aghlabids and made Ifriqiya the base from which they conquered Egypt in 969 and founded Cairo. Their Zirid deputies retained Ifriqiya, but in the 1050s, after the Zirids renounced Fatimid allegiance, the invasions of the Banu Hilal Bedouin devastated the region's agriculture and cities. After periods of Almohad and other rule, the Hafsid dynasty (1229–1574) governed Ifriqiya from Tunis in relative prosperity. The term fell out of use as Ottoman and modern geography supplanted it.

Significance

Medieval Ifriqiya was a major centre of Islamic scholarship and commerce, linked by trade routes to sub-Saharan Africa, Egypt, Sicily, and al-Andalus. Kairouan's Great Mosque became a model of Maghrebi architecture, and the region's jurists and theologians, such as Sahnun, shaped the Maliki school of law dominant in North and West Africa.
