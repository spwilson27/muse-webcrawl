Global South

The term Global South refers broadly to the regions of Latin America, Africa, Asia (excluding certain high-income states) and Oceania that lie outside the wealthy, industrialised "Global North". It is used in development studies, international relations and political discourse to describe countries characterised by relatively lower income, colonial histories and shared political interests. Although the term has a geographical flavour, it is primarily a socio-economic and political category: Australia and New Zealand, for instance, are southern but considered part of the Global North, while much of the "South" lies in the northern hemisphere.

History of the term

The phrase is often traced to activist Carl Oglesby in 1969, writing about the Vietnam War, but it gained wide currency after the Cold War as a less pejorative alternative to "Third World" (from the Non-Aligned Movement era) and "developing countries". The Brandt Report of 1980 popularised a North–South divide drawn along a line of global wealth. In the 21st century, "Global South" became common in United Nations usage, academic work and South–South cooperation forums such as the G77 and BRICS summits.

Usage and criticism

Supporters argue the term captures common experiences of colonialism, unequal trade and under-representation in global governance, and underpins collective bargaining by developing states on issues such as climate finance and trade. Critics note its imprecision: the category lumps together highly diverse states, from China and India to small island nations, and some argue it obscures growing economic differences within the group or functions mainly as a rhetorical and geopolitical identity.
