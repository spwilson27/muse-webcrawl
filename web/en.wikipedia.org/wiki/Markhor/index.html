The markhor (Capra falconeri) is a large wild goat native to the mountains of Central Asia. It is famous for the male's spectacular corkscrew-shaped horns, which can measure up to 160 cm (63 in) along the curve in some subspecies. The markhor is the national animal of Pakistan.

Description

Markhor stand 65 to 115 cm at the shoulder and weigh 32 to 110 kg. The coat is grizzled reddish-brown in summer and longer and greyer in winter; adult males develop a long beard and mane of hair on the chest and shins. Both sexes have corkscrew horns, but those of males are far larger, flaring outward in tight spirals. The name derives from Persian mār (snake) and khor (eater) — folklore holds that markhor kill and eat snakes, and that froth from their mouths when chewing cud can neutralise snake venom.

Distribution and habitat

The species inhabits scrub forest and open rocky slopes at elevations of roughly 600 to 3,600 m across the mountain ranges of Afghanistan, Pakistan (including Gilgit-Baltistan, Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa), northern India, Tajikistan, southern Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan. Recognised subspecies include the Astor (flare-horned) markhor, Kabul markhor, Sulaiman markhor and Bukharan markhor.

Behaviour and ecology

Markhor are grazers and browsers, active mainly in the early morning and late afternoon. Females gather in herds of up to nine, while males are more solitary outside the autumn–winter rut, when they contest dominance with horn-clashing displays. Predators include snow leopards, wolves and lynx. Gestation lasts about 135–170 days, producing one or two kids.

Conservation

Long hunted for trophies and meat and threatened by habitat pressure and competition with livestock, the markhor declined severely in the 20th century and was listed as Endangered. Community-based conservancies in Pakistan and Tajikistan, funded partly by tightly controlled trophy-hunting programmes whose fees go largely to local communities, have produced substantial recoveries, and in 2015 the IUCN downlisted the species to Near Threatened. In 2023 the UN General Assembly designated 24 May as the International Day of the Markhor.
