Moment of inertia

The moment of inertia, otherwise known as the mass moment of inertia, angular mass, second moment of mass, or most accurately, rotational inertia, of a rigid body is a quantity that determines the torque needed for a desired angular acceleration about a rotational axis, akin to how mass determines the force needed for a desired acceleration. It depends on the body's mass distribution with respect to the axis, and larger moments of inertia require more torque to change the body's rate of rotation by a given amount.

It is an extensive (additive) property: for a point mass the moment of inertia is simply the mass times the square of the perpendicular distance to the axis of rotation. The moment of inertia of a rigid composite system is the sum of the moments of inertia of its component subsystems (all taken about the same axis). Its simplest definition is the second moment of mass with respect to distance from an axis.

Definition

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis with angular velocity ω, the moment of inertia I relates torque τ and angular acceleration α by:

τ = Iα

For a system of point masses, the moment of inertia about an axis is:

I = Σ mi ri²

For a continuous body with mass density ρ(r), the moment of inertia about an axis is the volume integral:

I = ∫V ρ(r) d² dV

where d is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the volume element. The SI unit of moment of inertia is the kilogram metre squared (kg·m²).

Parallel axis theorem

The parallel axis theorem, also known as the Huygens–Steiner theorem, states that the moment of inertia of a body about any axis equals its moment of inertia about a parallel axis through its centre of mass plus the total mass times the square of the distance between the axes:

I = Icm + Md²

Examples

  • Solid sphere about a diameter: I = (2/5)MR²
  • Hollow sphere about a diameter: I = (2/3)MR²
  • Solid cylinder about its axis: I = (1/2)MR²
  • Thin rod about its centre: I = (1/12)ML²
  • Thin rod about one end: I = (1/3)ML²
