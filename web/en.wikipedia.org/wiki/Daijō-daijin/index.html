Daijō-daijin

The Daijō-daijin or Dajō-daijin (Japanese: 太政大臣, "Chancellor of the Realm") was the head of the Daijō-kan (Council of State) and the highest-ranking official in the imperial government of Japan under the ritsuryō system established by the Taihō Code of 701 and refined by the Yōrō Code. Modelled on the senior chancellorships of Tang-dynasty China, the post stood above the Sadaijin (Minister of the Left) and Udaijin (Minister of the Right).

Role

The Daijō-daijin was nominally the chief counsellor of the emperor and president of the Council of State, which supervised the eight ministries of the ritsuryō bureaucracy. Because the office was considered a dignity rather than an ordinary working post, it was frequently left vacant or filled honorarily by an imperial prince or a senior court noble; the code provided that if no suitably virtuous candidate existed, the post should remain empty. In practice, during the Heian period real power lay with the Fujiwara regents (sesshō and kampaku), while the Daijō-daijin often functioned as a capstone honour for retired regents and members of the highest aristocracy. The office conferred the senior first rank (shō ichi-i) or junior first rank at court.

History

The position originated in the 7th-century Taika Reforms. It was briefly revived after the Meiji Restoration: in 1868 the Meiji government restored the Daijō-kan system, and Sanjō Sanetomi served as the last Daijō-daijin from 1871 until the office was abolished on 22 December 1885, when Itō Hirobumi replaced it with the modern cabinet system as Japan’s first prime minister. Holders of the ancient office included Prince Ōtomo (the first, appointed 671) and leading statesmen of the Fujiwara clan such as Fujiwara no Fuhito and Fujiwara no Michinaga.
