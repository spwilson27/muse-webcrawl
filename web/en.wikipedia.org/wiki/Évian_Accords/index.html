The Évian Accords were a series of agreements signed on 18 March 1962 at Évian-les-Bains, France, between the French government of President Charles de Gaulle and the Provisional Government of the Algerian Republic (GPRA), the political arm of the National Liberation Front (FLN). The accords ended the Algerian War, which had begun on 1 November 1954, establishing an immediate ceasefire and providing for a referendum on self-determination in Algeria. The referendum of 1 July 1962 produced an overwhelming vote for independence, and France recognised Algeria as an independent state on 3 July; Algeria celebrates independence on 5 July 1962.

Background

By 1960 the war had become politically untenable for France: the army could not fully suppress the FLN, international opinion (including at the United Nations, which recognised the Algerian right to self-determination in December 1960) had turned, and conscript casualties poisoned French politics. De Gaulle, returned to power in 1958 by the army and the European settlers (pieds-noirs) partly in the expectation he would keep Algeria French, instead announced a policy of self-determination in September 1959. Secret contacts with the FLN began in 1960 (the "Si Salah" affair and Melun talks), while enraged settlers mounted the "week of the barricades" (January 1960) and dissident generals attempted a putsch in April 1961. The terrorist campaign of the settler Organisation armée secrète (OAS) — plastiquages, assassinations, and eventually a scorched-earth campaign — overshadowed the final negotiations.

Terms

Negotiations at Évian (May–June 1961, then February–March 1962, with preliminary sessions at Lugrin and Les Rousses) produced agreements covering:

  • An immediate ceasefire, effective at noon on 19 March 1962, and release of political prisoners; FLN leader Ahmed Ben Bella and his companions, held in France since 1956, were freed.
  • A transitional executive in Algeria and a self-determination referendum within six months.
  • Guarantees for the European population: a three-year period in which they could choose Algerian citizenship or retain French nationality, protection of property, and representation — safeguards largely overwhelmed by the exodus of 1962.
  • French economic and technical cooperation, aid for development, and preferential arrangements for Saharan hydrocarbons, discovered at Hassi Messaoud in 1956.
  • French military rights: retention of the Mers El Kébir naval base for fifteen years and Saharan test sites (including nuclear facilities at Reggane and In Ekker) for five years after independence.

Aftermath

The ceasefire held between France and the FLN, but violence by the OAS and reprisals against the harkis (Algerians who had served with the French army) marred the transition. In the summer of 1962 roughly a million pieds-noirs fled to France in the space of months. Tens of thousands of harkis were killed in Algeria in the months after independence; those who reached France were often confined to camps — a grievance festering in Franco-Algerian memory politics for decades. The 19 March ceasefire date is commemorated in France as the national day of remembrance for the Algerian War, a choice long contested by veterans' and pied-noir groups. In Algeria, the accords' economic clauses were progressively overturned, culminating in the 1971 nationalisation of hydrocarbons, but the Évian framework remained the legal basis of Franco-Algerian relations.
