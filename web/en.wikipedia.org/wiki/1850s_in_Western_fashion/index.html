1850s in Western fashion

Fashion plate of 1856

The 1850s in Western fashion saw the triumph of the wide skirt for women, supported first by layers of petticoats and then, from 1856, by the lightweight cage crinoline, while men's clothing settled into the sober three-piece suit of frock coat, waistcoat, and trousers that would define Victorian menswear. Industrialisation — the sewing machine, synthetic dyes, and mass-produced clothing — transformed how fashion was made and spread.

Women's fashion

At the decade's start, skirts were held out by multiple horsehair ("crinoline" proper) and flounced petticoats; bodices were tight, pointed at the waist, with sloping shoulders and pagoda sleeves worn over white undersleeves. In 1856 the patented cage crinoline of steel hoops replaced the heavy petticoats, allowing skirts of enormous circumference with far less weight. Skirt fronts remained flat while fullness moved to the back by the late 1850s. Day dresses were of printed cotton or wool challis; evening dresses of silk with low necklines and short sleeves. Small white collars and cuffs, cameo brooches, and fringed shawls were ubiquitous accessories.

Men's fashion

Men wore the frock coat — knee-length, waisted, and skirted — for business and formal day wear, with the looser sack coat appearing for informal occasions. Waistcoats remained colourful, often of figured silk or velvet, while trousers grew plainer, in checks, stripes, or matching wool. Shirts with detachable collars, small neckties and cravats, and tall silk top hats completed the respectable outfit; beards became fashionable after the Crimean War.

Technology and industry

The decade brought the commercial sewing machine (Singer's patents of the 1850s), the first synthetic dye — Perkin's mauveine (1856) — and expanding ready-made clothing production. Fashion plates and magazines such as Godey's Lady's Book and the Englishwoman's Domestic Magazine spread the new styles rapidly across Europe and America.
