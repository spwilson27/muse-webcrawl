Convergent boundary

A convergent boundary (also known as a destructive boundary) is an area on Earth where two or more lithospheric plates collide. One plate eventually slides beneath the other, a process known as subduction. The collision zone can create mountains, island arcs, oceanic trenches, and intense earthquake and volcanic activity.

Types

Oceanic–continental convergence

Where dense oceanic lithosphere meets continental lithosphere, the oceanic plate subducts, forming a deep oceanic trench and a volcanic mountain chain on the continent, as in the Andes along the west coast of South America.

Oceanic–oceanic convergence

One oceanic plate subducts beneath the other, producing a volcanic island arc such as the Mariana Islands or the Japanese archipelago, fronted by a deep trench.

Continental–continental convergence

Because continental crust is too buoyant to subduct deeply, the two landmasses crumple and thicken, thrusting up great mountain ranges. The Himalayas, formed by the collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates, are the classic example.

Effects

Convergent boundaries are sites of the Earth's most powerful earthquakes (megathrust events), explosive volcanism, and the recycling of crustal material into the mantle. Over geological time they close ocean basins and assemble supercontinents.
