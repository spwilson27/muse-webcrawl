Sumapaz Páramo

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Sumapaz Páramo is a large páramo ecosystem — a high-altitude tropical moorland — in the Cordillera Oriental of the Colombian Andes, located south of Bogotá in the departments of Cundinamarca, Meta, and Huila. It is considered the largest páramo in the world, covering roughly 330,000 hectares, of which a substantial portion is protected within Sumapaz National Natural Park, established in 1977.

Ecology

Lying mostly above 3,500 metres, the páramo is characterised by tussock grasses, cushion plants, lagoons, and the emblematic frailejones (Espeletia) — giant rosette plants adapted to cold, mist, and intense ultraviolet radiation. The wetlands and peaty soils of Sumapaz act as a sponge, storing water in the wet season and releasing it slowly, supplying rivers including the Sumapaz, Cabrera, and Blanco, and forming a crucial part of the water supply of Bogotá and the wider region. Wildlife includes spectacled bears, páramo tapirs, white-tailed deer, and many endemic birds and amphibians.

Human history

The region was inhabited in pre-Columbian times by Muisca and Sutagao peoples, for whom the lakes held ritual significance. In the twentieth century, Sumapaz was a stronghold of peasant self-organisation and agrarian movements, and later an area contested during Colombia's armed conflict, with FARC fronts operating in the mountains; it consequently remained closed to visitors for long periods. Peasant communities today advocate recognition of campesino reserve zones alongside conservation.

Conservation

Threats include agricultural encroachment, particularly potato farming, burning, and climate change, which pushes species upslope. A 2018 Colombian law on páramos strengthened protections, prohibiting mining and restricting agriculture in these ecosystems.
