Alexander Calder (22 July 1898 – 11 November 1976) was an American sculptor known for inventing the mobile, a type of kinetic sculpture made with delicately balanced or suspended components that move in response to air currents. He also created static monumental sculptures, dubbed "stabiles" by Jean Arp.

Early life and Cirque Calder

Born into a family of artists — his father Alexander Stirling Calder and grandfather Alexander Milne Calder were both sculptors — Calder trained first as a mechanical engineer before studying art in New York. In Paris in the late 1920s he created the Cirque Calder, a miniature wire circus he performed for avant-garde audiences, and began making wire portraits and animals. A 1930 visit to Piet Mondrian's studio pushed him toward abstraction.

Mobiles and stabiles

Calder's first mobiles, some motor-driven and others wind-blown, were exhibited in Paris in 1931–32; Marcel Duchamp coined the term "mobile" for them. His hanging mobile Lobster Trap and Fish Tail (1939) was commissioned for the Museum of Modern Art in New York. After the Second World War he produced large-scale stabiles for public spaces worldwide, including Flamingo (1974) in Chicago and La Grande Vitesse (1969) in Grand Rapids, Michigan.

Other work and legacy

Calder also painted, designed jewellery, stage sets, tapestries and even painted airliners for Braniff International. He won the Grand Prize for Sculpture at the 1952 Venice Biennale. The Calder Foundation, established in 1987, preserves his legacy.
