Thomas Attwood (6 October 1783 – 6 March 1856) was a British banker, economist and political campaigner. He was the founder and leader of the Birmingham Political Union, a key organisation in the agitation that produced the Great Reform Act of 1832, and he was the leading figure of the "Birmingham School" of economists, which argued that economic depressions were caused by deficient demand and could be cured by an expansion of the paper currency.

Life and career

Attwood was born in Halesowen, then part of Worcestershire, into a family of ironmasters and bankers, and took over his father's bank in Birmingham. The depression that followed the Napoleonic Wars convinced him that the return to the gold standard and the scarcity of money were responsible for mass unemployment, and he argued for an expansion of paper currency as the remedy — a position that placed him against the deflationary orthodoxy of the day.

The Birmingham Political Union

In 1830 Attwood founded the Birmingham Political Union (BPU), an alliance of middle-class and working-class reformers pressing for parliamentary reform. Its disciplined mass meetings made it a model for later popular organisations, and it played a celebrated role during the "Days of May" crisis of 1832, when Attwood promised that the Union's tens of thousands of members stood ready to resist any attempt to frustrate reform — a stand widely credited with helping secure the Reform Act. After the Act, Attwood became one of the first MPs for the new borough of Birmingham, sitting in Parliament from 1832 to 1839.

Economics and later years

Historians of economic thought have recognised Attwood as an early proponent of demand-management ideas later associated with Keynes: he held that idle resources coexisted with unmet wants because of insufficient purchasing power, a doctrine sometimes called "currency reform" or "underconsumptionism". Disappointed by the limited franchise of 1832, he supported broader reform and in 1839 presented to Parliament the first of the great Chartist petitions, though he disapproved of physical-force Chartism. He retired from public life soon after.

Legacy

Attwood is commemorated in Birmingham by a statue in Chamberlain Square; the reclining bronze figure was notably re-cast and repositioned during the square's redevelopment in the 2010s. A suburb of Canberra, Attwood-adjacent street names, and the survival of the Birmingham School's reputation among historians of economics preserve his memory.
