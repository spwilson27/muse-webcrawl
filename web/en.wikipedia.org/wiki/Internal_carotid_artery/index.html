Internal carotid artery

Internal carotid artery
Source: Common carotid artery
Branches: Ophthalmic, posterior communicating, anterior choroidal, anterior cerebral, middle cerebral arteries
Supplies: Brain, eye, forehead, nose

The internal carotid artery (ICA) is one of the two terminal branches of the common carotid artery, arising at about the level of the fourth cervical vertebra. It supplies the anterior part of the brain, the eye and its adnexa, and parts of the forehead and nose, and—unlike the external carotid artery—gives off no branches in the neck.

Segments

The Bouthillier classification divides the ICA into seven segments: cervical (C1), petrous (C2), lacerum (C3), cavernous (C4), clinoid (C5), ophthalmic (C6), and communicating (C7). The artery enters the skull through the carotid canal in the petrous temporal bone, crosses over the foramen lacerum, runs through the cavernous sinus in an S-shaped "carotid siphon", and pierces the dura to terminate by dividing into the anterior and middle cerebral arteries.

Branches

  • Ophthalmic artery
  • Posterior communicating artery
  • Anterior choroidal artery
  • Anterior cerebral artery and middle cerebral artery (terminal)

Clinical significance

Narrowing (stenosis) of the ICA by atherosclerosis is a major cause of ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack, and may be treated with carotid endarterectomy, stenting, or medical therapy. Dissection of the ICA can also cause stroke and Horner's syndrome. The artery contributes (via anastomoses and the circle of Willis) collateral flow to the brain.
