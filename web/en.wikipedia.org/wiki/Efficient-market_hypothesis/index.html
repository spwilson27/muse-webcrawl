Efficient-market hypothesis

The efficient-market hypothesis (EMH) is a hypothesis in financial economics that states that asset prices reflect all available information. A direct implication is that it is impossible to "beat the market" consistently on a risk-adjusted basis since market prices should only react to new information. Because the EMH was formulated in the 1970s and widely accepted in academia for decades, it shaped research and investment practice, encouraging the growth of index funds.

Under the EMH, prices adjust rapidly to new information, following a random walk.

History

The concept that stock prices follow a random walk dates to the French mathematician Louis Bachelier's 1900 dissertation on speculation. The modern hypothesis was developed most influentially by Eugene Fama of the University of Chicago, whose 1970 survey "Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work" codified the framework. Paul Samuelson gave the theory a rigorous foundation, arguing that properly anticipated prices fluctuate randomly.

Forms

Fama classified market efficiency into three forms based on the information set reflected in prices:

  • Weak form — all past trading information (prices, volume) is already incorporated, so technical analysis cannot produce excess returns.
  • Semi-strong form — all publicly available information is reflected, so neither technical nor fundamental analysis can beat the market consistently.
  • Strong form — all information, public and private (insider information), is reflected; even insiders cannot profit systematically.

Evidence and criticism

Evidence supporting the EMH includes the difficulty professional fund managers have in outperforming broad indexes after fees, and the rapid incorporation of news into prices. Critics, particularly from behavioral finance, point to anomalies such as the momentum effect, value premium, post-earnings-announcement drift, bubbles and crashes (notably 1929 and 2008), and episodes of apparent mispricing. Robert Shiller argued that prices are far more volatile than dividend fundamentals justify. Fama himself acknowledged that tests of efficiency are joint tests with a model of expected returns (the "joint hypothesis problem").

The 2013 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences was awarded jointly to Fama, Shiller, and Lars Peter Hansen — a pairing often read as emblematic of the field's unresolved tension. A pragmatic middle view holds that markets are "efficiently inefficient": competition makes them hard to beat, but the prospect of profit is what keeps prices informative (the Grossman–Stiglitz paradox notes that perfectly efficient markets would give no incentive to gather information).
