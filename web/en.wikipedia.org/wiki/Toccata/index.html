Toccata
EtymologyItalian toccare, "to touch"
InstrumentsKeyboard instruments (organ, harpsichord, piano)
PeriodsRenaissance to present

A toccata (from Italian toccare, "to touch") is a virtuoso piece of music typically for a keyboard or plucked-string instrument featuring fast-moving, lightly fingered or virtuosic passages, with or without imitative or fugal interludes, generally emphasising the dexterity of the performer's fingers.

History

The form first appeared in late Renaissance keyboard music, in works by composers such as Claudio Merulo and Andrea and Giovanni Gabrieli. Girolamo Frescobaldi's two books of toccatas (1615, 1627) are milestones of the Baroque genre. In Germany the organ toccata was developed by Dieterich Buxtehude and reached its height in Johann Sebastian Bach's Toccata and Fugue in D minor, BWV 565, among the most famous organ works ever written.

Later toccatas

The form was revived in the 19th and 20th centuries for piano, in toccatas by Robert Schumann, and by composers including Sergei Prokofiev, Maurice Ravel (the final movement of Le tombeau de Couperin), and Aram Khachaturian, whose Toccata in E-flat minor is a popular encore piece.
