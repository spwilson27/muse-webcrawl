Containment building

A containment building, in its most common usage, is a reinforced steel, concrete or lead structure enclosing a nuclear reactor. It is designed, in any emergency, to contain the escape of radioactive steam or gas to a maximum pressure in the range of 60 to 200 psi (410 to 1,380 kPa). The containment is the fourth and final barrier to radioactive release (part of a nuclear reactor's defence in depth strategy), the first being the fuel ceramic itself, the second being the metal fuel cladding tubes, and the third being the reactor vessel and coolant system.

Design

Containment buildings for pressurised water reactors (PWRs) are typically large cylindrical or spherical structures of steel-lined reinforced or prestressed concrete, designed to withstand the pressure of steam released in a loss-of-coolant accident as well as external hazards such as earthquakes, tornadoes and aircraft impact. Boiling water reactors (BWRs) generally use smaller containments divided into a drywell and a wetwell (torus or suppression pool), in which steam is condensed by a pool of water.

The containment is equipped with isolation valves on piping penetrations, airlocks for personnel access, and systems such as sprays and fan coolers to control pressure and temperature after an accident. Hydrogen recombiners or igniters may be installed to control combustible gas.

History

Containment structures became standard on civilian power reactors in the United States from the late 1950s. The performance of containments was a central issue in the accidents at Three Mile Island (1979), where the containment largely retained fission products, and at Fukushima Daiichi (2011), where hydrogen explosions damaged the reactor buildings. The Chernobyl disaster (1986) involved an RBMK reactor that lacked a full Western-style containment building.

Other uses

The term is also used for structures enclosing other hazardous processes, and for containment in research reactors and nuclear fuel facilities.
