Territorial claims in the Arctic

Territorial claims in the Arctic concern the sovereignty and maritime rights of the states bordering the Arctic Ocean: Canada, Denmark (via Greenland), Iceland, Norway, Russia, and the United States. Unlike Antarctica, which is governed by the Antarctic Treaty System, the Arctic has no overarching territorial treaty; claims are governed primarily by the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) and by domestic law.

Maritime boundaries and the continental shelf

Under UNCLOS, coastal states may claim an exclusive economic zone extending 200 nautical miles from their coasts, and may extend their continental shelf beyond that limit where geological evidence supports it. Several states have made overlapping submissions to the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf concerning the Lomonosov Ridge, an undersea feature running beneath the North Pole. Russia's 2001 submission was the first; in August 2007 a Russian expedition planted a titanium flag on the seabed at the North Pole, a symbolic act with no legal effect. Canada and Denmark have also filed claims encompassing the geographic North Pole area.

Disputed areas

Notable disputes include Hans Island, a small uninhabited island between Canada and Greenland, whose sovereignty was resolved in 2022 by an agreement dividing the island; the status of the Northwest Passage, which Canada considers internal waters and the United States and others regard as an international strait; and the Svalbard archipelago, where the 1920 Svalbard Treaty grants Norway sovereignty while reserving economic rights to other signatories.

Cooperation

The five Arctic Ocean coastal states affirmed in the 2008 Ilulissat Declaration that the law of the sea framework suffices to manage overlapping claims. The Arctic Council provides a forum for cooperation among the eight Arctic states, though its remit excludes military security.
