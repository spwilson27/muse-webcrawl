Auxiliary police are law enforcement officers who support a regular police force on a part-time, reserve or volunteer basis. Depending on the jurisdiction they may be unpaid volunteers, paid part-timers or reservists with full or limited police powers, deployed for patrol, traffic control, crowd management at public events, disaster response and community policing, freeing regular officers for other duties.

By country

In the United States, many departments maintain auxiliary or reserve units. The New York City Police Department Auxiliary Police, established in its modern form in 1950, fields thousands of uniformed volunteers who patrol in marked cars but are unarmed and have limited arrest authority. American reserve officers in other states may be fully sworn and armed when on duty.

In the United Kingdom, the equivalent institution is the Special Constabulary: volunteer officers who hold the full powers of a constable while on duty, dating from the Special Constables Act 1831 and greatly expanded in wartime. Singapore's Volunteer Special Constabulary, Canada's auxiliary constable programmes, Hong Kong's Auxiliary Police Force (a paid part-time force), and Malaysia's Police Volunteer Reserve follow similar models. Historical auxiliaries include the wartime German Hilfspolizei and various colonial auxiliary forces, whose legacies are more controversial.

Powers and training

Training requirements range from a few dozen hours of classroom instruction to academy courses comparable to those for regular officers. Powers vary correspondingly: some auxiliaries may only observe and report, while others can detain suspects, direct traffic and carry firearms. Uniforms are usually similar to but distinguishable from those of regular police, and auxiliaries typically serve under the same disciplinary structures.
