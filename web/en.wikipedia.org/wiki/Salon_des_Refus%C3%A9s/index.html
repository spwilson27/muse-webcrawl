The Salon des Refusés (French for "exhibition of rejects") was an art exhibition first held in Paris in 1863, displaying works rejected by the jury of the official Paris Salon. Authorised by Emperor Napoleon III, it became a landmark event in the history of modern art, giving public exposure to artists who would lead the avant-garde.

Background

The Paris Salon, run under the auspices of the Académie des Beaux-Arts, was the most important art exhibition in the Western world, and acceptance was crucial to an artist's career. In 1863 the jury was exceptionally harsh, rejecting more than half of the works submitted — some 2,800 of roughly 5,000 entries. The outcry from rejected artists was so great that Napoleon III visited the Salon, examined some of the rejected works, and decreed that they be shown in a separate exhibition so that the public could judge for itself.

The 1863 exhibition

The Salon des Refusés opened on 17 May 1863 in the Palais de l'Industrie, adjacent to the official Salon. Visitors flocked to it, many to mock. The greatest sensation was Édouard Manet's Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe (then titled Le Bain), whose depiction of a nude woman picnicking with fully clothed men scandalised and baffled the public. James McNeill Whistler's Symphony in White, No. 1: The White Girl also drew attention, and the exhibition included works by Gustave Courbet, Camille Pissarro, Johan Jongkind and Henri Fantin-Latour, among others.

Legacy

Although later Salons des Refusés were held sporadically (1873, 1875 and 1886), the 1863 exhibition's importance was symbolic: it legitimised the idea that artists rejected by the academic establishment could appeal directly to the public. It foreshadowed the independent exhibitions of the Impressionists beginning in 1874 and the eventual collapse of the Salon's monopoly on artistic reputation. The term salon des refusés is still used for exhibitions of rejected or outsider work.
