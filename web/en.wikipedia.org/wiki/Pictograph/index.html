A pictograph (or pictogram) is a graphic symbol that conveys its meaning through pictorial resemblance to a physical object. Pictographs stand for a word or idea by depicting what they mean — a sun, a person, a boat — rather than by representing sounds. They are among the earliest forms of written communication and remain ubiquitous in modern life, from road signs and airport wayfinding to Olympic sport symbols and emoji.

In early writing

The earliest writing systems evolved largely from pictographs. Tokens and drawings used in Mesopotamia developed into the pictorial precursors of cuneiform; Egyptian hieroglyphs included many pictorial signs; and a significant class of Chinese characters (象形, xiangxing) originated as drawings of objects. In such systems, pictographs were gradually stylised, combined, and extended by the rebus principle to express sounds and abstract ideas, giving rise to full writing. Pre-conquest Mesoamerican codices used pictographic and logographic systems to record histories, tribute, and genealogies.

Rock art

Painted images on rock are also called pictographs, in contrast to petroglyphs, which are pecked or carved (as at Petroglyph National Monument). Painted pictograph traditions include Indigenous rock art of the American Southwest and the Chumash painted caves of California.

Modern pictograms

Twentieth-century designers sought universal, language-independent symbols: Otto Neurath's Isotype system of the 1920s–30s pioneered pictorial statistics, and the 1964 Tokyo Olympics and the US Department of Transportation/AIGA symbol signs standardised the public-information pictogram — the running man, wheelchair symbol, and luggage icons now read worldwide. In statistics, "pictograph" also names a chart using repeated icons to represent quantities.
