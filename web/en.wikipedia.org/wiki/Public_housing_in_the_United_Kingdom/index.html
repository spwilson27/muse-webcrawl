Public housing in the United Kingdom

Public housing in the United Kingdom, commonly called council housing when owned by local authorities, forms a large part of the country's housing stock. Together with homes owned by housing associations, it constitutes the social rented sector, housing around 17% of households in England.

History

Local authority housing began in the late 19th century and expanded greatly after the Housing Act 1919 (the "Addison Act"), which promised "homes fit for heroes" after the First World War. Building peaked in the post-1945 decades under both Labour and Conservative governments, producing large estates and, in the 1950s–60s, high-rise tower blocks. By 1980, roughly a third of British households lived in council housing.

Right to Buy and decline

The Housing Act 1980 introduced the Right to Buy, allowing tenants to purchase their homes at a discount; over two million homes were sold, and restrictions on using receipts to build replacements shrank the stock. Much remaining stock was transferred to housing associations through large-scale voluntary transfers.

Present day

Social housing allocation is needs-based, with long waiting lists in many areas. The Grenfell Tower fire of 2017 led to major reforms of building safety and social housing regulation under the Social Housing (Regulation) Act 2023.
