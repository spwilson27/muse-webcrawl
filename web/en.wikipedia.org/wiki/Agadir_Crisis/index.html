Agadir Crisis

The Agadir Crisis, also called the Second Moroccan Crisis, was an international crisis in 1911 triggered by the deployment of French troops to Morocco and Germany's response: the dispatch of the gunboat SMS Panther to the Moroccan port of Agadir on 1 July 1911. The crisis briefly raised fears of a general European war.

Background

Under the Algeciras Conference of 1906, France had a recognized special role in Morocco, but Germany sought to uphold Moroccan independence and protect its commercial interests. In spring 1911, a revolt against Sultan Abdelhafid prompted France to send an expeditionary force to Fez, which Germany viewed as a step toward annexation.

The crisis

Germany sent Panther ostensibly to protect German nationals, demanding territorial compensation — the French Congo — in exchange for a free hand for France in Morocco. Britain, alarmed by the prospect of a German naval base on the Atlantic coast of Morocco, signalled support for France; Chancellor of the Exchequer David Lloyd George's Mansion House speech of 21 July warned that Britain would not be treated as of "no account". War scares roiled markets before diplomacy prevailed.

Settlement

By the Treaty of Fez settlement and the Franco-German convention of 4 November 1911, Germany recognized the French protectorate over Morocco and received strips of the French Congo (Neukamerun) in return. The crisis strengthened the Anglo-French Entente, hardened German resentment, and is generally seen as a step on the road to the First World War.
