Rope (film)

Directed by: Alfred Hitchcock

Screenplay by: Arthur Laurents

Based on: Rope (1929 play) by Patrick Hamilton

Starring: James Stewart, John Dall, Farley Granger

Released: 1948

Running time: 80 minutes

Rope is a 1948 American psychological thriller film directed by Alfred Hitchcock. Adapted from Patrick Hamilton's 1929 play of the same name, which was itself loosely inspired by the 1924 Leopold and Loeb murder case, it stars James Stewart, John Dall, and Farley Granger. The film is famous for being shot to appear as a single continuous take, and it was Hitchcock's first film in Technicolor.

Plot

Two brilliant young aesthetes, Brandon Shaw and Phillip Morgan, strangle their former classmate David Kentley with a length of rope in their Manhattan penthouse, motivated by a Nietzschean belief in their own superiority and a desire to commit the "perfect murder." They hide the body in a wooden chest and then host a dinner party for the victim's family and friends, serving food from atop the chest. Among the guests is their former school housemaster, Rupert Cadell, whose casual philosophical talk about murder had partly inspired them. Over the course of the evening, Rupert grows suspicious; after the party he returns, opens the chest, and confronts the killers, firing a gun out of the window to summon the police.

Production

Because Technicolor cameras of the era could hold only about ten minutes of film, Hitchcock shot the film in a series of long takes of up to ten minutes each, disguising the cuts by panning across a dark surface — such as a character's back — at each reel change. The entire film takes place in real time on a single set with movable walls that slid silently out of the way of the camera. The skyline backdrop outside the penthouse window changed gradually from daylight to night, with fibre-optic neon signs lighting up as dusk fell.

Reception

Initial reviews were mixed, and the film underperformed commercially; it was withdrawn from circulation for decades as one of the five "lost" Hitchcock films whose rights reverted to the director. On its re-release in 1984 it was reappraised, and it is now regarded as one of Hitchcock's most daring formal experiments, frequently appearing on lists of the greatest thriller films.
