Tax expenditure

A tax expenditure programme is government spending through the tax code.

A tax expenditure is a revenue loss attributable to a provision of the tax laws that allows a special exclusion, exemption, or deduction from gross income or that provides a special credit, a preferential rate of tax, or a deferral of tax liability. The concept was developed by tax scholar Stanley Surrey in the late 1960s and was enshrined in U.S. law by the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, which requires an annual tax expenditure budget.

Concept

Surrey's insight was that many tax provisions function as spending programmes administered through the tax system: a deduction for charitable contributions, for example, is economically equivalent to a matching grant. Measuring tax expenditures requires a "normal tax" baseline, which is itself contested—for instance, whether preferential capital gains rates count as a deviation. Estimates for the United States place total federal tax expenditures at well over a trillion dollars per year, comparable to discretionary spending.

Major examples

Among the largest U.S. tax expenditures are the exclusion of employer contributions for health insurance, preferential rates on capital gains and dividends, retirement saving deductions and exclusions, the mortgage interest deduction, and the exclusion of municipal bond interest. Many other countries publish tax expenditure statements, though methods differ.

Criticism and reform

Reformers argue that tax expenditures escape the scrutiny applied to appropriations, are often regressive because deductions are worth more to higher-bracket taxpayers (an "upside-down subsidy"), and complicate the code. Others defend them as efficient delivery mechanisms or as corrections to the tax base itself.
