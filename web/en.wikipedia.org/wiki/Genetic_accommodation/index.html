Genetic accommodation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genetic accommodation is a process by which a novel phenotype, initially produced in response to a novel environmental condition or mutation, is refined and canalized by natural selection acting on standing genetic variation. The concept was developed by the evolutionary biologist Mary Jane West-Eberhard in her book Developmental Plasticity and Evolution (2003) as a more general and precise successor to older ideas such as the Baldwin effect and Conrad Hal Waddington's concept of genetic assimilation.

Overview

According to the theory, when a population encounters a new environment, developmental plasticity allows some individuals to produce a new phenotypic variant. If that variant confers a fitness advantage, selection favors genetic changes that improve its reliability, efficiency, and regulation. Over generations, the trait may become more precisely adapted, may become expressed only under specific conditions (a polyphenism), or may lose its environmental dependence altogether — the special case called genetic assimilation.

Relationship to other concepts

Genetic accommodation differs from genetic assimilation in that assimilation refers only to the endpoint in which a trait becomes constitutively expressed. Accommodation is the broader process of adaptive refinement and can occur in either direction: selection can increase or decrease the environmental sensitivity of a trait. It also subsumes aspects of the Baldwin effect, which proposed that learned or plastic responses could guide genetic evolution.

Examples

Frequently cited examples include the evolution of horn polyphenism in dung beetles of the genus Onthophagus, diet-induced jaw morphologies in cichlid fishes, and environmentally cued seasonal forms in butterflies such as Bicyclus anynana. Laboratory selection experiments on tobacco hornworm larvae demonstrated that a heat-shock-induced color variant could be refined by selection into a stable polyphenic switch.
