Opechancanough (c. 1554–1646) was a tribal chief of the Powhatan Confederacy in what is now Virginia. A younger brother of Chief Powhatan (Wahunsenacawh) and uncle of Pocahontas, he became the effective leader of the Powhatan peoples after his brother's death in 1618 and is best known for organising two major coordinated attacks on the English colony at Jamestown, in 1622 and 1644.

Rise to power

Opechancanough was a respected war leader (a cockacoeske-era captain of the Pamunkey) long before he formally succeeded as paramount chief. English accounts recall that he was involved in the capture of Captain John Smith in 1607, an event followed, according to Smith's later writings, by the famous intervention of Pocahontas. After Wahunsenacawh's death, Opechancanough ruled in the name of his brother Opitchapam and became paramount chief in his own right by about 1630.

The uprisings of 1622 and 1644

On 22 March 1622, Opechancanough launched a carefully planned surprise attack — often called the Indian massacre of 1622 — in which Powhatan warriors killed about 347 English settlers, roughly a quarter of the colony. The attack followed years of English expansion onto tobacco land and the killing of the Powhatan war chief Nemattanew. The colony survived and retaliated with a decade of warfare.

In April 1644, when he was about ninety years old and so frail he had to be carried on a litter, Opechancanough struck again, killing some 400–500 settlers in a second uprising. This time the better-established colony recovered quickly; in 1646 Governor William Berkeley captured the aged chief, who was carried to Jamestown and died there — shot by a guard, according to contemporary accounts, while a prisoner.

Legacy

Opechancanough's resistance delayed but could not halt English colonisation. The treaties following his capture in 1646 established some of the first reservations in British America for the Pamunkey and other Powhatan tribes, whose descendants remain in Virginia today.
