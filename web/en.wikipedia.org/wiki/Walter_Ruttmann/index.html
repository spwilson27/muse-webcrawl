Walter Ruttmann

Born: 28 December 1887, Frankfurt am Main, German Empire
Died: 15 July 1941 (aged 53), Berlin, Nazi Germany
Occupation: Film director, painter
Known for: Berlin: Symphony of a Great City (1927)

Walter Ruttmann (28 December 1887 – 15 July 1941) was a German cinematographer and filmmaker, a pioneer of abstract film and best known for the 1927 documentary Berlin: Symphony of a Great City. His early animated "Opus" films were among the first abstract works shown publicly in cinema.

Early work

Ruttmann studied painting and graphic design and served in the First World War before turning to film. His hand-painted abstract shorts Lichtspiel: Opus I (1921) through Opus IV (1925) synchronised moving shapes to music, establishing him alongside Hans Richter and Viking Eggeling in the German absolute film movement. He also created the dream sequence for Fritz Lang's Die Nibelungen and worked in advertising, including the famous animated ads of the 1920s.

Berlin: Symphony of a Great City

Ruttmann's masterpiece documents a single day in Weimar-era Berlin, from dawn trains and empty streets through the rush of work, leisure, and nightlife, structured as a five-act symphony. Photographed by Karl Freund with hidden cameras, the film avoided individual protagonists in favour of the rhythms of the metropolis itself, influencing the "city symphony" genre worldwide. In 1928–29 Ruttmann also made Weekend, an experimental radio piece montaging recorded sound — an early landmark of audio art.

Later career

Ruttmann remained in Germany after 1933 and worked within the Nazi film industry on industrial and propaganda documentaries, a chapter that complicates his legacy. He died in July 1941 of an embolism following treatment for a wartime injury sustained while filming at the front.
