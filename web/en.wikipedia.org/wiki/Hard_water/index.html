Hard water

Hard water is water that has a high mineral content, in contrast with "soft water". Hard water is formed when water percolates through deposits of limestone, chalk, or gypsum, which are largely made up of calcium and magnesium carbonates, bicarbonates, and sulfates.

Drinking hard water may have moderate health benefits, since it can supply dietary calcium and magnesium. However, hard water can cause limescale deposits in water heaters, boilers, and pipes, reduce the effectiveness of soaps and detergents, and leave spots on dishes and fixtures. Water hardness is usually expressed in milligrams of calcium carbonate equivalent per litre, or in various national units such as degrees of hardness.

Formation

Rainwater and distilled water are soft, because they contain few dissolved ions. As rainwater falls through the atmosphere it absorbs carbon dioxide, becoming slightly acidic; when it passes through soil and rock containing calcium carbonate, it dissolves some of the mineral, carrying calcium and bicarbonate ions into groundwater. Water drawn from aquifers in limestone regions is therefore typically hard, whereas water from igneous rock regions tends to be soft.

Types of hardness

Temporary hardness

Temporary hardness is caused by dissolved calcium bicarbonate. It can be removed by boiling, which drives off carbon dioxide and precipitates calcium carbonate as scale:

Ca(HCO3)2 → CaCO3 + CO2 + H2O

Permanent hardness

Permanent hardness is caused by calcium and magnesium sulfates and chlorides, which do not precipitate on boiling. It can be reduced by chemical softening, such as the lime–soda process, or removed by ion-exchange water softeners, which replace calcium and magnesium ions with sodium or potassium ions.

Effects

Hard water reduces soap lathering because calcium ions react with soap to form an insoluble scum. In industrial settings, scale build-up reduces heat-transfer efficiency and can damage boilers. Some studies have suggested an association between water hardness and lower rates of cardiovascular disease, though the evidence is not conclusive. Many consumers notice differences in taste between hard and soft water.

Measurement

Hardness is commonly reported as mg/L (parts per million) of CaCO3 equivalent. Classifications vary, but water above roughly 120 mg/L is often described as hard, and above 180 mg/L as very hard. Other units include the German degree (°dH), the French degree (°fH), and grains per gallon.
