Classics

Greek and Latin texts form the core of the discipline.

Classics, also called classical studies or classical antiquity, is the academic study of the languages, literatures, histories, philosophies, and material cultures of ancient Greece and Rome. Its traditional core is the study of classical Greek and Latin, together with the canonical authors of those languages, including Homer, Plato, Sophocles, Virgil, Cicero, and Tacitus. The field also encompasses classical archaeology, ancient history, and the reception of antiquity in later cultures.

Scope

Classical antiquity is conventionally dated from the earliest Greek texts (about the 8th century BCE) to the fall of the Western Roman Empire (5th century CE), with late antiquity sometimes extending the span. Sub-disciplines include philology (the study of texts and languages), papyrology, epigraphy, numismatics, and archaeology of the Mediterranean world.

History of the discipline

The word derives from Latin classicus, "of the highest class" (of authors). Renaissance humanists revived classical learning and pedagogy; in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, classics became the centerpiece of elite education in Europe and North America, and the professionalized discipline of Altertumswissenschaft arose in German universities. Although its curricular dominance has declined since the mid-twentieth century, the field continues in universities worldwide and has expanded to include the study of slavery, gender, and the wider Mediterranean and Near Eastern context of the Greco-Roman world.

Education

Students of classics typically learn Attic Greek and classical Latin, read major texts in the original languages, and study textual criticism—the reconstruction of texts from their manuscript transmission. Degrees may be titled Literae Humaniores (at Oxford, "Greats"), Classics, or Classical Studies where language requirements are reduced.
