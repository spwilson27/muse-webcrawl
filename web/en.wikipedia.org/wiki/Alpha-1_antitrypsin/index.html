Alpha-1 antitrypsin (also α1-antitrypsin, A1AT, or AAT) is a protein belonging to the serpin superfamily of protease inhibitors. In humans it is encoded by the SERPINA1 gene. It is produced mainly in the liver and secreted into the bloodstream, where it is the most abundant serine protease inhibitor in plasma. Its principal function is to protect tissues — above all the lungs — from damage by enzymes released from inflammatory cells, particularly neutrophil elastase.

Function

Alpha-1 antitrypsin irreversibly inhibits a wide range of serine proteases, including neutrophil elastase, trypsin, chymotrypsin, thrombin, and plasmin. Like other serpins, it presents a reactive centre loop as a bait; when a target protease cleaves the loop, the serpin undergoes a dramatic conformational change that traps and inactivates the enzyme. The protein is also an acute-phase reactant: its plasma concentration rises several-fold during inflammation, infection, and pregnancy.

Deficiency

Inherited alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (A1AD) is a genetic condition, most often caused by the Z allele (Glu342Lys) of SERPINA1, in which misfolded protein polymerises within hepatocytes instead of being secreted. The result is both a toxic accumulation in the liver, which can cause hepatitis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, and a deficiency in the circulation, which leaves the lung unprotected against elastase and leads to early-onset panacinar emphysema and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Smoking greatly accelerates the lung disease. Treatment includes augmentation therapy with purified human A1AT given intravenously, bronchodilators, and, in advanced cases, lung or liver transplantation.

History and nomenclature

The protein was named for its migration in the alpha-1 band of serum protein electrophoresis and its ability to inhibit trypsin in vitro. The association of its deficiency with emphysema was described by Laurell and Eriksson in 1963, and the link to liver disease was recognised later in the decade. The gene's alleles are described by the "Pi" (protease inhibitor) system: PiMM is the normal phenotype, PiZZ the severe deficiency phenotype.
