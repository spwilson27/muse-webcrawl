Kakiemon

Kakiemon (Japanese: 柿右衛門様式, Kakiemon yōshiki) is a style of Japanese porcelain with overglaze enamel decoration, traditionally associated with the kilns of Arita in Saga Prefecture. It flourished from the 1670s onward and is named after Sakaida Kakiemon, the potter credited with developing the technique of enamel-on-glaze porcelain in Japan.

Characteristics

Kakiemon ware is distinguished by its brilliant palette of iron red, turquoise green, dark blue, yellow and aubergine enamels applied with delicate brushwork over a milky white porcelain body known as nigoshide. Designs typically feature asymmetrical compositions with flowering plants, birds, dragons and figures drawn from nature, leaving generous areas of undecorated white space. Common motifs include the "quail and millet" pattern, prunus and pine sprays, and the sanshu no ginomi combination of pine, plum and bamboo.

History

Porcelain production began at Arita around 1616 after Korean potters were brought to Japan. According to tradition, Sakaida Kakiemon I (1596–1666) learned the technique of akae (red enamel) decoration and passed the secrets to his descendants, who ran the Kakiemon kiln for fourteen generations. From the 1650s the Dutch East India Company exported Arita porcelain to Europe, where Kakiemon ware became highly prized.

Influence in Europe

European manufactories such as Meissen, Chantilly and Chelsea imitated Kakiemon designs extensively in the eighteenth century, and the style strongly influenced the development of European porcelain decoration and the broader taste for Chinoiserie and Japonisme.
