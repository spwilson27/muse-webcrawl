Harp seal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The harp seal (Pagophilus groenlandicus), also known as the saddleback seal or Greenland seal, is a species of earless seal native to the northernmost Atlantic Ocean and parts of the Arctic Ocean. Adults are distinguished by a black harp- or wishbone-shaped marking on their silvery-grey backs, which gives the species its name.

Life cycle

Each winter, three populations — in the White Sea, off Jan Mayen, and off Newfoundland and Labrador — gather on pack ice to give birth. Pups are born with long white fur (lanugo) that makes the "whitecoat" pups famous; the coat is shed after about two weeks. Mothers nurse the pups for roughly 12 days, during which the pups may triple their weight, before being abruptly weaned. Harp seals feed on fish such as capelin and Arctic cod and on invertebrates, and are preyed on by polar bears, killer whales and sharks.

Hunting and conservation

Harp seals have long been hunted commercially for their pelts, oil and meat, most prominently in Canada and Greenland. The hunt, and especially images of whitecoat pups, has been the focus of animal-welfare campaigns; imports of seal products are banned in the European Union, the United States and other markets. The species as a whole remains abundant and is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN.
