The Morris Canal was a 102.1-mile (164.3 km) canal across northern New Jersey in the United States, running from Phillipsburg on the Delaware River eastward to Newark and, after an 1836 extension, to Jersey City on the Hudson River. Built between 1825 and 1832, its principal cargo was anthracite coal from Pennsylvania's Lehigh Valley to the growing markets of Newark and New York City, together with iron ore, iron, and agricultural goods. It is famous as the first canal in the United States — and a pioneering example worldwide — to use inclined planes extensively to surmount major changes in elevation.

History

The canal was promoted by Morristown businessman George P. Macculloch, who is said to have conceived the idea in 1822 after visiting British canals. The Morris Canal and Banking Company was chartered in 1824, and construction began in 1825. The canal crossed some of the most difficult terrain of any American canal, climbing 760 feet from Newark Bay to its summit at Lake Hopatcong and descending 914 feet to the Delaware — a total elevation change of about 1,674 feet, far more than any lock-based canal of the era could handle cheaply.

Inclined planes

To overcome the elevation, engineer Ephraim Beach and others designed 23 inclined planes on which boats were floated onto submerged cradles and hauled, on rails laid up the slope, by water-powered machinery (later Scotch turbines). The planes, combined with 23 conventional locks, let the canal climb grades impossible for locks alone. Boats were built in two hinged sections so they could flex as they crested each plane. The largest plane, at Boonton, raised boats nearly 80 feet over a run of about 500 feet.

Decline and legacy

Traffic peaked in 1866 at nearly 900,000 tons, but railroads — which the canal company itself helped finance — steadily undercut it. The Lehigh Valley Railroad leased the canal in 1871, chiefly to control its rights of way and water rights. Traffic dwindled, and the State of New Jersey took over the canal in 1922 and formally abandoned it in 1924; much of the channel was drained and filled, and parts of its route were used for the Newark City Subway and roads. Surviving features include lock and plane sites, the canal's path across Lake Hopatcong, and preserved inclined plane No. 9 West at Port Warren. The Morris Canal Greenway is a developing trail project following its route.
