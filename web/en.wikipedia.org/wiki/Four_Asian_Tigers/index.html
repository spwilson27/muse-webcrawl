Four Asian Tigers

The Four Asian Tigers (also known as the Four Asian Dragons) are the economies of Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, and Taiwan. Between the early 1960s and the 1990s, they underwent rapid industrialisation and maintained exceptionally high growth rates of more than 7 percent a year, transforming from low-income economies into advanced, high-income ones.

Development model

The Tigers pursued export-oriented industrialisation, initially in light manufactures such as textiles and electronics assembly, and later moving into heavy industry, semiconductors, and financial services. Their governments invested heavily in education and infrastructure, maintained macroeconomic stability, and in varying degrees used industrial policy—subsidies, directed credit, and protection of infant industries—to promote strategic sectors. Economists have debated how much of the growth reflected state intervention versus market forces, and how much rested on factor accumulation (savings, investment, and expanding labour input) rather than productivity growth.

Later history

All four economies were affected by the 1997 Asian financial crisis, South Korea most severely, but they recovered quickly and today rank among the world's wealthiest economies by income per capita. Their experience influenced later developers in Southeast Asia, sometimes called the Tiger Cub economies.
