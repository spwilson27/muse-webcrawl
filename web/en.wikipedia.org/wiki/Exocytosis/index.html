Exocytosis

Exocytosis is a form of active transport and bulk transport in which a cell transports molecules (such as neurotransmitters and proteins) out of the cell. As an active transport mechanism, exocytosis requires the use of energy to transport material. It is the process by which a large amount of molecules are released; thus it is a form of bulk transport, and the opposite of endocytosis.

Mechanism

In exocytosis, membrane-bound secretory vesicles are carried to the cell membrane, where they dock and fuse at porosomes — cup-shaped structures in the membrane — releasing their contents into the extracellular environment. The fusion of the vesicle membrane with the plasma membrane is driven by SNARE proteins: v-SNAREs on the vesicle pair with t-SNAREs on the target membrane, pulling the two membranes together.

Types

Two broad pathways are recognised. In constitutive exocytosis, vesicles release their contents continuously, supplying membrane proteins and lipids to the cell surface. In regulated exocytosis, vesicles accumulate and fuse only in response to a signal — classically a rise in intracellular calcium — as in the release of neurotransmitters at synapses, insulin from pancreatic beta cells, and digestive enzymes from acinar cells.

Retrieval

After fusion, vesicle membrane is retrieved by endocytosis so that the cell's surface area and membrane composition remain balanced, and vesicles can be refilled and reused, particularly at nerve terminals.
