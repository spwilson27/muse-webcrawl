Melrose Abbey, formally the Abbey of St Mary, is a ruined Cistercian abbey in Melrose in the Scottish Borders. It was founded in 1136 by Cistercian monks at the request of King David I of Scotland, and was the first Cistercian monastery in Scotland. Its church is celebrated as one of the finest examples of Gothic architecture in Britain, noted for its elaborate stone carving.

History

The abbey stood near an earlier monastery founded in the 7th century by St Aidan and associated with St Cuthbert, who was educated there. Melrose's monks came from Rievaulx Abbey in Yorkshire. The abbey grew rich on the wool trade and Border lands granted by the Crown, but its position near the English border exposed it to repeated destruction: English armies burned it in 1322 and again, devastatingly, in 1385 under Richard II. Rebuilding produced the ornate Decorated Gothic church whose ruins survive.

In 1544–45 the abbey was again damaged during the "Rough Wooing". After the Scottish Reformation of 1560 the monastic community dwindled; the last monk died in 1590. The abbey's estates passed to secular commendators.

Burials and the heart of Robert the Bruce

Melrose was a royal burial place: Alexander II was buried there in 1249. Most famously, the embalmed heart of Robert the Bruce — which had been carried on crusade after his death in 1329 and returned to Scotland — was buried at Melrose. A lead casket believed to contain the heart was excavated in the chapter house in the 20th century and reburied in 1998 with a memorial marker.

The ruins

The surviving ruins include much of the church's east end, transepts and crossing tower, famed for carved details including a gargoyle-like pig playing the bagpipes. In 1822 Sir Walter Scott, who lived nearby at Abbotsford, helped fund repairs. The abbey also preserves the Commendator's House museum with Roman and medieval artefacts.
