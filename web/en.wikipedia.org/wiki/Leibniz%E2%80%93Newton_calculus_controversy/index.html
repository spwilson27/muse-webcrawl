Leibniz–Newton calculus controversy

The Leibniz–Newton calculus controversy was an argument between the mathematicians Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz over who had first invented calculus. The question was a major intellectual controversy of the early 18th century, beginning quietly in 1699 and erupting in full force in 1711, and it divided English-speaking mathematicians from continental European ones for many years.

Background

Newton developed his "method of fluxions" in 1665–1666 but published little of it for decades. Leibniz developed his differential and integral calculus independently in the mid-1670s and published first, in 1684, using the notation (dy/dx and the integral sign) that eventually became standard. Each man's supporters accused the other of plagiarism, fuelled by a correspondence network and by the affair of Nicolas Fatio de Duillier's insinuations against Leibniz.

The Royal Society report

In 1712 the Royal Society appointed a committee that produced a report, the Commercium Epistolicum, favouring Newton. It later emerged that Newton himself, as president of the Society, had heavily influenced — effectively drafted — the supposedly impartial verdict.

Modern assessment

Historians of mathematics now generally credit both men with independent invention: Newton arrived at the ideas first but published later; Leibniz published first and devised the superior notation. The dispute nonetheless slowed British mathematics, which clung to Newton's fluxional notation for a century.
