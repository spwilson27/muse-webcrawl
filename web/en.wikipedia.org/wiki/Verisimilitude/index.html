Verisimilitude

Verisimilitude (from Latin verisimilitudo, "likeness to the truth") is the appearance of being true or the quality of seeming to be true. In philosophy, and particularly in the philosophy of science, the term has been used to describe the degree to which a theory or proposition approximates reality. In literature, theatre and rhetoric it denotes the semblance of reality in fictional works — the extent to which a narrative rings true to life even though its events are invented.

Verisimilitude: the appearance of truth in art

Philosophy of science

The philosopher Karl Popper introduced verisimilitude as a technical concept in his work on the growth of scientific knowledge, most notably in Conjectures and Refutations (1963). Popper argued that science progresses toward the truth not by verifying theories but by formulating bold conjectures with greater truth-content than their predecessors and subjecting them to attempted falsification. A false theory could still be closer to the truth — have greater verisimilitude — than another false theory if it entails more true consequences and fewer false ones.

Popper's original formal definition compared the truth content and falsity content of competing theories, but later work by David Miller and Pavel Tichý showed in 1974 that the definition fails for false theories in general, since no false theory can be closer to the truth than another on Popper's ordering. This result prompted alternative accounts of truthlikeness developed by philosophers such as Ilkka Niiniluoto, Graham Oddie and Theo Kuipers, who treated verisimilitude as a matter of similarity between a theory's consequences and the actual structure of the world, often relativized to a language or conceptual framework.

Literature and narrative

In literary criticism, verisimilitude describes the internal consistency and plausibility that make a fictional world credible. Aristotle's Poetics already argued that a poet should prefer probable impossibilities to improbable possibilities: an impossible event that is convincing serves the work better than a possible one that is not. Samuel Taylor Coleridge's phrase "the willing suspension of disbelief" captures the reader's side of the bargain — audiences provisionally accept a work's premises so long as the work honours them.

Writers achieve verisimilitude through consistent detail, plausible motivation and adherence to the genre's conventions of realism. In genres such as science fiction and fantasy, verisimilitude depends less on correspondence with the actual world than on rigorous consistency with the fictional world's own rules, a principle sometimes called secondary belief.
