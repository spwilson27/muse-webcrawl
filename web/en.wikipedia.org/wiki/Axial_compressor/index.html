Axial compressor

TypeRotodynamic gas compressor
Flow directionParallel to the axis of rotation
Stage pressure ratioTypically 1.1 – 1.4 per stage
ApplicationsJet engines, gas turbines, industrial compression

An axial compressor is a gas compressor in which the gas flows parallel to the axis of rotation through successive rows of rotating blades (rotors) and stationary blades (stators), being compressed progressively at each stage. Axial compressors are an integral part of the design of large gas turbines and jet engines, where their high efficiency, high flow capacity, and small frontal area for a given mass flow make them the dominant compressor type.

Working principle

Each stage consists of a rotor row followed by a stator row. The rotor, driven by the turbine through a shaft, accelerates the gas and increases its kinetic energy and tangential (swirl) velocity; the stator vanes then act as diffusers, converting velocity into static pressure while removing swirl so the flow enters the next stage approximately axially. Because a single stage produces only a modest pressure rise — limited by blade loading and the risk of flow separation — large overall pressure ratios (30:1 or more in modern aero engines) require many stages, often arranged as separate low-, intermediate-, and high-pressure spools rotating at different speeds.

Design considerations

Designers must manage aerodynamic instability: at low flow rates compressors can enter rotating stall or surge, a violent flow reversal mitigated by variable stator vanes, variable inlet guide vanes, and bleed valves. Blade geometry is described using terms such as reaction — the share of static pressure rise occurring in the rotor — with 50% reaction a common design choice. Losses arise from profile drag, secondary flows, tip leakage, and shock waves in transonic stages.

History

Axial compressor concepts date to the 19th century; Sir Charles Parsons built axial machines for industrial use, and A. A. Griffith's 1926 aerodynamic theory of turbine design laid the theoretical foundation. Practical axial-flow jet compressors were developed in the 1930s–40s in Britain and Germany, notably in the Junkers Jumo 004 engine and in Metropolitan-Vickers designs, and they rapidly displaced centrifugal compressors in large engines.
