Mithra

Mithra (Avestan: Miθra; Persian: Mehr) is an ancient Iranian god of covenants, light, and oath, and the protector of truth. He is a major deity in Zoroastrianism, where he acts as a guardian of contracts and a judge of souls, and he is cognate with the Vedic god Mitra in Hinduism, reflecting a common Indo-Iranian origin.

In Zoroastrianism

In the Avesta, Mithra is celebrated in the Mihr Yasht, a hymn describing him as the wide-pastured lord who watches over covenants, rides in a chariot drawn by white horses, and precedes the rising sun. With Rashnu and Sraosha he forms a triad of judges who weigh the deeds of the dead at the Chinvat Bridge. In the later Zoroastrian calendar and tradition, the sixteenth day of each month and the seventh month (Mehr) are dedicated to him, and the festival of Mehregan celebrates his name.

Roman Mithras

In the Roman Empire, a mystery religion centred on a god called Mithras flourished from the 1st to the 4th century CE, especially among soldiers. Roman Mithras is depicted being born from a rock and slaying a bull (the tauroctony). The relationship between the Iranian Mithra and the Roman Mithras — whether the Roman cult derived directly from Iranian religion or was a largely new creation using Iranian names and imagery — remains debated among scholars.
