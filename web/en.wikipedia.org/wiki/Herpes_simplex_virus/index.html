Herpes simplex virus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Herpes simplex virus (HSV) refers to two closely related human herpesviruses, HSV-1 and HSV-2 (human alphaherpesviruses 1 and 2), which cause herpes simplex infections. HSV-1 is mainly associated with oral herpes (cold sores) and HSV-2 with genital herpes, although either can infect either site. Both are among the most common human infections worldwide: a majority of adults carry HSV-1.

Biology

HSV is a large, enveloped, double-stranded DNA virus of the family Herpesviridae. After primary infection of epithelial cells, the virus travels along sensory nerves to establish lifelong latency in neuronal ganglia — the trigeminal ganglia for HSV-1 and sacral ganglia for HSV-2 — periodically reactivating to cause recurrent lesions or asymptomatic shedding.

Disease and treatment

Most infections are mild or unnoticed, but HSV can cause painful blistering eruptions, keratitis (a leading infectious cause of blindness), neonatal herpes, and encephalitis, the most serious manifestation. There is no cure or licensed vaccine, but antiviral drugs — acyclovir, valacyclovir, and famciclovir — shorten outbreaks and, taken daily, suppress recurrences and transmission.
