Taxila (ancient Takṣaśilā, "city of cut stone") is an ancient city and archaeological site in Punjab, Pakistan, near the modern city of the same name. Strategically placed at the junction of routes linking India with Central Asia and the West, it was one of the great centres of learning and commerce of the ancient world and served as a capital of the Gandhara region.

History

Taxila was successively ruled by the Achaemenid Empire, Alexander the Great (who received its king's submission in 326 BCE), the Mauryan Empire — under whom it was a provincial capital associated with the emperor Ashoka — and then Indo-Greek, Indo-Scythian, Parthian and Kushan rulers. Under the Kushans it became a major centre of Buddhist art and scholarship; the apostle Thomas is legendarily said to have visited the court of Gondophares here. The city was destroyed by the White Huns in the 5th century CE and never recovered.

Learning and archaeology

Ancient sources describe Taxila's famed teachers attracting students from across India to study medicine, law, military science and the Vedas; the grammarian Pāṇini and the strategist Chanakya are traditionally associated with it. Sir John Marshall's excavations (1913–1934) revealed three successive city sites — Bhir Mound, Sirkap and Sirsukh — plus monasteries and stupas such as the Dharmarajika and Jaulian. The ruins were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1980.
