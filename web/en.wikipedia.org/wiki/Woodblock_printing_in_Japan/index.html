Woodblock printing in Japan (木版画, mokuhanga) is a technique best known for its use in the ukiyo-e artistic genre of single sheets, but it was also used for printing books in the same period. Although similar to woodcut in Western printmaking in some regards, the mokuhanga technique differs in that it uses water-based inks—as opposed to Western woodcut, which often uses oil-based inks. The Japanese water-based inks provide a wide range of vivid colours, glazes, and transparency.

History

Woodblock printing arrived in Japan from China by the 8th century, initially for reproducing Buddhist scriptures. Among the earliest surviving examples are the Hyakumantō Darani (One Million Pagoda Charms), printed around 770 CE under Empress Shōtoku. For centuries the technique was used mainly for texts, produced in temple and commercial workshops.

During the Edo period (1603–1868), woodblock prints developed into a major commercial art form. The publisher commissioned an artist to design an image; a carver cut a separate block for the key lines and for each colour; a printer then printed the sheets by hand. This division of labour allowed mass production of inexpensive pictures of kabuki actors, beautiful women, landscapes, and scenes from literature — the "pictures of the floating world" known as ukiyo-e.

Technique

The artist's design is pasted face-down on a block of cherry wood and carved so that the drawing's lines remain in relief. Registration marks (kentō) carved into each block align the paper for successive colour impressions. The printer applies water-based pigment mixed with rice paste to the block, lays dampened washi paper over it, and rubs the back with a flat pad called a baren to transfer the ink. Graduated colour effects (bokashi) are achieved by wiping pigment across the block by hand.

Notable artists and works

Hishikawa Moronobu pioneered the single-sheet print in the late 17th century. Kitagawa Utamaro became famous for close-up portraits of women (ōkubi-e), and Tōshūsai Sharaku for dramatic actor portraits. In the 19th century, Katsushika Hokusai's Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji (c. 1830–32), including The Great Wave off Kanagawa, and Utagawa Hiroshige's Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō and One Hundred Famous Views of Edo defined the landscape print. Utagawa Kuniyoshi was celebrated for warrior prints and fantastical subjects.

Later developments and influence

After the Meiji Restoration, Western printing technology displaced commercial woodblock production. Two revival movements emerged: shin-hanga ("new prints"), which retained the traditional collaborative workshop system, and sōsaku-hanga ("creative prints"), in which the artist designed, carved, and printed the work alone. Japanese prints exhibited in Europe profoundly influenced the Impressionists and Post-Impressionists, a phenomenon known as Japonisme. Mokuhanga continues to be practised by contemporary artists in Japan and internationally, valued for its non-toxic materials and expressive colour.
