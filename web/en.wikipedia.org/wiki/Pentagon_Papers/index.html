Pentagon Papers

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Pentagon Papers, officially the Report of the Office of the Secretary of Defense Vietnam Task Force, are a classified US Department of Defense history of the United States' political and military involvement in Vietnam from 1945 to 1968 (47 volumes, completed 1969), commissioned by Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara. In 1971 the analyst Daniel Ellsberg — a contributor who had turned against the war — photocopied the study and leaked it, first to the New York Times (published from 13 June 1971) and then to the Washington Post and other papers.

The Nixon administration sought prior restraint; in New York Times Co. v. United States (1971) the Supreme Court, 6–3 per curiam, refused to enjoin publication — a landmark of press freedom — while leaving open post-publication prosecution (Ellsberg's Espionage Act trial ended in dismissal for government misconduct in 1973, the break-in at his psychiatrist's office by the White House “Plumbers” having poisoned the case and fed the Watergate scandal). The Papers documented that successive administrations had privately doubted the war's winnability while publicly claiming progress — a credibility wound that reshaped American trust in government. Ellsberg became the archetype of the national-security whistleblower, a lineage later extended by the 2013 Snowden disclosures.
