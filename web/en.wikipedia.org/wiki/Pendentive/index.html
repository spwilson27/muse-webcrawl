In architecture, a pendentive is a constructive device permitting the placing of a circular dome over a square room or of an elliptical dome over a rectangular room. The pendentives are triangular segments of a sphere that fill the upper corners of the room and serve as the transition from the square substructure to the circular base of the dome. By transferring the weight of the dome to massive piers at the four corners, pendentives allow domes to crown square bays without the visual or structural awkwardness of earlier solutions.

Geometry and structure

Geometrically, a pendentive is part of the surface of a sphere whose centre lies at the centre of the square bay and whose radius is half the diagonal of the square. Four arches are raised on the sides of the square; the pendentives fill the spaces between the arches and support a horizontal circular ring (cornice) on which the dome sits. The load of the dome is thus channelled down through the pendentives and arches to the four corner piers.

History

Experiments with pendentive-like forms appear in Roman and early Byzantine architecture, but the first fully developed use on a monumental scale is the Hagia Sophia in Constantinople (built 532–537) by Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus, whose vast dome rests on four great pendentives. The technique became a hallmark of Byzantine church architecture, spread to Islamic architecture (notably Ottoman mosques, where Sinan refined it), and was taken up in Renaissance, Baroque and Revival architecture in the West.

Comparison with the squinch

The main alternative device is the squinch, an arch or series of arches thrown across the corner of the square. Squinches were used earlier, especially in Sasanian and later Islamic and Romanesque architecture. Pendentives give a smoother, more unified visual transition and a complete circular base for the dome, whereas squinches produce an octagonal transition zone.
