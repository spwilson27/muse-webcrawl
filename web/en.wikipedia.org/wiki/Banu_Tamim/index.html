Banu Tamim

The Banu Tamim (Arabic: بنو تميم) is one of the largest and most historically prominent Arab tribes. In the pre-Islamic period its clans inhabited eastern Arabia, the Najd and southern Iraq, controlling caravan routes and pastures. The tribe converted to Islam in the lifetime of Muhammad (a delegation visited Medina in 630) and went on to play a major role in the early Islamic conquests of Persia; members of Tamim later became numerous in Basra, Khorasan and across the Muslim world. Today Tamimis are found throughout the Arabian Peninsula and beyond; the Al Thani ruling family of Qatar descends from the Banu Tamim.

Origins

Genealogists trace the tribe to Tamim ibn Murr, of the Mudar group of northern (Adnanite) Arabs. Its major branches included the clans of Amr ibn Tamim, Sa'd ibn Zayd Manat and Hanzala. The prophetess Sajah, a rival of early Islam during the Ridda wars, was of Tamimi-Christian background from the Taghlib milieu of Iraq.

Islamic era

After accepting Islam, Tamimi contingents fought in the conquests of Iraq and Persia under the Rashidun and Umayyad caliphates. The tribe supplied famous commanders such as al-Qa'qa' ibn Amr, renowned poets including Jarir, and the Kharijite and Ibadi movements drew heavily on Tamimi adherents in Basra and Oman. In the 18th century the religious reformer Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, founder of the Wahhabi movement, was himself a Tamimi of the Najd.

Modern distribution

Banu Tamim families remain prominent in Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Iraq, Jordan, Palestine and North Africa. The Al Thani of Qatar, the Al Buainain and numerous Najdi families claim Tamimi descent.
