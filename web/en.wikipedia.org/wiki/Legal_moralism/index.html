Legal moralism

Legal moralism is the view in legal and political philosophy that the law may legitimately be used to prohibit or regulate conduct simply because that conduct is immoral, even when it does not harm others. On this view, the immorality of an act is itself a sufficient justification for criminalizing it.

Background

Legal moralism is typically contrasted with the harm principle associated with John Stuart Mill, who argued in On Liberty (1859) that the only purpose for which power can rightfully be exercised over an individual against their will is to prevent harm to others. Legal moralism rejects that restriction, holding that society may enforce shared morality through the criminal law.

The Hart–Devlin debate

The position is most famously associated with the English judge Patrick Devlin, whose 1959 Maccabaean Lecture, published as The Enforcement of Morals, argued that a society is bound together by a shared morality and may use the law to defend that morality, much as it defends itself against treason. Devlin was responding to the Wolfenden Report's recommendation that homosexual acts between consenting adults in private should not be a crime. The legal philosopher H. L. A. Hart replied in Law, Liberty and Morality (1963), defending a liberal position close to Mill's and criticizing Devlin's arguments. The exchange, known as the Hart–Devlin debate, became one of the central debates of twentieth-century jurisprudence.

Varieties and criticism

Legal moralism is related to, but distinct from, legal paternalism, which justifies coercion for the subject's own good. Critics contend that mere majority disapproval is not a defensible ground for coercion, that moral views shift over time, and that enforcing morality can license oppression of minorities. Defenders respond that all law embodies moral judgments and that the question is only which moral norms may properly be enforced.
