Exner equation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Exner equation is a statement of the conservation of mass that governs how the elevation of a sediment bed — such as the bed of a river or the bedforms of a desert or seabed — changes over time as sediment is transported by a flow. It is named after the Austrian meteorologist and geomorphologist Felix Maria von Exner, who introduced it in the 1920s in his studies of river-bed deformation and dune movement.

Formulation

In its one-dimensional form the equation reads:

(1 − λp) ∂η/∂t = − ∂qs/∂x

where η is the bed elevation, t is time, x is the downstream coordinate, λp is the porosity of the bed sediment, and qs is the volumetric sediment transport rate per unit width. In words: the bed rises (aggrades) where the sediment flux decreases downstream, and falls (degrades or erodes) where the flux increases downstream, with the factor (1 − λp) converting between solid volume and the bulk volume of the porous bed.

Applications

The Exner equation is the foundation of morphodynamic modelling. Coupled with flow equations (such as the shallow-water equations) and a sediment transport formula — bed-load relations such as the Meyer-Peter and Müller formula, or total-load relations — it predicts the migration of dunes and bars, the scour around bridge piers, the infilling of reservoirs, the response of channels to dams and gravel extraction, and the evolution of beaches and deltas. Multi-dimensional forms include separate terms for cross-stream flux, and extensions account for multiple grain-size fractions, sorting and armouring of the bed surface.
