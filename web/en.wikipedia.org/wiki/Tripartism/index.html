Tripartism

Tripartism is the principle and practice of cooperation and negotiation between three parties — governments, employers' organisations, and workers' organisations (trade unions) — in making decisions and policies, especially on labour and social matters. It is the founding governance principle of the International Labour Organization (ILO), one of the specialized agencies of the United Nations.

Tripartism in the ILO

Founded in 1919, the ILO gives each member state's delegation two government, one employer and one worker delegate, who vote independently. Its governing body and the annual International Labour Conference adopt conventions and recommendations through this tripartite structure — a unique arrangement among international organisations. ILO Convention No. 144 (1976) requires tripartite consultations on international labour standards.

National practice and theory

At national level, tripartism underlies social dialogue, collective bargaining frameworks, minimum-wage boards and economic and social councils, notably in European corporatist and Nordic models. Supporters argue it produces legitimate, workable labour standards supporting decent work; critics note it may exclude informal workers and non-union voices.
