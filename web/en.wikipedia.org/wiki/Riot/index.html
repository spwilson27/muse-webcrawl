A riot is a form of civil disorder commonly characterised by a group lashing out in a violent public disturbance against authority, property, or people. Riots typically involve the destruction of property, public or private, and may include arson, looting, and physical confrontations with police or rival groups. The term is used with varying precision: crowds, protests, and demonstrations that turn violent may be described as riots, though participants and authorities often contest the label because of its legal and moral connotations.

Causes and dynamics

Riots have arisen from grievances over food prices and shortages ("bread riots"), racial and ethnic tensions, police actions, sporting events, religious conflict, political repression, and celebrations that escalate. Social scientists describe how gatherings develop through trigger incidents, rumour, crowd contagion, and escalating exchanges with police ("flashpoints"). Classic crowd-psychology accounts (Le Bon's "mad mob") have been criticised by modern research showing that crowd members usually act according to shared social identities and perceived legitimacy rather than mindless irrationality. Sociologist Charles Tilly treated riots as part of the historical repertoire of collective action.

Legal aspects

At English common law, riot required three or more persons assembled with a common violent purpose; the Riot Act 1714 empowered magistrates to order dispersal "in the name of the King" — the origin of the phrase "reading the Riot Act" — and remained on the books until 1973. Modern statutes include the UK's Public Order Act 1986 (riot requires 12 or more persons using or threatening unlawful violence) and the U.S. federal Anti-Riot Act, together with state laws and offences such as incitement to riot. Authorities respond with crowd-control tactics ranging from negotiated management to riot police using shields, batons, tear gas, and water cannon; heavy-handed responses have themselves triggered further unrest, as with the 1968 Chicago convention clashes and many subsequent cases.

Notable historical examples

Famous riots include the Nika riots in Constantinople (532), the Gordon Riots in London (1780), the New York City draft riots (1863), race riots in Tulsa (1921) and Detroit (1943, 1967), the disturbances following the assassination of Martin Luther King Jr. (1968), the 1992 Los Angeles riots, the 2005 French banlieue unrest, the 2011 England riots, and the unrest of 2020 following the murder of George Floyd. Each case is studied for its precipitating incident, the role of media, and the policy debates it ignited.
