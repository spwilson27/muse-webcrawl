Trover

Trover is a form of action in common law for the recovery of damages for the wrongful conversion of personal property (chattels) to the defendant's own use. The name comes from the French trouver, "to find": the original fiction of the action was that the plaintiff had lost goods which the defendant had found and refused to return.

History and elements

The action of trover developed in 16th- and 17th-century England out of actions of detinue and trespass, and by dispensing with the fiction of loss it became the general remedy for conversion. The plaintiff had to show a right to possession of the goods, and a conversion — an act of dominion by the defendant inconsistent with the plaintiff's rights, such as taking, selling, destroying, or wrongfully refusing to return the goods. Unlike detinue, trover did not seek return of the specific goods but damages equal to their full value, effectively compelling a sale.

Modern law

In England and Wales the old forms of action were abolished in the 19th century, and the substance of trover survives in the statutory tort of conversion under the Torts (Interference with Goods) Act 1977. In the United States, "trover" remains a recognized label for conversion claims in some jurisdictions.
