Belém (Lisbon)

Belém (Portuguese for Bethlehem) is a district (freguesia) in western Lisbon, Portugal, on the northern bank of the Tagus estuary. It is famed for monuments of the Portuguese Age of Discovery: from Belém's harbour, Vasco da Gama set sail for India in 1497. The Belém Tower and the Jerónimos Monastery, masterpieces of the Manueline style, are UNESCO World Heritage Sites, and the district is also the home of the presidential palace and of the celebrated pastéis de Belém custard tarts.

History

Originally the port of Restelo, Belém grew in importance as Lisbon's maritime gateway in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. King Manuel I founded the Jerónimos Monastery in 1501 to commemorate da Gama's voyage, and built the Belém Tower (1514–1520) as part of the Tagus defences. After surviving the 1755 earthquake relatively intact — the royal family was in residence at Belém that day and escaped — the district became an aristocratic quarter. It was a separate municipality from 1885 until its reincorporation into Lisbon in the early twentieth century.

Landmarks

Chief sights include the Jerónimos Monastery and its Maritime Museum and National Archaeology Museum; the Belém Tower; the Monument to the Discoveries (Padrão dos Descobrimentos, 1960); the Belém Cultural Centre (CCB), opened in 1993; the MAAT museum of art, architecture and technology (2016); the Berardo Collection museum of modern art; and the National Coach Museum. The Pastéis de Belém bakery beside the monastery has made custard tarts to a secret recipe since 1837, drawing long queues daily. The Belém Palace is the official residence of the President of Portugal.
