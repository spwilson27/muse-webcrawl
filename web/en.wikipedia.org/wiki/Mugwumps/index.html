The Mugwumps were Republican political activists in the United States who, in the presidential election of 1884, refused to support their party's nominee, James G. Blaine of Maine, and instead backed the Democratic candidate, Grover Cleveland of New York. Preoccupied with political reform and personal morality, the Mugwumps judged Blaine corrupt — he was tainted by scandals involving railway favours — and Cleveland, the reform governor of New York, comparatively clean. Their defection, concentrated among educated professionals in New York and New England, was widely credited with swinging New York State, and with it the election, to Cleveland.

The name

The label was popularised by the New York Sun editor Charles A. Dana. "Mugwump" derives from an Algonquian word, mugquomp or "great chief", used in John Eliot's 17th-century translation of the Bible. The term was applied mockingly: Republican loyalists joked that a mugwump was a bird sitting on a fence with its "mug" on one side and its "wump" on the other — a fence-sitter too fastidious for ordinary party politics.

Beliefs and legacy

The Mugwumps championed civil service reform (the merit system embodied in the Pendleton Act of 1883), honest government, free trade and lower tariffs, and the gold standard. They never organised as a separate party, but as a type — the independent, reform-minded voter who prizes character and competence over party loyalty — the mugwump has remained a recurring figure in American politics, and the word survives as a term for political independents and bolters.
