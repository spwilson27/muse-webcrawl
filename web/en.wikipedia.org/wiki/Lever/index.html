Lever

A lever is a simple machine consisting of a beam or rigid rod pivoted at a fixed hinge, or fulcrum. A lever is a rigid body capable of rotating on a fixed point. On the basis of the locations of fulcrum, load, and effort, the lever is divided into three types: first class, second class, and third class. Early use of the lever is evidenced in ancient Egypt and Greece; the principle was described mathematically by Archimedes in the 3rd century BC.

Principle

A lever amplifies an input force to provide a greater output force; the ratio of output to input force is the mechanical advantage. For an ideal lever, mechanical advantage equals the ratio of the distances from the fulcrum to the points of application of effort and load: a force applied at a greater distance from the fulcrum trades distance moved for increased force at the load. In equilibrium, the torques balance: F1 × d1 = F2 × d2, the law of the lever proved by Archimedes in On the Equilibrium of Planes.

Classes

Class 1

The fulcrum lies between the effort and the load: examples include the seesaw, the crowbar, and a pair of scissors (two class-1 levers).

Class 2

The load lies between the fulcrum and the effort, always giving a mechanical advantage greater than one: examples include the wheelbarrow, the nutcracker, and the bottle opener.

Class 3

The effort lies between the fulcrum and the load, trading force for range and speed of movement: examples include tweezers, the human forearm (elbow as fulcrum), and a fishing rod.

History

The earliest evidence of lever mechanisms dates to the ancient Near East and Egypt, where they were used in balance scales and in the shadouf for raising water. Archimedes formalised the principle and is famously reported by Pappus to have said, "Give me a place to stand, and I shall move the Earth." Leonardo da Vinci and Galileo later studied levers, and the lever remains one of the six classical simple machines defined by Renaissance scientists.
