Abū Tamīm Maʿad al-Mustanṣir bi-llāh (1029–1094) was the eighth Fatimid caliph, reigning in Egypt from 1036 to 1094 — at nearly 58 years, the longest reign of any caliph in Islamic history. He succeeded his father al-Zahir as a child of seven. The early decades of his reign, governed by his mother and the vizier al-Jarjara'i, continued Fatimid prosperity, but his later reign saw military factionalism, famine and the loss of Syria and the Hejaz.

Reign

The caliph's long rule divides into a prosperous first period and a crisis known as the al-shidda al-ʿuẓmā ("great calamity") of roughly 1065–1073, when low Nile floods, famine in Cairo and fighting between Turkish and Sudanese regiments nearly destroyed the state. Al-Mustansir summoned the Armenian general Badr al-Jamali, who restored order in 1074 and effectively ruled as vizier, founding a line of military viziers.

Succession and schism

On his death in 1094, the vizier al-Afdal installed his younger son al-Musta'li as caliph, bypassing the elder son Nizar, whose supporters — the Nizari Ismailis, led in Persia by Hassan-i Sabbah — rejected the succession. The resulting Nizari–Musta'li schism permanently divided Ismaili Islam.

Al-Mustansir's name is borne by the Al-Mustansiriya University in Baghdad (founded by the later Abbasid caliph al-Mustansir of Baghdad, a namesake) and by Cairo landmarks of his era such as the gates rebuilt by Badr al-Jamali.
