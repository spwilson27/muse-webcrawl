Finn Erling Kydland (born 1 December 1943) is a Norwegian economist known for his contributions to macroeconomics, in particular the theory of real business cycles and the time inconsistency of economic policy. Together with Edward C. Prescott he received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2004 "for their contributions to dynamic macroeconomics: the time consistency of economic policy and the driving forces behind business cycles".

Life and career

Kydland grew up on a farm near Ålgård in southwestern Norway. He studied at the Norwegian School of Economics (NHH), graduating in economics in 1968, and earned his PhD from Carnegie Mellon University in 1973 under Edward Prescott and Robert Lucas Jr. After teaching at NHH, he joined Carnegie Mellon, and later held the Henley Professorship of Economics at the University of California, Santa Barbara, with affiliations including the Hoover Institution and several central banks.

Contributions

In their 1977 paper "Rules Rather than Discretion: The Inconsistency of Optimal Plans", Kydland and Prescott showed that a policy that is optimal when announced may cease to be optimal later, tempting policymakers to renege — the problem of time inconsistency. Rational private agents anticipate this, so discretionary policy can produce worse outcomes (such as higher inflation with no output gain) than commitment to a fixed rule. The paper transformed thinking about central bank independence and rules-based monetary policy. Their 1982 paper "Time to Build and Aggregate Fluctuations" launched real business-cycle theory: it showed that a calibrated dynamic general-equilibrium model driven by productivity (technology) shocks could reproduce much of the observed co-movement of output, consumption, investment, and hours worked, founding the quantitative dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) approach that dominates modern macroeconomics.
