The Wash

LocationEast coast of England (Norfolk and Lincolnshire)
TypeSquare-mouthed estuary / bay
RiversWitham, Welland, Nene, Great Ouse

The Wash is a roughly square bay and estuary on the east coast of England, between Norfolk and Lincolnshire, where four rivers — the Witham, Welland, Nene and Great Ouse — flow into the North Sea. It is one of Britain's most important nature conservation areas, noted for its vast intertidal mudflats and salt marshes.

Geography

At low tide much of the Wash is exposed sand and mud crossed by shifting channels; at high tide it becomes a wide, shallow sea. The embayment is the remnant of a much larger bay eroded after the last Ice Age, and its margins have been progressively reclaimed for farmland since Roman times — the Fens to the south were largely won from marsh and shallow water in the seventeenth century and after.

Wildlife and legend

The mudflats support enormous numbers of wading birds and wildfowl and a notable colony of common seals; much of the area is protected as a National Nature Reserve and Ramsar site. According to tradition, King John lost part of his baggage train — popularly, the Crown Jewels — to the incoming tide in the Wash in 1216, shortly before his death. The shifting channels also made the Wash treacherous for shipping; the port of Boston was once among England's most important.
