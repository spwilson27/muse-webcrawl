Matthias Jakob Schleiden

Matthias Jakob Schleiden
Born: 5 April 1804, Hamburg, German Confederation
Died: 23 June 1881, Frankfurt am Main, German Empire
Nationality: German
Known for: Co-founding cell theory

Matthias Jakob Schleiden (5 April 1804 – 23 June 1881) was a German botanist who, with Theodor Schwann, co-founded cell theory. In 1838 Schleiden published "Contributions to Phytogenesis," in which he concluded from microscopic study that all plants are composed of cells and that the cell is the basic unit of plant life.

Life and work

Schleiden studied law at Heidelberg and practiced as a lawyer in Hamburg, but after a period of depression and a suicide attempt he turned to botany, studying under his uncle Johann Horkel in Berlin and at Göttingen. He became a professor of botany at the University of Jena, where he championed the use of the microscope and inductive methods in botany, attacking speculative nature-philosophy in works such as Principles of Scientific Botany (1842–1843), one of the first modern botany textbooks.

Cell theory

Schleiden's dinner-table conversation with zoologist Theodor Schwann is credited with prompting Schwann to extend the cell idea to animals, resulting in the cell theory of 1838–1839, a founding unifying principle of biology. Schleiden mistakenly believed that new cells arise by crystallization from a structureless substance around the nucleus (the "cytoblast"), an idea later corrected by Robert Remak and Rudolf Virchow, who established that cells arise only from pre-existing cells (omnis cellula e cellula). He also correctly recognized the importance of the cell nucleus, described by Robert Brown in 1831.

Later life

Schleiden left Jena after disputes with colleagues and worked in Dresden and as a professor at Dorpat (Tartu) in the Russian Empire, writing popular lectures on science and the history of knowledge. He died in Frankfurt am Main in 1881.
