Bathypelagic zone

The bathypelagic zone, also known as the bathyal zone or midnight zone, is the part of the open ocean that extends from a depth of 1,000 to 4,000 metres (3,300 to 13,100 ft) below the ocean surface. It lies between the mesopelagic zone above and the abyssopelagic zone below. Because no sunlight reaches this depth, the zone is in perpetual darkness except for light produced by the organisms themselves through bioluminescence.

Physical characteristics

Temperatures in the bathypelagic zone average around 4 °C and remain nearly constant. Hydrostatic pressure ranges from about 100 to 400 atmospheres. With no photosynthesis possible, primary production within the zone is negligible; the ecosystem depends on organic matter sinking from above — so-called marine snow — supplemented near hydrothermal vents by chemosynthetic communities.

Ecology

Despite the harsh conditions, the bathypelagic zone supports a diverse fauna. Animals here are typically black or red in colouration (red appears black at depth) and many are bioluminescent. Characteristic inhabitants include anglerfish, gulper eels, vampire squid, giant squid, rattails (grenadiers), and the frilled shark. Many species have slow metabolisms, large mouths and expandable stomachs to exploit rare meals. Some organisms, such as certain squid and fish, migrate upward at night into the mesopelagic zone to feed.

Exploration

The zone can only be studied with deep submersibles, remotely operated vehicles and specialised sampling equipment. The bathyscaphe Trieste passed through the bathypelagic zone on its record descent to the Challenger Deep in 1960. Study of the zone is important for understanding the ocean's biological carbon pump, since bathypelagic microbes and zooplankton process much of the organic carbon sinking from surface waters.
