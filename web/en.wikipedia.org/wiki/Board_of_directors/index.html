Board of directors

A board of directors is a group of individuals elected or appointed to jointly oversee the activities of a company or organisation. The board is the governing body of a corporation: it appoints and supervises the chief executive, sets strategy and major policies, approves large transactions and financial statements, and owes fiduciary duties to the company and its shareholders.

Structure

Boards typically mix executive directors (senior managers, such as the CEO) and non-executive directors, some of whom are independent of management. A chair leads the board; in some countries the chair and CEO roles are combined, while governance codes elsewhere recommend separation. Committees of the board handle audit, remuneration, and nominations. Unitary boards (as in the UK and US) contrast with two-tier systems in countries such as Germany, where a supervisory board oversees a separate management board.

Duties and liability

Directors generally owe a duty of care — to act as a reasonably prudent person — and a duty of loyalty to put the company's interests first. Failures can lead to personal liability; reforms such as the Sarbanes–Oxley Act (2002) in the US and the UK Corporate Governance Code tightened requirements after scandals including Enron, where board oversight famously failed.

Selection and debates

Shareholders formally elect directors at annual general meetings, though in practice nominees are proposed by the board itself. Ongoing debates concern board diversity, independence, size, staggered terms, activist investors, and the role of boards in managing climate and other long-term risks.
