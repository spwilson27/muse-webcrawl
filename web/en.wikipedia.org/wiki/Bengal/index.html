Bengal

Bengal (Bengali: বঙ্গ, Bôṅgo) is a historical and geographical region in the eastern part of the Indian subcontinent, at the apex of the Bay of Bengal. It is today politically divided between the Indian state of West Bengal and the independent nation of Bangladesh. The region is characterised by the vast Ganges–Brahmaputra delta, the largest river delta in the world, and by a dense network of rivers that has shaped its agriculture, settlement and culture for millennia.

Geography

Most of Bengal lies in the low-lying Ganges–Brahmaputra delta, formed by the confluence of the Ganges, Brahmaputra and Meghna rivers and their tributaries. The delta covers roughly 105,000 km² and is one of the most fertile regions on Earth, though it is also prone to severe flooding and cyclones from the Bay of Bengal. The Sundarbans, the world's largest mangrove forest and habitat of the Bengal tiger, stretches along the coast.

History

Bengal was home to early kingdoms such as Vanga, Gangaridai and the Gauda Kingdom, and later formed part of the Maurya, Gupta and Pala empires. The Pala dynasty (8th–12th centuries) made Bengal a centre of Mahayana Buddhism. From the 13th century the region came under Muslim rule, first through the Bengal Sultanate and then as a wealthy province (Subah) of the Mughal Empire, famed for its muslin textiles and rice.

The British East India Company's victory at the Battle of Plassey in 1757 began British rule in India from Bengal. Calcutta (now Kolkata) became the capital of British India until 1911. The region was partitioned in 1905 by Lord Curzon, a decision annulled in 1911, and again in 1947 at independence, when Muslim-majority East Bengal became East Pakistan (later Bangladesh) and Hindu-majority West Bengal joined India.

Culture

Bengal is renowned for its literature, music and intellectual life, often called the cultural capital of India. The Bengali language, spoken by more than 230 million people, produced figures such as Rabindranath Tagore, the first non-European Nobel laureate in Literature. Baul folk music, Durga Puja festivities, and a distinctive cuisine centred on rice and fish define the region's identity. The Bengali language movement of 1952 in Dhaka inspired UNESCO's International Mother Language Day.

Economy

Agriculture dominates, with rice, jute, tea and fish the principal products. Bengal was historically the world's leading jute producer. Today the Bangladeshi side hosts a globally significant garment industry, while West Bengal's economy centres on Kolkata's services, manufacturing and trade.
