Civil partnership

A civil partnership is a legally recognised arrangement similar to marriage, created primarily as a means for same-sex couples to obtain legal recognition of their relationship when marriage was not available to them. Civil partnerships may also be available to opposite-sex couples in some jurisdictions. Partners in a civil partnership typically acquire most of the same rights and obligations as married couples, including inheritance, tax, pension and next-of-kin rights, though terminology and some details may differ.

Civil partnerships confer legal recognition similar to marriage.

History

Denmark introduced the first registered partnership scheme for same-sex couples in 1989, followed by other Scandinavian countries and, in 1999, France's civil solidarity pact (PaCS), which is open to both same-sex and opposite-sex couples. The United Kingdom's Civil Partnership Act 2004 came into force in December 2005. In the United States, similar statuses include civil unions (first in Vermont, 2000) and domestic partnerships.

After marriage equality

Following the legalisation of same-sex marriage in many countries, the role of civil partnerships changed. Some jurisdictions abolished new partnerships or converted existing ones into marriages; others retained them as an alternative. In England and Wales, after the Supreme Court's decision in Steinfeld (2018), civil partnerships were extended to opposite-sex couples from the end of 2019. In Scotland, mixed-sex civil partnerships became available in 2021.

Differences from marriage

Differences vary by jurisdiction but historically included the absence of a required spoken vow or religious content in the formation ceremony, different terms for dissolution, and in some places distinctions in pension or parental rights, many of which have been equalised by later legislation.
