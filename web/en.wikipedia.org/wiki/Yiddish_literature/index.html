Yiddish literature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Yiddish literature encompasses all literature written in the Yiddish language, the historic vernacular of Ashkenazi Jews. It spans roughly a thousand years, from medieval glosses and epics through the classic prose of the 19th century, the poetry and theatre of the early 20th century, and writing produced after the Holocaust.

Old Yiddish literature

The earliest Yiddish texts date from the 12th–13th centuries, including glosses on Hebrew texts and rhymed epics adapted from European romance, such as the Dukus Horant preserved in the Cambridge Codex of 1382. From the 16th century, printed books flourished: the Bovo Bukh of Elia Levita (1541) and, above all, devotional works for women such as the Tsene-rene (c. 1590s), a paraphrase of the Bible and commentaries that was reprinted for centuries. Hasidic storytelling, including the tales of Nachman of Breslov (1810s), added a rich vein of religious narrative.

The classical era

Modern Yiddish literature is conventionally dated from the 1860s. Its three classic writers (di klasiker) laid its foundations: Mendele Mocher Sforim (Sholem Yankev Abramovitsh), the satirical chronicler of shtetl life; Sholem Aleichem (Sholem Rabinovitch), creator of Tevye the Dairyman and the fictional town of Kasrilevke; and I. L. Peretz, author of neo-Hasidic and folk-inspired tales. Jacob Gordin and others built a popular Yiddish theatre.

Twentieth century

Before the Second World War, Yiddish letters flourished in Warsaw, Vilna, Kiev, Moscow, and New York, with poets such as Mani Leib, Moyshe-Leyb Halpern, Anna Margolin, and the Inzikhistn and Yung Vilne movements, and prose writers including Der Nister, David Bergelson, and Israel Joshua Singer. The Holocaust destroyed most of Yiddish-speaking Europe's readership and many of its writers. Post-war literature includes major work by Isaac Bashevis Singer — who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1978 — his brother's rediscovered novels, the poetry of Abraham Sutzkever and Kadya Molodowsky, and Chaim Grade's prose of lost Vilna.

Legacy

Institutions such as YIVO (founded 1925 in Vilna, now in New York) and the Yiddish Book Center preserve the literature, which continues to be written, translated, and studied.
