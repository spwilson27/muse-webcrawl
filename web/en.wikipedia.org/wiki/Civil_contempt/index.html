Civil contempt

Civil contempt
TypeCourt sanction
PurposeCoerce compliance / compensate
BurdenClear and convincing evidence (U.S.)

Civil contempt is a legal sanction imposed by a court to compel a person to obey a court order or to compensate another party for losses caused by disobedience. Unlike criminal contempt, which punishes past misconduct, civil contempt is forward-looking and remedial.

[Image: A judge's gavel resting beside court documents in a courtroom]

Characteristics

A person held in civil contempt typically holds what courts describe as the keys to their own release: by complying with the order — for example by producing documents, paying money owed, or ceasing prohibited conduct — the contemnor can purge the contempt and end the sanction. Fines may accrue daily until compliance, and imprisonment may continue so long as it remains coercive rather than punitive.

Civil vs. criminal contempt

The distinction turns on purpose. If the sanction is intended to vindicate the authority of the court and punish a completed act of disobedience, it is criminal in nature and carries criminal protections, including proof beyond a reasonable doubt. If the sanction is conditional and can be avoided by compliance, or is paid to the injured party as compensation, it is civil. The U.S. Supreme Court addressed the line in cases such as Gompers v. Bucks Stove & Range Co. (1911) and International Union, UMWA v. Bagwell (1994).

Procedure

Civil contempt proceedings are usually initiated by motion of the party benefiting from the order. The moving party must generally show by clear and convincing evidence that a valid order existed, that the respondent knew of it, and that it was violated. Defences include inability to comply, ambiguity in the order, or substantial compliance. Due process requires notice and an opportunity to be heard.

Examples

Common examples include a parent who refuses to pay court-ordered child support, a witness who refuses to testify after being granted immunity, and a litigant who withholds records in discovery. Journalists who refuse to reveal sources have also been jailed for civil contempt, released once the underlying investigation ended.
