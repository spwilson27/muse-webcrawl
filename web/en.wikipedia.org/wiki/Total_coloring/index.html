Total coloring

In graph theory, total coloring is a type of graph coloring on the vertices and edges of a graph. When used without any qualification, a total coloring is always assumed to be proper in the sense that no adjacent vertices, no adjacent edges, and no edge and its incident vertices are assigned the same color. The total chromatic number χ″(G) of a graph G is the fewest colors needed in any total coloring of G.

The total graph T = T(G) of a graph G has as its vertices the vertices and edges of G, with two elements adjacent in T if they are adjacent or incident in G. A total coloring of G is then exactly a proper vertex coloring of its total graph, and χ″(G) = χ(T(G)).

Bounds and the total coloring conjecture

For any graph, Δ(G) + 1 ≤ χ″(G) ≤ Δ(G) + 2 is conjectured to hold, where Δ is the maximum degree. This statement, the total coloring conjecture, was proposed independently by Mehdi Behzad and by Vadim Vizing in the 1960s and remains unproven in general, though it has been verified for many classes of graphs (including bipartite graphs, planar graphs with large maximum degree, and complete graphs). Graphs achieving the lower bound are called type 1, and those requiring Δ + 2 colors type 2.

Examples

The total chromatic number of a path or cycle can be computed directly: cycles Cn are type 1 unless n is divisible by 3, in which case they need Δ + 2 = 4 colors. Complete graphs Kn are type 1 when n is odd and type 2 when n is even.

Determining the total chromatic number of a graph is NP-hard in general.
