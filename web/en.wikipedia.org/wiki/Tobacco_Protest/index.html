Tobacco Protest

Protests in Persia (Iran) against the British tobacco concession, 1891.
Date1891–1892
LocationQajar Persia (Iran)
CauseTobacco concession granted to Major G. F. Talbot
OutcomeConcession cancelled; Shah forced to borrow £500,000

The Tobacco Protest (Persian: نهضت تنباکو) was a Shi'a Islamic resistance movement in Qajar Persia (Iran) against a tobacco concession granted by Naser al-Din Shah Qajar to the British Empire in 1890. The protest is generally regarded as the first mass political movement in modern Iranian history and a precursor to the Persian Constitutional Revolution of 1905–1911.

Background

In March 1890, Naser al-Din Shah granted Major Gerald F. Talbot a concession giving the Imperial Tobacco Corporation of Persia a monopoly over the production, sale and export of all tobacco in the country for fifty years. Tobacco was one of Persia's most valuable export crops and was widely consumed domestically; the concession threatened the livelihoods of farmers, merchants and small shopkeepers and placed a major sector of the economy under foreign control.

The boycott

Opposition spread through the bazaars and the Shi'a clergy. In December 1891 a fatwa attributed to the leading marja', Mirza Hasan Shirazi, declared that the use of tobacco amounted to war against the Hidden Imam, effectively banning smoking. The ruling was obeyed almost universally — even the Shah's own household stopped smoking, and waterpipes across the country were put away.

Outcome

Faced with nationwide unrest, the Shah cancelled the internal monopoly in January 1892 and then the concession altogether. Persia was obliged to pay £500,000 in compensation, raised through its first major foreign loan from the Imperial Bank of Persia — a debt that deepened foreign financial involvement in the country. The protest demonstrated that an alliance of clergy, merchants and ordinary people could compel the monarchy to retreat, setting a pattern repeated in later Iranian movements.
