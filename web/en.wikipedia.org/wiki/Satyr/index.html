In Greek mythology, a satyr (Greek: σάτυρος) is a male nature spirit with the ears and tail of a horse, a prominent permanent erection, and a boisterous love of wine, women, music and dancing. Satyrs form the rowdy retinue of the god Dionysus, roaming the woods and mountains with the maenads, and are among the most popular figures in ancient Greek art.

A satyr balancing a drinking cup, from Attic red-figure pottery.

Depiction and character

Archaic and classical art shows satyrs as stocky, bearded men with equine ears and tails; the goat legs and horns common in later depictions came from conflation with the god Pan and with the Roman faun. Satyrs embodied the untamed side of nature and the effects of wine: they are depicted dancing, playing the aulos, pursuing nymphs and maenads (usually unsuccessfully), and engaging in slapstick mischief. Their leader and father figure is the aged, drunken Silenus.

Satyr plays

Satyrs gave their name to the satyr play, a genre of burlesque drama performed after tragedies at the Dionysia in Athens, in which a chorus of satyrs, led by Silenus, was placed in incongruous mythological situations. The only complete surviving example is Euripides' Cyclops; Sophocles' Ichneutae survives in large part.
