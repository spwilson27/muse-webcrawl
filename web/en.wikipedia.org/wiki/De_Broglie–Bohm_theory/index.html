The de Broglie–Bohm theory, also known as the pilot wave theory or Bohmian mechanics, is an interpretation of quantum mechanics in which particles have definite positions at all times and are guided by a wavefunction that evolves according to the Schrödinger equation. It is a deterministic, explicitly nonlocal hidden-variable theory, and it reproduces all the statistical predictions of standard quantum mechanics.

History

Louis de Broglie presented the first pilot-wave model at the 1927 Solvay Conference, but it was largely abandoned after criticism and the ascendancy of the Copenhagen interpretation. David Bohm independently rediscovered and extended the theory in 1952, showing that it could account for measurement without a separate collapse postulate. Bohm's work prompted John Stewart Bell to investigate hidden-variable theories, leading to Bell's theorem and the realization that any such theory must be nonlocal—as Bohm's explicitly is.

Principles

In Bohmian mechanics, a system consists of both a wavefunction and an actual configuration of particles. The wavefunction evolves by the Schrödinger equation and guides the particles via a "guiding equation" that fixes each particle's velocity in terms of the phase of the wavefunction. Measurement outcomes are determined by the initial configuration; the apparent randomness of quantum mechanics arises from ignorance of the exact initial positions, which in "quantum equilibrium" are distributed according to the Born rule (|ψ|²).

Criticism and reception

Critics object that the theory adds structure without new predictions, that its treatment of relativity and quantum field theory is difficult, and that empty wavefunction branches persist after measurement. Supporters argue that it solves the measurement problem with a clear ontology and that it has inspired productive work in foundations of physics, including hydrodynamic pilot-wave analogues in classical fluids.
