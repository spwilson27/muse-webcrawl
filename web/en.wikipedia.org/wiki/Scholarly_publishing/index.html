Scholarly publishing

Academic journals are the main vehicle of scholarly publishing

Scholarly publishing, also called academic publishing, is the system by which research is distributed to the academic community and the public. Its principal products are peer-reviewed journal articles, scholarly books (monographs), and conference proceedings.

History

The first scientific journals, the Journal des sçavans and the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society, both appeared in 1665. The modern system of peer review developed gradually, becoming standard in the 20th century. Scholarly publishing expanded rapidly after the Second World War, giving rise to large commercial publishers alongside university presses and learned-society publishers.

Process

Authors submit manuscripts to a journal; editors send promising papers to independent experts for peer review, and the work is revised, accepted, or rejected. Publication confers priority and reputation and is central to academic careers, summarised in the maxim "publish or perish".

Economics and open access

The traditional subscription model, in which institutions pay high prices for bundles of journals, has been criticised as the "serials crisis". The open access movement advocates free availability of research, funded instead by article-processing charges, institutional agreements, or subsidies. Preprint servers such as arXiv have also transformed dissemination in many fields.
