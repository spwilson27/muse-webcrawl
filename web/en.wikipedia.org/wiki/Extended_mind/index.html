Extended mind

Otto's notebook: the canonical illustration of the extended mind thesis.

The extended mind thesis (EMT) says that the mind does not exclusively reside in the brain or even the body, but extends into the physical world. It was proposed by philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers in their 1998 paper "The Extended Mind", published in the journal Analysis.

The parity principle and Otto's notebook

Clark and Chalmers argue from a parity principle: if a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would unhesitatingly recognise as part of a cognitive process, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process. Their famous example contrasts Inga, who remembers the location of the Museum of Modern Art biologically, with Otto, who has Alzheimer's disease and reliably records and consults information in a notebook. When Otto looks up the museum's address in his notebook, the thesis holds, the information plays the same functional role as Inga's standing memory belief — so Otto's mind extends to include the notebook.

Criticism

Critics such as Fred Adams and Ken Aizawa argue the thesis commits a coupling–constitution fallacy: the fact that an external object is causally coupled to a cognitive process does not make it a constituent of that process. Defenders reply that functional role, not skull-boundaries, should decide what counts as part of a mind, and the debate has expanded to smartphones, the internet, and collective and distributed cognition.
