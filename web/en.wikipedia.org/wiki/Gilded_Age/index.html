Gilded Age

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Gilded Age is the period in United States history from about the 1870s to the late 1890s, characterised by rapid industrialisation, spectacular economic growth, mass immigration, conspicuous wealth — and deep poverty, labour conflict and political corruption beneath the glittering surface. The term comes from Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner's 1873 novel The Gilded Age: A Tale of Today, satirising an era gilded, rather than solidly golden.

Economic transformation

Railroad mileage tripled, steel, oil and finance were consolidated into vast corporations and trusts run by industrialists such as John D. Rockefeller, Andrew Carnegie, Cornelius Vanderbilt and J. P. Morgan — celebrated as captains of industry or condemned as "robber barons". Wages rose in real terms, but work was dangerous and insecure, and workers responded with unions and strikes, from the Great Railroad Strike of 1877 to Haymarket (1886) and Pullman (1894).

Politics and society

National politics was closely contested between Republicans and Democrats and marked by patronage and corruption scandals, though it also produced civil-service reform (the Pendleton Act, 1883) and the first federal regulation of business (the Interstate Commerce Act, 1887, and the Sherman Antitrust Act, 1890). Millions of immigrants, increasingly from southern and eastern Europe, transformed American cities. The era ended amid the depression of the 1890s, and many of its problems were taken up by the ensuing Progressive Era.
