Incidence geometry is the study of geometric structures in terms of the incidence relations between their objects — which points lie on which lines, which lines pass through which points, and so on — abstracted away from notions of distance, angle or measurement. It forms the axiomatic foundation underlying Euclidean, affine and projective geometry, and the combinatorics of finite geometries.

Incidence structures

An incidence structure consists of a set of points, a set of lines (blocks), and an incidence relation specifying which points belong to which lines. More generally one may include planes and higher-dimensional flats. Such structures are described by incidence axioms, for example: any two distinct points lie on a unique line; every line contains at least two points; and there exist at least three non-collinear points. The incidence matrix of a finite structure records these relations numerically.

Examples

The ordinary Euclidean plane satisfies the incidence axioms together with a parallel postulate; deleting the parallel postulate or replacing it yields affine and projective planes. The smallest projective plane, the Fano plane, has seven points and seven lines, each line containing three points and each point lying on three lines. Finite incidence geometries generalise these examples and connect geometry with combinatorics and design theory; configurations in which every point lies on the same number of lines and every line contains the same number of points are called symmetric configurations.

Important results

The Sylvester–Gallai theorem states that any finite set of points in the Euclidean plane, not all collinear, determines a line containing exactly two of the points. The de Bruijn–Erdős theorem in incidence geometry states that n non-collinear points determine at least n lines. Levi graphs (incidence graphs) provide a bipartite-graph representation used both in proofs and in applications such as finite-element meshes, coding theory and computer graphics, where incidence queries — which faces meet at an edge, which vertices bound a face — are fundamental data-structure operations.
