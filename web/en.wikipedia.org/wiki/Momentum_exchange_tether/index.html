A momentum exchange tether is a kind of space tether that could theoretically be used as a launch system, to change spacecraft velocities, or as part of a momentum-transfer system. A momentum exchange tether works by transferring momentum between the tether system and a payload: when a payload is accelerated, the tether loses an exactly corresponding amount of momentum (and orbital energy), which must later be restored, for example by catching a down-going payload or by using electrodynamic propulsion.

Rotating tethers (rotovators)

The best-known design is the rotovator: a long, strong cable rotating in orbit like the spokes of a wheel. The tip of the tether periodically dips toward a planet, moving slowly relative to the surface at the bottom of its rotation, grips a payload, and flings it into a higher orbit half a rotation later. A rotovator in low Earth orbit could lift payloads from a suborbital trajectory to orbit without rocket propulsion for the payload itself.

Non-rotating skyhooks

A non-rotating design, sometimes called a skyhook, hangs vertically under gravity-gradient stabilization. A payload rendezvous with the lower end is boosted to a higher orbit by being carried up the tether or released from the upper end. Such a tether must extend either side of its centre of mass, with a counterweight beyond the upper end.

Challenges

The concept demands materials with extremely high strength-to-weight ratios, precise rendezvous with a rapidly moving tether tip, and survival against micrometeoroids and atomic oxygen. The tether's orbit decays with each payload boosted unless momentum is restored by equal down-mass traffic or by an electrodynamic drive pushing against a planetary magnetic field.
