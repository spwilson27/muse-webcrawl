White noise

DefinitionRandom signal with constant power spectral density

White noise is a random signal having equal intensity at different frequencies, giving it a constant power spectral density. The term is used in physics, acoustics, telecommunications, and statistics for a noise process whose samples are uncorrelated and have zero mean and finite variance. It is named by analogy with white light, which mixes all visible wavelengths.

Properties

In continuous time, ideal white noise has a flat spectrum across all frequencies and therefore infinite total power — a useful mathematical abstraction rather than a physically realisable signal. In discrete time, a white noise sequence consists of statistically independent (or at least uncorrelated) random samples; a common model is Gaussian white noise. Its autocorrelation function is a delta function at zero lag, which is why white noise is the building block from which coloured noise processes are constructed by filtering.

Applications

White noise is used to test and measure the frequency response of audio equipment and concert-hall acoustics, as a mask to obscure conversations and reduce the intelligibility of background sounds (sound masking), and in sleep machines that blend environmental noises. In statistics and econometrics it models unpredictable disturbances; in image processing it appears as sensor noise; and in music, synthesised noise is a standard sound source for percussion and effects. Random number generation and dithering also exploit white-noise sequences.

Perception

To the ear, white noise sounds like a steady hiss, similar to static or the sound of a waterfall. Because human hearing is more sensitive at some frequencies, signals perceived as uniformly "noisy" are often closer to pink noise, which has equal energy per octave. Prolonged exposure at high volume can damage hearing, but at moderate levels many people find white noise soothing and use it to aid sleep or concentration.
