Huỳnh Phú Sổ

BornJanuary 15, 1920, Hòa Hảo village, An Giang Province, French Indochina
Died1947 (aged 27), South Vietnam
Known forFounder of the Hòa Hảo Buddhist movement

Huỳnh Phú Sổ (15 January 1920 – 1947), popularly known as the Virtuous Master (Đức Thầy), was a Vietnamese religious leader and mystic who founded the Hòa Hảo tradition of Buddhism in 1939. The movement, based in the Mekong Delta of southern Vietnam, attracted millions of followers and became a significant religious and political force in South Vietnam during the First Indochina War and beyond.

Early life

Huỳnh Phú Sổ was born in the village of Hòa Hảo, in present-day An Giang Province, into a family of prosperous landowners. As a youth he was frail and frequently ill; conventional medicine failed to cure him, and he was taken to hermits and healers in the Thất Sơn (Seven Mountains) region. Accounts in the Hòa Hảo tradition hold that he attained enlightenment and miraculous healing powers during this period.

Founding of Hòa Hảo

On 18 May 1939 (the 18th day of the fourth lunar month, according to tradition), Huỳnh Phú Sổ began preaching publicly. He taught a simplified, reformed Buddhism emphasizing prayer at home rather than elaborate temple ritual, moral conduct, patriotism, and social charity. He wrote verses and scriptures in an accessible style — including the Sấm Giảng (Oracular Teachings) — that resonated with the peasantry of the Mekong Delta.

The French colonial authorities, alarmed by his rapidly growing following, confined him and later exiled him, but the movement continued to spread. During the 1940s the Hòa Hảo developed armed forces and became one of the politico-religious movements — alongside the Cao Đài — that contended for power in southern Vietnam.

Death

In April 1947, amid conflict with the Việt Minh, Huỳnh Phú Sổ disappeared after being invited to a meeting intended to reconcile the factions; he is widely believed to have been killed by the Việt Minh. His death made him a martyr figure, and his birthday and the founding day of the religion remain major festivals for followers, who number in the millions in Vietnam and in the Vietnamese diaspora.

Teachings

Hòa Hảo teaching centers on the "Four Debts of Gratitude" (Tứ Ân): to one's ancestors and parents, to one's country, to the Three Jewels of Buddhism, and to one's fellow human beings. Lay practice, ancestor veneration, and social welfare are emphasized, and the movement dispenses with much of the clerical and ritual apparatus of traditional Vietnamese Mahayana Buddhism.
