Meaning"Silk and bamboo" — the materials of string and wind instruments
Best-known formJiangnan sizhu (江南絲竹), ensemble music of the Shanghai–Jiangsu region

Silk and bamboo (Chinese: 絲竹; pinyin: sīzhú) is a traditional Chinese classification of musical instruments—and by extension the chamber music played on them—derived from the materials from which the instruments are made: silk for strings (historically strung with silk) and bamboo for winds. The terms are two of the "eight materials" (bayin) of the ancient Chinese classification of instruments. In modern usage "silk and bamboo" most often refers to the refined amateur ensemble tradition of the lower Yangtze region, Jiangnan sizhu.

Jiangnan sizhu

Jiangnan sizhu flourished from the late Qing dynasty in teahouses and private music clubs of Shanghai, Suzhou and Hangzhou. A typical ensemble centres on the erhu (or gaohu) and dizi, supported by small sanxian, pipa, yangqin, xiao, sheng and zheng, with a small drum and wooden clappers marking the beat. Its style is fluid and lyrical: the string and wind parts weave heterophonic variations around a mother tune (qupai), the dizi and erhu improvising ornamented lines in turn. The repertory is anchored by the "Eight Great Pieces" (ba da qu), including Huan Le Ge ("Song of Joy"), Xing Jie, Si He Ru Yi and the suite Zhong Hua Liu Ban, the slow decorated source-melody from which many variations derive.

Other silk-and-bamboo traditions

Similar sizhu groupings exist elsewhere: Cantonese music (Guangdong yinyue) shares instruments and some repertory but has its own brighter style; Chaozhou (Teochew) string music, Fujian nanyin (a distinct, slower tradition of pipa-led chamber song), and the wind-and-percussion chuida genres are related regional forms. Professional conservatory ensembles in the twentieth century arranged sizhu repertory for the modern Chinese orchestra, but the intimate club tradition continues to be cultivated by amateur societies in the Yangtze delta and in overseas Chinese communities.
