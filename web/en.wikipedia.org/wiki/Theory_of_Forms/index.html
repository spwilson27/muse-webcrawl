Theory of Forms

FounderPlato
Key textsPhaedo, Republic, Symposium, Timaeus, Parmenides
Core claimSensible things depend on intelligible, unchanging Forms
CriticsPlato himself in the Parmenides; Aristotle

The theory of Forms (or theory of Ideas) is a central doctrine in Plato’s philosophy. It holds that the changing objects encountered by the senses are not the deepest reality. Beautiful things, equal sticks, just actions and living organisms are intelligible because they “participate in” or imitate stable Forms—Beauty itself, Equality itself, Justice itself and the like. Forms are non-sensible, eternal and perfectly what particulars only imperfectly are.

Motivation

Plato inherited problems from Heraclitus, Parmenides and Socrates. Sensible things are in flux and admit opposite descriptions: a stick can be long compared with one thing and short compared with another; a face is beautiful in one respect and not in another. Yet knowledge seems to require objects that are stable and definitions that are not hostage to appearance. Socrates’ search for definitions—what is piety? what is courage?—is transformed by Plato into a metaphysical claim that the “what it is” exists separately as a Form.

The arguments in the middle dialogues

In the Phaedo, Socrates explains causation by Forms: beautiful things are beautiful by the Beautiful. The dialogue also links Forms to recollection and the immortality of the soul: learning is the recovery of knowledge possessed before birth. In the Republic, the divided line and the allegory of the cave rank images, sensible objects, mathematical objects and Forms in ascending order of clarity and being. The Form of the Good stands at the summit, compared to the sun as source of visibility, growth and intelligibility.

The Symposium presents an ascent from love of a single beautiful body to Beauty itself. The Timaeus later describes a divine craftsman, the Demiurge, ordering becoming by looking to eternal models. Scholars disagree over how literally to take these myths and over whether Plato’s view changed across dialogues, but the middle-period picture remains the classic statement of Platonism.

Participation and separation

Two notions generate much of the difficulty. Forms are separate (chorista) from sensible particulars, and particulars participate (metechein) in them. If separation means complete independence, how do particulars depend on Forms, and how can embodied knowers grasp them? If participation means sharing a property, the “Third Man” regress threatens: if a man and the Form of Man are both men by sharing a further Form, another Form is needed without end.

Self-criticism and Aristotle

Plato’s Parmenides stages objections so forcefully that some readers treat it as abandoning the theory, while others see a training exercise in dialectic that refines it. Aristotle rejected separate universals and argued that form is in things as their essence and actuality. His criticisms—useless duplication of the sensible world, failure to explain motion and generation, and the Third Man—set the terms for later debates about universals.

Legacy

Through Middle Platonism, Neoplatonism, Christian theology, Islamic and Jewish philosophy, medieval realism and modern mathematical Platonism, the theory has been transformed repeatedly. Even philosophers who reject Plato’s metaphysics often retain his question: how can unstable experience and discourse be answerable to stable structures of intelligibility?
