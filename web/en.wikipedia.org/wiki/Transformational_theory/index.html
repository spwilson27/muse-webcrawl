Transformational theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transformational theory is a branch of music theory that models musical motion as transformations between elements of a musical space, expressed using the mathematical machinery of group theory. Rather than asking what a musical object is in absolute terms (for example, which notes form a chord), transformational analysis asks what operation carries one object into another — a shift of analytical focus from intervals between pitches to the actions a listener or performer experiences as movement.

David Lewin

The theory is associated above all with David Lewin, whose book Generalized Musical Intervals and Transformations (1987) founded the field. Lewin introduced the Generalized Interval System (GIS), a formal structure consisting of a set of musical objects, a group of intervals between them, and a function assigning intervals consistently, generalising the familiar notions of transposition and interval. He also argued for a phenomenological shift: analysis should capture the intuition "I move from s to t by this transformation" rather than merely measuring static distances.

Neo-Riemannian theory

The best-known application is neo-Riemannian theory, which studies the three contextual operations on major and minor triads — P (parallel: C major ↔ C minor), L (leading-tone exchange: C major ↔ E minor) and R (relative: C major ↔ A minor) — derived from the writings of Hugo Riemann. Combinations of these operations generate the PLR group, whose action on the 24 major and minor triads can be visualised on the Tonnetz, a lattice of pitch classes connected by fifths and thirds. Neo-Riemannian analysis has proved especially fruitful for chromatic music of the 19th century (Schubert, Wagner, Liszt) and for film music, where triads move smoothly by shared tones in ways that tonal Roman-numeral analysis describes awkwardly.

Networks and later work

Lewin and his successors developed transformational networks: graphs whose nodes are musical objects and whose arrows are labelled with transformations, subject to consistency conditions. Subsequent theorists have extended the approach to atonal set classes, rhythm, and tuning systems, and have explored the mathematics of group actions, duality and voice-leading geometry. Lewin's posthumous Studies in Music with Text (2006) applied the outlook to song and texted music.
