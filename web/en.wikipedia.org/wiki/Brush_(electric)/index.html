Brush (electric)

Carbon brushes conducting current to a motor's commutator.

A brush or carbon brush is a sliding electrical contact used to transfer electric current between stationary wires and moving parts, most commonly a rotating shaft in an electric motor or generator. Brushes are typically made of carbon or a carbon-graphite composite, held against the rotating commutator or slip rings by springs.

Function

In a DC machine, brushes bear against the segments of the commutator, reversing the current in the armature windings at the proper instant so that torque (or generated voltage) remains in one direction. In AC machines with slip rings, brushes feed excitation current to the rotor. Their sliding contact forms a self-lubricating, slightly resistive film that protects the rotating surface.

Materials and wear

Brush grades range from natural graphite and carbon-graphite to electrographitic and metal-graphite (copper- or silver-loaded) compositions chosen for current density and speed. Because they slide against a moving surface, brushes are consumable parts that wear down and must be inspected and replaced; worn brushes can cause sparking, loss of power and commutator damage.

Alternatives

Brushless motors eliminate brushes by electronic commutation, offering longer life and less maintenance at the cost of more complex drive electronics.
