Chipotle

A chipotle (US: /tʃɪˈpoʊtleɪ/, UK: /tʃɪˈpɒtleɪ/; Spanish: [tʃiˈpotle]) is a smoke-dried ripe jalapeño chili pepper used for seasoning, principally in Mexican and Tex-Mex cuisine. The name comes from the Nahuatl chīlpōctli, meaning "smoked chili".

Production

Chipotles are made by smoke-drying fully ripened red jalapeños in smoking chambers over several days, until they resemble raisins or prunes. Roughly ten pounds of fresh jalapeños yield one pound of chipotles. The two best-known varieties are the morita ("little blackberry"), which is smoked for less time and is most common in the United States, and the larger, longer-smoked meco, prized in Mexico for its deeper flavour.

Uses

Chipotles are frequently sold canned in adobo sauce — a tangy tomato-and-vinegar sauce — and lend a smoky, earthy heat (typically 2,500–8,000 Scoville units) to salsas, soups such as tlalpeño, marinades, and dishes like tinga de pollo. Ground chipotle powder is also a common spice in rubs and barbecue sauces.
