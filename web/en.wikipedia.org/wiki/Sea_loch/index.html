Sea loch

A sea loch is a loch — a Scottish Gaelic word for a lake or inlet — that is an arm of the sea. Sea lochs are a characteristic feature of the deeply indented western coast of Scotland, where they were carved by glaciers during the ice ages and later flooded as sea levels rose. They are essentially the Scottish equivalent of the Norwegian fjords, although many are shorter and less steep-sided. Related terms include "sea lough" in Ireland.

Formation

During the last glaciation, ice flowing from the Scottish Highlands deepened pre-existing valleys, often gouging their floors below present sea level. When the ice melted and the sea rose, these over-deepened troughs flooded, producing long, narrow inlets with characteristically shallow sills at their mouths, where the ice deposited moraine or where the rock resisted erosion. A freshwater loch can also become a sea loch if the barrier between it and the sea is breached.

Notable sea lochs

Among the best known are Loch Linnhe, on which Fort William stands; Loch Fyne, famous for its herring and oysters and at over 60 km the longest sea loch in Scotland; Loch Etive, noted for its tidal rapids at the Falls of Lora; Loch Long and the Gare Loch, which host naval facilities on the Clyde; and the remote Loch Torridon in the northwest Highlands. Many sea lochs have sheltered waters used for fish and shellfish farming, particularly salmon and mussels.

Ecology

The restricted exchange of water over the shallow entrance sills can make deep basins of sea lochs poorly ventilated, producing distinctive ecosystems; some contain cold-water coral reefs and rare burrowing fauna that are protected within marine protected areas.
