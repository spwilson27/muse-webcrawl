Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome

Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) is a condition characterised by an abnormal increase in heart rate upon standing, accompanied by symptoms of orthostatic intolerance such as light-headedness, palpitations, tremulousness, fatigue and brain fog. It is a form of dysautonomia. The diagnostic criterion in adults is a sustained heart-rate rise of at least 30 beats per minute within ten minutes of standing or head-up tilt, without orthostatic hypotension (at least 40 bpm in adolescents).

Causes and mechanisms

POTS is a syndrome with several proposed mechanisms rather than a single disease: partial autonomic neuropathy with impaired vasoconstriction in the legs (neuropathic POTS), excessive sympathetic activity (hyperadrenergic POTS), low blood volume, deconditioning, and autoantibodies against adrenergic receptors. It frequently co-occurs with hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, mast cell activation syndrome, chronic fatigue syndrome and autoimmune conditions, and many cases follow an infectious illness; notable numbers of cases have been reported after COVID-19 as part of long COVID.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on demonstrating the characteristic heart-rate response during a tilt-table test or a ten-minute standing test, together with chronic orthostatic symptoms (typically for at least three to six months). Blood tests and cardiac evaluation exclude mimics such as anaemia, hyperthyroidism, dehydration and inappropriate sinus tachycardia.

Treatment

Management is usually non-pharmacological first: increased fluid (2–3 litres daily) and salt intake, compression garments, counter-pressure manoeuvres, and a graded exercise programme beginning with recumbent exercise such as rowing or swimming. Medications used include fludrocortisone, midodrine, low-dose beta blockers, ivabradine and pyridostigmine. Many patients improve substantially over time, though symptoms can persist for years.
