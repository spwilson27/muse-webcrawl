Financial instability hypothesis

The financial instability hypothesis is an interpretation of the economic theory of debt deflation developed by economist Hyman Minsky. Credit booms, in Minsky's view, are inherently destabilising: periods of stability and prosperity encourage borrowers and lenders to take on ever more risk, so that the financial system moves endogenously from robustness to fragility. Minsky conducted much of his research at Washington University in St. Louis and the Levy Economics Institute.

Three financing postures

Minsky classified borrowers into three types. Hedge units can repay both interest and principal from their cash flows. Speculative units can pay interest but must roll over principal. Ponzi units cannot cover even interest payments and depend on rising asset prices to refinance or sell assets. Over a long boom, the economy's mix shifts from hedge toward speculative and Ponzi finance, until a rise in interest rates or a fall in asset prices triggers defaults, forced selling, and crisis — a turning point sometimes called a "Minsky moment".

Influence

Long marginal in mainstream economics, Minsky's hypothesis drew renewed attention during the financial crisis of 2007–2008, which many commentators read as a Minsky cycle, and it remains foundational in post-Keynesian economics.
