The Brabant Revolution (French: Révolution brabançonne) was an armed insurrection in the Austrian Netherlands in 1789–1790, sparked by resistance to the centralising reforms of Emperor Joseph II. It briefly created an independent confederation, the United Belgian States.

Causes

Joseph II's administrative, judicial and religious reforms abolished historic provincial privileges guaranteed by the Joyous Entry of Brabant, provoking opposition from the estates, the legal profession and the Church. In exile at Breda, lawyer Jan Frans Vonck organised a patriot army, while the conservative Hendrik Van der Noot led the "Statist" opposition; the democratic "Vonckists" favoured popular sovereignty, the Statists the restoration of the old estates.

Course

On 24 October 1789 the patriot army invaded from the Dutch Republic; at the Battle of Turnhout (27 October) Austrian troops were driven out of the town, and by December Brussels was liberated. On 11 January 1790 the provinces proclaimed the United Belgian States. Infighting between Statists and Vonckists — Van der Noot's faction suppressed the democrats in March 1790 — fatally weakened the new republic.

Suppression and legacy

Emperor Leopold II, Joseph's successor, offered concessions to the estates while preparing reconquest; by December 1790 Austrian forces had restored Habsburg rule. The revolution is remembered in Belgium as a foundational episode of national resistance, preceding both French annexation and Belgian independence in 1830.
