Patient-centered care

Patient-centered care (also person-centred care) is an approach to healthcare that consciously adopts the perspectives of individuals, carers, families, and communities, and sees them as participants in, and beneficiaries of, trusted health systems organised around their comprehensive needs rather than around diseases or providers.

Definition and dimensions

The Institute of Medicine (now the National Academy of Medicine) defined patient-centered care as care that is "respectful of and responsive to individual patient preferences, needs, and values, and ensuring that patient values guide all clinical decisions." It was named one of six aims for quality improvement, alongside safety, effectiveness, timeliness, efficiency, and equity. Commonly cited dimensions include respect for patients' values and preferences; coordination and integration of care; information, communication, and education; physical comfort; emotional support; involvement of family and friends; continuity and transition; and access to care.

Principles

Core practices include shared decision-making, eliciting the patient's goals and concerns, cultural competence, health literacy–appropriate communication, and care planning that accounts for the person's social context. Related models include the patient-centered medical home in primary care and family-centered care in paediatrics.

Evidence and outcomes

Research associates patient-centered approaches with greater patient satisfaction, improved adherence, better self-management of chronic disease, and in some settings improved clinical outcomes and reduced utilisation. Measurement uses instruments such as patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs).

Criticism

Critics note that the term is used loosely, that structural barriers (time pressure, payment models, inequities) limit implementation, and that centring individual preferences must be balanced against evidence-based practice and population health.
