The Kantō Massacre (Japanese: 関東大虐殺) was a mass killing that took place in Japan's Kantō region in the chaotic days and weeks following the Great Kantō earthquake of 1 September 1923. Vigilante groups, in some cases aided or encouraged by police and military units, murdered ethnic Koreans, Chinese, and others, acting on rumours that Koreans were poisoning wells, setting fires, and preparing revolt.

Background

The 1923 earthquake devastated Tokyo and Yokohama, killing well over 100,000 people. In the absence of reliable information, rumours spread rapidly. The Home Ministry and police transmitted reports of Korean rioting, which helped legitimise vigilante violence. Martial law was declared, and self-defence groups (jikeidan) formed across the region.

Killings

Koreans were identified by checkpoints where suspects were forced to pronounce Japanese phrases — those who failed were beaten or killed. Victims were murdered with bamboo spears, clubs, swords, and firearms. Japanese citizens mistaken for Koreans, Chinese labourers, and political dissidents were also killed; among the notable victims were the anarchists Ōsugi Sakae, Itō Noe, and labour leader Hirasawa Keishichi, murdered by police or military officers in separate incidents.

Death toll and legacy

Estimates of the dead commonly centre on about 6,000 Koreans, though figures vary. The Japanese government suppressed investigation and denied the scale of the killings for decades. Annual memorial services are held in Tokyo, and the massacre remains a sensitive subject in Japan–Korea relations and in debates over official commemoration.
