Chinese classics

The Chinese classics or canonical texts (經, jīng) are the authoritative ancient texts that formed the core of Confucian education and the Chinese imperial examination system for over two thousand years. The canon is conventionally organised around the Four Books and Five Classics (四書五經), though broader lists of Nine and Thirteen Classics also developed.

The Five Classics

The Five Classics, traditionally associated with Confucius as editor or transmitter, are: the I Ching (Classic of Changes), a divination manual; the Shujing (Classic of Documents), speeches and records of early rulers; the Shijing (Classic of Poetry), 305 poems; the Liji (Book of Rites), on ritual and social norms; and the Chunqiu (Spring and Autumn Annals), a terse chronicle of the state of Lu.

The Four Books

The Song-dynasty philosopher Zhu Xi elevated four texts as the essential gateway to Confucian learning: the Great Learning and the Doctrine of the Mean (chapters of the Liji), the Analects of Confucius, and the Mencius. Zhu Xi's commentaries became the orthodox interpretation for the civil service examinations from 1313 until the system was abolished in 1905.

Transmission and influence

Many classics were lost or damaged in the Qin dynasty's "burning of books" (213 BCE) and reconstructed under the Han from memory and hidden copies, giving rise to the Old Text/New Text controversy over their authenticity. Mastery of the classics shaped Chinese political culture, historiography and ethics, and spread with Confucian learning to Korea, Japan and Vietnam.
