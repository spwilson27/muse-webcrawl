The Epipaleolithic (or Epipalaeolithic) is a period in prehistory occurring at the end of the Paleolithic, after the Last Glacial Maximum and before the Neolithic Revolution. It applies to regions whose hunter-gatherer cultures continued a Paleolithic way of life, using microlithic stone tools, while adapting to post-glacial warming — in contrast to the term Mesolithic, which is generally used for the corresponding period in northern Europe.

Characteristics

Epipaleolithic cultures were made up of mobile hunter-gatherers who exploited a broadening range of foods: small game, fish, shellfish, and intensively gathered wild plants. Their toolkits are characterised by microliths — small geometric stone blades hafted into composite tools such as arrows, sickles, and harpoons. In the Levant, the period includes the Kebaran and the later Natufian culture, whose semi-sedentary settlements, stone architecture, and intensive harvesting of wild cereals immediately preceded the domestication of plants and animals in the Neolithic.

Terminology

The term was introduced for cultures that seemed to continue Paleolithic traditions beyond the Pleistocene without developing Neolithic traits. Usage overlaps confusingly with "Mesolithic": Epipaleolithic is preferred for the Near East, North Africa, and Iberia, while Mesolithic is standard for post-glacial hunter-gatherers further north in Europe.
