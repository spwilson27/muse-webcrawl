Wine grape

Wine grapes are grapes grown for the production of wine. Wine grapes can be eaten fresh, but they are quite different from table grapes: they are smaller, usually seeded, and have thick skins and higher sugar content when ripe. Nearly all wine grapes belong to the species Vitis vinifera, which originated in the Caucasus region and spread through Europe and later to the Americas, Australia and elsewhere.

Characteristics

Wine grapes ripen to a balance of sugars, acids, tannins and aromatic compounds that determines the style and quality of the resulting wine. Red wine grapes have dark skins rich in anthocyanin pigments and phenolic compounds; white wine grapes have pale skins and are typically pressed soon after harvest to separate the juice from the skins.

Varieties

Thousands of grape varieties are used for wine, though a relatively small number dominate global plantings. Major international varieties include Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Pinot noir, Syrah (Shiraz), Chardonnay, Sauvignon blanc and Riesling. Many regions also cultivate indigenous varieties such as Nebbiolo, Tempranillo, Sangiovese and Grüner Veltliner.

Viticulture

The cultivation of wine grapes, viticulture, is strongly influenced by climate, soil and topography — collectively expressed in the concept of terroir. Vines are trained and pruned to manage yield and fruit exposure, and harvest timing is chosen to achieve the desired ripeness profile.
