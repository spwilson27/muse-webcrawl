Harappa

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Excavated granary platforms and brick structures at Harappa]

Harappa was an ancient Indus Valley city in the Punjab, in modern Pakistan, inhabited from about 3300 to 1300 BC, which gave the Harappan civilisation its alternative name. A major urban centre of perhaps 23,500 people at its Mature Harappan height (2600–1900 BC), it traded widely, produced seals, beads and pottery in standardised styles, and buried its dead in cemeteries that archaeology has used to probe Indus society. The site was badly damaged in the 19th century — its bricks robbed for railway ballast — before its significance was recognised and it became, with Mohenjo-daro, one of the two first-identified Indus cities.

The city

Harappa's mounds (among them the citadel-like Mound AB and the workmen's quarters and granary platforms of Mound F) show the Indus pattern: planned streets, fired-brick construction, craft quarters for shell, faience, carnelian and steatite working. The Cemetery H and R-37 burials, modest by the standards of Egypt or Mesopotamia, hint at the civilisation's relative egalitarianism — or at least its different display of wealth.

Damage and excavation

Visited by the deserter-explorer Charles Masson in 1826 and surveyed by Alexander Cunningham, Harappa's upper levels were largely destroyed when contractors mined its bricks for the Lahore–Multan railway in the 1850s. Systematic excavation began in the 1920s under the Archaeological Survey of India; since 1986 the Harappa Archaeological Research Project has applied modern methods to its long occupation sequence. Like all Indus sites, Harappa keeps its central secrets — its script undeciphered, its rulers invisible.
