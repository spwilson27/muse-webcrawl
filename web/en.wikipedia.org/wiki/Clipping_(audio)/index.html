Clipping (audio)

Clipping is a form of waveform distortion that occurs when an amplifier is overdriven and attempts to deliver an output voltage or current beyond its maximum capability. When an amplifier is severely overdriven, the output waveform is "clipped" — its peaks are flattened at the maximum level the amplifier can produce.

Causes

In the frequency domain, clipping produces strong harmonics. In the time domain, the waveform is truncated at the clipping threshold. Clipping can occur at any stage of an audio chain: in a microphone preamplifier, in an analogue-to-digital converter, in digital processing, or in a power amplifier driving a loudspeaker.

Effects

Audibly, clipping introduces harsh distortion and can generate high-frequency energy that may damage tweeters. In digital systems, samples that would exceed 0 dBFS are simply held at the maximum value, producing flat-topped waveforms; digital clipping is generally considered harsher-sounding than the gradual soft clipping of analogue equipment.

Not all clipping is unwanted: guitar amplifiers and effects pedals deliberately clip the signal to produce distortion and overdrive tones, and soft-clipping circuits are used in mastering limiters to shave peaks transparently.

Hard and soft clipping

Hard clipping flattens the waveform abruptly at the threshold, producing many high-order harmonics. Soft clipping, typical of valve (tube) amplifiers, rounds the waveform gradually as it approaches the limit, producing proportionally more low-order harmonics and a subjectively warmer sound.

Avoidance

Clipping is avoided by gain staging — keeping signal levels within the headroom of each device — and by the use of limiters to catch transient peaks. In digital recording, levels are typically set so peaks remain several decibels below full scale.
