Headstone

A traditional upright headstone.

A headstone, tombstone or gravestone is a marker, usually of stone, that is placed over or by the head of a grave in a cemetery or elsewhere. The term is also used for a marker placed at the foot of a grave (a footstone). Headstones typically bear the name of the deceased, their dates of birth and death, and often a personal message or epitaph, together with religious symbols, floral designs or other ornament.

History

Marking graves is an ancient practice: stelae were erected in the ancient Near East and in Greece and Rome, where carved stone monuments recorded the name, family and achievements of the dead. In medieval Europe, burial within churches was marked by ledger stones set into the floor and by effigies; those buried outside were often commemorated only by wooden markers or mounds.

The familiar churchyard headstone became common in Britain and its colonies from the seventeenth century, as a growing middle class sought permanent memorials. Slate, sandstone and marble were used in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — slate allowing fine, durable lettering, and marble permitting elaborate carving of motifs such as the winged death's head, the soul effigy, urns and willows. The Victorian era brought large, ornate monuments in garden cemeteries, and polished granite became the dominant material in the twentieth century because of its hardness and resistance to weathering.

Materials and manufacture

Traditional gravestones are made of slate, sandstone, limestone, marble or granite. Lettering was cut by hand with chisel and mallet; modern stones are commonly cut with diamond tools, shaped by computer-controlled machinery, and lettered by sandblasting or laser etching, which permits portraits and detailed images. Stones may be upright tablets, obelisks, crosses, columns, statues, or flat "ledger" markers laid flush with the ground, as required by many modern lawn cemeteries.

Inscriptions

A typical inscription gives the full name of the deceased, dates of birth and death, and relationships ("beloved wife of…"). Epitaphs range from brief phrases such as "Rest in peace" (from the Latin requiescat in pace) to verses, quotations from scripture, humorous lines and statements of achievement. Jewish headstones commonly include the Hebrew abbreviation for "Here lies", the Hebrew name and father's name, and the date of death by the Hebrew calendar; Muslim graves are traditionally marked with simple stones, reflecting a preference for modesty. Fraternal, military and national symbols — such as the emblems supplied for veterans' graves by the United States Department of Veterans Affairs — are also common.

Conservation

Older stones suffer from weathering, biological growth, vandalism and subsidence. Conservators clean stones with gentle methods (water and soft brushes), since abrasive cleaning and household chemicals accelerate damage to soft stone such as marble and sandstone. Genealogists and local historians record inscriptions as a primary source for family and demographic history, and techniques such as "rubbing" a stone with paper and charcoal were once widely used, though they are now discouraged where they may erode the surface.
