Herbert Wayne Boyer (born July 10, 1936) is an American biologist and entrepreneur. Together with Stanley N. Cohen he performed the first successful experiments in recombinant DNA cloning, work that founded the biotechnology industry. In 1976 he co-founded Genentech with venture capitalist Robert A. Swanson, the first company formed to commercialise recombinant DNA technology.

Early life and research

Boyer grew up in western Pennsylvania and studied biology and chemistry at Saint Vincent College, earning his PhD in bacteriology from the University of Pittsburgh in 1963. After postdoctoral work at Yale he joined the University of California, San Francisco, in 1966. His laboratory characterised restriction enzymes — notably EcoRI — that cut DNA at specific sequences, leaving "sticky ends" that could be rejoined.

Recombinant DNA

In 1973 Boyer and Stanford's Stanley Cohen combined these tools with Cohen's plasmid work to splice a foreign gene into a bacterial plasmid and clone it in E. coli — the first recombinant DNA organism. The Cohen–Boyer patents, licensed by Stanford and UCSF, became among the most lucrative in university history, earning hundreds of millions in royalties.

Genentech

In January 1976 the 29-year-old venture capitalist Robert Swanson persuaded Boyer over a beer at a San Francisco bar to start a company. Genentech (from "genetic engineering technology") made its name in 1977 by producing the human hormone somatostatin in bacteria, and in 1978 cloned human insulin, licensed to Eli Lilly and marketed in 1982 as Humulin — the first pharmaceutical product of genetic engineering. Genentech's 1980 initial public offering was a landmark Wall Street event that launched the biotech sector. Boyer served as a vice president and director of the company.

Honours

Boyer shared the 1980 Albert Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research, received the National Medal of Science in 1990 and National Medal of Technology in 1989, and in 2004 shared (with Cohen) the Shaw Prize in Life Science and Medicine. The Boyer Center for Molecular Medicine at Yale is named for him.
