Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) is the ability of electrical equipment and systems to function acceptably in their electromagnetic environment, by limiting the unintentional generation, propagation and reception of electromagnetic energy which may cause unwanted effects such as electromagnetic interference (EMI) or even physical damage. The goal of EMC is the correct operation of different equipment in a common electromagnetic environment.

Emissions and immunity

EMC engineering addresses two complementary requirements. Emissions — energy radiated from a device through the air or conducted along its cables — must be kept below limits so as not to disturb radio services and other equipment. Immunity (the inverse of susceptibility) is the ability of a device to keep working when subjected to disturbances such as electrostatic discharge, fast transients, surges and radio-frequency fields.

Interference mechanisms

Disturbance couples between a source and a victim by conduction, capacitive or inductive (near-field) coupling, or radiation. Countermeasures operate at the source, along the path, or at the victim: careful printed-circuit-board layout, decoupling, shielded enclosures and cables, mains filters, ferrite chokes, twisted pairs, proper grounding and spread-spectrum clocking are among the standard techniques.

Regulation and testing

Most countries legally require EMC compliance before equipment is sold: in the United States under FCC rules (Part 15 for unintentional radiators), and in the European Union under the EMC Directive, with harmonised standards from the IEC/CISPR 61000 family. Compliance is demonstrated by standardised measurements in anechoic chambers or open-area test sites, and immunity tests that zap, surge and irradiate the product to specified levels.
