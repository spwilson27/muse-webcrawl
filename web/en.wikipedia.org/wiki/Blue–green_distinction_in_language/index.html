Blue–green distinction in language

The boundary between blue and green varies across languages

The blue–green distinction is a feature of many languages concerning the naming of colours. Many languages do not distinguish between what English calls "blue" and "green", using a single term — sometimes called "grue" — to cover both. The phenomenon is a central case in the study of colour terms and linguistic relativity.

Overview

In their 1969 study Basic Color Terms, Brent Berlin and Paul Kay proposed that languages develop basic colour terms in a predictable sequence: languages with few terms merge blue and green, and the distinction appears only at a later stage, after terms for black, white, red, green or yellow, and blue. Many languages of Africa, Asia, and the Americas historically used one word for the blue–green range. Ancient Greek and Biblical Hebrew also lacked a dedicated word for blue.

Examples

Japanese traditionally used ao for both blue and green; the word midori for green became a basic term only in the modern era, and green traffic lights are still called ao shingō. Vietnamese xanh covers both, qualified as "sky blue" or "leaf green". Welsh glas spans blue, green, and grey. In many Bantu languages a single term likewise covers the range.

Significance

The distinction (or its absence) has been used in debates about the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis: experiments suggest that language boundaries can influence the speed of colour discrimination, though perception of the spectrum itself is continuous and shared.
