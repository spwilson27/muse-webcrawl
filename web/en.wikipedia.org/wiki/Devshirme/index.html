Devshirme

Devshirme recruits being registered by Ottoman officials.

Devshirme (Ottoman Turkish: دوشیرمه, "collecting"; also known in English as the "blood tax") was the Ottoman practice of forcibly recruiting soldiers and administrators from among the young Christian boys of the empire's Balkan provinces. Taken from their families, converted to Islam, and rigorously educated, the most promising recruits were trained for service in the Janissary corps or the imperial administration, where some rose to the highest offices, including Grand Vizier.

Origins and mechanism

The system developed in the late 14th century under Murad I as a way of building a military and administrative elite loyal personally to the sultan, counterbalancing the Turkish nobility. Ottoman officials periodically toured Balkan villages and selected boys, generally aged about 8 to 18, taking roughly one boy from every forty households, with various exemptions.

Training

Recruits were converted to Islam and circumcised, sent to Turkish families or training corps to learn Turkish and Islamic practice, and then sorted: the most able entered palace service and education, while others joined the Janissaries or other military units. The system produced celebrated figures such as Sokollu Mehmed Pasha, grand vizier under three sultans, and the architect Mimar Sinan is also traditionally said to have been a devshirme recruit.

Decline

The system decayed in the late 16th and 17th centuries as Janissary membership became hereditary and Muslims sought entry for their own sons; the last levies occurred in the early 18th century. In Balkan memory the devshirme is remembered as a heavy burden on Christian families, while historians also note the extraordinary social mobility it offered recruits.
