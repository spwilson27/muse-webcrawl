Maya architecture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Maya architecture spans many thousands of years, several eras of political change, and architectural innovation, and includes the monumental stone cities, pyramids, palaces, temples and ballcourts of the Maya civilization of Mesoamerica. A unique aspect of Maya architecture is the integration of text and iconography into the built environment: carved lintels, stelae and façade sculpture recorded dynastic history, mythology and calendrical information.

Materials and construction

Maya builders used locally available stone — principally limestone in the lowlands — set in lime mortar, and finished buildings with lime stucco that was often painted, commonly red. The characteristic interior space was formed by the corbel vault (the "Maya arch"), in which successive courses of stone step inward until capped by a slab; this limited room widths but allowed great height. Roofs of perishable material (thatch over wooden beams) crowned domestic buildings, while masonry roof combs soared above temples.

Principal building types

  • Pyramids and temples — stepped platforms, often built over earlier structures in successive enlargements, supporting summit shrines.
  • Palaces — long, multi-room range structures on raised platforms, housing royal courts.
  • Ballcourts — I-shaped masonry courts for the Mesoamerican ballgame, present at nearly every major city.
  • E-groups and acropolises — formal complexes used for astronomical observation and royal burials.
  • Observatories — such as the Caracol at Chichen Itza.

Regional styles

Classic-period styles include the towering temples of Tikal in the Petén, the stucco sculpture of Palenque, the stone mosaics of the Puuc hills (Uxmal, Kabah), and the Rio Bec and Chenes styles of the central Yucatán peninsula. The northern Puuc style is noted for precisely cut veneer masonry and elaborate upper-façade masks of the rain god Chaac.

Urban planning

Maya cities were organised around ceremonial cores linked by raised causeways (sacbeob). Construction was aligned to astronomical events — solstices, equinoxes and Venus cycles — and to sacred geography, including cenotes and caves.
