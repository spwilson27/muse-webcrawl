The Silver Swan (song)

The Silver Swan

Genre: Madrigal

Composer: Orlando Gibbons

Published: 1612

Source: The First Set of Madrigals and Motets of 5 Parts

The Silver Swan is a madrigal by Orlando Gibbons (1583–1625), first published in 1612 in his First Set of Madrigals and Motets of 5 Parts. It is one of the best-known English madrigals, frequently performed by choirs and vocal ensembles, and is celebrated for its understated melancholy and its strikingly modern-sounding lyric.

Text

The anonymous poem contrasts the silver swan, which possesses music but sings only once — at its death — with the geese and other birds that chatter foolishly while alive. The closing lines are directed at human failings:

"The silver swan, who living had no note,
When death approached, unlocked her silent throat;
Leaning her breast against the reedy shore,
Thus sung her first and last, and sung no more:
'Farewell, all joys; O death, come close mine eyes;
More geese than swans now live, more fools than wise.'"

Music

The madrigal is scored for five voices (SSATB). Unlike many madrigals of the period it avoids elaborate word-painting, instead presenting the text in a largely syllabic, hymn-like style whose restraint heightens the pathos of the final line. The piece exemplifies the late flowering of the English madrigal school, drawing on the model of William Byrd, Gibbons's mentor.

Legacy

The song has been arranged for many forces, recorded by ensembles including The King's Singers and The Hilliard Ensemble, and its final couplet is often quoted in discussions of the "swan song" metaphor in literature. It remains a staple of the English choral repertoire.
