Canalisation (genetics)

Canalisation is a measure of the ability of a population to produce the same phenotype regardless of variability of its environment or genotype. It is a form of evolutionary robustness. The term was coined by C. H. Waddington in 1942 in the same paper that he first used the word epigenetics; Waddington pictured development as a ball rolling down an "epigenetic landscape" of valleys ("chreodes"), such that the deeper the valley, the more canalised the developmental outcome.

Concept

Canalisation may be environmental — the phenotype is buffered against environmental perturbations such as temperature — or genetic — the phenotype is buffered against mutations. A classic demonstration came from Waddington's experiments on Drosophila, in which heat-shocked pupae produced a crossveinless wing phenotype that, after generations of selection, appeared even without the heat shock, a phenomenon he called genetic assimilation. Canalisation implies that genetic variation can accumulate silently (cryptic genetic variation) and be released when buffering breaks down, for example when the chaperone protein Hsp90 is impaired, potentially contributing to rapid evolutionary change.

Related ideas

Canalisation is closely related to developmental stability, phenotypic plasticity (its inverse in some sense), and the Baldwin effect. Modern work in systems biology treats canalisation as a network property, arising from redundancy, feedback loops, and distributed robustness in gene regulatory networks. The concept remains central to evolutionary developmental biology (evo-devo) and to debates about how robustness and evolvability coexist.
