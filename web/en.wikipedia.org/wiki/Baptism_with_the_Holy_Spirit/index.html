Baptism with the Holy Spirit

Baptism with the Holy Spirit is a term used in Christianity to describe a spiritual experience in which a believer is filled with, or immersed in, the Holy Spirit. The phrase derives from the New Testament, where John the Baptist contrasts his baptism with water to the coming one who would "baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire" (Matthew 3:11). The concept is central to Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity, though it is interpreted differently across traditions.

Biblical basis

All four Gospels record John the Baptist's prediction of a baptism with the Holy Spirit. In the Acts of the Apostles, the risen Jesus instructs his disciples to wait in Jerusalem because "in a few days you will be baptized with the Holy Spirit" (Acts 1:5). This promise is associated with the events of Pentecost in Acts 2, when the disciples were filled with the Holy Spirit and began speaking in other tongues. Further accounts of believers receiving the Spirit appear in Acts 8 (Samaria), Acts 10 (the household of Cornelius), and Acts 19 (disciples at Ephesus).

Pentecostal interpretation

In classical Pentecostal theology, baptism in the Holy Spirit is understood as a distinct experience subsequent to conversion and regeneration. It is regarded as an empowerment for Christian service and witness, and in many Pentecostal denominations speaking in tongues (glossolalia) is held to be the initial physical evidence of the experience. Pentecostal denominations such as the Assemblies of God teach that this baptism is available to all believers.

Charismatic and Catholic views

The charismatic movement, which spread through mainline Protestant churches and the Roman Catholic Church from the 1960s, also emphasizes baptism in the Holy Spirit, though often understanding it as a release or actualization of graces already received in sacramental baptism and confirmation. Catholic charismatic renewal received encouragement from successive popes.

Other interpretations

Many evangelical and Reformed traditions hold that baptism with the Holy Spirit occurs at conversion, when the Spirit incorporates the believer into the body of Christ (1 Corinthians 12:13), and is therefore not a separate second experience. Cessationist traditions further argue that the extraordinary gifts associated with the apostolic age have ceased.
