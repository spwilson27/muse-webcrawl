Subsidence

Subsidence is a general term for the vertical lowering of the ground surface — the sinking or settling of the Earth's surface with little or no horizontal motion. It can occur naturally through geological processes or be induced by human activity, and it ranges from slow, regional sagging over thousands of years to sudden collapse into underground voids.

Causes

  • Groundwater extraction: pumping water from aquifers compacts aquitards, permanently lowering the land. Severe examples include Mexico City, California's San Joaquin Valley (up to about 8.5 m of subsidence in places), and Jakarta.
  • Oil and gas extraction: withdrawal of hydrocarbons reduces pore pressure and compacts reservoirs, as at the Ekofisk field in the North Sea.
  • Mining: collapse of abandoned mine workings and longwall coal extraction can produce surface depressions and sinkholes.
  • Dissolution: groundwater dissolving limestone, gypsum, or salt creates karst cavities that may collapse into sinkholes.
  • Natural compaction and tectonics: young deltaic sediments compact under their own weight (a major factor in Venice and New Orleans), while tectonic downwarping and isostatic adjustment lower entire basins over geological time.
  • Thawing permafrost and the drainage and oxidation of peat soils also cause widespread surface lowering.

Effects

Subsidence damages buildings, roads, pipelines, and drainage systems, increases flood risk in coastal and riverine areas (combining with sea-level rise), and can reactivate faults. Because aquifer compaction is largely irreversible, subsidence also permanently reduces groundwater storage capacity. Modern monitoring uses precise leveling, GPS, and satellite radar interferometry (InSAR) to measure ground motion to millimetre precision.
