Granville Stanley Hall (February 1, 1846 – April 24, 1924) was an American psychologist and educator, a pioneering figure in the development of psychology in the United States. He founded the first formal American laboratory of psychology at Johns Hopkins University in 1883, founded and served as the first president of Clark University (1889), and in 1892 became the founding president of the American Psychological Association.

After study in Germany, where he encountered the work of Wilhelm Wundt, Hall earned the first American PhD in psychology (Harvard, 1878, under William James). At Johns Hopkins he founded the laboratory and, in 1887, the American Journal of Psychology, the first American journal in the field. At Clark he organised the celebrated 1909 twentieth-anniversary conference at which Freud and Jung lectured — psychoanalysis's formal introduction to America.

Work

Hall's research centred on child development and adolescence, pursued through the "child study" movement and questionnaire methods. His two-volume Adolescence (1904) popularised the notion of adolescence as a distinct developmental stage of "storm and stress" (Sturm und Drang). He interpreted development through recapitulation theory — the idea that individual development replays the evolutionary history of the species — which, along with some of his views on race and eugenics, has since been rejected. Nevertheless, his institution-building permanently established psychology as an American academic and professional discipline.
