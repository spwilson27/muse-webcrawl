Principate

The Principate is the name sometimes given to the first phase of the Roman Empire, extending from the beginning of the reign of Augustus in 27 BC to the end of the Crisis of the Third Century in AD 284, when Diocletian established the more openly autocratic Dominate.

Characteristics

The term derives from princeps, "first citizen" — the title Augustus adopted, echoing the republican princeps senatus. Augustus carefully preserved the forms of the Republic: the Senate continued to meet, consuls were elected, and the emperor's authority was legally expressed as a bundle of republican powers — tribunician power, proconsular imperium, and priesthoods — rather than a crown. In practice, control of the army and the wealth of Egypt made the princeps a monarch.

Development

Successive dynasties — Julio-Claudian, Flavian, Nerva–Antonine, and Severan — gradually exposed the monarchical reality behind the republican façade. Emperors were increasingly acclaimed by the army, and after the chaotic third century, with its rapid turnover of soldier-emperors, Diocletian's reforms dropped the fiction altogether.

Historiography

The Principate/Dominate distinction was systematised by modern historians, notably drawing on Theodor Mommsen; ancient writers did not use the terms, and modern scholars caution that the difference was one of style and ideology more than of constitutional substance.
