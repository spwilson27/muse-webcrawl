Undue influence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In judicial practice, especially in law of equity, undue influence (also known as supplantation) is a doctrine which entails one person taking advantage of a position of power over another person. This inequity in power between the parties can vitiate one party's consent as they are unable to freely exercise their independent will. As a result, transactions procured by undue influence are liable to be set aside by the courts.

Common law doctrine

Undue influence developed in the English courts of equity as a ground for rescinding contracts and gifts, and for challenging wills (where it is a form of testamentary incapacity claim, albeit harder to prove than in equity's more protective approach). It is distinct from duress: the victim's will is not overcome by threats but subsumed by the relationship or by improper pressure that equity will not tolerate.

In the leading modern authority, Royal Bank of Scotland plc v Etridge (No 2) [2001], the House of Lords reorganised the doctrine into actual undue influence, where the claimant proves affirmative improper conduct, and presumed undue influence, where the relationship between the parties (such as solicitor–client, doctor–patient, parent–child, or spiritual adviser–disciple) and a transaction calling for explanation together raise a presumption that the stronger party must rebut, ordinarily by showing the weaker party received independent advice.

Applications

The doctrine frequently arises in cases involving elderly or dependent persons transferring property to carers or relatives; guarantees given by spouses for a partner's business debts (the Etridge context); contracts procured by religious or spiritual advisers; and challenges to wills made in favour of a person in a position of dominance over the testator.

Related fields

"Undue influence" is also used in psychology and elder law for manipulation within relationships of trust, in some jurisdictions as an element of elder financial abuse statutes; in electoral law it refers to improper interference with voters; and in the law of charities and contracts it overlaps with doctrines of unconscionability and coercion.
