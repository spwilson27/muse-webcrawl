Confabulation

Confabulation

FieldPsychology, psychiatry, neurology
Associated conditionsKorsakoff syndrome, dementia, brain injury

In psychology, confabulation is a memory error defined as the production of fabricated, distorted, or misinterpreted memories about oneself or the world. It is generally associated with certain types of brain damage (especially anterior communicating artery aneurysm, Korsakoff syndrome, Alzheimer's disease, and schizophrenia) or a specific set of cognitive deficits. People who confabulate present incorrect memories ranging from subtle inaccuracies to surreal fabrications, and they may present them with confidence — crucially, without the intention to deceive.

Types

  • Provoked confabulation: false statements produced in response to questioning, filling gaps in memory; also seen in mild form in healthy people under memory pressure.
  • Spontaneous confabulation: unprompted, often fantastic false narratives that the patient may act upon, associated with frontal lobe damage and a failure of reality monitoring.

Mechanisms

Theories attribute confabulation to combined failures of memory retrieval, source monitoring, and executive verification: fragments of real memories are misattributed in time or context, and impaired monitoring fails to reject them. The phenomenon informs the broader study of false memory, including eyewitness error, and the term has been borrowed in other fields (including discussions of large language models producing fluent falsehoods) for confident false output.

History

Sergei Korsakoff described the symptom in the 1880s in patients with the amnesic syndrome that bears his name. Confabulation research expanded through the twentieth century with neuropsychological studies of frontal-lobe patients by researchers such as Armin Schnider.
