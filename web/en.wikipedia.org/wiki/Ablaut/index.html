In linguistics, ablaut (from German ab- "off/down" + Laut "sound"), also called vowel gradation or apophony, is a system of apophony in which the vowel of a word changes to indicate grammatical function, as in English sing–sang–sung and foot–feet. Ablaut was a central feature of Proto-Indo-European morphology and survives most visibly in the strong verbs of the Germanic languages.

Indo-European ablaut

Proto-Indo-European roots characteristically contained the vowel *e, which alternated in predictable "grades" according to accent and morphological category:

GradeVowelTypical use
e-grade (full)*epresent stems, nominative singulars
o-grade*operfect singulars, derived nouns
zero grade∅ (vowel lost)weak forms, plurals of the perfect
lengthened grade*ē / *ōcertain nominatives, aorists

Thus the root *bʰer- "carry" gives *bʰér- (e-grade), *bʰór- (o-grade) and *bʰr̥- (zero grade, with syllabic r), forms continued in different ways in Greek, Sanskrit, Latin and Germanic.

In English

Old English strong verbs were organised into seven ablaut classes reflecting the Indo-European alternations; about 180 survive, often irregular today: drive–drove–driven (class I), choose–chose–chosen (class II), begin–began–begun (class III) and so on. Ablaut also appears in noun plurals inherited from old stem alternations (foot–feet, via i-mutation, a related umlaut process) and in derivational pairs such as song/sing and blood/bleed.
