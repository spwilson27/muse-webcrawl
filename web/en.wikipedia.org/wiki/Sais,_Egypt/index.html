Sais (Ancient Greek: Σάϊς; Egyptian: Zau, modern Sa el-Hagar) was an ancient Egyptian city in the western Nile Delta. It was the capital of the fifth nome of Lower Egypt and became the seat of royal power under the Twenty-fourth Dynasty and, most famously, the Twenty-sixth (Saite) Dynasty (664–525 BC), the last great native dynasty of pharaonic Egypt, which took its common name from the city.

Religious significance

Sais was the great cult centre of the goddess Neith, a war and weaving goddess depicted wearing the red crown of Lower Egypt, worshipped here from very early times. Her temple was among the most celebrated in Egypt: Herodotus, who describes the city in Book II of his Histories, recounts its royal tombs, the shrine of Osiris, the great Festival of Lamps held in the goddess's honour, and a monolithic shrine that took years to transport from Elephantine. Plato's Timaeus locates at Sais the temple where the priest told Solon the story of Atlantis, and Plutarch reports that a statue of the goddess in her temple bore the inscription: "I am all that has been, and is, and shall be, and my robe no mortal has yet uncovered."

History

Occupation at Sais reaches back to the predynastic period, and the city rose to political prominence in the eighth century BC, when its prince Tefnakht founded the Twenty-fourth Dynasty and attempted to unify Egypt. A century later Psamtik I, a Saite prince raised to power with Assyrian and later Greek and Carian mercenary support, reunified the country and inaugurated the Saite renaissance — a period of renewed art, administration, and archaic revival in Egyptian culture centred on Sais. Saite kings including Necho II and Amasis were buried in the royal necropolis within the temple enclosure, as Herodotus describes. After the Persian conquest of 525 BC the city's importance ebbed, though its temples remained in use into Roman times.

Archaeology

Almost nothing stands above ground at Sa el-Hagar today: the stone was quarried for building and the mudbrick dug out by sebakhin (fertiliser diggers), leaving a vast, pitted mound. Auguste Mariette and Heinrich Brugsch noted scattered remains in the 19th century, and excavations by the Egypt Exploration Society, directed by Penelope Wilson from 1997, have mapped the enormous temple enclosure of Neith, the Great Pit feature, and settlement remains using survey and augering, reconstructing in part the plan of the lost city.
