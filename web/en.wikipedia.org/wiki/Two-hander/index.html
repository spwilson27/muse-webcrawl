Two-hander

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In theatre, a two-hander is a play or other dramatic piece with only two characters, normally two performers. The format concentrates the drama on the relationship, conflict and chemistry between the pair, and is economical to produce, making it popular with small companies and fringe theatres. The term is also used more loosely in film and television criticism for a work dominated by two lead characters.

Characteristics

With no other characters to relieve the tension or carry exposition, the two-hander typically relies on sustained dialogue, reversals of power between the pair, and gradual revelation of character. Settings are often confined or single-location — a room, a prison cell, a park bench — which heightens the intensity and keeps production costs low. The form is well suited to psychological drama, verbal duelling and comedy built on a double act.

Examples

Well-known stage two-handers include Edward Albee's The Zoo Story (1958), Willy Russell's Educating Rita (1980), John Logan's Red (2009), about the painter Mark Rothko and his assistant, Conor McPherson's The Birds, and Duncan Macmillan's Lungs. In film, Richard Linklater's Before Sunrise (1995) is frequently cited as a two-hander, as is My Dinner with Andre (1981).

Related forms

The two-hander is related to the monologue and the one-person show (with even fewer performers), and contrasts with the ensemble piece. A musical two-hander is sometimes called a chamber musical.
