Psychological manipulation

Psychological manipulation
FieldSocial psychology, clinical psychology
Common tacticsGaslighting, guilt-tripping, love bombing, silent treatment, triangulation, victim playing
Associated traitsMachiavellianism, narcissism, psychopathy (Dark Triad)
Related conceptsCoercive control, brainwashing, propaganda, social engineering

Psychological manipulation is a type of social influence that aims to change the behaviour or perception of others through indirect, deceptive, or underhanded tactics. Unlike open persuasion, manipulation advances the manipulator's interests — often at the expense of the target — while concealing its true intent. Because the tactics exploit psychological vulnerabilities such as the desire for approval, fear of conflict, or uncertainty about one's own perceptions, manipulation is widely regarded as exploitative and is associated with abusive relationships and coercive control.

Tactics

Recognised manipulation tactics include:

  • Gaslighting — persistently denying or distorting events so that the victim doubts their memory and judgement.
  • Love bombing — overwhelming a target with affection and attention early in a relationship to create dependency.
  • Silent treatment — withholding communication as punishment or leverage.
  • Triangulation — introducing a third party into a conflict to provoke jealousy or insecurity.
  • Playing the victim — presenting oneself as injured or helpless to evoke guilt and compliance.
  • Guilt-tripping, blame-shifting and moving the goalposts — keeping the target perpetually at fault and off balance.

Personality correlates

Research in personality psychology links habitual manipulation to the Dark Triad traits: Machiavellianism (a cynical, strategic willingness to deceive), subclinical narcissism, and subclinical psychopathy. George K. Simon's clinical work on manipulative personalities, and Harriet Braiker's Who's Pulling Your Strings? (2004), popularised frameworks for recognising manipulative behaviour in everyday relationships.

Contexts

Manipulation occurs in intimate relationships, families, workplaces and politics. At a mass scale it overlaps with propaganda and disinformation; in security contexts, related techniques are studied as social engineering. Many jurisdictions have begun to criminalise patterns of coercive and controlling behaviour in domestic settings.

Defences

Psychologists recommend recognising tactics, maintaining independent sources of information and social support, setting boundaries, and documenting interactions where manipulation is suspected.
