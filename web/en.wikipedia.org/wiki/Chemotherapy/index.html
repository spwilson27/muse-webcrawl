Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy is a type of cancer treatment that uses one or more anti-cancer drugs (chemotherapeutic agents) to kill or slow rapidly dividing cells. Given in cycles, usually intravenously or orally, it may be curative, adjuvant (after surgery/radiation to prevent recurrence), neoadjuvant (before, to shrink a tumour), or palliative.

Because chemotherapy targets division, its side effects hit the body's other fast-dividing tissues: hair loss, nausea and vomiting (much better controlled with modern antiemetics), bone-marrow suppression (infection, anaemia), and infertility. The field began from observations of nitrogen mustard (a chemical weapon) on blood cells in the 1940s, and from Sidney Farber's antifolate treatment of childhood leukaemia (1948). Modern practice increasingly combines or replaces classic cytotoxics with targeted drugs, hormonal therapy and immunotherapy matched to the tumour's molecular profile.
