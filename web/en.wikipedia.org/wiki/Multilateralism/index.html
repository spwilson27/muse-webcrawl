Multilateralism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Multilateralism is the practice of coordinating national policies among three or more states, in contrast to unilateral action by one state or bilateral dealings between two. In its post-1945 institutional form, multilateralism denotes general principles of conduct — indivisibility of interests, diffuse reciprocity and dispute-resolution by agreed rules — embodied in organisations such as the United Nations, the WTO and the international financial institutions, forming the core of the "liberal international order".

History and stress

Precursors include the Concert of Europe and the League of Nations; the system's zenith followed the Cold War, with near-universal membership of global institutions and waves of treaty-making. Since the 2010s multilateralism has faced strain from great-power rivalry, Security Council deadlock, WTO appellate paralysis, withdrawal politics (treaties on climate, arms control and health exited or re-entered across US administrations) and the rise of "minilateral" groupings (G7, G20, BRICS, Quad, AUKUS) that supplement or bypass universal bodies. Defenders argue interdependence — climate, pandemics, finance — makes rule-based cooperation indispensable; critics dispute its distributional fairness and its constraint on democratic self-government.
