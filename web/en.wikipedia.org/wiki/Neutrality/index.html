Neutrality (international relations)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Neutrality is the legal and political status of a state that does not participate in a war between other states, and — in its permanent form — commits in advance to abstain from all future wars and military alliances. The laws of neutrality were codified in the Hague Conventions of 1907 (rights and duties of neutral powers: inviolability of territory, impartiality, restrictions on belligerent use of neutral territory and on state supply of war material).

Permanent neutrality

Switzerland's perpetual neutrality (recognised in 1815) is the archetype, joined by Austria's constitutional neutrality (1955, the price of Soviet withdrawal), and in different forms by Ireland (military neutrality without constitutional codification), Turkmenistan (UN-recognised, 1995) and historically by Belgium and Laos. Finland and Sweden maintained Cold War neutrality or non-alignment — abandoned for NATO membership in 2023 and 2024 after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, leaving European neutrality a smaller camp. Neutrality survives in tension with UN collective-security obligations (resolved in practice by participating in sanctions and peacekeeping short of war), EU mutual-assistance clauses, and arms-export politics, as Swiss re-export refusals in the Ukraine war illustrated.
