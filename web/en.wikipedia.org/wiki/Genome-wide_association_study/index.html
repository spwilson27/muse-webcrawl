Genome-wide association study

A genome-wide association study (GWAS), also known as a whole-genome association study, is an observational study of a genome-wide set of genetic variants in many individuals, used to determine whether any variant is statistically associated with a trait or disease. GWAS typically focus on associations between single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and traits such as major human diseases, but can equally be applied to any other genetic variant and any other organism.

Method

The standard design compares two large groups: people with a disease (cases) and similar people without it (controls). DNA from each participant is genotyped, usually on SNP arrays reading hundreds of thousands to millions of positions. If allele frequencies of a variant differ significantly between the groups, the variant is said to be associated with the disease. Because stringent multiple-testing correction is required, the conventional threshold for genome-wide significance is p < 5×10−8. Results are commonly visualised as Manhattan plots, and cumulative findings are collected in resources such as the GWAS Catalog.

History and findings

The approach became practical in the mid-2000s with the International HapMap Project's map of linkage disequilibrium and affordable genotyping arrays. An early landmark study in 2005 identified variants in the complement factor H gene associated with age-related macular degeneration. In 2007 the Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium published associations for seven common diseases, and GWAS have since been run for thousands of traits.

GWAS have identified thousands of robust associations — for example loci influencing height, type 2 diabetes, coronary artery disease, schizophrenia and inflammatory bowel disease — illuminating previously unsuspected biological pathways and drug targets. Polygenic scores aggregating many small effects can now stratify disease risk, though their predictive power and portability across ancestries remain active research questions.

Limitations

Most associated variants lie in non-coding regions and confer small effects, so the causal gene and mechanism are often unclear. Findings may also be confounded by population stratification, and early studies were criticised for over-representing participants of European ancestry, a bias later efforts have worked to correct.
