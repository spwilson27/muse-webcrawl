Participle

Part of speechNon-finite verb form
English typesPresent participle (-ing); past participle

A participle (from Latin participium, "sharing, partaking", a calque of Greek metochḗ) is a non-finite verb form that has some of the characteristics and functions of both verbs and adjectives. More narrowly, participles have been defined as "verb forms used as adjectives" and as verb forms that combine with auxiliaries to form tenses.

In English

English has two kinds of participle:

  • The present participle, ending in -ing (e.g. running, speaking), used with forms of be to make progressive tenses ("she is running") and as an adjective ("running water"). The same -ing form used as a noun is traditionally called a gerund.
  • The past participle, typically ending in -ed, -en or irregularly formed (e.g. walked, written, sung), used with have in perfect tenses ("she has written") and with be in passive constructions ("it was written"), and as an adjective ("a broken window").

Participial phrases such as "Walking down the street, she saw…" function adverbially; when the phrase fails to attach to the subject of the main clause, usage writers call it a "dangling participle".

In other languages

Many languages have richer participial systems. Ancient Greek had participles marked for tense, voice and mood and declined for case, gender and number. Latin had present active, perfect passive, future active and future passive participles. Slavic languages distinguish adverbial participles (transgressives) from adjectival ones. In such languages a single participle can carry the content of an entire subordinate clause.
