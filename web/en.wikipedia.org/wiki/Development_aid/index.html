Development aid

Development aid is financial aid given by governments and other agencies to support the economic, environmental, social and political development of developing countries. When provided by governments it is usually termed Official Development Assistance (ODA), a concept formalised by the OECD's Development Assistance Committee. Unlike humanitarian aid, which responds to emergencies, development aid targets long-term growth and poverty reduction.

Forms of aid

Aid may be given as grants, concessional loans, technical assistance, debt relief, food aid or project funding. Tied aid must be spent on goods and services from the donor country, while untied aid may be procured anywhere. Aid is delivered bilaterally (government to government), multilaterally (through institutions such as the World Bank and UNDP), or through charities and NGOs.

History

Modern aid began with post-war reconstruction programmes such as the Marshall Plan and expanded during decolonisation. In 1970 the UN set the target of 0.7% of gross national income for ODA, a level only a few countries (such as Norway, Sweden and Luxembourg) regularly meet.

Effectiveness debate

Scholars debate whether aid promotes growth. Supporters cite successes in health (smallpox and near-eradication of polio, antiretroviral programmes) and the Green Revolution; critics such as William Easterly and Dambisa Moyo argue aid can entrench corruption and dependency. The Paris Declaration (2005) and later agreements sought to improve ownership, alignment and results measurement.
