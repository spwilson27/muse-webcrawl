Thermopolium

A thermopolium (from Greek thermos, "hot", and poleo, "to sell") was a commercial establishment in the ancient Roman world where patrons could buy ready-to-eat food and drink — essentially an ancient fast-food counter or snack bar. Because many urban Romans lived in apartment blocks without kitchens, thermopolia were ubiquitous.

Description

A typical thermopolium had a masonry counter facing the street, into which large earthenware jars (dolia) were set, holding hot food and drinks such as wine. Many had small dining rooms at the back and colourful wall paintings advertising their offerings. Around 80 thermopolia have been identified at Pompeii alone; a particularly well-preserved example, the Thermopolium of Asellina, retains its counter, jars and even coins. In December 2020 archaeologists announced a fully excavated Pompeian thermopolium with vivid frescoes of a sea nymph, a rooster and a dog, and food residues including duck, goat and snail.

Social standing

Elite Roman writers regarded thermopolia as disreputable haunts of the lower classes, and emperors periodically tried to restrict them, but they remained central to everyday urban life.
