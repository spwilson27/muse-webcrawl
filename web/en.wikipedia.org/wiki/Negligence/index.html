Negligence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Negligence is a failure to exercise appropriate care — doing something a reasonably prudent person would not do, or failing to do what they would do — which causes loss or harm to another and amounts, in law, to a breach of a duty of care. It is the most important modern tort, underpinning liability for road traffic accidents, workplace injuries, defective products in part, and professional (including medical) malpractice.

Elements

A claimant must generally prove: a duty of care owed by the defendant; breach of that duty (judged against the reasonable person, or the standard of a competent professional for skilled defendants); causation (factual "but-for" causation and legal remoteness/foreseeability); and actual loss or damage. Landmark cases include Donoghue v Stevenson (1932) — the snail in the ginger-beer bottle — founding manufacturers' duties to consumers, and Palsgraf (1928) in US law on foreseeability's limits.

Defences and apportionment

Defences include contributory or comparative negligence (reducing recovery by the claimant's share of fault; in a few US states any fault bars recovery entirely), voluntary assumption of risk, and statutory immunities. Insurance — compulsory for driving and common for professionals — in practice funds most negligence compensation, making the system as much about loss distribution as fault.
