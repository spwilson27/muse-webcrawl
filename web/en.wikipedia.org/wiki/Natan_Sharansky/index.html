Natan Sharansky
Born: Anatoly Borisovich Shcharansky, January 20, 1948, Stalino (now Donetsk), Ukrainian SSR, Soviet Union
Nationality: Israeli
Known for: Soviet refusenik and human rights activist; Israeli politician

Natan Sharansky (Hebrew: נתן שרנסקי; born Anatoly Borisovich Shcharansky, January 20, 1948) is an Israeli political figure and author. A Soviet dissident and refusenik, he spent nine years in Soviet prisons after being convicted on fabricated charges of treason and espionage; following his release in 1986 he emigrated to Israel, where he founded a political party, served in several ministerial posts, and later chaired the Jewish Agency for Israel.

Soviet dissent and imprisonment

Sharansky, a computer scientist and talented chess player, applied to emigrate to Israel in 1973 and was refused on security grounds, becoming a prominent "refusenik." He became a leading spokesman for the Soviet Jewish emigration movement and a member of the Moscow Helsinki Watch Group. In 1977 he was arrested by the KGB and in 1978 convicted of treason and spying for the United States and sentenced to 13 years of imprisonment and forced labor. He spent much of his sentence in solitary confinement and on hunger strikes, while his wife Avital campaigned internationally for his release.

Release and Israeli career

On February 11, 1986, after sustained international pressure, Sharansky was freed in a prisoner exchange at the Glienicke Bridge in Berlin and immediately emigrated to Israel, where he was greeted by Prime Minister Shimon Peres. He founded the Yisrael BaAliyah party to represent Russian-speaking immigrants and served as Minister of Industry and Trade, Minister of the Interior, Minister of Construction and Housing, and Deputy Prime Minister, as well as Minister for Jerusalem and Diaspora Affairs. From 2009 to 2018 he chaired the Executive of the Jewish Agency for Israel.

Writings

His memoir Fear No Evil (1988) recounts his imprisonment. In The Case for Democracy (2004), co-authored with Ron Dermer, he argued that free societies are inherently more peaceful, distinguishing between "free societies" and "fear societies"—ideas that influenced American foreign-policy debate.
