Albert Kesselring (30 November 1885 – 16 July 1960) was a German military officer and convicted war criminal who served in the Luftwaffe during World War II. In a career which spanned both world wars, Kesselring reached the rank of the Generalfeldmarschall (Field Marshal) and became one of Nazi Germany's most highly decorated commanders.

Kesselring was born in Marktsteft, Bavaria, and joined the Imperial German Army in 1904 as an artillery officer. He served on both the Western and Eastern fronts in the First World War and remained in the Reichswehr, transferring to the newly created Luftwaffe in 1933, where he became its chief of staff in 1936. During the Second World War he commanded air fleets in the invasions of Poland and France and in the Battle of Britain.

Commander in the Mediterranean

From late 1941 Kesselring served as Commander-in-Chief South (Oberbefehlshaber Süd), directing German forces in the Mediterranean and North Africa, where he worked alongside Erwin Rommel. After the Allied invasion of Italy in 1943 he conducted a skilful defensive campaign up the Italian peninsula, holding positions such as the Gustav Line at Monte Cassino and delaying the Allied advance until the final weeks of the war. In March 1945 he was transferred to command Germany's forces in the West.

War crimes trial and later life

Kesselring was tried by a British military court in Venice in 1947 for war crimes, including his role in reprisal orders that led to the Ardeatine massacre in Rome, in which 335 Italian civilians and prisoners were murdered. He was sentenced to death, but the sentence was commuted to life imprisonment, and he was released in 1952 on health grounds. He published memoirs and became a figurehead for veterans' organisations before dying in Bad Nauheim in 1960.
