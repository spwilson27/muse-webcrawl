Mecha

A giant piloted robot, the archetypal mecha of Japanese animation

In science fiction, mecha (Japanese: メカ, derived from the English word "mechanical") are large piloted machines, usually humanoid walking vehicles. In Japanese usage, the term originally applied broadly to mechanical devices of all kinds, but internationally it has come to denote the giant robots that are a staple of Japanese manga, anime, and video games.

Characteristics

Mecha vary enormously in size and design, from powered exoskeletons little larger than a human to robots tens or hundreds of metres tall. Most are controlled by a pilot seated in a cockpit, although some are remote-controlled or autonomous. In fiction they are used for warfare, labour, exploration, and sport, and they are often depicted with remarkable agility despite their size.

Japanese mecha are commonly divided into two broad categories. Super robots, exemplified by Mazinger Z (1972), are heroic, near-magical machines with fantastic weapons, piloted by hot-blooded heroes. Real robots, introduced by Mobile Suit Gundam (1979), treat mecha as mass-produced military hardware subject to maintenance, logistics, and realistic physics, operated by ordinary soldiers rather than superheroes.

History

Giant robots appeared in Japanese popular culture as early as the 1940s, and the manga and anime Tetsujin 28-go (1956) featured a remote-controlled giant robot. Go Nagai's Mazinger Z established the piloted cockpit format. Yoshiyuki Tomino's Mobile Suit Gundam shifted the genre toward realism and became an enormous franchise, supported by plastic model kits (Gunpla) produced by Bandai. Subsequent landmark series include Super Dimension Fortress Macross, Neon Genesis Evangelion, and Gurren Lagann.

In other media

Mecha appear widely in Western media as well, including the films Pacific Rim and Avatar, the BattleTech/MechWarrior franchise, and games such as Titanfall. Real-world walking vehicles and powered exoskeletons are sometimes compared to fictional mecha, though practical bipedal military vehicles remain infeasible with current technology.
