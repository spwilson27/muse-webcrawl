The Caliphate of Córdoba (Arabic: خلافة قرطبة) was an Islamic state ruled by the Umayyad dynasty from 929 to 1031, controlling much of the Iberian Peninsula (al-Andalus) and part of North Africa, with Córdoba as its capital.

History

Abd al-Rahman III, emir of Córdoba since 912, proclaimed himself caliph in 929, asserting independence from the Abbasid Caliphate of Baghdad. Under him and his son al-Hakam II, Córdoba became one of the largest and most prosperous cities in the world, renowned for its libraries, scholarship, and the Great Mosque.

Decline

After the death of the powerful chamberlain al-Mansur, civil war (the Fitna of al-Andalus) fractured the state. The caliphate was abolished in 1031 and splintered into independent taifa kingdoms.

Culture

The caliphate was a major center of learning, where Muslim, Christian, and Jewish scholars worked in relative coexistence, advancing medicine, astronomy, philosophy, and agriculture.
