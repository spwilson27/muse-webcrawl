Historicity of the Bible

Historicity of the Bible
Field: Biblical studies; archaeology of the Levant
Related: Biblical criticism; maximalism/minimalism debate

The historicity of the Bible concerns the degree to which the narratives of the Hebrew Bible and New Testament correspond to historical events, and the use of the Bible as a source for reconstructing the history of ancient Israel and the early church.

Overview

Scholarly positions range from "biblical maximalism", which accepts much of the narrative as historical unless disproved, to "minimalism", associated with the Copenhagen School, which treats the Bible primarily as a literary work of the Persian and Hellenistic periods. Mainstream scholarship generally finds firm historical grounding from roughly the period of the Israelite and Judahite kingdoms onward, corroborated by inscriptions such as the Mesha Stele and Tel Dan Stele, while treating the patriarchal narratives, Exodus and conquest accounts as largely legendary or heavily theologised traditions.

New Testament

For the New Testament, historians broadly accept the baptism and crucifixion of Jesus as historical events, while debating the miraculous elements. Archaeology and external sources such as Josephus and Tacitus provide partial corroboration.

References: Dever, What Did the Biblical Writers Know and When Did They Know It? (2001); Finkelstein & Silberman, The Bible Unearthed (2001).
