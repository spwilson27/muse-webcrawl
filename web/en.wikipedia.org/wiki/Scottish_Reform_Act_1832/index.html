Scottish Reform Act 1832

Representation of the People (Scotland) Act 1832
Citation2 & 3 Will. IV c. 65
Enacted1832
JurisdictionScotland

The Scottish Reform Act 1832, formally the Representation of the People (Scotland) Act 1832, was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that reformed the election of MPs from Scotland to the House of Commons. It was the Scottish counterpart to the Reform Act 1832 for England and Wales.

Background

Before 1832, Scotland's 45 MPs were chosen by a tiny electorate: county voters numbered under 3,000, and burgh MPs were elected by self-perpetuating town councils. Reform agitation, led by figures such as Francis Jeffrey, demanded change.

Provisions

The Act increased Scottish seats from 45 to 53, distributing them to growing industrial cities including Glasgow, Edinburgh, Aberdeen, Dundee, and Paisley. In counties the franchise went to £10 owners and certain tenants; in burghs to £10 householders. The electorate rose roughly from under 5,000 to about 65,000.

Effects

The Act broke the oligarchic control of burgh councils over parliamentary elections and ended the most extreme anomalies, though the franchise remained limited and further reforms followed in 1868 and 1884–85.
