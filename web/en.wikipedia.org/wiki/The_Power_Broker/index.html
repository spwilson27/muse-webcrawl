The Power Broker: Robert Moses and the Fall of New York is a 1974 biography of urban planner and public official Robert Moses by American journalist and historian Robert Caro. Running to more than 1,200 pages, the book chronicles Moses's accumulation of unelected power over half a century — through control of authorities such as the Triborough Bridge and Tunnel Authority and a dozen simultaneous posts — and how he used that power to reshape New York City and its suburbs with expressways, bridges, parks, beaches and housing, displacing hundreds of thousands of residents. The Power Broker won the Pulitzer Prize for Biography in 1975 and is consistently ranked among the greatest non-fiction books of the twentieth century; the Modern Library placed it among the 100 best English-language nonfiction books of the century.

Research and writing

Caro, then a reporter at Newsday, conceived the book after taking a course on urban planning at Harvard and realising how little anyone knew about how Moses had actually acquired his power. He spent seven years on the project, conducting hundreds of interviews with Moses's allies and enemies and reading mountains of archival material; famously, Moses, who initially cooperated, broke off relations as the book's critical portrait emerged. Caro's editor at Knopf, Robert Gottlieb, cut some 350,000 words from the manuscript. The author later said the work taught him his enduring lesson: that political power, properly understood, reveals itself through its effects on the powerless, not only through the lives of the powerful.

Contents and argument

The biography follows Moses from his idealistic beginnings as a reformer trained at Yale, Oxford and Columbia, through his invention of the public authority as a self-financing engine beyond electoral control, his alliance with Governor Al Smith, his wars with Franklin D. Roosevelt, and his postwar dominance — the building of Jones Beach, the Triborough and Verrazzano-Narrows bridges, Lincoln Center, the 1964 World's Fair, and the expressway network, including the ruinous Cross Bronx Expressway driven through settled neighbourhoods. Caro also documents the racial dimensions of Moses's planning, including low parkway bridges allegedly intended to keep buses — and thus poorer and Black New Yorkers — from Jones Beach (a claim debated by later scholars), and the exclusionary design of some public amenities. The book closes with Moses's decline: stripped of power in 1968 when Governor Nelson Rockefeller merged the Triborough authority into the Metropolitan Transportation Authority.

Influence and reassessment

The book transformed the historiography of American cities, made "power" itself its central analytical subject, and became a set text in planning, politics and journalism. Moses's reputation has been partly rehabilitated by some later writers and exhibitions, who credit his achievements and question aspects of Caro's account, but the biography's framework — and its author's subsequent fame through the Lyndon B. Johnson volumes, whose method it established — remain dominant. During the COVID-19 pandemic the book's cover became a familiar sight on the shelves behind television interviewees, a meme widely remarked in the press.
