Historical regions of Latvia

The historical regions of Latvia are the culturally and historically distinct areas within Latvia whose boundaries have formed over centuries of shifting rule. The Constitution of Latvia recognises four historical lands: Vidzeme, Latgale, Kurzeme (Courland) and Zemgale (Semigallia); Sēlija (Selonia) is often counted as a fifth cultural region, and in 2022 Selonia was formally recognised in law as a historical land as well.

The regions

Vidzeme

Vidzeme, in the northeast, is the most populous region and contains the capital, Riga. Its name means "Middle Land". Historically known as Livonia, it was ruled by Sweden in the 17th century — the era remembered as the "good Swedish times" — before passing to the Russian Empire.

Latgale

Latgale, in the southeast, is distinguished by its predominantly Catholic faith and the Latgalian language, a distinct variety of Latvian. It developed separately as part of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, and its culture retains strong Polish and Russian influences.

Kurzeme (Courland)

Kurzeme occupies the west, centred on Liepāja and Ventspils. It was the heartland of the Duchy of Courland and Semigallia, which in the 17th century maintained its own overseas colonies in Tobago and the Gambia. The Livonian coast in the north preserves the heritage of the Livonian people.

Zemgale (Semigallia)

Zemgale, the fertile central-southern plain around Jelgava, formed the other half of the Duchy of Courland and Semigallia and is Latvia's breadbasket, home to the baroque Rundāle Palace.

Sēlija (Selonia)

Sēlija, in the southeast along the Daugava, takes its name from the Selonian tribe. Sparsely populated and heavily forested, it was given formal recognition as a historical Latvian land by legislation adopted in 2021–2022, including its own coat of arms.

Symbolism

Each region possesses its own coat of arms, and regional identities remain significant in Latvian cultural life, folklore and dialect, even though the regions have no separate administrative governments. The official statistical and planning regions of modern Latvia are based loosely on, but do not exactly match, the historical lands.
