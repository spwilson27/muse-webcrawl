Payday loan

A payday loan (also called a payday advance, salary loan, or cash advance loan) is a small, short-term, high-cost loan intended to cover a borrower's expenses until their next payday. Typically a few hundred dollars advanced for about two weeks, it carries a flat fee — commonly $15–$30 per $100 borrowed — that corresponds to an annual percentage rate (APR) of several hundred per cent. Payday lending is one of the most debated products in consumer finance: the industry presents it as emergency credit for the underserved, while regulators and consumer advocates criticise it as a debt trap.

How it works

Traditionally offered through storefront lenders in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and elsewhere, a payday loan requires proof of income and a bank account; the borrower writes a post-dated cheque or authorises an automatic debit for the loan amount plus fee, due on payday. Because the loans are small and borrowers often have poor credit, lenders argue that high fixed fees are needed to cover underwriting and default costs. Online lenders later moved the model onto the internet, sometimes operating across borders or under tribal or offshore structures to avoid state rules.

Borrowers and the "debt trap" debate

Research by the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) found that most payday loans are taken by repeat borrowers: a large majority of loans are rolled over or followed within two weeks by another loan, and many borrowers end up paying more in fees than they originally borrowed. Defenders of the industry counter that customers are aware of the costs, use the product rationally for emergencies, and would face worse alternatives — bank overdraft fees, utility reconnection charges, or illegal lenders — if payday credit were banned. The typical borrower in US studies is a renter earning modest wages without access to a credit card.

Regulation

Rules vary widely. In the United States, lending is mainly governed by state law: some states cap rates effectively banning the product, others license lenders with fee limits and databases to prevent multiple concurrent loans; the Military Lending Act caps APR at 36 per cent for service members. The CFPB's 2017 rule requiring ability-to-repay underwriting was largely rescinded in 2020, though payment provisions survived litigation. In the UK, the Financial Conduct Authority imposed a price cap in 2015 — interest capped at 0.8 per cent per day and total cost at 100 per cent of the amount borrowed — after which loan volumes fell sharply and major lender Wonga collapsed in 2018.

Alternatives and criticism

Proposed substitutes include small-dollar loans from credit unions (such as "payday alternative loans" in the US), employer salary advances, earned-wage access apps, and community lending funds. Critics argue the core problem is income volatility and low wages among borrowers rather than the loan product itself; the industry argues that restricting supply without substitutes harms the very consumers regulators aim to protect.
