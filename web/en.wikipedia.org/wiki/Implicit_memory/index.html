Implicit memory

Implicit memory is a type of long-term memory in which previous experiences influence current behaviour or performance without conscious recollection of those experiences. It contrasts with explicit (declarative) memory, in which facts and events are recalled deliberately. Implicit memory is revealed through facilitation in task performance — a phenomenon called priming — and through the learning of skills and habits.

Implicit memory operates without conscious awareness of the original learning episode.

Forms of implicit memory

The best-known form is procedural memory: knowing how to ride a bicycle, type, or play an instrument persists even when the learner cannot describe when or how the skill was acquired. Other forms include perceptual and conceptual priming, in which prior exposure to a word or picture makes it easier to identify later, and classical conditioning, in which an emotional or physiological response becomes attached to a previously neutral stimulus.

Evidence from amnesia

Much of the evidence for a separate implicit system comes from patients with amnesia. The famous patient H.M., who lost the ability to form new explicit memories after surgery on the medial temporal lobes, could nevertheless learn new motor skills such as mirror tracing, improving day by day while denying he had ever performed the task. Amnesic patients also show normal priming on word-completion tests while failing to recognise the same words as ones they studied. These dissociations indicate that implicit memory depends on brain structures — including the basal ganglia, cerebellum, and neocortex — distinct from the hippocampal system that supports explicit recall.

Everyday significance

Implicit memory underlies habits, preferences formed by mere exposure, and many of the automatic processes of skilled performance. Because it operates outside awareness, it is also invoked in explanations of implicit bias and of the subtle influence of advertising.
