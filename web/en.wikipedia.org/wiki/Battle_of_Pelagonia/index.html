The Battle of Pelagonia took place in September 1259, between the Empire of Nicaea and an anti-Nicaean alliance consisting of the Despotate of Epirus, the Principality of Achaea, and the Kingdom of Sicily under Manfred. It was a decisive event in the history of the eastern Mediterranean, paving the way for the Nicaean recovery of Constantinople and the restoration of the Byzantine Empire in 1261.

The Pelagonian plain, where the Nicaean army destroyed the allied host in 1259.

Background

The Nicaean emperor Michael VIII Palaiologos sought to expand into Greece and recover Constantinople from the Latin Empire. Against him, Michael II of Epirus formed an alliance with William II Villehardouin, Prince of Achaea, and Manfred of Sicily, who sent troops and whose alliance was sealed by marriages. The allied army marched into Macedonia in 1259.

The battle

The Nicaean army, commanded by the emperor's brother John Palaiologos, exploited the divisions in the allied camp. The illegitimate son of Michael II, John Doukas, deserted to the Nicaeans with his troops, and Manfred's and Epirote contingents quarrelled with their allies. In the ensuing battle on the plain of Pelagonia the allied army was routed; William of Achaea was captured hiding under a haystack, and much of the flower of Frankish chivalry in Greece was killed or taken prisoner.

Aftermath

The victory left Nicaea dominant in the Balkans. William was released in 1262 only after surrendering the fortresses of Monemvasia, Mistra, and Maina, giving the Byzantines their foothold in the Morea. Two years after Pelagonia, Nicaean forces retook Constantinople, ending the Latin Empire.
