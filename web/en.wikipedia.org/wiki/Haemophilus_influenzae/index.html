Haemophilus influenzae

Haemophilus influenzae (formerly Pfeiffer's bacillus) is a Gram-negative, non-motile, coccobacillary bacterium in the family Pasteurellaceae. It was first described by Richard Pfeiffer in 1892 during an influenza pandemic, and was mistakenly thought to cause influenza until the viral cause was identified in 1933.

Biology

H. influenzae requires haem (factor X) and NAD (factor V) for growth, growing on chocolate agar. Encapsulated strains are classified into serotypes a–f; serotype b (Hib) is the most virulent. Non-typeable strains commonly colonise the upper respiratory tract.

Disease

Before vaccination, Hib was a leading cause of bacterial meningitis in young children, and also caused epiglottitis, pneumonia, bacteraemia and septic arthritis. Non-typeable strains cause otitis media, sinusitis, conjunctivitis and exacerbations of COPD.

Vaccine

The Hib conjugate vaccine, introduced in the late 1980s and early 1990s, dramatically reduced invasive Hib disease in immunised populations and is part of routine childhood immunisation schedules worldwide.
