Qajar Iran, officially the Sublime State of Iran and known to Europeans as Persia, was the Iranian state under the Qajar dynasty from 1789 to 1925. The Qajars, a Turkmen tribe, reunified Iran under Agha Mohammad Khan, who made Tehran his capital, and ruled the country through a period of territorial loss, great-power rivalry and, ultimately, constitutional revolution.

History

Under Fath-Ali Shah (r. 1797–1834), Iran fought two disastrous wars with the Russian Empire, losing the Caucasian territories by the treaties of Gulistan (1813) and Turkmenchay (1828). The long reign of Naser al-Din Shah (1848–1896) brought the telegraph, photography, early modernising reforms under the minister Amir Kabir, and a notorious series of economic concessions to British and Russian interests — the tobacco concession of 1890 provoked a nationwide boycott and was cancelled. Anglo-Russian rivalry over Iran, the "Great Game", culminated in the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907, which divided the country into spheres of influence.

The Constitutional Revolution of 1905–1911 forced Mozaffar ad-Din Shah to grant a constitution and parliament (Majles), but civil war, foreign intervention and the discovery of oil at Masjed Soleyman in 1908 deepened Iran's entanglement with Britain. During the First World War, occupied and famine-stricken Iran remained nominally neutral. The last Qajar shah, Ahmad Shah, was deposed in 1925 by Reza Khan, who founded the Pahlavi dynasty.

Society and culture

Qajar Iran was a largely rural, tribally organised society dominated by the court, landowners and the Shia clergy (ulama), whose authority grew over the period. Qajar art is noted for its large-scale oil portraits of the shahs, enamelwork, and photography, which the court embraced earlier than most non-European monarchies.
