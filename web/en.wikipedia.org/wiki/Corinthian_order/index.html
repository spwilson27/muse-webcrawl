Corinthian order

The Corinthian order is the last developed of the three principal classical orders of ancient Greek and Roman architecture (the others being the Doric and Ionic). It is distinguished by its ornate capital, carved with two rows of acanthus leaves and small volutes (scrolls). The Roman writer Vitruvius credited its invention to the sculptor Callimachus, inspired by acanthus leaves growing around a votive basket.

Characteristics

A Corinthian column shaft is usually fluted and stands on a base; its slender proportions are similar to the Ionic. The capital consists of an inverted bell shape (the kalathos) decorated with acanthus leaves, from which rise volutes supporting the abacus. The entablature above follows the Ionic pattern, with a continuous frieze that may carry relief sculpture.

History and use

The order was used sparingly in Greek architecture — an early example is the Temple of Apollo Epicurius at Bassae — but was enthusiastically adopted by the Romans, for example on the Pantheon in Rome. Revived in Renaissance, Baroque and Neoclassical architecture, it became the order of choice for grand public buildings, banks and courthouses. In the United States it appears prominently on the Capitol and many state capitols.
