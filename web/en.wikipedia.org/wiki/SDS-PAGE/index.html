SDS-PAGE (sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis) is a discontinuous electrophoretic system developed by Ulrich K. Laemmli, used to separate proteins according to their electrophoretic mobility, which for SDS-denatured proteins is essentially a function of molecular mass alone. It is the most widely used protein separation technique in biochemistry and molecular biology, and a first step for techniques such as Western blotting and many proteomics workflows.

Principle

Samples are boiled in sample buffer containing the anionic detergent SDS and a reducing agent such as β-mercaptoethanol or dithiothreitol. SDS binds along the polypeptide backbone at a roughly constant ratio (about 1.4 g SDS per gram of protein), unfolding the protein and swamping its intrinsic charge with a uniform negative charge per unit length. As a result, in an electric field all proteins migrate toward the anode at rates determined mainly by the sieving effect of the gel — that is, by their size.

The Laemmli system

The classic system uses two gel layers at different pH and acrylamide concentrations. In the low-percentage stacking gel (pH 6.8), a moving ionic boundary compresses the sample into a thin band; in the resolving gel (pH 8.8) the proteins unstack and separate by size. Gels of higher acrylamide percentage resolve smaller proteins. After the run, proteins are visualised by staining, commonly with Coomassie brilliant blue or silver stain, and molecular weights are estimated against a ladder of standard proteins.

Applications

SDS-PAGE is used to assess protein purity, estimate molecular weight, monitor expression and purification, and analyse protein samples before immunoblotting or mass spectrometry. Variants include native PAGE (no SDS), blue native PAGE, and two-dimensional gel electrophoresis, which combines isoelectric focusing with SDS-PAGE.
