Album-oriented rock (AOR, originally called album-oriented radio) is an FM radio format created in the United States in the 1970s that focused on the full repertoire of rock album tracks rather than only commercial singles. It developed out of the freeform and progressive rock stations of the late 1960s.

History

In the late 1960s, FM stations such as WNEW-FM in New York and KSAN in San Francisco pioneered freeform programming in which disc jockeys played extended album cuts of their own choosing. During the early 1970s, consultants such as Lee Abrams systematized this approach into the AOR format: still album-based, but with researched playlists of proven tracks and tighter presentation, aimed largely at young adult male listeners. By the late 1970s AOR had become the dominant rock format on American FM radio.

Characteristics

AOR playlists emphasized established rock artists — bands such as Led Zeppelin, Pink Floyd, Boston, Journey, Foreigner, Styx and Rush — and the format was closely associated with the commercial peak of arena rock. Critics charged that researched AOR playlists narrowed musical diversity and marginalized punk, new wave, and Black artists, while the format's proponents credited it with making the rock album the central commercial artifact of the era.

Decline and legacy

In the 1980s and 1990s the AOR format fragmented into successor formats, including classic rock (drawn from AOR's older library), mainstream rock, active rock and alternative rock. The album-oriented approach nonetheless shaped the economics of the record industry and the canon of classic rock that remains a staple of radio and streaming playlists.
