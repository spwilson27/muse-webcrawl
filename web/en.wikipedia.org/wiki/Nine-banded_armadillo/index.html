The nine-banded armadillo (Dasypus novemcinctus), also called the nine-banded long-nosed armadillo or common long-nosed armadillo, is a species of armadillo native to North, Central, and South America, making it the most widespread of the armadillos. Its ancestors originated in South America and reached North America as part of the Great American Interchange, and the species has continued to expand its range northward through the United States over the past century.

Description

Adults typically weigh 2.5–6.5 kg (5.5–14 lb) and measure 38–58 cm (15–23 in) in body length, with a tail of 12–45 cm. The animal is covered by an ossified bony shell (carapace) of scutes, with about nine movable bands across the midsection that give the species its name and allow flexibility. It has a long, pointed snout, large upright ears, and strong claws used for digging. When startled, a nine-banded armadillo may leap straight upward 60–120 cm into the air — a behaviour that can be dangerous on roads, as frightened animals may jump into passing vehicles.

Habitat and behaviour

The species inhabits rainforest, grassland, and scrub, favouring areas near permanent water. Armadillos are solitary, primarily nocturnal foragers that dig burrows and feed on insects — especially termites and ants — as well as earthworms, small vertebrates, eggs, and some plant matter. They have poor eyesight but a keen sense of smell. They are strong swimmers and can hold their breath underwater for several minutes.

Reproduction

Nine-banded armadillos exhibit obligate polyembryony: a single fertilized egg splits to produce genetically identical quadruplets in every litter. Females can also delay implantation of the embryo for months. Young are born with soft, leathery skin that hardens within weeks.

Disease research

The nine-banded armadillo is one of the few non-human animals known to carry Mycobacterium leprae, the bacterium that causes leprosy (Hansen's disease), and wild armadillos in the southern United States have been linked to a small number of human infections. Because of its low body temperature and unique reproduction, the species has been an important laboratory model for leprosy research.
