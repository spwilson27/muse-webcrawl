The AMPA receptor (AMPAR) is an ionotropic transmembrane receptor for glutamate — a non-NMDA-type receptor — that mediates fast excitatory synaptic transmission in the central nervous system. It is named after its selective synthetic agonist AMPA.

Structure and function

AMPA receptors are tetramers assembled from four types of subunits, GluA1–GluA4 (historically GluR1–4), each with an extracellular amino-terminal and ligand-binding domain and a transmembrane region forming the ion channel. They are expressed throughout the brain, concentrated at postsynaptic densities. Most AMPA receptors contain the GluA2 subunit, whose RNA editing (Q/R site) makes the channel impermeable to calcium and linearises its current–voltage relation; GluA2-lacking receptors are calcium-permeable and inwardly rectifying.

Role in synaptic plasticity

Changes in the number and conductance of postsynaptic AMPA receptors are the principal expression mechanism of long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD), the leading cellular models of learning and memory. NMDA-receptor-dependent calcium influx triggers trafficking of AMPA receptors into (LTP) or out of (LTD) the synapse.

Clinical relevance

Excessive AMPA receptor activation contributes to excitotoxicity in stroke and epilepsy; the antagonist perampanel is an approved antiepileptic drug, and NBQX is a classic research antagonist. AMPA receptor modulators ("ampakines") have been investigated for cognitive enhancement and depression; autoantibodies against AMPA receptors occur in a form of limbic encephalitis.
