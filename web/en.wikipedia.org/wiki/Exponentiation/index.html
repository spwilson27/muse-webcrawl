Exponentiation

Exponentiation: bn is the product of multiplying the base b by itself n times.

Exponentiation is a mathematical operation, written as bn, involving two numbers: the base b and the exponent or power n. When n is a positive integer, exponentiation corresponds to repeated multiplication of the base: bn is the product of multiplying b by itself n times. Thus 23 = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8.

Definitions and extensions

The definition extends beyond positive integers. A zero exponent gives 1 (for nonzero base); a negative integer exponent gives the reciprocal, b−n = 1/bn. Rational exponents are defined through roots: b1/n is the nth root of b. Real exponents are defined by continuity or via the exponential function, bx = ex ln b, and complex exponents through Euler's formula. The case 00 is left undefined in many contexts, though it is conventionally treated as 1 in combinatorics and algebra.

Laws of exponents

For nonzero bases and real exponents, the principal identities are: bmbn = bm+n; bm/bn = bm−n; (bm)n = bmn; and (ab)n = anbn. Unlike multiplication, exponentiation is neither commutative (23 ≠ 32) nor associative, so towers of powers are conventionally read from the top down (right-associative).

History

The notation of a raised exponent was introduced by René Descartes in La Géométrie (1637). Words such as "square" and "cube" preserve the geometric origins of powers in Greek and Indian mathematics. Michael Stifel's Arithmetica integra (1544) developed exponent arithmetic systematically, and John Wallis and Isaac Newton extended exponents to fractional and negative values in the 17th century.

Applications

Exponentiation underlies exponential growth and decay, compound interest, the decibel and Richter scales, scientific notation, polynomial algebra, and algorithms such as exponentiation by squaring used in modular arithmetic and cryptography.
