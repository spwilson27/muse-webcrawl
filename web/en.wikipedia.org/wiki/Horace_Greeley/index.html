Horace Greeley

Horace Greeley
Born: February 3, 1811, Amherst, New Hampshire
Died: November 29, 1872, Pleasantville, New York
Occupation: Newspaper editor, politician
Known for: Founding the New-York Tribune; 1872 presidential candidate

Horace Greeley (February 3, 1811 – November 29, 1872) was an American newspaper editor and publisher who was the founder and editor of the New-York Tribune, among the most influential newspapers of its time. Long active in politics, he served a brief term in the United States House of Representatives and was the nominee of the Liberal Republican Party, and subsequently of the Democratic Party, in the 1872 presidential election.

Early life and career

Born to a poor farming family in New Hampshire, Greeley was apprenticed as a printer at fourteen. He moved to New York City in 1831 nearly penniless and worked as a compositor and editor. In 1834 he founded The New Yorker, a literary weekly, and in 1841 he launched the New-York Tribune, whose daily and weekly editions soon reached an enormous national readership.

The Tribune

Under Greeley the Tribune championed Whig and then Republican politics, attacked slavery, and promoted reform causes from temperance and labor rights to women's suffrage and vegetarianism. Greeley hired notable contributors including Margaret Fuller and Karl Marx, who served as a European correspondent. He is popularly associated with the phrase "Go West, young man", encouraging western settlement, though he credited the saying to John B. L. Soule. During the Civil War his editorials sometimes clashed with President Abraham Lincoln, and in 1864 he pursued an unsuccessful peace feeler with Confederate agents at Niagara Falls.

1872 campaign and death

In 1872 Greeley broke with President Ulysses S. Grant and accepted the Liberal Republican nomination for president; the Democrats also nominated him. The campaign was a failure, and his wife died shortly before the election, which he lost in a landslide. Broken by the defeat and personal loss, Greeley died on November 29, 1872, before the Electoral College met; his electoral votes were scattered among other candidates.
