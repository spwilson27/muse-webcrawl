Sriracha

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A bottle of sriracha sauce with its rooster logo]

Sriracha is a type of hot sauce made from a paste of chili peppers, distilled vinegar, garlic, sugar and salt. The name comes from the coastal city of Si Racha in Thailand, where a sauce of this kind was first made (the Thai original, associated with Thanom Chakkapak in the 1930s, is thinner and sweeter). The world's best-known version is the California-made Huy Fong Foods sauce — founded by Vietnamese-born David Tran in 1980, in the clear bottle with a green cap and rooster logo — which became a global culinary phenomenon in the 2000s–2010s.

History

Tran, an ethnic-Chinese refugee from Vietnam, began selling sauces in Los Angeles and named his company after the cargo ship (Huy Fong) that carried him out of Vietnam. The sauce spread from phở shops to mainstream American kitchens largely by word of mouth, since the company famously did not advertise. Imitators and spin-offs followed, and "sriracha" became a generic flavour label on snacks, mayonnaise and even lip balm.

Production and shortages

Huy Fong's sauce uses red ripe jalapeños grown for it in Mexico. Supply shocks — drought in Mexico, and a dispute with its longtime Californian pepper grower Underwood Ranches — caused widely reported shortages in 2022–2023, with bottles resold at large markups. Other producers launched rival versions; Underwood now sells its own sauce.

Use

Sriracha is squirted over phở and other noodle soups, eggs, pizza, burgers, sushi rolls and fries, mixed into mayonnaise ("sriracha mayo"), and used as a marinade and cocktail ingredient. Its heat is moderate (about 2,200 Scoville units for the Huy Fong version), with garlic and a fermented tang.
