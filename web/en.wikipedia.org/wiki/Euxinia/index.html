Euxinia

Euxinia is the condition of a body of water being both anoxic (depleted of dissolved oxygen) and sulfidic (containing free hydrogen sulfide). The term derives from the name of the Black Sea — the ancient Greek Pontos Euxeinos, "Hospitable Sea" — whose deep waters are the largest euxinic reservoir in the modern world.

Modern examples

Euxinic conditions develop where water-column stratification restricts circulation and oxygen supply to deep water while organic matter decomposition generates sulfide. Modern examples include the deep basin of the Black Sea below roughly 150–200 metres, the Cariaco Basin off Venezuela, various silled fjords such as Framvaren in Norway, and seasonally, parts of the Baltic Sea deeps.

Euxinia in Earth history

Geochemical evidence — including iron speciation, molybdenum isotopes and biomarkers such as isorenieratane from photosynthetic sulfur bacteria — indicates that euxinia was far more widespread in the ancient oceans, particularly during oceanic anoxic events of the Mesozoic and in the Proterozoic, when a sulfidic deep ocean (the "Canfield ocean") may have persisted for hundreds of millions of years. Euxinic expansion has been implicated in several mass extinctions, including the end-Permian and end-Triassic events, where hydrogen sulfide toxicity and associated climate effects stressed marine and terrestrial life.
