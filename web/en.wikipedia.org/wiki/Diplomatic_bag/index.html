Diplomatic bag

A diplomatic bag, also known as a diplomatic pouch, is a container with certain legal protections used for carrying official correspondence or other items between a diplomatic mission and its home government or other diplomatic, consular, or otherwise official entities. The physical concept of a "bag" is flexible; it may be a briefcase, a cardboard box, a shipping crate or even a shipping container. Under Article 27 of the 1961 Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, the diplomatic bag "shall not be opened or detained" by the host country, and the packages constituting it must bear visible external marks of their character.

Legal basis and practice

The Convention requires that the bag be used only for diplomatic documents or articles intended for official use, and that the sending state allow receiving states to challenge misuse through diplomatic channels rather than by opening. Diplomatic couriers carrying bags are likewise protected. In practice states sometimes abuse the bag — for smuggling currency, narcotics or weapons components — and receiving states have responded by x-ray scanning (technically short of "opening") or refusing oversized containers. A famous incident occurred in 1984, when the kidnapped former Nigerian minister Umaru Dikko was found drugged in a crate at Stansted Airport labelled as a diplomatic bag; because the crate lacked the required external marks, British authorities held it was not a lawful bag, and the episode led Nigeria and Britain to tighten procedures. In 1964 an Egyptian diplomatic bag opened in Rome was found to contain a bound and gagged Israeli intelligence agent, and in 2012 Italy intercepted a bag-related arms shipment linked to Iran, illustrating recurring controversies.

Related concepts

Non-embassy destinations also receive "bag" services — for example, the U.S. Department of State's Diplomatic Pouch and Mail service supplies overseas posts; Britain's equivalent historically was carried by the Queen's (now King's) Messengers. Consular bags enjoy similar, slightly weaker protections under the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations (1963).
