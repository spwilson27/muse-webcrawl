Bird ringing

A bird ringer applying a leg ring during a ringing session.
Also calledBird banding (North America)
PurposeIndividual identification for migration, survival and population studies

Bird ringing (known as bird banding in North America) is the attachment of a small, individually numbered metal or plastic tag to the leg or wing of a wild bird to enable individual identification. This identification makes it possible to study bird migration, longevity, survival rate, behaviour, morphology and population changes.

History

Early attempts at marking birds date back centuries, but scientific ringing began with the Danish schoolteacher Hans Christian Cornelius Mortensen, who in 1899 fitted aluminium rings to European starlings. Organised schemes rapidly followed: the British scheme began in 1909, the German Rossitten observatory scheme in 1903 under Johannes Thienemann, and the United States federal banding programme in 1920. Co-ordinating centres such as EURING (Europe) and national ringing centres collate recoveries; the British scheme is administered by the British Trust for Ornithology (BTO).

Methods

Birds are typically caught in mist nets at constant-effort ringing stations or observatories, or ringed as nestlings in the nest. Trained, licensed ringers record species, age, sex, wing length, weight and moult before release. Rings are made of aluminium or harder alloys such as incoloy and stainless steel for long-lived seabirds. Larger birds and special studies may use colour rings, neck collars, wing tags, geolocators or GPS trackers readable at a distance.

Data and findings

Recoveries of ringed birds — through recapture, resighting or birds found dead — revealed the great migration flyways, wintering areas, and longevity records, such as a Manx shearwater that lived more than fifty years. Only a small fraction of ringed birds are ever recovered, but the very large numbers ringed annually (millions worldwide) yield statistically powerful data on survival and dispersal, essential to bird conservation.
