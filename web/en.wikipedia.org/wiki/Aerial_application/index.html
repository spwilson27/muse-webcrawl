Aerial application, commonly called crop dusting, is the use of aircraft to apply protective or nutritive materials — pesticides, herbicides, fungicides, fertilisers, and sometimes seed — to agricultural crops, forests, or public-health targets such as mosquito breeding areas. Purpose-built agricultural aircraft fly low passes over fields, releasing material from hoppers through booms or spreaders guided by GPS swath-guidance systems.

History

The first aerial application experiments took place in 1921, when a Curtiss JN-6 biplane dusted lead arsenate over catalpa trees in Ohio to control sphinx moth larvae; the U.S. Department of Agriculture and the aviation pioneer Étienne Dormoy developed early dedicated equipment in the following years. After the Second World War, converted trainers such as the Boeing-Stearman were widely used, and manufacturers including Grumman (Ag Cat), Piper (Pawnee), Thrush, and Air Tractor produced purpose-built agricultural aircraft, increasingly turboprop-powered and able to carry several thousand litres.

Operations

Agricultural pilots ("ag pilots") fly precise, low-level patterns a few metres above the crop, using GPS light-bar or display guidance to avoid skipped or double-covered swaths, and load chemicals through closed systems to reduce exposure. Applications include aerial seeding of cover crops and rice, fertiliser top-dressing (long important in New Zealand hill country), forest spraying, and emergency locust control.

Regulation and concerns

Because of the risk of spray drift onto neighbouring land, water, and people, aerial application is tightly regulated: buffer zones, weather limits, and applicator certification are standard. Health and environmental controversies — from the use of Agent Orange defoliants in Vietnam to banana-plantation spraying disputes in Latin America — have made it a recurring subject of litigation and debate. Since the 2010s, multirotor drones have taken over a growing share of precision spraying, especially in East Asia.
