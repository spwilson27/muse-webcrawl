Chestnut blight is a devastating fungal disease of chestnut trees caused by Cryphonectria parasitica. Introduced to North America on imported Asian chestnut stock around the turn of the 20th century, it destroyed the American chestnut as a canopy tree across eastern forests — one of the worst ecological disasters in recorded forestry history.

Disease cycle

The fungus enters through bark wounds and forms sunken orange cankers that girdle the stem, killing the cambium and starving the crown. Spores spread by wind, rain, and birds. Infection kills the tree above the canker, though roots often survive and send up sprouts that are themselves eventually infected.

Impact

Identified in 1904 at the Bronx Zoo, the blight spread through the entire range of the American chestnut within about 50 years, killing an estimated four billion trees. The loss removed a keystone wildlife food source (chestnuts fed bears, deer, and turkeys) and a valuable rot-resistant timber.

Control and restoration

In Europe the disease is moderated by naturally occurring hypoviruses that weaken the fungus. North American restoration efforts include backcross breeding with blight-resistant Chinese chestnut (led by The American Chestnut Foundation) and the development of transgenic blight-tolerant trees expressing a wheat oxalate oxidase gene.
