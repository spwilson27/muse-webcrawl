Epirus is a geographical and historical region in southeastern Europe, now shared between northwestern Greece and southern Albania. The core of Epirus lies in the modern Greek region of Epirus, with its capital at Ioannina, while the northern part extends into Albania (sometimes called Northern Epirus by Greek sources). It is a rugged, mountainous land dominated by the Pindus range, bounded by the Ionian Sea to the west.

History

In antiquity Epirus was inhabited by Greek-speaking tribes — the Chaonians, Molossians, and Thesprotians — and hosted the oracle of Zeus at Dodona, among the oldest in Greece. Under the Molossian king Pyrrhus (319/318–272 BC), whose costly victories over Rome gave us the term "Pyrrhic victory", Epirus briefly became a major Hellenistic power. Conquered by Rome in 167 BC, it later formed part of the Byzantine world; after the Fourth Crusade, the Despotate of Epirus (1205–1479) was one of the Byzantine successor states. The region then endured centuries of Ottoman rule until the southern part joined Greece in the Balkan Wars of 1912–13.

Geography and culture

Epirus is Greece's most mountainous and rainiest mainland region, known for the Vikos Gorge, the stone villages of Zagori, traditional polyphonic singing (recognised by UNESCO), and a historically pastoral economy supplemented by emigration and remittances. The region's inscribed monuments and inscriptions are key sources for its ancient history.
