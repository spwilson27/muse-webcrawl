Joseph John Campbell (March 26, 1904 – October 30, 1987) was an American writer and professor of literature at Sarah Lawrence College who worked in comparative mythology and comparative religion. His work covers many aspects of the human experience. Campbell's best-known work is his book The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949), in which he discusses his theory of the journey of the archetypal hero shared by world mythologies, termed the monomyth. It has had a notable influence on film and literature, including the work of George Lucas on Star Wars.

Early life and education

Joseph Campbell was born in White Plains, New York, on March 26, 1904, the eldest son of Josephine (née Lynch) and Charles William Campbell, a hosiery importer. He was raised in an upper-middle-class Irish Catholic family. In 1919, a fire destroyed the family home in New Rochelle, killing his grandmother and injuring his father, who was attempting to rescue her.

Campbell attended Dartmouth College, where he studied biology and mathematics, and later transferred to Columbia University, where he received his Bachelor of Arts degree in English literature in 1925 and a Master of Arts degree in medieval literature in 1927. A keen athlete, Campbell excelled at track and field and was for a time among the fastest half-mile runners in the world.

Career

Campbell continued his studies in Paris and Munich, immersing himself in medieval literature, Sanskrit, and the work of modernist artists and writers such as James Joyce and Thomas Mann. Returning to the United States at the onset of the Great Depression, he spent several years reading in a cabin in Woodstock, New York, before joining the faculty of Sarah Lawrence College in 1934, where he taught comparative literature until his retirement in 1972.

In 1944, Campbell co-authored, with Henry Morton Robinson, A Skeleton Key to Finnegans Wake, the first major study of Joyce's final novel. His 1949 work The Hero with a Thousand Faces, awarded the Bollingen Prize, synthesized mythological narratives from around the world into a single pattern of the hero's adventure. Between 1959 and 1968 he published the four-volume The Masks of God, covering primitive, Oriental, Occidental, and creative mythology.

The monomyth

Campbell described the basic narrative pattern of the hero's journey as follows: "A hero ventures forth from the world of common day into a region of supernatural wonder: fabulous forces are there encountered and a decisive victory is won: the hero comes back from this mysterious adventure with the power to bestow boons on his fellow man." The journey proceeds through stages of departure (or separation), initiation, and return.

Later years and death

Campbell spent his later years in Honolulu, Hawaii, lecturing widely. In 1985 he received the National Arts Club Gold Medal of Honor in Literature. He died of esophageal cancer on October 30, 1987, shortly after completing the filming of a series of interviews with journalist Bill Moyers at George Lucas's Skywalker Ranch. The interviews were broadcast posthumously in 1988 as the PBS series Joseph Campbell and the Power of Myth, accompanied by a bestselling book of the same name. Campbell is also known for the advice "Follow your bliss."

Influence

Campbell's ideas have influenced writers, filmmakers, and artists. George Lucas credited The Hero with a Thousand Faces with shaping the narrative structure of Star Wars. The Joseph Campbell Foundation, founded in 1990, preserves and promotes his work, including the publication of his Collected Works.
