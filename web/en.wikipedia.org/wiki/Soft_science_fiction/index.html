Soft science fiction

Soft science fiction, or soft SF, is a category of science fiction with two possible meanings: (1) fiction that explores the "soft" sciences — psychology, anthropology, sociology, linguistics, political science, and economics — rather than physics and engineering; and (2) science fiction in which the science is not rigorously plausible, serving mainly as a backdrop for story and character. It is conventionally contrasted with hard science fiction, which emphasizes scientific accuracy and technical detail.

Definition and usage

The terms "hard" and "soft" SF are modeled on the division between the natural sciences and the social sciences. The first sense — fiction about the soft sciences — is the older: stories about first contact through anthropology, alien cultures, psychology, and social change. Ursula K. Le Guin, trained in an anthropologist's household, exemplified this with works such as The Left Hand of Darkness (1969), which examines gender and society on an alien world. The second sense — fiction loose with physical science — is often applied, sometimes dismissively, to space opera and science fantasy, such as Star Wars or the works of Ray Bradbury, whose The Martian Chronicles uses Mars as a poetic setting rather than a scientific one.

History

The New Wave movement of the 1960s and 1970s pushed literary style, psychology, and social speculation to the forefront, expanding soft SF's prestige. Writers associated with soft SF include Le Guin, Bradbury, Joanna Russ, Samuel R. Delany, and later Kim Stanley Robinson in his climate-focused work. The distinction remains informal: many works combine rigorous social speculation with rigorous physics, and critics caution that "hard" and "soft" describe emphases, not quality.

Examples

  • The Left Hand of Darkness and The Dispossessed by Ursula K. Le Guin
  • The Martian Chronicles and Fahrenheit 451 by Ray Bradbury
  • Woman on the Edge of Time by Marge Piercy
  • Stand on Zanzibar by John Brunner
