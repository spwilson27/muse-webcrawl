Gigantes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The Gigantes in battle with the Olympian gods, as depicted on an ancient Greek vase.

In Greek mythology, the Gigantes (Greek: Γίγαντες, singular Gigās), often rendered in English as the Giants, were a race of great strength and aggression, though not necessarily of great size. They were the children of Gaia (Earth), born from the blood that fell upon her when Uranus (Sky) was castrated by his son Cronus.

The Gigantomachy

The Gigantes are best known for the Gigantomachy, their war against the Olympian gods for supremacy of the cosmos. According to prophecy, the Giants could not be killed by gods alone but required the aid of a mortal hero; Zeus therefore enlisted Heracles, whose arrows finished off the Giants struck down by the gods. Prominent Gigantes include Alcyoneus, who was immortal in his homeland of Pallene; Porphyrion, who attacked Hera and Zeus; Enceladus, buried under Mount Etna; and Polybotes, crushed beneath the island of Nisyros.

Depictions

In early Greek art the Gigantes appear as heavily armed hoplite warriors, but from the fourth century BC onward they were increasingly depicted with serpent-like legs. The Gigantomachy was a popular subject in Greek sculpture and vase painting, most famously on the frieze of the Pergamon Altar, and symbolized the triumph of order over chaos and of Greek civilization over barbarism.

Distinction from the Titans

The Gigantes are distinct from the Titans, an earlier generation of divine beings who fought the Olympians in the Titanomachy. Although later authors sometimes conflated the two conflicts, in the earliest sources the Gigantomachy and Titanomachy are separate wars.
