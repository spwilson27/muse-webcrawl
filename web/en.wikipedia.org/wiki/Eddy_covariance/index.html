Eddy covariance

The eddy covariance (also called eddy correlation or eddy flux) technique is a key atmospheric measurement method for quantifying vertical turbulent fluxes of heat, water vapour, and gases such as carbon dioxide and methane within atmospheric boundary layers. It is the standard micrometeorological technique used to measure ecosystem–atmosphere exchange over areas ranging from fields and forests to oceans.

Method

The technique uses fast-response instruments — typically a three-dimensional sonic anemometer paired with an infrared gas analyser — sampling at 10–20 Hz. The flux is computed as the covariance between fluctuations in vertical wind speed and fluctuations in the quantity of interest (temperature, humidity, or gas concentration) over averaging periods of about 30 minutes. The area contributing to the measurement, the flux footprint, typically extends upwind by hundreds of metres.

Applications

Eddy covariance towers form global networks such as FLUXNET, whose data underpin understanding of the terrestrial carbon cycle, evapotranspiration, and ecosystem responses to climate change. Measurements require extensive corrections — for density fluctuations (the Webb–Pearman–Leuning correction), sensor separation, spectral losses, and periods of weak turbulence at night — and are subject to a well-known tendency to underestimate fluxes, seen in poor surface energy-balance closure.
