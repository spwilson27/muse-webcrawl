Thou

The word thou (/ðaʊ/) is a second-person singular pronoun in English. In standard modern English, thou continues to be used in some regional dialects, particularly in parts of Northern England, and in literary, liturgical, and poetic contexts — most famously in the works of Shakespeare and in the King James Bible — but elsewhere it has been almost entirely displaced by you.

History

From Old English þū, thou was originally the singular counterpart to the plural ye/you. In Middle and Early Modern English, usage developed along the lines of the T–V distinction found in many European languages: thou (with its forms thee, thy, thine) addressed intimates, children, social inferiors, or God in prayer, while you was used deferentially and to superiors. During the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, you generalised to all singular address in standard speech, and thou acquired an archaic or solemn flavour — paradoxically the opposite of its earlier intimacy.

Surviving uses

Members of the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers) long used "plain speech" with thee and thou on grounds of equality. Dialectal forms survive in areas such as Yorkshire and Lancashire, often reduced to tha. The pronoun also survives in fixed phrases ("fare thee well"), in the Lord's Prayer and other liturgy, and in dialects of other English-speaking regions. Its verb forms took distinctive endings, such as thou art, thou hast, and thou dost.

Grammar

Thou is the nominative form; the oblique form is thee; possessives are thy (before consonants) and thine (before vowels and as a pronoun); the reflexive is thyself.
