The Axial Age (German: Achsenzeit) is a term coined by the German philosopher Karl Jaspers in The Origin and Goal of History (1949) to describe the period from roughly 800 to 200 BCE, during which, he argued, remarkably similar revolutionary developments in religious and philosophical thought occurred — independently and in parallel — across China, India, Persia, the Near East, and Greece. Jaspers described this era as an "axis" of world history, a turning point on which later spiritual and intellectual life pivoted.

Characteristics

According to Jaspers, the Axial Age saw the emergence of universalising ethical and metaphysical thinking that broke with earlier mythic and ritual frameworks. Human beings began, in his account, to reflect consciously on existence as a whole, on the self, and on transcendence. Characteristic figures include Confucius and Laozi in China; the authors of the Upanishads, the Buddha and Mahavira in India; Zoroaster in Persia; the Hebrew prophets such as Isaiah and Jeremiah; and in Greece the philosophers from Thales to Plato, together with Homer and the tragedians.

Common themes identified by later scholars include a new emphasis on individual morality and conscience, universal ethics over tribal custom, scepticism toward ritual and myth, and the conceptual separation of a transcendent order from the mundane world.

Explanations

Scholars have proposed various explanations for the apparent simultaneity of these developments: the growth of cities and trade, rising prosperity and literacy in the wake of the Iron Age, increased warfare and political fragmentation (as in China's Warring States period), and the psychological effects of growing social complexity. Sociologists such as Robert Bellah, in Religion in Human Evolution (2011), and Shmuel Eisenstadt, who developed the related theory of "multiple modernities", have extended and revised Jaspers's thesis.

Criticism

Critics question whether the parallels are as striking or as simultaneous as the thesis requires, note that dating for figures such as Zoroaster and Laozi is highly uncertain, and argue that the concept imposes a Eurocentric, teleological framework on diverse civilisations. Others object that it neglects Egypt and Mesopotamia, whose reflective traditions long predate the period, and that the "breakthroughs" were the work of small literate elites with limited social reach.
