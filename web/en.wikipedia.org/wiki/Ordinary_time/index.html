Ordinary time

Ordinary Time (Latin: Tempus per annum, "time through the year") is the part of the liturgical year in the Roman Rite of the Catholic Church — and in similar calendars of many Western churches — that falls outside the major seasons of Advent, Christmas, Lent and Easter. Rather than celebrating a particular aspect of the mystery of Christ, Ordinary Time is devoted to the mystery of Christ in all its aspects.

Structure

Ordinary Time comprises 33 or 34 weeks and is divided into two periods. The first begins on the Monday after the Feast of the Baptism of the Lord (the end of the Christmas season) and lasts until the day before Ash Wednesday. After the Easter season, it resumes on the Monday after Pentecost and continues until the First Sunday of Advent. Sundays are numbered ("the Nth Sunday in Ordinary Time"), and the lectionary readings work semi-continuously through the Gospels and epistles.

Name

The word "ordinary" does not mean unimportant: it derives from the Latin ordinalis, referring to the numbered (ordered) weeks. The season is nevertheless the longest of the year and includes major feasts such as Trinity Sunday, Corpus Christi, and the Solemnity of Christ the King, with which the liturgical year ends.

Colour and practice

The liturgical colour is green, symbolising hope and growth, except on feasts and solemnities when white, red or violet may be used.
