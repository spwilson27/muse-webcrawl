Argumentum ad populum

[Image: Argumentum ad populum — A political rally, an appeal to popular sentiment]

In argumentation theory, an argumentum ad populum (Latin for "appeal to the people") is a fallacious argument which is based on claiming a truth or affirming something is good because most people think so.

Forms

The argument takes several common forms:

  • Bandwagon appeal — "everyone is doing/believing this, so you should too."
  • Appeal to popularity — a product or idea must be good because it is popular ("the best-selling...").
  • Appeal to the majority — a proposition is true because most people believe it.
  • Appeal to tradition and common practice — closely related forms in which long-standing or widespread practice is cited as proof of correctness.

Analysis

The fallacy is an informal fallacy of relevance: the popularity of a claim bears no logical relation to its truth. Nevertheless, appeals to majority opinion are ubiquitous in advertising and politics, and social psychology documents strong conformity effects (such as the Asch conformity experiments) that make the appeal effective in practice.

Philosophers note that majority opinion may have evidential weight in restricted circumstances — for example, where the majority consists of relevant experts judging independently — which distinguishes the ad populum fallacy from legitimate appeals to consensus, such as scientific consensus reached through peer review.
