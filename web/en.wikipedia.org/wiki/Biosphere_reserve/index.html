Biosphere reserves are areas of terrestrial, marine, and coastal ecosystems designated under UNESCO's Man and the Biosphere Programme (MAB) to promote the conservation of biodiversity together with sustainable development. They serve as "living laboratories" for testing integrated approaches to land and resource management.

Structure

Each biosphere reserve is organised into three interrelated zones: a legally protected core area devoted to conservation; a surrounding buffer zone where activities compatible with conservation — research, monitoring, education, and low-impact tourism — take place; and an outer transition area where sustainable economic and human activities are fostered in cooperation with local communities.

History

The MAB Programme was launched in 1971; the first biosphere reserves were designated in 1976. The Seville Strategy and Statutory Framework, adopted in 1995, clarified the three functions of reserves — conservation, development, and logistic support for research and education. The World Network of Biosphere Reserves grew to more than 700 sites in over 130 countries by the 2020s, covering a surface area comparable to that of China. Well-known reserves include Yellowstone (USA), the Danube Delta (Romania), Uluru (Australia), and the Sundarbans region shared by India and Bangladesh.

Designation and review

Sites are nominated by national governments and reviewed by the MAB International Coordinating Council; designation does not transfer sovereignty and remains under national jurisdiction. A periodic review every ten years evaluates whether reserves still meet the criteria, and sites have occasionally been withdrawn from the network.
