The Tune ship (Norwegian: Tuneskipet) is a Viking ship of the karvi type, built around 900 AD from oak. It was found in a boat grave at Haugen farm on Rolvsøy in Østfold, Norway, in 1867 and excavated by archaeologist Oluf Rygh. It is named for the former Tune municipality in which the farm lay. The ship is now displayed at the Viking Ship Museum in Oslo, alongside the better preserved Oseberg and Gokstad ships.

Description

The Tune ship is built of oak and measures about 18.7 metres long with a beam of 4.2 metres. It would have carried twelve pairs of oars and a crew of roughly 24–30 men, including a helmsman. The mast fish and keelson arrangement indicates a strong mast partner, suggesting a sturdy vessel able to carry a large square sail. Compared with the Gokstad ship, the Tune ship is smaller and somewhat more lightly built; its strong mast suggests it was a fast ship, suited to travel in both coastal and open waters.

Discovery and excavation

The burial mound at Haugen, roughly 60 metres across, was opened in 1867. Inside lay the ship, though the grave had been robbed in antiquity and much of the wood had rotted away. The burial chamber, built of oak logs amidships behind the mast, had been plundered; only fragments of grave goods survived, including pieces of wood, parts of a shield, and remains of a feather bed. The human remains in the chamber indicate a man of high status was buried in the ship around 910 AD.

Preservation

Because of its fragmentary state, the Tune ship is displayed in a reconstruction showing its preserved timbers. It is the third Viking ship in the Viking Ship House collection at Bygdøy, Oslo, managed by the Museum of Cultural History of the University of Oslo.
