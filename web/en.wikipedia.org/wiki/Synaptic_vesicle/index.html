Synaptic vesicle

In a neuron, synaptic vesicles (or neurotransmitter vesicles) store various neurotransmitters that are released at the synapse. These small, spherical membrane-bound organelles — about 40 nanometres in diameter — accumulate in the presynaptic terminal near the active zone. When an action potential arrives and voltage-gated calcium channels open, calcium influx triggers the vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane and discharge their contents into the synaptic cleft by exocytosis, transmitting the signal to the postsynaptic cell. The release is quantised: each vesicle contains a roughly standard packet ("quantum") of a few thousand neurotransmitter molecules.

Composition

Synaptic vesicles contain neurotransmitters such as glutamate, GABA, acetylcholine, dopamine or serotonin, loaded by specific vesicular transporter proteins powered by a proton gradient generated by a vesicular H+-ATPase. Their membranes are enriched in proteins essential for trafficking and fusion: synaptobrevin (VAMP), synaptotagmin (the calcium sensor for fast release), synaptophysin, synapsins (which tether vesicles to the reserve pool), and small GTPases such as Rab3. Estimates suggest a typical vesicle displays a stereotyped copy number of each protein, making synaptic vesicles among the best-characterised organelles in cell biology.

The vesicle cycle

After exocytosis, vesicle membrane is retrieved by endocytosis — including fast "kiss-and-run" retrieval and clathrin-mediated endocytosis — and vesicles are refilled locally and recycled into pools: the readily releasable pool docked at the active zone, a recycling pool, and a larger reserve pool. The SNARE complex (synaptobrevin, syntaxin and SNAP-25) drives membrane fusion, and its cleavage by clostridial neurotoxins (tetanus and botulinum toxins) blocks release, causing paralysis.

History

Vesicles were visualised by electron microscopy in the 1950s, and Bernard Katz's quantal hypothesis — supported by recordings of miniature end-plate potentials — established that neurotransmitter is released in discrete vesicular packets, work recognised by the 1970 Nobel Prize. Later, cell-free reconstitution and genetic studies in the labs of Reinhard Jahn, Thomas Südhof and others defined the molecular fusion machinery, earning Südhof a share of the 2013 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for vesicular trafficking.
