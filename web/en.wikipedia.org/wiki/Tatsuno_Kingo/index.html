Tatsuno Kingo, architect of Tokyo Station

Tatsuno Kingo (辰野 金吾, 13 October 1854 – 25 March 1919) was a Japanese architect born in Karatsu, Saga Prefecture, and a graduate of the Imperial College of Engineering. He later studied in England after being sent by the Ministry of Education. He was a professor at Tokyo Imperial University and is credited with training a generation of Japanese architects, including Nakano Kenkō and others who shaped Meiji and Taishō-era building.

Tatsuno is best known for designing Tokyo Station (opened 1914) and the head office of the Bank of Japan (1896), both of which still stand in central Tokyo.

Biography

Tatsuno was born in 1854 in Karatsu, in what is now Saga Prefecture. He entered the Imperial College of Engineering (Kōbu Daigakkō) in Tokyo, where he studied under the British architect Josiah Conder, the "father of Japanese modern architecture". Graduating in 1879, Tatsuno travelled to Britain in 1880 under a government scholarship, working in the office of the Gothic Revival architect William Burges and studying at University College London.

On returning to Japan in 1883, he became a professor at his alma mater, eventually becoming dean of its successor institution, the College of Engineering of Tokyo Imperial University. In 1886–1887 he briefly resigned to work for the Bank of Japan on the design of its new head office, one of the first modern bank buildings in Japan. He later founded his own architectural practice, Tatsuno Kasai Architects, together with Kasai Manji, completing hundreds of commissions — banks, schools, railway stations, and public buildings — throughout Japan and its territories.

Notable works

  • Bank of Japan head office, Nihonbashi, Tokyo (1896) — a Neo-Baroque stone building, designated an Important Cultural Property.
  • Bank of Japan, Kyoto branch (1906).
  • Tokyo Station, Marunouchi (1914) — the famous red-brick station building, restored to its original appearance in 2012.
  • Manseibashi Station, Tokyo (1912, demolished).
  • Bank of Korea building, Seoul (1912).
  • Nara Hotel (1909), partly attributed to his office.

Legacy

Tatsuno's red-brick, Western-style monumental architecture helped define the appearance of modernising Japan. Tokyo Station's Marunouchi building, his most famous work, survived wartime bombing and was restored in 2012, and remains a symbol of the capital.
