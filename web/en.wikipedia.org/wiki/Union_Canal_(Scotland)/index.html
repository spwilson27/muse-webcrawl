Union Canal (Scotland)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Union Canal is a canal in Scotland, running 31.5 miles (50.7 km) from Lochrin Basin in Edinburgh to Falkirk, where it connects with the Forth and Clyde Canal. Opened in 1822, it was built to carry coal and other minerals from the Lanarkshire coalfields into the capital. Its chief engineer was Hugh Baird, advised by Thomas Telford. Remarkably, the canal is a "contour canal": it follows a single level 73 metres (240 feet) above sea level for its entire length, so it needs no locks along its route — but it crosses deep valleys on three major aqueducts: the Avon, Almond, and Slateford aqueducts, the last carrying the canal over the Water of Leith in Edinburgh.

Decline and revival

Like most British canals, the Union lost freight traffic to the railways after the Edinburgh and Glasgow Railway opened in 1842, and commercial carrying dwindled; the canal was formally closed in 1965, culverted and built over in places. Restoration came with the Millennium Link project, completed in 2001–2002 with National Lottery funding, which reopened the canal for leisure navigation. At Falkirk, the link to the lower Forth and Clyde Canal — whose eleven locks had been demolished — was restored by the Falkirk Wheel (2002), the world's only rotating boat lift, which raises boats 24 metres between the two canals. Today the towpath is a popular walking and cycling route between Edinburgh and Falkirk, and the canal is managed by Scottish Canals.
