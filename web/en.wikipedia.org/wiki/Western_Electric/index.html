Western Electric Co., Inc. was an American electrical engineering and manufacturing company that served from 1869 to 1995 as the primary manufacturer, supplier, and purchasing agent for the companies of the Bell System. The company was responsible for many technological innovations in telecommunications and industrial management, and its Hawthorne Works in Cicero, Illinois, was the site of the famous Hawthorne studies of worker productivity.

History

The company began in 1869 as a partnership between Elisha Gray and Enos Barton in Cleveland, Ohio, manufacturing electrical equipment including telegraph instruments. In 1872 it moved to Chicago and took the name Western Electric Manufacturing Company. In 1881 the American Bell Telephone Company purchased a controlling interest, and Western Electric became the exclusive manufacturer of telephones and exchange equipment for the Bell System.

Western Electric's research arm was consolidated in 1925 with part of AT&T's engineering department to form Bell Telephone Laboratories, jointly owned by AT&T and Western Electric. During the Second World War the company produced radar and military communications equipment.

The Bell System era

For most of the 20th century, telephones in the United States were not sold but leased from the local Bell operating company, and nearly all were manufactured by Western Electric. Its best-known products included the Model 302, the Model 500 (introduced in 1951, designed by Henry Dreyfuss), and the Princess and Trimline telephones. The company also managed the Bell System's supply chain through its distribution houses and teletype subsidiary.

Under a 1956 consent decree, Western Electric was restricted largely to manufacturing for the Bell System. Following the 1984 breakup of AT&T, the company became AT&T Technologies; in 1996 its systems and technology units were spun off as part of Lucent Technologies, and the Western Electric name was retired.

Hawthorne Works and management studies

The Hawthorne Works, a vast plant complex in Cicero, Illinois, employed tens of thousands of workers producing telephone equipment. Experiments conducted there from 1924 to 1932 by Elton Mayo and others gave rise to the "Hawthorne effect" — the observation that workers' productivity responds to the attention they receive — and influenced the human relations school of management.
