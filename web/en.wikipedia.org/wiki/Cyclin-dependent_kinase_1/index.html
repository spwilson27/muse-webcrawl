Cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1), also known as cell division cycle protein 2 homolog (CDC2), is a serine/threonine protein kinase that plays a key role in the regulation of the eukaryotic cell cycle. CDK1 partners with cyclin B to form the maturation-promoting factor (MPF), which drives entry into mitosis; its activity is also involved in the G1/S and G2/M transitions.

History

The gene was first identified in fission yeast as cdc2 in the work of Paul Nurse, who showed that a single CDK could control both major cell cycle transitions. The mammalian homolog was cloned in 1987, demonstrating conservation across eukaryotes. Together with the discovery of cyclins, this work earned the 2001 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Regulation

CDK1 is activated by binding to cyclins (principally cyclin B, and also cyclin A) and by phosphorylation of its activation loop by the CDK-activating kinase (CAK). It is held inactive during G2 by inhibitory phosphorylation on Tyr15 (and Thr14) by the kinases Wee1 and Myt1. At the G2/M boundary, the phosphatase Cdc25 removes these phosphates, triggering a positive feedback loop that produces the abrupt activation of CDK1–cyclin B. Inactivation through cyclin B degradation by the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) is required for exit from mitosis.

Function in mitosis

Active CDK1–cyclin B phosphorylates hundreds of substrates to orchestrate mitotic events, including chromosome condensation, nuclear envelope breakdown (through phosphorylation of lamins), centrosome separation, spindle assembly, and Golgi fragmentation. CDK1 also influences apoptosis and stem cell pluripotency.

Clinical relevance

Because CDK1 is essential for division, it is a target of interest in cancer therapy, though its essential role in normal cells limits selectivity; pan-CDK and CDK1 inhibitors have been investigated in clinical studies.
