Hydrophone

A hydrophone (from Greek hydro-, "water", and phone, "sound") is a microphone designed for use underwater to record or listen to underwater sound. Most hydrophones are based on piezoelectric transducers that generate an electrical signal when subjected to pressure changes. Hydrophones were developed during the First World War for detecting submarines and remain central to sonar, marine biology, oceanography, and underwater communications.

History

The first practical hydrophones were developed in 1914–1916 by French and British teams, including the work of Paul Langevin and Robert Boyle, initially as listening devices and then as components of active echo-ranging systems that became ASDIC and sonar. During the Cold War, the United States deployed vast fixed arrays of hydrophones in the SOSUS network to track Soviet submarines.

Operation

A typical hydrophone consists of a piezoelectric ceramic element (often lead zirconate titanate, PZT) sealed in a waterproof housing, sometimes with a preamplifier to drive long cables. Sensitivity is expressed in decibels relative to 1 volt per micropascal. Because the speed of sound in water (~1,480 m/s) is much greater than in air, a single hydrophone cannot determine direction; directional information is obtained from arrays of hydrophones using beamforming, exploiting the time differences of arrival between elements.

Applications

  • Military sonar and submarine detection
  • Recording whale and dolphin vocalizations for bioacoustics research
  • Monitoring underwater earthquakes and volcanic activity
  • Leak detection in pipelines and inspection of offshore structures
  • Seismic surveying for oil and gas exploration, usually as towed streamers
