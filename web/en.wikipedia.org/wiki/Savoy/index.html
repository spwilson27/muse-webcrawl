Savoy (French: Savoie; Arpitan: Savouè) is a historical and cultural region in the Western Alps of eastern France, corresponding roughly to the modern departments of Savoie and Haute-Savoie. For most of its history it was the core territory of the House of Savoy, whose lands grew into the Kingdom of Sardinia and then the Kingdom of Italy, before Savoy itself was ceded to France in 1860.

The Savoy Alps contain Mont Blanc, Western Europe's highest peak, on the region's border with Italy.

History

The counts of Savoy emerged in the 11th century under Humbert I, controlling the strategic Alpine passes between France and Italy, including the Mont Cenis and the Great and Little St Bernard routes. Their state expanded on both sides of the Alps, acquiring Piedmont and, in 1720, the crown of Sardinia. Although the dynasty's seat moved to Turin, Savoy remained its ancestral homeland. By the Treaty of Turin (1860), the Kingdom of Sardinia ceded Savoy and Nice to France in exchange for French support against Austria; a plebiscite endorsed the annexation, and the region's ruling dynasty went on to become kings of a united Italy in 1861.

Geography and culture

Savoy is dominated by high mountains, deep glacial valleys and large lakes, notably Lake Geneva (shared with Switzerland), Lake Annecy and the Lac du Bourget. It contains some of Europe's most famous ski resorts, including Chamonix, Courchevel and Val d'Isère, and hosted the 1992 Winter Olympics at Albertville. The traditional language is Savoyard, a dialect of Franco-Provençal (Arpitan), now spoken by a small minority. Savoyard cuisine is known for cheese dishes such as fondue, raclette and tartiflette.
