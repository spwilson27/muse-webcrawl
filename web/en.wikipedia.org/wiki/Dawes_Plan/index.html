Dawes Plan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Dawes Plan (1924) was an arrangement negotiated by an international committee chaired by American banker Charles G. Dawes to resolve the crisis over German First World War reparations, which had led to hyperinflation and the Franco-Belgian occupation of the Ruhr in 1923.

Terms

The plan did not fix a total reparations sum but restructured payments to match Germany's capacity: payments began at 1 billion gold marks, rising to 2.5 billion by 1928–29, partly indexed to economic prosperity. The Reichsbank was reorganised under foreign supervision, the new Rentenmark currency stabilised, and a foreign loan of 800 million gold marks — largely raised in the United States — restarted payments. French and Belgian troops withdrew from the Ruhr, and reparations were to be funded from railway bonds, industrial debentures and the budget. Dawes shared the 1925 Nobel Peace Prize with Austen Chamberlain.

Effects and replacement

The plan eased the immediate crisis and underpinned the relative stabilisation of the Weimar Republic in the "Golden Twenties", but it left Germany dependent on short-term American credit: US loans paid German reparations, which funded Allied war-debt payments to the US — a circular flow that collapsed after the Wall Street Crash of 1929. The Dawes Plan was superseded by the Young Plan of 1929–30, which set a final sum and schedule, itself overtaken by the Depression and the Hoover Moratorium.
