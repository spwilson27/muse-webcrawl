Vitamin A is a family of fat-soluble retinoids, principally retinol, retinal, and retinoic acid, essential for vision, immune function, reproduction, embryonic development, and the maintenance of epithelial tissues. It is obtained from animal foods as preformed vitamin A (retinyl esters) and from plant foods as provitamin A carotenoids such as beta-carotene, which the body converts to retinol as needed.

Functions

Retinal is the light-absorbing component of the visual pigments rhodopsin and the iodopsins of the rod and cone photoreceptor cells; deficiency first manifests as night blindness. Retinoic acid acts as a hormone-like transcription regulator, binding nuclear receptors (RAR and RXR) to control genes involved in cell growth and differentiation. Vitamin A also supports immune defences and the integrity of skin and mucosal barriers.

Sources and requirements

Preformed vitamin A is concentrated in liver, fish liver oils, dairy products and eggs; in many countries foods are fortified. Orange and dark-green vegetables — carrots, sweet potatoes, spinach — supply carotenoids. Requirements are expressed in retinol activity equivalents (RAE); the U.S. Recommended Dietary Allowance is 900 µg RAE/day for men and 700 µg RAE/day for women.

Deficiency and excess

Vitamin A deficiency remains the leading preventable cause of blindness in children in low-income countries and increases mortality from infections such as measles. Conversely, excessive intake of preformed vitamin A is toxic: chronic hypervitaminosis A causes liver damage, bone pain and skin changes, and high doses during pregnancy are teratogenic. Beta-carotene from food does not carry the same toxicity risk because its conversion is regulated.
