Lambda phage

Lambda phage (Escherichia virus Lambda, colloquially the lambda phage, λ phage) is a bacterial virus, or bacteriophage, that infects the bacterial species Escherichia coli. Lambda phage is a temperate phage: it can integrate its genome into the host chromosome and remain dormant as a prophage (the lysogenic cycle), or it can enter the lytic cycle, in which the host cell is lysed to release progeny virions. Because of this bistable switch, lambda phage became one of the most important model systems in molecular biology, and the study of its genetic circuitry helped found the field of gene regulation.

Discovery

Lambda phage was discovered by Esther Lederberg in 1950, during experiments at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. She found that a strain of E. coli designated K-12 was lysogenic — it carried an inducible prophage that could be released by ultraviolet irradiation. Jacob and Wollman subsequently used lambda to study bacterial conjugation and genetic recombination. Salvador Luria and others placed lambda at the centre of phage genetics.

Structure

The virion consists of an icosahedral protein head, roughly 60 nm in diameter, containing the linear double-stranded DNA genome, and a long flexible non-contractile tail about 150 nm long ending in a single tail fibre. The genome is 48,502 base pairs with about 73 protein-coding genes. At the ends of the linear genome are 12-nucleotide single-stranded overhangs (the cos sites) that are complementary to each other, allowing the DNA to circularise immediately after infection.

Life cycle

Lysogeny

After adsorption to the LamB receptor on the E. coli surface and injection of its DNA, the phage genome circularises. If the lysogenic pathway is chosen, the phage integrase catalyses site-specific recombination between the phage attachment site (attP) and the bacterial site (attB), inserting the prophage into the chromosome between the gal and bio operons. The prophage is maintained by the CI repressor, which binds the operators OL and OR and shuts down the lytic genes.

Lytic cycle

DNA damage triggers the host SOS response; RecA stimulates autocleavage of CI, derepressing the lytic promoters. Cro protein accumulates and represses the maintenance promoter, committing the cell to lysis. The phage genome is excised, replicated (first by theta replication, then rolling-circle replication), packaged into heads, and the cell is lysed by holin, endolysin and spanin proteins, releasing around 100 progeny particles.

Genetic switch

The CI/Cro regulatory circuit of lambda is the canonical example of a genetic switch. Work by Mark Ptashne and colleagues showed how cooperative binding of CI dimers to operator sites produces stable, heritable gene-expression states, a paradigm later recognised in developmental biology. The lambda system also provided key tools: lambda vectors were among the first used for DNA cloning, and the lambda Red recombination system underlies modern recombineering.
