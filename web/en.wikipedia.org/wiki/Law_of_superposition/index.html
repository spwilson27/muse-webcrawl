Law of superposition

The law of superposition is an axiom that forms one of the bases of the sciences of geology, archaeology and other fields pertaining to geological stratigraphy. In its plainest form, it states that in undeformed stratigraphic sequences, the oldest strata will be at the bottom of the sequence while successively higher strata are progressively younger.

History

The principle was first articulated by the Danish scientist Nicolas Steno in 1669 in his work De solido intra solidum naturaliter contento dissertationis prodromus. It was later incorporated into the relative dating methods developed by William Smith and others in the late 18th and early 19th centuries.

Application

Superposition allows sedimentary layers to be placed in a relative chronological order: a layer is younger than the one beneath it and older than the one above it, unless the sequence has been overturned by folding, faulting or intrusion. Combined with the principles of original horizontality, lateral continuity, cross-cutting relationships and faunal succession, it underpins relative dating in geology. In archaeology, the same logic — formalised in the Harris matrix — orders contexts in an excavation.

Limitations

The law applies only where strata have not been disturbed. Overthrust faults can place older rocks above younger ones, and overturned folds can invert a sequence entirely; structural analysis is needed to detect such cases.
