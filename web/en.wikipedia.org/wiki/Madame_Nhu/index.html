Madame Nhu

Madame Nhu (22 August 1924 – 24 April 2011), born Trần Lệ Xuân, was the sister-in-law of South Vietnam's president Ngô Đình Diệm and the wife of his younger brother and chief adviser, Ngô Đình Nhu. Because Diệm was a lifelong bachelor, she acted as the country's de facto First Lady from 1955 until the 1963 coup that overthrew and killed both brothers. Beautiful, flamboyant and ferociously outspoken, she became one of the most controversial figures of the Vietnam War era, nicknamed the "Dragon Lady" in the Western press.

Rise to influence

Born into a wealthy mandarin family in Hanoi, she converted to Catholicism on marrying Nhu in 1943. After Diệm became president in 1955, Madame Nhu carved out her own power base: she led the Women's Solidarity Movement, a paramilitary women's organisation, and pushed through "morality laws" banning abortion, contraceptives, divorce, dancing and beauty contests. She and her husband were widely believed to dominate the regime, which favoured Catholics in a majority-Buddhist country.

Buddhist crisis and fall

During the Buddhist crisis of 1963, her inflammatory remarks — most notoriously dismissing the self-immolation of the monk Thích Quảng Đức as a "barbecue" — destroyed what remained of American patience with the regime. She was on a speaking tour of the United States when, on 1–2 November 1963, a military coup overthrew the government; Diệm and Nhu were captured and murdered. Madame Nhu blamed the Kennedy administration for the coup and never forgave the United States.

Later life

She lived in exile chiefly in France and Italy, largely out of public view, reportedly working on memoirs that were never published in her lifetime. She died in Rome in 2011. Her parents had publicly disowned her during the crisis; her father, Trần Văn Chương, South Vietnam's ambassador to Washington, resigned in protest in 1963.
