Formal fallacy

A formal fallacy, also called a deductive fallacy or logical fallacy in the strict sense, is an error in the form or structure of a deductive argument, rendering the argument invalid: even if all its premises are true, its conclusion does not necessarily follow. Formal fallacies are detectable purely by examining the argument's structure, without regard to the content of its claims. This contrasts with informal fallacies, whose errors lie in content, context, or language.

Overview

In a valid deductive argument, the truth of the premises guarantees the truth of the conclusion. A formally fallacious argument breaks this guarantee: it presents a pattern of inference that can lead from true premises to a false conclusion. Because validity depends only on form, such errors can be exposed by substituting different terms of the same logical category and showing the pattern fails.

Common formal fallacies

  • Affirming the consequent — "If P then Q. Q, therefore P." (If it rained, the street is wet; the street is wet, therefore it rained — but a sprinkler could have done it.)
  • Denying the antecedent — "If P then Q. Not P, therefore not Q."
  • Affirming a disjunct — treating an inclusive "or" as exclusive: "P or Q. P, therefore not Q."
  • Fallacy of the undistributed middle — in a categorical syllogism, the middle term is not distributed in either premise ("All cats are mammals; all dogs are mammals; therefore all dogs are cats").
  • Illicit major / illicit minor — a term distributed in the conclusion but not in the premises.
  • Fallacy of four terms — an equivocal "middle" term makes the syllogism have four terms rather than three.
  • Masked-man fallacy — substituting one referring expression for another in intensional contexts, where identity of reference does not preserve truth.
  • Modal fallacies — confusing the scope of necessity, as in inferring that because something must be either P or Q, it must be P or must be Q.

Relation to informal fallacies

Many named fallacies in the traditional lists — appeals to authority, ad hominem attacks, straw men — are informal: they can occur in arguments whose bare logical form is fine but which rely on irrelevant, ambiguous, or insufficient premises. Philosophers distinguish the two classes to mark where the failure of reasoning lies.
