Tissue culture

Tissue culture
FieldCell biology, biotechnology
Related termsCell culture, organ culture, micropropagation

Tissue culture is the growth of tissues or cells in an artificial medium separate from the parent organism. This technique is also called micropropagation when applied to plants. It is typically facilitated via the use of a liquid, semi-solid, or solid growth medium, such as broth or agar. Tissue culture commonly refers to the culture of animal cells and tissues; the equivalent term for plants is more specifically plant tissue culture.

History

In 1885 Wilhelm Roux removed a section of the medullary plate of an embryonic chicken and maintained it in a warm saline solution for several days, establishing the basic principle of tissue culture. Ross Granville Harrison, working with medullary tissue in 1907–1910, developed the hanging-drop technique and demonstrated the growth of nerve fibres. In the 1940s and 1950s, the development of defined nutrient media and antibiotics greatly expanded the field, enabling the establishment of continuous cell lines such as HeLa in 1951.

Animal tissue culture

In modern usage, tissue culture generally refers to growing cells derived from multicellular eukaryotes, usually mammals. Cells may be isolated from tissue using enzymatic or mechanical means. Primary cultures are derived directly from excised tissue; after repeated passaging they may become immortalized cell lines. Cultures require sterile technique, controlled temperature and gas exchange (usually 37 °C and 5% CO2 for mammalian cells), and complex media supplying amino acids, vitamins, salts, and growth factors, historically supplemented with animal serum.

Plant tissue culture

Plant tissue culture exploits the totipotency of many plant cells — their ability to regenerate a whole plant from a single cell or explant. Techniques include callus culture, organogenesis, somatic embryogenesis, protoplast culture, and meristem culture (used to produce virus-free plants). The classic growth medium is Murashige and Skoog medium (1962), supplemented with plant growth regulators such as auxins and cytokinins. Applications include clonal propagation of ornamentals and crops, conservation of rare species, production of secondary metabolites, and genetic engineering.

Applications

Tissue culture is a foundational tool in cell and molecular biology, virology, vaccine production, cancer research, drug screening and toxicity testing, regenerative medicine, and assisted reproduction. It also reduces reliance on laboratory animals for some experiments, though it cannot wholly replace in vivo studies.
