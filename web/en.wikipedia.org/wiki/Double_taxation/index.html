Double taxation

Double taxation is the levying of two or more taxes on the same income, asset, or financial transaction. It arises in two main settings. International double taxation occurs when two countries tax the same income — for example, the country where income is earned (source) and the country where the taxpayer resides (residence). Economic double taxation occurs within one country when the same economic income is taxed in two hands, most famously when corporate profits are taxed at the company level and again as dividends in shareholders' hands.

Double tax treaties allocate taxing rights between countries to prevent the same income being fully taxed twice.

Relief mechanisms

Countries relieve international double taxation through a network of bilateral tax treaties, largely based on the OECD and UN model conventions, and through unilateral measures such as:

  • Foreign tax credit — the residence country credits tax paid abroad against its own tax on the same income.
  • Exemption — the residence country exempts foreign-source income (a territorial system).
  • Deduction — foreign tax is treated as a deductible expense.

Treaties also set reduced withholding tax rates on dividends, interest, and royalties, and provide tie-breaker rules for dual residents and mutual agreement procedures for disputes.

Corporate double taxation

The taxation of dividends after corporate profits have already been taxed is a longstanding debate in public finance. Many countries mitigate it through imputation credits (as in Australia), dividend-received deductions, reduced dividend tax rates, or integration of corporate and personal taxes. In the United States, C corporation income is the classic example of double taxation, while pass-through entities such as S corporations and partnerships are taxed only once.
