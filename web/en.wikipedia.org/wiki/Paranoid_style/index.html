Paranoid style

The paranoid style is a term coined by the American historian Richard Hofstadter in his essay "The Paranoid Style in American Politics," first delivered as a lecture in 1963, published in Harper's Magazine in November 1964, and later included in his 1965 book of the same name, which won the Pulitzer Prize in nonfiction. Hofstadter used the phrase — borrowing "style" from art history — to describe a recurring mode of political rhetoric characterized by "heated exaggeration, suspiciousness, and conspiratorial fantasy."

Concept

Hofstadter was explicit that he used "paranoid" not in a clinical sense but to describe a way of seeing the world: the paranoid spokesman regards history as a vast conspiracy driven by demonic forces, sees the enemy as a perfect model of malice engaged in a total struggle for the nation's fate, and treats the situation in apocalyptic terms — there is no room for compromise or ordinary political error. He also noted the paranoid style's pedantic accumulation of "evidence" and its imitation of the scholarly methods of the enemy it opposes.

Historical examples

Hofstadter traced the style through American movements across the political spectrum: anti-Masonic agitation in the 1820s–30s, anti-Catholic nativism, fears of international bankers and the "money power," and the anti-communism of McCarthyism in his own era. He emphasized that the style was not confined to the fringe, and that it tends to flourish among groups experiencing status anxiety and dispossession.

Influence

The essay became one of the most cited works in American political history and is regularly invoked in commentary on conspiracy theories and populist movements. Critics have argued that the framework risks pathologizing dissent, while defenders note Hofstadter's own cautions about distinguishing the style from ordinary — sometimes justified — suspicion of power.
