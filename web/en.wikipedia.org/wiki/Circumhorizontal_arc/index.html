Circumhorizontal arc

A circumhorizontal arc in cirrus cloud.

A circumhorizontal arc is an optical phenomenon produced by the refraction of sunlight or moonlight in plate-shaped ice crystals suspended in high cirrus or cirrostratus clouds. It appears as a wide, brightly coloured band running parallel to the horizon, well below the sun, with red on the upper edge nearest the sun — colours as pure and vivid as a rainbow's, which has earned it the popular name "fire rainbow", although it is neither a rainbow nor related to fire.

Formation

The arc forms when light enters the vertical side faces of flat hexagonal plate crystals floating with their large faces horizontal and exits through their bottom faces, being refracted through a 90-degree prism angle. This geometry requires the sun to be very high — at least 58 degrees above the horizon — so circumhorizontal arcs are seasonal and latitude-dependent: they occur around midday in summer at mid-latitudes, are common in much of the United States, become rarer at higher latitudes (never visible from countries such as Denmark or northern Canada), and are most frequent near the equator where the sun is often high.

The circumhorizontal arc is the counterpart of the circumzenithal arc, which forms when the sun is instead low and light passes through the crystals in the opposite orientation.
