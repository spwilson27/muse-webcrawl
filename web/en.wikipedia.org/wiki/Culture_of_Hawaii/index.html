Culture of Hawaii


Hula, central to Native Hawaiian cultural expression

The culture of Hawaii encompasses the indigenous culture of Native Hawaiians together with the customs brought by successive waves of settlers from the United States, East Asia, the Philippines, Portugal, Puerto Rico, and elsewhere. Indigenous Hawaiian culture — its language, hula, music, feather work, voyaging and land stewardship traditions — forms its foundation, while the plantation era produced a distinctive multiethnic local culture and the creole language known as Hawaiian Pidgin.

Native Hawaiian culture

Native Hawaiian society was organised around the ahupuaʻa land division running from mountain to sea, the kapu system of sacred law, and chiefly (aliʻi) rule. Hula, chant (mele), and oral tradition preserved genealogy and history. Voyaging in double-hulled canoes such as the Hōkūleʻa revived traditional Polynesian navigation from the 1970s onward. The Hawaiian Renaissance of that era also spurred recovery of the Hawaiian language, now an official state language taught in immersion schools.

Local culture

Plantation immigration created shared institutions and foods: plate lunch, spam musubi, saimin, shave ice, and loco moco blend Hawaiian, Japanese, Filipino, Chinese, Portuguese, and American influences. Local identity is often expressed through Pidgin (Hawaiʻi Creole), "local" humour, and customs such as removing shoes indoors and bringing food to gatherings. The word aloha denotes not only a greeting but an ethic of hospitality and care that is central to the state's self-image.

Arts and festivals

Hawaiian music — slack-key guitar, ukulele, falsetto singing, and steel guitar — has influenced global popular music. Major festivals include the Merrie Monarch Festival in Hilo, the world's premier hula competition, Lei Day (May 1), and the Aloha Festivals. Surfing, developed by Native Hawaiians as heʻe nalu, remains emblematic of the islands.
