Tundra orbit

TypeHighly elliptical, geosynchronous orbit
Orbital periodOne sidereal day (23 h 56 min)
Inclination63.4°
Eccentricity~0.27
Apogee altitude~46,000 km
Used bySirius Satellite Radio; QZSS

A Tundra orbit (Russian: орбита «Тундра») is a highly elliptical geosynchronous orbit with an inclination of 63.4° and an orbital period of one sidereal day. A satellite in such an orbit spends most of its time over a designated area of the Earth — particularly high northern or southern latitudes that geostationary satellites serve poorly — during its slow passage around apogee, following a teardrop-shaped ground track.

The teardrop ground track of a Tundra orbit

Characteristics

The inclination of 63.4° is the "critical inclination" at which the argument of perigee does not drift under the effect of Earth's equatorial bulge (the J2 perturbation), keeping apogee fixed over the chosen hemisphere. With eccentricity around 0.27, the satellite lingers near apogee for roughly eight usable hours of each revolution; a constellation of satellites spaced eight hours apart can provide continuous coverage. The orbit was described in Soviet space literature and named for the Siberian latitudes it serves.

Uses

The Tundra orbit is best known for its use by Sirius Satellite Radio, whose three-satellite constellation (launched in 2000) provided continuous radio coverage of North America, with each satellite active over the continent for about 16 hours a day in the original design. Japan's Quasi-Zenith Satellite System uses a related quasi-zenith orbit with lower eccentricity. Compared with the similar, lower Molniya orbit (with a half-day period), the Tundra orbit needs fewer satellites for continuous coverage but requires more launch energy and its satellites pass through the Van Allen belts less frequently.
