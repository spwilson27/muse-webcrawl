Ambergris

Ambergris (from Old French ambre gris, "grey amber") is a solid, waxy, flammable substance of a dull grey or blackish colour produced in the digestive system of sperm whales. Freshly produced ambergris has a marine, faecal odour, but as it ages it acquires a sweet, earthy scent. It has been highly valued for centuries by perfumers as a fixative that allows a fragrance to last much longer.

Formation

Ambergris is formed in the intestines of a small proportion of sperm whales, apparently around indigestible objects such as squid beaks. The material is expelled or released after death and may float in the ocean for years, oxidising and hardening before washing ashore. Its principal constituent is ambrein, a triterpene alcohol that can be processed into ambroxan and other odorants.

Uses

In perfumery, ambergris was prized as a fixative and for the complex, musky warmth it lends to scents; most modern perfumers use synthetic ambroxan instead. Historically it was also used in medicine, as an aphrodisiac, in incense, and as a luxurious food flavouring — a favourite of Charles II of England, whose eggs were reportedly served with ambergris.

Legality

Because it derives from an endangered species, possession or trade of ambergris is prohibited in some jurisdictions, including the United States and Australia, while it remains legal in others such as the United Kingdom, France, and New Zealand. Beach-found lumps occasionally fetch very high prices.
