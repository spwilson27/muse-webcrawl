Hofmann voltameter

A Hofmann voltameter is an apparatus for electrolysing water, invented by the German chemist August Wilhelm von Hofmann in 1866. It demonstrates visually that water is composed of hydrogen and oxygen in a two-to-one volume ratio.

Description

The apparatus consists of three joined, upright glass cylinders. The two outer cylinders are open at the top and fitted with stopcocks; the central cylinder is used to add water, usually acidified with a little sulfuric acid to improve conductivity. At the base of the two outer cylinders are platinum electrodes connected to a source of direct current.

Operation

When current flows, electrolysis splits the water: hydrogen gas collects above the cathode in one outer tube and oxygen above the anode in the other. Because each water molecule yields two atoms of hydrogen for every atom of oxygen, the hydrogen volume rises twice as fast as the oxygen volume — a simple demonstration of the composition H2O. The gases can be tested through the stopcocks: hydrogen burns with a characteristic pop, while oxygen relights a glowing splint.

The name "voltameter" reflects the device's earlier use in measuring quantity of electricity by the volume of gas evolved, related to Faraday's laws of electrolysis.
