Holiday camp

A holiday camp is a type of holiday accommodation, primarily in the United Kingdom, that provides chalets or similar lodging together with entertainment and recreational facilities on site, usually at an all-inclusive price. Holiday camps flourished from the 1930s to the 1970s, offering affordable seaside holidays to working-class and lower-middle-class families.

History

The first holiday camps were philanthropic ventures, such as the camp established on the Isle of Man in the 1890s to give industrial workers healthy outdoor breaks. The commercial era began in the 1930s: Billy Butlin opened his first camp at Skegness in 1936, followed by competitors including Pontins (founded 1946) and Warner's. Butlin's camps, staffed by red-coated entertainers known as Redcoats, offered three meals a day, knobbly-knees contests, beauty pageants and talent shows under the slogan "A week's holiday for a week's wage".

Decline and adaptation

The arrival of cheap package holidays to the Mediterranean in the 1960s and 1970s drew British holidaymakers abroad, and many traditional camps closed or were sold. Surviving operators moved upmarket, rebranding as holiday villages or parks with self-catering lodges, spas and year-round facilities. Center Parcs, which opened its first British village in 1987, represented a new premium model of forest holiday park.

In popular culture

The holiday camp was affectionately satirised in the BBC sitcom Hi-de-Hi! (1980–1988), set in a fictional 1950s camp called Maplins, and appears in the film Holiday Camp (1947), which introduced the Huggett family. The camps remain a nostalgic symbol of post-war British leisure.
