Zimmermann Telegram

The Zimmermann Telegram (or Zimmermann Note) was a secret diplomatic communication issued from the German Foreign Office on 11 January 1917, proposing a military alliance between Germany and Mexico if the United States entered the First World War against Germany. In return, Germany promised to support Mexico in recovering Texas, New Mexico and Arizona, territories it had lost to the United States in the nineteenth century.

Interception and disclosure

The telegram was sent by German Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann to the German ambassador in Mexico, Heinrich von Eckardt. Because Germany's transatlantic cables had been cut, it was transmitted partly over lines the United States had made available for peace diplomacy. British Naval Intelligence's Room 40, led by Admiral Reginald "Blinker" Hall, intercepted and decrypted it. To protect their codebreaking capability, the British obtained a copy of the telegram as re-sent from Washington to Mexico City in an older code, allowing them to reveal it without exposing that they had broken Germany's newest code.

Effect

President Woodrow Wilson released the telegram to the press on 1 March 1917. Its publication inflamed American public opinion — especially combined with Germany's resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare — and Zimmermann's public admission that the telegram was genuine eliminated doubts about its authenticity. On 6 April 1917 the United States declared war on Germany. Mexico, after military assessment, declined the proposal, recognising it could not defeat the United States.

Legacy

The telegram is regarded as one of the most consequential intelligence coups in history and a classic case study in signals intelligence, strategic deception and the politics of disclosure.
