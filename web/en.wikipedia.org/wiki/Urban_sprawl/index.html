Urban sprawl

Urban sprawl is the unrestricted growth in many urban areas of housing, commercial development and roads over large expanses of land, with little concern for consequences or urban planning. It is characterised by low-density, single-use, automobile-dependent development on the periphery of cities, often consuming farmland and open space faster than population grows. The term is usually pejorative, though sprawling development patterns also appear in rapidly growing cities worldwide.

Causes

Sprawl was enabled by mass car ownership, highway building, cheap peripheral land, mortgage finance favouring new suburbs, and zoning codes that separate homes from jobs and shops. In the postwar United States these produced the classic suburb; later decades added "edge cities," exurbs and big-box retail landscapes. In developing countries, sprawl often takes the form of informal settlements and unplanned peripheral growth around fast-growing megacities.

Effects

Critics associate sprawl with loss of farmland and habitat, longer commutes and higher transport emissions, costly infrastructure per resident, social segregation, and health effects linked to driving-dependent lifestyles. Defenders argue that sprawl reflects consumer preference for space and affordability, and that low-density development delivers cheaper housing and shorter local trips than assumed. Research generally finds that land consumption per capita rises as metropolitan areas decentralise.

Responses

Planning responses include smart growth policies, New Urbanism's walkable mixed-use neighbourhoods, transit-oriented development, infill incentives and urban growth boundaries such as Portland's. These aim to concentrate growth in existing centres while preserving surrounding rural land.
