The banhu (Chinese: 板胡; pinyin: bǎnhú) is a Chinese traditional bowed string instrument in the huqin family. It is used primarily as a solo instrument and in ensembles in northern China, and is especially associated with the folk music and narrative singing traditions of the northern provinces.

Construction

The defining feature of the banhu is its sound box, made from a half coconut shell over which a very thin wooden board — rather than the snakeskin used on the erhu — is glued as the soundboard; this wooden face gives the instrument its name, from ban (板, "board") and hu (胡, short for huqin). A long fretless neck passes through the shell, and two steel (traditionally silk) strings are tuned a fifth apart. Unlike the erhu, the banhu has a fretted fingerboard glued to the neck, against which the strings are stopped.

Playing and repertoire

The instrument is held vertically and played with a bow whose hair passes between the two strings. The frets allow rapid sliding and ornamentation, and the thin wooden soundboard gives the banhu a bright, clear, and penetrating tone, noticeably louder and crisper than the erhu. It is a leading melodic instrument in genres such as Hebei bangzi opera accompaniment, Pingju, and the narrative ballad singing of the north, and in the 20th century a solo concert repertoire was developed for it, including pieces that imitate birdsong and folk melodies.
