Times New Roman is a serif typeface commissioned by the British newspaper The Times in 1931 and designed by Stanley Morison, typographic advisor to Monotype, together with Victor Lardent, a lettering artist in the newspaper's advertising department. It was first used by the newspaper on 3 October 1932 and released commercially the following year. It became one of the most popular typefaces of the 20th century, ubiquitous in books, documents, and (through its inclusion in Microsoft Windows and Apple operating systems) on personal computers.

History

The design followed Morison's public criticism of The Times for being typographically antiquated. The paper challenged him to design something better. Working from Morison's brief, Lardent drew the letterforms, which were influenced by the 16th-century types of Christophe Plantin and by Perpetua and Baskerville, but narrowed to improve economy of space and given a larger x-height and sharper serifs to survive high-speed newspaper printing and cheap paper. Morison supervised refinements throughout his life and remained famously protective of the design's reputation.

Usage

The Times itself moved on to successor faces (Times Europa and later Times Modern), but Times New Roman spread everywhere else: it became the default typeface of Microsoft Word for many years (until Calibri replaced it in 2007) and remains a standard requirement for academic manuscripts, legal filings, and theses. The very name distinguishes it from the older face it replaced, "Times Old Roman." Monotype's Times New Roman and Linotype's Times Roman are closely related but not identical releases.

Characteristics

Times New Roman is a compact, sturdy text face with moderate stroke contrast, a relatively large x-height, short descenders, and economical letter widths that set substantial amounts of legible text in limited space — qualities designed for newspaper columns that proved equally suited to books and documents. Its ubiquity has also made it a byword for typographic defaultness, and it is frequently criticized as over-familiar.
