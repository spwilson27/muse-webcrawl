Formula of Concord

The Formula of Concord (Latin: Formula concordiae; German: Konkordienformel), often called simply the Formula, is an authoritative Lutheran statement of faith (called a confession) that, in two parts — the Epitome and the Solid Declaration — attempted to settle a number of disputed doctrines that had arisen within Lutheranism after the death of Martin Luther in 1546. It was signed in 1577 by over 8,000 Lutheran pastors and theologians and by political rulers, and was published in 1580 as part of the Book of Concord, the authoritative collection of Lutheran confessional documents. It is still held as authoritative in confessional Lutheran churches today.

Background

After Luther's death and the Schmalkaldic War, Lutheran theologians divided into factions — "Gnesio-Lutherans" and "Philippists" (followers of Philipp Melanchthon) — over issues including the Augsburg Interim, adiaphora (indifferent ceremonies), synergism in conversion, the role of good works, and the Lord's Supper. Civil rulers, especially Elector Augustus of Saxony, pressed for a unifying settlement. Through a series of meetings and drafts (including the Swabian Concord, the Maulbronn Formula and the Torgau Book), theologians led by Jakob Andreae and Martin Chemnitz produced the final text, signed at Bergen Abbey near Magdeburg in 1577.

Contents

The Formula treats twelve articles, each with an affirmative statement of orthodox teaching and a rejection of contrary errors:

  1. Original sin
  2. Free will
  3. The righteousness of faith before God
  4. Good works
  5. Law and Gospel
  6. The third use of the Law
  7. The Holy Supper of Christ
  8. The person of Christ
  9. Christ's descent into hell
  10. Church rites (adiaphora)
  11. Eternal election (predestination)
  12. Other sects and heresies

Reception

The Formula succeeded in defining a confessional Lutheran orthodoxy, but it was rejected in some territories (such as Hesse, Zweibrücken and Denmark, where its publication was banned), and some churches, including the Church of Sweden, accepted the Book of Concord without the Formula. It sharpened Lutheran distinctiveness against both Roman Catholic and Reformed theology — especially in its insistence on the real presence in the Eucharist and the communication of attributes in Christology.
