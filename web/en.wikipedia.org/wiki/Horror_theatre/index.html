Horror theatre is a genre of live theatrical performance intended to frighten, shock, or horrify the audience, using elements of the macabre, the supernatural, violence, and psychological dread. Although frightening material has been staged since the ghost-ridden tragedies of Seneca and the Jacobean revenge plays of Shakespeare's contemporaries, horror as a self-conscious genre of theatre reached its most famous form in the Parisian Théâtre du Grand-Guignol.

Early horror on stage

Ghosts, witches, and on-stage murders appear in Macbeth and Hamlet, and the bloody revenge tragedies of Thomas Kyd and John Webster revelled in spectacle and corpses. The Gothic novels of the late 18th century were quickly adapted into melodramas: Matthew Lewis's The Monk and stage versions of Frankenstein (from 1823) drew crowds with supernatural effects. The long-running 19th-century stage success of Sweeney Todd melodramas and Dion Boucicault's sensation dramas kept stage horror popular, and in 1897 Bram Stoker's Dracula (he was a theatre business manager) began a long theatrical afterlife.

Grand Guignol

The Théâtre du Grand-Guignol in Pigalle, Paris, founded in 1897 by Oscar Méténier, specialised in short naturalistic horror plays interspersed with comedies ("hot and cold showers"). Under director Max Maurey and playwright André de Lorde it staged graphic depictions of murder, mutilation, insanity, and medical horror, with elaborate stage effects for eye-gougings and acid attacks; a house doctor was famously said to attend fainting spectators. The Grand Guignol closed in 1962, its manager remarking that it could not compete with the real horrors of the Second World War and the camps.

Later developments

Antonin Artaud's Theatre of Cruelty theorised an assault on the audience's senses; the 20th century also saw horror musicals and long-runners such as Stephen Mallatratt's adaptation of The Woman in Black (London, 1989–2023), immersive and site-specific horror experiences, and "splatter" and extreme-haunt attractions. Modern productions use sound design, darkness, confined spaces, and direct audience contact to create fright in ways distinct from horror film.
