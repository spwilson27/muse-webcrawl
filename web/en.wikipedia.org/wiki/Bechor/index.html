Bechor

A firstborn (bechor) animal, sanctified under biblical law.

Bechor (Hebrew: בְּכוֹר, plural bechorot) is the Hebrew term for the firstborn, or "firstling", in Jewish tradition. Under the law of the Torah, the firstborn — of humans, of livestock, and of certain produce — acquires a special sanctity (kedushat bechorah) and is subject to particular commandments described in the books of Exodus and Deuteronomy.

Firstborn animals

The firstborn male offspring of kosher domesticated animals (cattle, sheep and goats) was given to the kohanim (priests) and, in the time of the Temple in Jerusalem, offered as a sacrifice and eaten by the priests. If the animal had a disqualifying blemish, it could be eaten by anyone but not sacrificed. The firstborn of a donkey, an impure animal, was instead redeemed with a sheep (peter chamor).

Firstborn sons

Human firstborn sons are redeemed in the ceremony of Pidyon haben, in which the father gives five silver coins to a kohen thirty days after the birth. In the biblical narrative, the firstborn originally held the privileges of priesthood and a double portion of inheritance; after the Exodus, the Levites were taken in place of the firstborns. The status also gives rise to the fast of the firstborns observed on the eve of Passover.
