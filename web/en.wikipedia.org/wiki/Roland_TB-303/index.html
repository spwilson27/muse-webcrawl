Roland TB-303

The Roland TB-303 Bass Line (also known as the 303) is a bass synthesizer with a built-in sequencer. Released by the Roland Corporation in 1981, it was originally designed to accompany solo guitarists practising with the TR-606 drum machine. A commercial failure, it was discontinued in 1984 — but cheap second-hand units were adopted by Chicago house and later acid house producers, and its squelching, resonant sound became one of the defining timbres of electronic dance music.

Features

The TB-303 has a single oscillator (switchable sawtooth or square wave), a resonant low-pass filter with envelope modulation, and controls for tuning, cutoff, resonance, envelope mod, decay and accent. Its step sequencer stores patterns of up to 16 steps with slide (portamento) and accent per step — the slide between notes produces the instrument's characteristic liquid, twisting phrases. Programming it was widely considered unintuitive, which encouraged happy accidents.

History and influence

Designed by Tadao Kikumoto (who also designed the TR-909), the 303 failed commercially because its electronic tone sounded little like a real bass guitar. In the mid-1980s Chicago musicians such as Phuture bought discarded units cheaply; Phuture's "Acid Tracks" (1987), built from a tweaked 303, launched acid house. The sound spread through British rave culture and became central to techno, trance and big beat. Original units now change hands for several thousand dollars, and the instrument has been widely cloned in hardware and software, including Roland's own TB-03 and TB-3 reissues.
