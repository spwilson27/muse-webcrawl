Health effects from noise

Noise pollution, or sound pollution, is the propagation of noise or sound with harmful impact on the activity of human or animal life. Exposure to excessive noise has been linked to a range of adverse health outcomes, both auditory and non-auditory. Major sources include road traffic, aircraft, railways, industry, and neighbourhood activities.

Auditory effects

The most direct effect of noise exposure is noise-induced hearing loss, which results from damage to the hair cells of the cochlea. Occupational exposure to sustained levels above about 85 dBA can cause permanent threshold shifts; brief exposure to very intense sounds, such as explosions or gunfire, can cause acoustic trauma. Tinnitus — the perception of ringing or buzzing in the ears — frequently accompanies noise-induced hearing damage. Hearing protection and exposure limits are the principal controls in industrial settings.

Non-auditory effects

Cardiovascular disease

Epidemiological studies have associated long-term exposure to transportation noise with hypertension, ischaemic heart disease, and stroke. The World Health Organization's 2018 Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region recommended keeping average road traffic noise below 53 dB Lden, citing increased risk of cardiovascular disease above that level. The hypothesised mechanism involves repeated stress responses — release of cortisol and catecholamines, sleep disturbance, and oxidative stress — leading to endothelial dysfunction.

Sleep disturbance

Night-time noise can fragment sleep even when it does not fully awaken the sleeper, reducing slow-wave and REM sleep. Consequences include daytime sleepiness, impaired mood, and reduced cognitive performance. The WHO Night Noise Guidelines for Europe (2009) set a target of 40 dB Lnight outside bedrooms.

Cognitive and mental health effects

Children exposed to chronic aircraft and road noise at school have shown poorer reading comprehension and memory in studies such as the RANCH project around European airports. Noise annoyance, a subjective response varying between individuals, is associated with depression and anxiety in some research, and high annoyance is used by the WHO as a health-relevant endpoint.

Regulation

Responses include land-use planning that separates housing from major noise sources, noise barriers, building insulation, low-noise road surfaces, quieter vehicles and aircraft, and zoning or curfew rules. The European Union's Environmental Noise Directive (2002/49/EC) requires member states to produce strategic noise maps and action plans for large agglomerations and major transport routes.

Effects on wildlife

Anthropogenic noise interferes with animal communication, predator detection, and mating, and has been shown to alter the behaviour and distribution of birds, marine mammals, and other species. Underwater noise from shipping and seismic surveys is a particular concern for cetaceans that rely on echolocation.
