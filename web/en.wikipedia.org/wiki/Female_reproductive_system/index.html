The female reproductive system is made up of the internal and external sex organs that function in the reproduction of new offspring. In humans, the female reproductive system is immature at birth and develops to maturity at puberty to be able to produce gametes, and to carry a foetus to full term.

Internal organs

The internal female organs are the vagina, uterus, fallopian tubes and ovaries. The uterus (womb) is a hollow muscular organ in the pelvis whose lining, the endometrium, supports implantation of a fertilised egg and is shed during menstruation if no pregnancy occurs. The fallopian tubes carry the ovum from the ovary toward the uterus and are the usual site of fertilisation. The ovaries produce ova (egg cells) and the sex hormones oestrogen and progesterone. The cervix is the lower part of the uterus opening into the vagina.

External organs

The external organs are collectively called the vulva, comprising the labia majora and minora, the clitoris, the vestibule with the urethral and vaginal openings, and associated glands such as the Bartholin's glands.

Function

The system's functions include producing female gametes (ova), secreting sex hormones, providing a site for fertilisation, and gestating and delivering offspring. Its cyclical activity is governed by the menstrual cycle, regulated by hormones of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis.

Development and clinical significance

The internal genitalia develop largely from the Müllerian (paramesonephric) ducts. Common clinical conditions affecting the system include endometriosis, polycystic ovary syndrome, uterine fibroids, pelvic inflammatory disease and cancers of the cervix, uterus and ovary. The medical specialty concerned with the system is gynaecology.
