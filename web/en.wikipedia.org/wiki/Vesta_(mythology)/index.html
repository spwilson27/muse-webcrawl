Vesta is the virgin goddess of the hearth, home and family in Roman religion, the counterpart of the Greek Hestia. One of the oldest and most important deities of Rome, she was rarely depicted in human form; instead she was present in the sacred flame that burned perpetually in her round temple in the Roman Forum and, by extension, in every domestic hearth. As Ovid observed, Vesta is the living flame itself.

Cult

Vesta's public cult was served by the Vestal Virgins, six (later seven) priestesses chosen as girls who served for thirty years under the supervision of the Pontifex Maximus, guarding the sacred fire whose extinction was an ill omen for the state. Letting the flame die was punishable by whipping; breaking the vow of chastity, by being buried alive. The Vestals also kept the wills of prominent citizens and guarded sacred objects, including the Palladium, in the temple's inner shrine, which no man except the Pontifex Maximus might enter.

Festival and household worship

Her festival, the Vestalia (7–15 June), was especially associated with women and with bakers and millers, whose donkeys were rested and garlanded. In private life every Roman home maintained a flame or shrine, and a portion of each meal was offered to Vesta; a prayer to Vesta traditionally closed Roman prayers, as one to Janus opened them. The cult survived until the emperor Theodosius I suppressed pagan worship and extinguished the sacred fire in 394 CE.
