Chebyshev's inequality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Illustration showing the proportion of a distribution guaranteed to lie within two standard deviations of the mean]

In probability theory, Chebyshev's inequality (also spelled Tchebycheff, Tchebychev, or Tschebyscheff) is a result that gives a lower bound on the probability that a random variable lies within a certain number of standard deviations of its mean. It applies to any probability distribution with a finite mean and variance, and is named after the Russian mathematician Pafnuty Chebyshev, who stated it in 1867; it had first been formulated by his friend and colleague Irénée-Jules Bienaymé in 1853.

Statement

Let X be a random variable with mean μ and finite, non-zero variance σ². Then for any real number k > 0:

Pr(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k²

Equivalently, at least 1 − 1/k² of the distribution's values lie within k standard deviations of the mean. For example, at least 75% of values lie within two standard deviations, and at least 88.9% within three, regardless of the shape of the distribution.

Proof sketch

The inequality follows from Markov's inequality applied to the non-negative random variable (X − μ)²: the probability that (X − μ)² exceeds k²σ² is at most E[(X − μ)²]/(k²σ²) = 1/k².

Uses

Because it makes no assumptions about the form of the distribution, Chebyshev's inequality is widely used as a robust — though often loose — bound. It is a key step in one proof of the weak law of large numbers, and underlies Chebyshev's sum inequality and numerous generalisations, such as the one-sided Cantelli (Chebyshev–Cantelli) inequality.
