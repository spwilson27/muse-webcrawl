The Río de la Plata (Spanish for "River of Silver"), known in English as the River Plate, is the wide estuary formed by the confluence of the Paraná and Uruguay rivers on the south-east coast of South America, between Argentina and Uruguay. About 290 kilometres long, it widens from roughly 2 kilometres at its inner end to some 220 kilometres where it meets the Atlantic Ocean — among the widest river mouths in the world. On its southern shore stands Buenos Aires, Argentina's capital; on the northern shore, Montevideo, the capital of Uruguay, near the estuary's outer limit.

Geography

The Plata is a shallow, funnel-shaped estuary whose brown colour comes from the enormous sediment load of the Paraná, particularly the Bermejo River's contribution. The Paraná Delta, a vast wetland of islands and channels, advances into the estuary by tens of metres per year. A submerged shoal, the Barra del Indio, divides the estuary into an inner fluvial zone and an outer zone where fresh and salt water mix; salinity fronts and the strong south-easterly sudestada winds, which can drive water levels up several metres, strongly influence both coasts.

History

The estuary was explored by Juan Díaz de Solís in 1516, who was killed by Indigenous people on its shore, and named by Sebastian Cabot's expedition, which heard tales of a "Sierra de la Plata" (Silver Range) inland — legends that also gave Argentina its name (from argentum, silver). Spanish colonisation followed, with Buenos Aires founded in 1536 and refounded in 1580. The estuary was the theatre of British invasions of the Río de la Plata in 1806–07 and, in December 1939, of the Battle of the River Plate, the first major naval battle of the Second World War, which ended with the scuttling of the German pocket battleship Admiral Graf Spee off Montevideo.

Ecology and use

The estuary supports major fisheries and is home to the La Plata dolphin (franciscana), one of the smallest and most threatened cetaceans. Dredged navigation channels — including the Martín García and Emilio Mitre channels — keep the ports of Buenos Aires, La Plata and Montevideo accessible to ocean shipping, while the waters receive the drainage of a basin covering about a fifth of South America, bringing persistent pollution challenges to both capital cities' shores.
