Benign tumor

[Image placeholder: Histopathology slide of a benign lipoma showing mature fat cells with no atypia]

A benign tumor is a mass of cells (a neoplasm) that does not invade neighbouring tissue or spread to other parts of the body (metastasise) — the two properties that define malignant tumours, i.e. cancers. Benign tumours grow slowly, usually remain localised, and are often enclosed in a fibrous capsule. Most are harmless and require no treatment, but some can cause serious problems by pressing on vital structures, by secreting hormones, or, rarely, by undergoing malignant transformation.

Naming and types

Benign tumours are conventionally named with the suffix -oma attached to the tissue of origin: a lipoma (fat), fibroma (fibrous tissue), chondroma (cartilage), osteoma (bone), meningioma (meninges) and leiomyoma (smooth muscle — the common "fibroid" of the uterus). Benign epithelial tumours forming glandular patterns are adenomas; finger-like epithelial growths are papillomas. Some names are historical exceptions: melanoma, lymphoma and glioma are malignant despite the suffix. Common benign growths such as moles (melanocytic naevi), skin tags and uterine fibroids affect a large fraction of adults.

Effects and treatment

Although not cancers, benign tumours may harm by mass effect — a benign meningioma or pituitary adenoma can compress the brain or optic nerves; hormone-secreting adenomas can cause endocrine disease such as Cushing's syndrome or acromegaly. Some are precancerous precursors: colorectal adenomatous polyps are benign neoplasms that can progress to carcinoma, which is why they are removed at colonoscopy. Treatment, when needed, is usually complete surgical excision, after which prognosis is generally excellent.
