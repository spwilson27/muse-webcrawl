Badwater Basin is an endorheic basin in Death Valley National Park, California, noted as the lowest point in North America, with a depth of 282 feet (86 m) below sea level. The site itself consists of a small spring-fed pool of "bad water" — undrinkable because of accumulated salts — beside the road, next to a vast expanse of salt flats. Mount Whitney, the highest point in the contiguous United States at 14,505 feet (4,421 m), lies only about 85 miles (137 km) to the west.

Geography

The basin is the modern remnant of prehistoric Lake Manly, which filled Death Valley during the Pleistocene. As the climate warmed, the lake evaporated, leaving behind thick deposits of salt and borate minerals. The salt flats cover nearly 200 square miles (520 km²) and consist largely of sodium chloride (table salt), calcite, gypsum and borax. Rainstorms periodically flood the basin floor, dissolving and recrystallising the salt into geometric polygonal patterns; in unusually wet years a shallow temporary lake can form, as happened after flooding in 2023.

History and name

According to local lore, the basin was named when an early prospector's mule refused to drink from the pool, making it "bad water" — although the water is not truly poisonous, only extremely salty. The pool is home to an endemic species, the Badwater snail (Assiminea infirma), and to pickleweed growing at its margins.

Tourism and extremes

Badwater is one of Death Valley's most visited sites, reached by Badwater Road south of Furnace Creek. A sign on the cliff face above the parking area marks sea level, dramatically illustrating the depth of the basin. The area records some of the highest air temperatures on Earth; the nearby Furnace Creek station recorded 134 °F (56.7 °C) in 1913. Badwater is also the start line of the Badwater Ultramarathon, a 135-mile (217 km) footrace to Whitney Portal held in midsummer.
