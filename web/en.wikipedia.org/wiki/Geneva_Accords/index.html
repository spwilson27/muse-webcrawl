Geneva Accords

Geneva Accords (1954)
Signed20–21 July 1954, Geneva, Switzerland
ContextEnd of the First Indochina War
OutcomeVietnam temporarily partitioned at the 17th parallel; elections planned for 1956 (never held)

The Geneva Accords were a set of agreements reached at the Geneva Conference of 1954 that ended the First Indochina War and temporarily partitioned Vietnam. They were signed on 20–21 July 1954 by France and the Democratic Republic of Vietnam (Viet Minh), with agreements also covering Cambodia and Laos.

Terms

The Accords provided for a ceasefire in Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia. Vietnam was to be temporarily divided along the 17th parallel (near the Bến Hải River) into a communist North under the Viet Minh and a non-communist South, with a demilitarised zone between. Civilians could move freely between the zones for 300 days — some 900,000 people, mostly northern Catholics, moved south, while Viet Minh cadres regrouped north. Nationwide elections to reunify the country were scheduled for July 1956, to be supervised by an International Commission of India, Poland and Canada.

Consequences

The State of Vietnam (South) under Ngô Đình Diệm, backed by the United States (which had not signed the Accords but issued a declaration taking note of them), refused the 1956 elections, fearing a communist victory. The division hardened into two states, and the unresolved settlement led directly to the Vietnam War.
