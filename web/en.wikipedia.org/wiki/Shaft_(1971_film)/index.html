Shaft (1971 film)

Directed by: Gordon Parks
Starring: Richard Roundtree
Music by: Isaac Hayes
Release date: June 25, 1971
Running time: 100 minutes

Shaft is a 1971 American blaxploitation crime action film directed by Gordon Parks and produced by Joel Freeman for Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer. It stars Richard Roundtree as private detective John Shaft, and is adapted from Ernest Tidyman's 1970 novel of the same name. The film is regarded as one of the most important works of the blaxploitation genre and a landmark in American cinema.

Plot

John Shaft, a tough Harlem private detective, is approached by mob boss Bumpy Jonas, whose daughter Marcy has been kidnapped. Bumpy suspects the Black power movement but the kidnappers prove to be the Italian Mafia, who are trying to seize control of Harlem's rackets. Caught between the police, represented by Captain Vic Androzzi, the Mafia, and Harlem's criminal underworld, Shaft navigates a dangerous course to rescue Marcy and restore the uneasy peace of the streets.

Music

The film's score and soundtrack were composed by Isaac Hayes. The "Theme from Shaft", with its distinctive wah-wah guitar and hi-hat pattern, became a major pop hit, reaching number one on the Billboard Hot 100, and won the Academy Award for Best Original Song — making Hayes the first African American to win an Oscar in a non-acting category. The soundtrack album, released on Stax Records' Enterprise label, was also a commercial success and won several Grammy Awards.

Legacy

Shaft was an enormous commercial success, helping rescue MGM from near bankruptcy, and it transformed Hollywood's treatment of Black audiences and Black heroes: Shaft was capable, stylish, sexually confident, and answered to no one. The film produced two sequels, Shaft's Big Score! (1972) and Shaft in Africa (1973), a short-lived television series, and later revivals starring Samuel L. Jackson as Shaft's nephew (2000) and son (2019). In 2000 the film was selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry by the Library of Congress as being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant."
