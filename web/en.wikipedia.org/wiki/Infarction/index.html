Infarction

Infarction is tissue death (necrosis) due to inadequate blood supply to the affected area. It may be caused by artery blockages, rupture, mechanical compression, or vasoconstriction. The resulting lesion is referred to as an infarct (from the Latin infarctus, "stuffed into").

Causes

Most infarcts result from thrombosis or embolism obstructing an artery — as in myocardial infarction (heart attack), cerebral infarction (ischaemic stroke), pulmonary infarction, and splenic infarction. Blockage of venous drainage, torsion of an organ's vascular pedicle (as in testicular or ovarian torsion), and severe hypotension can also cause infarction. Risk factors include atherosclerosis, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, smoking, and hyperlipidaemia.

Classification

Pathologists classify infarcts by colour and the presence of infection. White (anaemic) infarcts occur in solid organs with end-arterial supply, such as the heart, spleen, and kidneys, where the tissue becomes pale and wedge-shaped. Red (haemorrhagic) infarcts occur in loose, spongy tissues with dual or collateral circulation — notably the lungs and intestines — where blood leaks into the necrotic area. Septic infarcts, seeded by infected emboli such as those from endocarditis, may progress to abscesses.

Pathology and outcome

Ischaemic tissue typically undergoes coagulative necrosis, except in the brain, where liquefactive necrosis predominates. The clinical consequences depend entirely on the site: infarction of heart muscle may cause heart failure or death, cerebral infarction produces focal neurological deficits, while small splenic infarcts may be clinically silent. Treatment targets the underlying obstruction — for example thrombolysis or thrombectomy in stroke and heart attack — and prevention of recurrence.
