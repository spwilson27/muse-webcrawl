Megatsunami

Not to be confused with a tsunami or a tidal wave.

A megatsunami is a very large wave created by a large, sudden displacement of material into a body of water. Megatsunamis are distinct from ordinary tsunamis, which are usually caused by earthquakes. Megatsunamis are typically generated by massive landslides, volcanic flank collapses, or asteroid impacts, and they can reach run-up heights of hundreds of metres — far higher than earthquake-generated tsunamis.

Mechanism

When a huge volume of rock or ice falls into water, it displaces the water violently and generates a wave whose height near the source can be enormous. Because the source is a point-like mass movement rather than a long fault rupture, megatsunami waves tend to dissipate more quickly with distance than earthquake tsunamis, but near the source they are devastating. In a confined bay or fjord, the wave can be funnelled and amplified, producing extreme run-up on the opposite shore.

Notable examples

  • Lituya Bay, Alaska, 1958 — An earthquake-triggered rockslide of about 30 million cubic metres fell into the bay, generating a wave with a run-up of 524 metres (1,719 ft) on the opposite slope — the highest ever recorded. The bay's narrow shape amplified the wave.
  • Storegga Slide, Norway, c. 6100 BCE — A huge submarine landslide off the Norwegian coast sent waves across the North Sea, inundating parts of Scotland and the lowlands of what is now Doggerland.
  • Canary Islands / Cumbre Vieja — The volcanic island of La Palma has a flank considered potentially unstable; a future collapse has been modelled as capable of sending large waves across the Atlantic, though the size and likelihood of such an event are debated.
  • Chicxulub impact, 66 million years ago — The asteroid impact that ended the age of the dinosaurs generated a megatsunami in the Gulf of Mexico with waves estimated at over 100 metres high.

Prehistoric evidence

Geologists identify ancient megatsunamis from distinctive deposits: chaotic boulder fields, marine sediments found far inland or at high elevation, and tsunamiite beds in the geological record. Such evidence suggests megatsunamis have occurred repeatedly through Earth history, often associated with volcanic island collapses and large submarine slides.
