The Sabbath in Christianity concerns the application within Christian practice of the Sabbath — the day of rest commanded in the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:8–11), which in Judaism is observed on the seventh day of the week, Saturday. Christians have differed over whether the Sabbath command endures, and if so on which day and in what form: the historic majority transferred its observance, in modified form, to Sunday, the first day, while a minority continues to keep Saturday.

First-day (Sunday) observance

From the early centuries, most Christians assembled for worship on Sunday, "the Lord's Day" (Revelation 1:10), commemorating the resurrection of Jesus, which the Gospels place on the first day of the week. Writers such as Ignatius of Antioch and Justin Martyr describe Sunday worship, and Constantine's civil law of 321 gave the day legal rest status in the Roman Empire. In mainstream Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant teaching, Sunday is not the Sabbath moved but a distinct Christian feast: the obligation is to worship and, traditionally, to abstain from servile work. Reformed and Puritan traditions went further, developing a strict "Christian Sabbath" theology that applied the fourth commandment's rest to Sunday in detail — the basis of Sabbatarian blue laws — while Lutheran and Catholic traditions generally treated Sunday rest as church ordinance rather than Mosaic law.

Seventh-day observance

Seventh-day Sabbatarianism, which maintains that the Saturday Sabbath remains binding on Christians, arose within English Puritanism (the Seventh Day Baptists date from the seventeenth century) and became far more prominent with the Seventh-day Adventist Church, organised in 1863, for which Sabbath-keeping from Friday sunset to Saturday sunset is a cardinal doctrine. Other Saturday-keeping bodies include the Church of God (Seventh Day) and many Messianic Jewish congregations. Seventh-day advocates argue that the Sabbath was instituted at creation, was never abrogated in the New Testament, and was changed only by later ecclesiastical authority.

Non-Sabbatarian views

Some Christians hold that the Sabbath was a shadow fulfilled in Christ (citing Colossians 2:16–17) and that no particular day of rest is commanded, though regular worship and rest remain good practice. Passages such as Romans 14:5 and Galatians 4:10 are invoked to treat day-observance as a matter of conscience.
