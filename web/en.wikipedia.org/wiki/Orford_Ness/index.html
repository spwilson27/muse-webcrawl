Orford Ness

Orford Ness

Location: Suffolk coast, England

Type: Shingle spit / nature reserve

Managed by: National Trust

Known for: Secret military weapons research, 1913–1980s

Orford Ness is a cuspate foreland shingle spit on the Suffolk coast of England, stretching some ten miles along the North Sea near the town of Orford. It is one of the largest vegetated shingle spits in Europe and is internationally important for its coastal habitats and birdlife. For most of the 20th century the Ness was a secret military research establishment, and its strange derelict test buildings give it a distinctive, desolate landscape.

Military history

The War Office acquired the site in 1913, and during the First World War an airfield and the Armament Experimental Station tested aircraft guns, bombsights and aerial photography. Between the wars, pioneering experiments in what became radar were carried out here by Robert Watson-Watt's team in 1935 before radar work moved to Bawdsey Manor. During the Second World War the site tested armour, ammunition and bombing techniques, and captured German weapons were evaluated.

In the Cold War, the Atomic Weapons Research Establishment used Orford Ness for environmental testing of Britain's nuclear weapons: components were subjected to vibration, temperature extremes and impact in the distinctive concrete "pagoda" laboratories, designed so that their roofs would lift off in an explosion and direct blast upward. No fissile material was tested here, but the work was classified for decades. The Cobra Mist over-the-horizon radar operated from the site in the late 1960s and early 1970s.

Nature reserve

The Ministry of Defence sold the site to the National Trust in 1993. Much of the Ness is now a National Nature Reserve supporting rare shingle plants, breeding avocets, marsh harriers and other saltmarsh and lagoon wildlife. Public access is limited and guided, both for conservation and because of residual military hazards; visitors cross by ferry from Orford Quay. The Orfordness lighthouse, long a landmark at the spit's tip, was decommissioned in 2013 as coastal erosion advanced, and was dismantled in 2020.
