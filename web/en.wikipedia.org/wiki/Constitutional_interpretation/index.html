Constitutional interpretation

Constitutional interpretation is the process by which courts and other officials determine the meaning of a constitution and apply it to particular cases. Because constitutions are typically written in broad and abstract language, and are designed to endure for long periods, interpretation is central to constitutional law. Disagreement over the proper method of interpretation — the "interpretive debate" — is a perennial feature of constitutional democracies, most famously in the United States.

Methods of interpretation

Textualism

Textualism directs the interpreter to the words of the constitutional text itself, read in their ordinary public meaning. It is closely associated with but distinct from originalism.

Originalism

Originalism holds that the meaning of a constitutional provision was fixed at the time of its adoption, either by the intent of its drafters ("original intent") or by the understanding of the public that ratified it ("original public meaning"). Proponents argue that originalism constrains judicial discretion and respects democratic self-government; critics respond that it can entrench outdated understandings and that historical evidence is often indeterminate.

Living constitutionalism

Living constitutionalism (or the "living constitution" approach) maintains that constitutional meaning evolves with society, and that courts may adapt broad principles — such as liberty and equality — to new circumstances. Critics associate it with judicial activism, while defenders argue that the framers themselves chose abstract language precisely to allow development.

Structural and doctrinal approaches

Structural interpretation infers meaning from the overall architecture of the constitution — federalism, separation of powers, and the relationships among institutions. Doctrinal approaches give weight to precedent and the accumulated body of case law (stare decisis). Pragmatic or consequentialist approaches consider the practical effects of rival interpretations. John Hart Ely's "representation reinforcement" theory proposed that judicial review is most legitimate when it clears the channels of political change and protects minorities unable to protect themselves in the political process.

Comparative practice

Outside the United States, the proportionality test dominates rights adjudication in countries such as Canada, Germany, South Africa and Israel: courts ask whether a limitation of a right pursues a legitimate aim and is suitable, necessary and proportionate in the strict sense. Many constitutional courts also engage in comparative and international-law-informed interpretation.
