Nynorsk

TypeWritten standard of Norwegian
CreatedMid-19th century, by Ivar Aasen (as Landsmål)
UsersAbout 10–15% of Norwegians; concentrated in western Norway
StatusCo-official with Bokmål

Nynorsk (literally "New Norwegian") is one of the two official written standards of the Norwegian language, the other being Bokmål. Norwegian has no official spoken standard, but the two written forms reflect different dialect groupings and historical traditions. Nynorsk is used by roughly 10–15 percent of the population, mainly in western Norway, and all Norwegians learn to read both forms at school.

History

After Norway separated from Denmark in 1814, the written language remained Danish. In the 1840s and 1850s the self-taught linguist Ivar Aasen travelled the country collecting rural dialects and constructed from them a new written norm, which he called Landsmål ("language of the land"), published from 1848 onward. His aim was a Norwegian standard independent of Danish, based on the dialects he considered least influenced by it. Landsmål gained official equality with the Danish-derived form (Riksmål, later Bokmål) in 1885, and the name Nynorsk was adopted in 1929. A long official policy of merging the two standards into a common Samnorsk was pursued through reforms in 1917 and 1938 but was effectively abandoned in the early 2000s.

Use today

Municipalities choose their official form (or neutrality); Nynorsk predominates in much of Vestland, Møre og Romsdal and parts of other western and inland counties. Broadcasters such as NRK are required to use both forms, and government documents must be available in both. Nynorsk has a distinguished literary tradition, including writers such as Aasmund Olavsson Vinje, Arne Garborg, Olav Duun, Tarjei Vesaas and Jon Fosse, the 2023 Nobel laureate in literature.
