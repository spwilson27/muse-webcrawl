The Communist International (Comintern), also known as the Third International, was a Soviet-controlled international organization founded in 1919 that advocated world communism. It was led by the Communist Party of the Soviet Union and sought to coordinate communist parties worldwide in the struggle to overthrow the international bourgeoisie and create an international Soviet republic.

History

The Comintern was founded at a congress in Moscow in March 1919, in the aftermath of the Russian Revolution and the discrediting of the Second International, whose member parties had largely supported their national governments in the First World War. Vladimir Lenin and Leon Trotsky were among its founders, and Grigory Zinoviev was its first chairman. At its Second Congress in 1920 it adopted the "Twenty-one Conditions" for membership, requiring affiliated parties to model themselves on the Bolshevik party.

During the 1930s, under Joseph Stalin, the Comintern's policy shifted from denouncing social democrats as "social fascists" to promoting Popular Front alliances against fascism (from 1935). Stalin dissolved the Comintern in 1943, ostensibly to allay the fears of the Soviet Union's Western allies in the Second World War that it was interfering in other countries' politics.

Organization

Supreme authority nominally rested with the World Congress, of which seven were held between 1919 and 1935; between congresses, power lay with the Executive Committee of the Communist International (ECCI) in Moscow.
