Kushan Empire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Gold coin of Kanishka showing the king at an altar, with the Buddha on the reverse]

The Kushan Empire (c. AD 30 – c. 375) was a syncretic empire in Central and South Asia formed by the Yuezhi — nomads driven west from China's borders — which at its height under Kanishka the Great (r. c. 127–150) stretched from the Aral Sea and Tarim Basin to the Ganges plain, controlling the crossroads of the Silk Road between China, India, Parthia/Rome and the steppe. Its wealth from trade, its gold coinage bearing Greek, Iranian, Hindu and Buddhist deities, and its patronage of Mahayana Buddhism and Gandharan art made it one of the great cultural brokers of the ancient world, transmitting Buddhism to Central Asia and China.

Rise and Kanishka

The Yuezhi confederation, pushed from the Gansu corridor by the Xiongnu in the 2nd century BC, settled in Bactria; about AD 30–50 Kujula Kadphises united its five principalities into the Kushan realm and pushed into Kabul and Gandhara. His successors Vima Kadphises and Kanishka — whose era (from AD 127, by the now-dominant dating) structured Indian chronology — ruled from twin capitals at Purushapura (Peshawar) and Mathura, raised the great stupa at Peshawar (among the tallest structures of the ancient world), and convened, by Buddhist tradition, the Fourth Buddhist Council in Kashmir.

Culture and decline

Kushan coinage — among the earliest to depict the Buddha, labelled in Greek letters as 'BODDO' — documents a deliberate religious pluralism. Gandharan art fused Greek sculptural forms with Buddhist subjects (the first Buddha images in human form are credited to Gandhara and Mathura in this era), and Kushan merchants and monks carried Buddhism along the Silk Roads. From the 3rd century the empire split: the west fell to the Sasanian Kushanshahs, the Indian remnant shrank under Gupta pressure, and the last Kushan principalities were absorbed by the Kidarites by about 375.
