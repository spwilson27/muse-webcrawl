The cywydd (plural cywyddau) is one of the most important metrical forms in Welsh poetry. It consists of rhyming couplets, each line having seven syllables and ending in alternating stressed and unstressed rhymes, with the ornamentation of cynghanedd (consonantal harmony/alliteration) required in every line.

History

The cywydd rose to prominence in the 14th century, displacing the older awdl as the favored vehicle of the professional poets (beirdd yr uchelwyr) who sang for the Welsh gentry. It was brought to its height by Dafydd ap Gwilym, widely regarded as the greatest Welsh poet, whose cywyddau on love and nature are famous for their wit and immediacy. Other masters include Iolo Goch, Guto'r Glyn, and Tudur Aled.

Genres

Cywyddau were composed for many purposes: praise poems (cywydd mawl), elegies (marwnad), love poems, religious verse, satire, and poems of request (cywydd gofyn) asking patrons for gifts such as horses, weapons, or clothing — often paid for with a poem of thanks in reply.

Legacy

Though strict-metre poetry declined after the 17th century, the cywydd remains a living form in Wales, still composed and contested at eisteddfodau, and its seven-syllable couplet is a defining sound of Welsh literature.
