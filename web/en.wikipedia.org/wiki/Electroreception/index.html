Electroreception

Electroreception
Found inSharks, rays, many fish, platypus, echidnas, some amphibians
Sensory organsAmpullae of Lorenzini; tuberous organs; bill receptors
FormsPassive (ampullary) and active (electric organ discharge)
SensitivityDown to ~5 nV/cm in sharks

Electroreception or electroception is the biological ability to perceive natural electrical stimuli. It has been observed almost exclusively in aquatic or amphibious animals, because water is a much better conductor of electricity than air, though notable exceptions include the monotremes (platypus and echidnas), cockroaches and bees. Electroreception is used for electrolocation (detecting objects) and for communication between members of some species.

Overview

In passive electroreception, the animal senses the weak bioelectric fields generated by the muscles and nerves of other animals. Sharks and rays (elasmobranchs) possess the ampullae of Lorenzini — jelly-filled canals opening as pores on the skin, connected to sensory cells that can detect voltage gradients as small as a few nanovolts per centimetre. They use this sense in the final stage of attacks on prey and possibly for navigation using Earth's magnetic field.

In active electroreception, found in the weakly electric fish of the orders Gymnotiformes (South American knifefish) and Mormyriformes (African elephantfish), the animal generates its own electric field with an electric organ and detects distortions of that field caused by nearby objects — an electrical analogue of echolocation. These fish also communicate by modulating their discharges.

Monotremes

The platypus has nearly 40,000 electroreceptors in mucous glands along its bill, and echidnas have several hundred to a few thousand. Combined with mechanoreceptors, these allow the platypus to locate prey in murky water with its eyes, ears and nostrils closed while diving.

Evolution

Electroreception is an ancient sense: it was present in early vertebrates and is homologous across lampreys, cartilaginous fish and many bony fish, deriving from the lateral line system. It was lost in most terrestrial vertebrates and in modern ray-finned fish lineages, then evolved independently several times — in monotremes, in two orders of freshwater fish, and in some salamanders.
