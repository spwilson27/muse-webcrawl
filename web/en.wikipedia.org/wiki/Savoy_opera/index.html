Savoy opera

Savoy opera is a style of comic opera that developed in Victorian England in the late 19th century. The name derives from the Savoy Theatre in London, built by impresario Richard D'Oyly Carte to house the works of the celebrated partnership of librettist W. S. Gilbert and composer Arthur Sullivan. The term is often extended to all fourteen Gilbert and Sullivan operas, and sometimes to the works of Carte's other composers at the Savoy.

The Gilbert and Sullivan works

Between 1871 and 1896 Gilbert and Sullivan produced fourteen comic operas, including Trial by Jury (1875), H.M.S. Pinafore (1878), The Pirates of Penzance (1879), Patience (1881), Iolanthe (1882), The Mikado (1885), Ruddigore (1887), The Yeomen of the Guard (1888) and The Gondoliers (1889). Their hallmark is "topsy-turvy" satire: Gilbert's witty, inverted-logic plots mock British institutions — the law, the navy, the peerage, aestheticism — while Sullivan's music combines the craft of classical composition with the sparkle of operetta.

Style and influence

Savoy opera set the template for modern musical theatre: dialogue-integrated songs, patter songs for comic baritones, large choruses with distinct male and female ensembles, and plots driven by improbable premises played with complete seriousness. The works' enormous popularity in Britain, America and throughout the English-speaking world made them among the first international theatrical blockbusters, spawning pirated productions, touring companies, and a permanent performing tradition maintained after 1982 by amateur and professional companies worldwide, as well as the D'Oyly Carte Opera Company (1887–1982).
