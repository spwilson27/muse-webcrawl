Home front during World War II

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The home front during World War II covers the domestic, economic, social and cultural life of the civilian populations of the countries involved in the Second World War (1939–1945). The conflict was a "total war": governments mobilised entire economies, conscripted labour as well as soldiers, rationed food and materials, and directed propaganda at their own citizens, while civilians in many countries were exposed directly to bombing, occupation and, in Eastern Europe and Asia, mass violence.

Mobilisation and production

Belligerent states converted industry to war production on an enormous scale. In the United States, automobile plants turned to tanks and aircraft; employment soared and unemployment effectively disappeared. In Britain, the Emergency Powers Acts gave the government sweeping control over industry and labour, and women were conscripted into war work from 1941. The Soviet Union evacuated whole factories east of the Urals ahead of the German invasion of 1941.

Rationing and everyday life

Rationing of food, clothing and fuel was widespread. Britain introduced food rationing in January 1940 under the Ministry of Food, encouraged citizens to "Dig for Victory" by growing vegetables, and organised the evacuation of children from cities threatened by the Blitz. Blackouts, air-raid shelters and civil defence organisations such as the ARP became part of daily life. The United States rationed petrol, rubber, sugar and meat, and ran scrap drives; Germany, despite plundering occupied Europe, imposed severe shortages on its own and occupied populations.

Women and work

Millions of women entered paid employment in factories, shipyards and offices, symbolised in the United States by "Rosie the Riveter". In Britain the Women's Land Army worked in agriculture. The changes were only partly permanent, but they shifted expectations about women's work in many countries.

Propaganda and morale

Governments used posters, radio, newsreels and film to sustain morale, encourage production and savings, and demonise the enemy. Campaigns warned that "careless talk costs lives". In occupied countries, resistance movements ran clandestine presses, while in the Axis states propaganda ministries controlled nearly all media.

Civilians under attack

Strategic bombing devastated cities in Britain, Germany and Japan; the firebombing of Dresden and Tokyo and the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki each killed tens of thousands of civilians. Civilians also suffered siege (Leningrad), forced labour, deportation and genocide, above all the Holocaust, in which six million Jews were murdered by Nazi Germany and its collaborators.
