Sensory processing sensitivity

Sensory processing sensitivity (SPS) is a temperamental or personality trait involving increased sensitivity of the central nervous system and deeper cognitive processing of physical, social, and emotional stimuli. The trait was developed by psychologist Elaine Aron in the 1990s; people who score high in it are often described as highly sensitive persons (HSPs). It is estimated that about 15–20 percent of the population has the trait, which appears in over a hundred other animal species as well.

Characteristics

Aron summarizes the trait with the acronym DOES: Depth of processing, Overstimulation (becoming overwhelmed by intense or prolonged stimuli), Emotional reactivity and empathy, and Sensitivity to subtle stimuli. People high in SPS tend to notice subtleties others miss, process experiences deeply, and are more affected — for better and worse — by their environments, a pattern consistent with differential susceptibility theory.

Research and criticism

SPS is measured with the Highly Sensitive Person Scale and has been linked in neuroimaging studies to greater activation in brain regions involved in awareness and empathy. Researchers distinguish SPS from introversion (with which it overlaps only partially), neuroticism, and clinical sensory processing disorder; it is a normal temperament trait rather than a disorder. Critics note measurement issues and overlap with established dimensions of personality such as openness and neuroticism.
