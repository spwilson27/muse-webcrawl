Keratoconus (from Greek kéras, horn, and Latin conus, cone) is a disorder of the eye in which the normally round cornea progressively thins and bulges outward into a cone shape. This distorts vision, producing blurred and multiple images, streaking and sensitivity to light. It usually affects both eyes, often unequally, and typically begins in the late teens or early twenties, progressing for ten to twenty years before stabilising.

Signs and diagnosis

Early symptoms include mild blurring, frequent changes of spectacle prescription and increasing irregular astigmatism that glasses cannot fully correct. Clinical signs include Munson's sign (a V-shaped indentation of the lower lid when looking down), Fleischer rings of iron deposits around the cone, and Vogt's striae in the corneal stroma. Diagnosis is confirmed by corneal topography or tomography, which maps the characteristic inferior steepening of the cornea. Prevalence estimates vary widely, from roughly 1 in 2,000 to about 1 in 375 people in some studied populations.

Causes

The cause is unknown, but both genetic and environmental factors are implicated: a family history is present in a minority of cases, and the condition is associated with atopic disease, chronic eye rubbing, and connective-tissue disorders such as Down syndrome and Ehlers–Danlos syndrome.

Treatment

Mild cases may be managed with glasses or soft lenses, but most patients need rigid gas-permeable, hybrid or scleral contact lenses to mask the irregular corneal surface. Corneal collagen cross-linking with riboflavin and ultraviolet light can stiffen the cornea and halt progression, especially if applied early. Advanced cases with severe thinning or scarring may require corneal transplantation (keratoplasty), which has a high success rate; intrastromal corneal ring segments are another surgical option.
