Hundred (county division)

A hundred is an administrative division that is geographically part of a larger region. It was formerly used in England, Wales, some parts of the United States, Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Norway and in the German state of Schleswig-Holstein. It is still used in the Australian state of South Australia and in the Northern Territory.

England

In England the hundred was the main intermediate unit between the parish (or township and tithing) and the county (shire), introduced during the Anglo-Saxon period, probably in the 10th century. Traditionally it was said to comprise 100 hides of land — a hide being the area considered sufficient to support one family — though in practice hundreds varied greatly in size. Each hundred had its own court, meeting regularly under the sheriff's tourn or the local lord, dealing with minor crime, taxation and administrative business. Equivalent units were known as wapentakes in the Danelaw counties, wards in the far north, and rapes and lathes in Sussex and Kent.

Decline

From the 19th century, functions of the hundred courts passed to county courts, poor law unions and new local-government districts, and the Hundreds of England and Wales were effectively abolished as administrative units by the Local Government Act 1894. The names survive in local usage — for example the Chiltern Hundreds, whose stewardship is still nominally used by MPs to resign — and in place names.

Elsewhere

Scandinavian hundare and herred/härad served similar purposes; emigrants carried the term to Delaware, where hundreds remain the state's basic tax and census districts, and to South Australia, where counties are divided into hundreds for land-administration purposes.
