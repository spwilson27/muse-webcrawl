Wisdom of the crowd

The wisdom of the crowd is the collective opinion of a diverse, independent group of individuals rather than that of a single expert. The idea is that the aggregation of information in groups often results in decisions that are better than those made by the best individual in the group, because individual errors and biases cancel out when estimates are averaged.

Origins

The classic demonstration comes from Francis Galton's 1907 report Vox Populi: at a livestock fair, some 800 people guessed the dressed weight of an ox. The median guess was within about one percent of the true weight — closer than most experts' estimates. James Surowiecki popularised the phenomenon in his 2004 book The Wisdom of Crowds, arguing that wise crowds need diversity of opinion, independence of judgement, decentralisation (local knowledge) and a mechanism for aggregating private judgements into a collective decision.

Applications and limits

Prediction markets, forecasting tournaments and ensemble methods in statistics and machine learning exploit the effect; so, in a rough sense, do markets and juries. The effect fails when participants influence each other (information cascades and groupthink), when the crowd lacks relevant knowledge, or when the problem has no verifiable answer. Social influence experiments show that even mild interaction can undermine the accuracy of the aggregate by reducing diversity.
