The Siege of Limerick in 1691 was the second and concluding siege of the city in the Williamite War in Ireland, following the failed Williamite siege of 1690. After the Jacobite defeat at the Battle of Aughrim in July 1691, the surviving Irish army withdrew behind the walls of Limerick and Galway. The Williamite army under the Dutch general Godert de Ginkel invested Limerick in late August. The siege ended with negotiated surrender: the Treaty of Limerick was signed on 3 October 1691, ending the war.

Background

In 1690 William III had personally besieged Limerick but was repulsed after Patrick Sarsfield's famous raid destroyed his siege train at Ballyneety. William raised the siege and left Ireland. The war continued under Ginkel, who in June 1691 stormed Athlone, crossing the Shannon, and on 12 July destroyed the main Jacobite field army at Aughrim, where the French commander Saint-Ruth was killed. Galway capitulated soon after, leaving Limerick the last major Jacobite stronghold.

The siege

The garrison, commanded in practice by Sarsfield—the most popular Irish commander—was divided between French officers who wished to hold out for French reinforcements and Irish officers sceptical of relief. Ginkel established batteries against the English Town on King's Island and the Irish Town across the Abbey River. In a daring operation on the night of 22–23 September, Williamite troops crossed the Shannon above the city by pontoon bridge, drove in the Clare-side defences and cut the garrison's last escape route into County Clare, though a French-led sortie partially recovered the position. Meanwhile a long-awaited French fleet arrived in the Shannon estuary too late and in insufficient strength.

The treaty

With relief hopeless, the Jacobites requested a truce. Negotiations produced the Treaty of Limerick, signed on 3 October 1691. Its military articles allowed the Irish soldiers to sail for France—the departure remembered as the Flight of the Wild Geese—where some 14,000 men entered French service. Its civil articles promised Irish Catholics freedom of worship "consistent with the laws of Ireland" and security of property for those who had defended Limerick. The civil articles were subsequently repudiated or whittled away by the Protestant-dominated Irish Parliament, whose penal laws of the following decade made the treaty's breach a lasting grievance; the fallen stone on which the treaty was supposedly signed is preserved in Limerick as the Treaty Stone.
