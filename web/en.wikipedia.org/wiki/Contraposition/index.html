Contraposition

In traditional logic and mathematics, contraposition is the inference of a conditional statement from another by negating and swapping its antecedent and consequent. The statement obtained is called the contrapositive of the original. A conditional statement and its contrapositive are logically equivalent: one is true if and only if the other is true.

A conditional statement and its contrapositive are logically equivalent.

Form

Given a proposition of the form "If P, then Q", its contrapositive is "If not Q, then not P":

(P → Q) ↔ (¬Q → ¬P)

For example, the contrapositive of "If it is raining, then the ground is wet" is "If the ground is not wet, then it is not raining." Contraposition must be distinguished from conversion (swapping P and Q without negation) and inversion (negating without swapping); neither conversion nor inversion preserves truth in general, although the converse and inverse are equivalent to each other.

Proof by contrapositive

Because the two forms are equivalent, a standard proof technique is proof by contrapositive: to prove "If P then Q", one instead proves "If not Q then not P". For example, to show that if n² is even then n is even, one proves that if n is odd then n² is odd, which follows directly from (2k+1)² = 4k² + 4k + 1.

In classical syllogistic logic

In Aristotelian term logic, contraposition applies fully to A propositions ("All S are P" → "All non-P are non-S") and E propositions, while I propositions do not admit contraposition and O propositions admit it only in the limited form sometimes called contraposition by limitation.

Intuitionistic caveat

In intuitionistic logic, only one direction is valid: from (P → Q) one may infer (¬Q → ¬P), but the converse requires double-negation elimination and is not generally available.
