Tort

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tort is a civil wrong that causes someone else to suffer loss or harm, resulting in legal liability for the person who commits the tortious act. Tort law aims primarily to provide relief to injured parties, impose liability on those responsible, and deter others from committing harmful acts. It contrasts with criminal law (state prosecution of offences) and contract law (voluntarily assumed obligations).

Main categories

Torts are conventionally grouped into intentional torts (battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass to land, defamation in some classifications), negligence, and strict-liability torts (such as liability for abnormally dangerous activities or defective products). Negligence — breach of a duty of care causing foreseeable harm — is the largest category, governing road accidents, medical injury, workplace safety and professional services. Landmark cases include Donoghue v Stevenson (1932), which founded the modern neighbour principle in Commonwealth law.

Remedies and reform

The principal remedy is compensatory damages — general (pain, suffering, loss of amenity) and special (quantifiable financial loss) — with injunctions available for continuing wrongs such as nuisance. Tort systems are debated for cost, delay and unpredictability ("compensation culture" controversies), and many jurisdictions have reformed them with no-fault schemes (notably New Zealand's Accident Compensation Corporation), caps, or proportionate liability rules.
