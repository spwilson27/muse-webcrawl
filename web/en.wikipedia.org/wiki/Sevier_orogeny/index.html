Sevier orogeny

The Sevier orogeny was a mountain-building event that affected western North America from northern Canada to northern Mexico, driven by subduction of the Farallon Plate beneath the western margin of the North American Plate. It is named after the Sevier River region of Utah and was most active during the Late Jurassic through the Cretaceous and into the early Cenozoic, roughly 160 to 50 million years ago.

Characteristics

Unlike the younger, basement-involved Laramide orogeny, the Sevier orogeny is characterised by thin-skinned deformation: vast sheets of sedimentary rock were pushed eastward along low-angle thrust faults that detached within weak layers of the stratigraphic section, stacking the rocks into a fold-and-thrust belt. Shortening was accommodated along major thrust systems such as the Paris–Willard, Charleston–Nebo, Paxton, and Canyon Range thrusts in the central part of the belt.

East of the thrust belt lay the Western Interior foreland basin, which subsided under the load of the thrust sheets and received thick deposits of sediment eroded from the rising mountains; the basin hosted the Western Interior Seaway at times of high sea level.

Tectonic setting

The orogeny is attributed to compression transmitted inland from the subduction zone along the Pacific margin, where oceanic lithosphere of the Farallon Plate was being consumed. Debate continues over the relative roles of plate-boundary forces, gravitational spreading of elevated terrain, and changes in subduction angle. The Sevier belt forms the eastern part of the Cordilleran orogenic system, behind the contemporary magmatic arc, and overlaps in both time and space with the Laramide orogeny to its east and southeast.
