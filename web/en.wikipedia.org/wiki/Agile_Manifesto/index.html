Agile Manifesto

The Manifesto for Agile Software Development, commonly known as the Agile Manifesto, is a declaration of values and principles for iterative, people-centred software development. It was written in February 2001 by seventeen software practitioners who met at the Snowbird resort in Utah, United States, to find common ground among lightweight development methods such as Scrum, Extreme Programming, Crystal, and Adaptive Software Development. The meeting also led to the formation of the Agile Alliance.

The four values

The manifesto states that its authors value:

  • Individuals and interactions over processes and tools
  • Working software over comprehensive documentation
  • Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
  • Responding to change over following a plan

The authors added that while there is value in the items on the right, they value the items on the left more.

The twelve principles

Behind the manifesto are twelve principles, including: satisfying the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable software; welcoming changing requirements, even late in development; delivering working software frequently, from a couple of weeks to a couple of months; daily collaboration between business people and developers; building projects around motivated individuals and trusting them; favouring face-to-face conversation; using working software as the primary measure of progress; maintaining a sustainable pace; continuous attention to technical excellence and good design; simplicity—maximising the amount of work not done; self-organising teams; and regular reflection and adjustment by the team.

Signatories

The seventeen authors included Kent Beck, Ward Cunningham, Martin Fowler, Robert C. Martin, Ken Schwaber, and Jeff Sutherland, several of whom had created the methods (Extreme Programming, Scrum, Crystal) the manifesto unified.

Impact and criticism

The manifesto launched the agile software development movement, profoundly influencing modern practice, project management beyond software, and frameworks such as Scrum and Kanban. It has also been criticised—for vagueness, for commercialisation into certification industries ("agile industrial complex"), and by some of its own signatories, who have argued that common corporate implementations betray its spirit.
