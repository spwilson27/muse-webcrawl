G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), also known as seven-(pass-)transmembrane domain receptors, 7TM receptors, heptahelical receptors, serpentine receptors, and G protein-linked receptors (GPLR), form a large group of evolutionarily related proteins that are cell surface receptors that detect molecules outside the cell and activate cellular responses. They are coupled with G proteins. They pass through the cell membrane seven times, in contrast to other receptors that pass through only once.

G protein-coupled receptors can be found only in eukaryotes, including yeast, plants, choanoflagellates, and animals. The ligands that bind and activate these receptors include light-sensitive compounds, odours, pheromones, hormones, and neurotransmitters. They vary in size from small (such as neurotransmitters and hormones) to large (such as proteins).

History and significance

The 2012 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to Brian Kobilka and Robert Lefkowitz for their work that was "crucial for understanding how G protein-coupled receptors function". There have been at least seven other Nobel Prizes awarded for some aspects of G protein-mediated signalling. As of 2012, two of the top three selling drugs worldwide — Salmeterol (Advair) and Rosuvastatin (Lipitor) — were GPCR agonists.

Classification

GPCRs can be grouped into six classes based on sequence homology and functional similarity: Class A (rhodopsin-like receptors), Class B (secretin receptor family), Class C (metabotropic glutamate/pheromone), Class D (fungal mating pheromone receptors), Class E (cyclic AMP receptors), and Class F (frizzled/smoothened). A very large group of them is the olfactory receptors.

Physiological roles

GPCRs are involved in a wide variety of physiological processes. Some examples include the visual sense (opsins use photoisomerisation), the sense of smell (olfactory receptors), behavioural and mood regulation (receptors that bind serotonin, dopamine, GABA and glutamate), regulation of immune system activity, autonomic nervous system transmission, and cell density sensing.

Structure

GPCRs are integral membrane proteins that possess seven membrane-spanning domains or transmembrane helices. The extracellular parts of the receptor can be glycosylated. These extracellular loops also contain two highly conserved cysteine residues that form disulfide bonds to stabilise the receptor structure. Some seven-transmembrane helix proteins (channelrhodopsin) that resemble GPCRs may contain ion channels within their protein structure.

Signalling mechanism

The GPCR signalling cycle: a ligand binds the receptor, which undergoes a conformational change and activates a bound heterotrimeric G protein by catalysing the exchange of GDP for GTP on the Gα subunit. The activated Gα–GTP and the released Gβγ dimer then modulate downstream effectors such as adenylyl cyclase, phospholipase C, and ion channels. Signalling is terminated by GTP hydrolysis, receptor phosphorylation by GPCR kinases (GRKs), and arrestin binding.
