Chinese constellations

System: Three Enclosures and Twenty-Eight Mansions

Total asterisms: Over 300 recorded historically

Chinese constellations are the traditional patterns of stars catalogued in Chinese astronomy, distinct from the constellations of the Western tradition. Chinese astronomers divided the sky into Three Enclosures (sān yuán) near the north celestial pole and Twenty-Eight Mansions (èrshíbā xiù) along the ecliptic, containing in total more than 300 named asterisms, generally smaller than Western constellations.

History

Star names are recorded from at least the Warring States period; the astronomers Shi Shen, Gan De and Wu Xian were credited with catalogues in the 4th century BC. The system was systematised during the Han dynasty and refined by Chen Zhuo in the 3rd century AD, who combined earlier schools into a framework of some 283 asterisms comprising about 1,464 stars. The oldest surviving Chinese star map is the Dunhuang star chart, dating to the Tang dynasty.

The Three Enclosures

The Purple Forbidden Enclosure surrounds the celestial pole, reflecting the emperor's palace on Earth; the Supreme Palace Enclosure and the Heavenly Market Enclosure lie nearby. Their asterisms are named for officials, palace buildings and market features of the imperial court.

The Twenty-Eight Mansions

The mansions are grouped into four palaces, each associated with a cardinal direction, season and mythical animal: the Azure Dragon of the East, the Black Tortoise of the North, the White Tiger of the West and the Vermilion Bird of the South. The Moon's nightly position among the mansions served calendrical and astrological purposes.

Modern use

When the International Astronomical Union fixed the 88 modern constellations in the 1920s, China adopted them for scientific use, while traditional names survive in star nomenclature — many bright stars retain Chinese-derived designations in Chinese usage — and in cultural and astrological contexts.
