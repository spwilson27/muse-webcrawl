James Larkin (21 January 1876 – 30 January 1947) was an Irish republican, socialist and trade union leader. Born in Liverpool to Irish parents, he founded the Irish Transport and General Workers' Union (ITGWU) and the Irish Labour Party, and is best remembered for leading the workers in the Dublin Lock-out of 1913, the great industrial dispute that shaped Irish labour history.

Early life and union career

Larkin worked from childhood as a docker in Liverpool and was dismissed for striking in 1905 before becoming an organiser for the National Union of Dock Labourers. Sent to Belfast in 1907, he organised both Protestant and Catholic dockers in a landmark strike, then moved to Dublin. Expelled from the NUDL, he founded the ITGWU in 1909 to organise Ireland's unskilled workers, employing the tactic of the sympathetic strike. His newspaper, The Irish Worker, reached a mass readership.

The 1913 Lock-out

In 1913 Dublin's employers, led by William Martin Murphy, attempted to destroy the ITGWU by locking out some 20,000 workers who refused to renounce the union. The dispute lasted from August 1913 to January 1914 and involved violent clashes, including "Bloody Sunday" on 31 August 1913, when police baton-charged a crowd on Sackville Street. Though the workers eventually returned on poor terms, the Lock-out made Larkin a legendary figure — "Big Jim" — and permanently altered Irish industrial relations.

America and later years

Larkin travelled to the United States in 1914 to raise funds. Caught up in the post-war "Red Scare," he was imprisoned in Sing Sing for "criminal anarchy" (1920–1923) before being pardoned and deported. Back in Ireland he clashed with his successor William O'Brien, founded the rival Workers' Union of Ireland in 1924, and sat in the Dáil for the Irish Workers' League and later Labour. He died in Dublin in 1947. His statue, arms flung wide in oratorical pose, stands on O'Connell Street, inscribed with his maxim: "The great appear great because we are on our knees. Let us rise."
