Geostationary orbit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The geostationary ring (not to scale).

A geostationary orbit (GEO) is a circular orbit 35,786 km above Earth's equator in which a satellite's orbital period exactly matches Earth's rotation, so the satellite appears fixed in the sky. Fixed ground antennas can therefore point at it permanently, making GEO the home of most communications and many weather satellites. The concept was popularised by science-fiction writer Arthur C. Clarke in 1945, and the ring is sometimes called the Clarke Belt.

Slots in the belt are allocated internationally because satellites must be spaced to avoid interference and collision. At the end of their lives, GEO satellites are required to boost themselves a few hundred kilometres higher into a 'graveyard orbit' rather than polluting the working ring. Reaching GEO costs far more Delta-v than Low Earth orbit, so satellites often travel via an elliptical geostationary transfer orbit and circularise with their own engines.
