Sea Island cotton

Sea Island cotton is a type of cotton grown in the Sea Islands of South Carolina and Georgia in the United States, as well as in the West Indies. It is a variety of Gossypium barbadense noted for its exceptionally long staple fibres, producing a fine, silky and strong yarn prized for luxury textiles.

History

The cultivar was developed in the late 18th century from seed originating in the Bahamas and the West Indies, adapted to the warm, humid climate and sandy soils of the barrier islands off the Carolina and Georgia coasts. By the early 19th century it commanded the highest prices of any cotton on the world market, and its cultivation was concentrated on islands such as St. Helena, Edisto, Wadmalaw and Johns Island.

Decline and revival

Production collapsed in the early 20th century: the boll weevil devastated crops from about 1917, and changes following the American Civil War had already eroded the plantation system on which production depended. Commercial US production ended, though small-scale cultivation continued in the Caribbean, and the name is still used for fine West Indian Sea Island cotton used by luxury shirtmakers.
