Service-oriented architecture

A service-oriented architecture (SOA) is an architectural style that focuses on discrete services instead of a monolithic design. A service is a self-contained unit of software functionality, or set of functionalities, that can be accessed remotely and acted upon and updated independently, such as retrieving a credit card statement online. Services expose well-defined interfaces and communicate over a network through standard protocols, allowing software components to be reused across applications.

Principles

SOA is commonly characterised by loosely coupled, discoverable, reusable and interoperable services that encapsulate their internal implementation. Services interact by exchanging messages, often coordinated through an enterprise service bus (ESB) that handles routing, transformation and protocol mediation. Standardised service contracts describe the messages a service accepts and returns without prescribing its technology.

History

The concepts behind SOA grew out of earlier distributed computing approaches such as CORBA and DCOM. The term gained prominence in the early 2000s alongside web services technologies such as SOAP, WSDL and UDDI, promoted by analysts including Gartner, which had used the term since the mid-1990s. From the 2010s, the finer-grained microservices style is often described as a successor or refinement of SOA ideas.
