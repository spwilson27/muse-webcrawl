AACS protects content on Blu-ray and HD DVD media

The Advanced Access Content System (AACS) is a standard for content distribution and digital rights management, intended to restrict access to and copying of protected optical discs. It was developed by the AACS Licensing Administrator, a consortium of entertainment and technology companies, and is used on Blu-ray Discs and the discontinued HD DVD format.

Design

AACS encrypts disc content with the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) using 128-bit keys. Each disc carries a volume ID and a set of encrypted title keys stored in a Media Key Block; each licensed player holds device keys that allow it to process the Media Key Block and recover a media key, which combined with the volume ID yields the volume unique key used to decrypt the title keys. Because device keys can be revoked by issuing new Media Key Blocks on future discs, compromised players can in principle be excluded — a feature not present in the earlier DVD CSS system.

Security history

Within months of the first releases, processing keys extracted from software players were published on the internet, most famously in early 2007, when a 128-bit processing key circulated widely and provoked large numbers of takedown notices and a celebrated protest in which the key was republished across thousands of websites. Backup utilities such as AnyDVD HD circumvented the system by decrypting content on the fly, and the episode is often cited as an example of the difficulty of enforcing DRM through key secrecy alone.

Related technologies

AACS works alongside BD+, a virtual-machine-based protection layer used only on Blu-ray, and the mandatory HDCP encryption of digital video outputs.
