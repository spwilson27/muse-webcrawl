Mary Dinsmore Ainsworth (née Salter; December 1, 1913 – March 21, 1999) was an American-Canadian developmental psychologist known for her work in the development of the attachment theory. She designed the Strange Situation procedure to study attachments in children.

Life and career

Ainsworth was born in Glendale, Ohio, and grew up in Toronto, where she earned her BA (1935), MA (1936), and PhD (1939) at the University of Toronto. After wartime service with the Canadian Women's Army Corps, she taught at Toronto before moving to London in 1950, where she worked at the Tavistock Clinic with John Bowlby on maternal deprivation. She later conducted pioneering observational research on mother–infant interaction in Uganda (reported in Infancy in Uganda, 1967), then at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore, and from 1975 at the University of Virginia.

The Strange Situation

In her Baltimore longitudinal study, Ainsworth developed the Strange Situation (1970), a structured laboratory procedure of separations and reunions between an infant, the caregiver, and a stranger. From infants' behaviour she identified three patterns of attachment: secure attachment (about 65% of US middle-class samples), insecure-avoidant, and insecure-resistant/ambivalent. Her student Mary Main later added a fourth, disorganised, category. Ainsworth's work linked attachment security to maternal sensitivity — the caregiver's prompt, appropriate responsiveness to the infant's signals — and provided the empirical foundation for Bowlby's attachment theory, detailed in Patterns of Attachment (1978, with Blehar, Waters, and Wall).

Legacy

Ainsworth was elected president of the Society for Research in Child Development and received the American Psychological Association's Award for Distinguished Contributions to Child Development. Her Strange Situation remains one of the most widely used research paradigms in developmental psychology.
