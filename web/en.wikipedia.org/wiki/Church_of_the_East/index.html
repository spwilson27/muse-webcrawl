The Church of the East, also known historically (often pejoratively) as the Nestorian Church, was an Eastern Christian church that developed in the Sasanian Empire (Persia) during the first centuries AD. Centred in Mesopotamia, it became one of the most geographically extensive churches of the pre-modern world, with dioceses stretching from the Mediterranean to China and India.

The Nestorian Stele (781) records the arrival of the Church of the East missionary Alopen in China in 635.

History

According to tradition, Christianity reached Mesopotamia through the apostles Thomas, Thaddeus (Addai), and Mari. The church organised itself under the Catholicos of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, and in 424 declared its independence from the western patriarchates. It affirmed a dyophysite Christology associated with Theodore of Mopsuestia and, after the Council of Ephesus (431), sheltered supporters of Nestorius, though modern scholarship stresses the church's theology was more nuanced than the "Nestorian" label implies.

Under the Sasanians the church alternated between toleration and persecution; under the Abbasid Caliphate its Catholicos moved to Baghdad, and its scholars — notably Hunayn ibn Ishaq — played a major role in translating Greek learning into Arabic. Missions along the Silk Road established churches in Central Asia, in Tang-dynasty China (recorded on the Xi'an Stele of 781), and in India, where the Saint Thomas Christians maintained communion with the East Syriac patriarchate.

Decline and divisions

The church suffered catastrophic losses under Timur in the late 14th century, shrinking to communities in northern Mesopotamia, Kurdistan, and India. A schism in 1552 produced a line of patriarchs in communion with Rome (ancestor of the Chaldean Catholic Church), while the traditionalist line continues today as the Assyrian Church of the East and the Ancient Church of the East.
