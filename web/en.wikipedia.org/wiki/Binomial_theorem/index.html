In elementary algebra, the binomial theorem describes the algebraic expansion of powers of a binomial. For a non-negative integer n, it states that (x + y)ⁿ equals the sum over k from 0 to n of the binomial coefficient "n choose k" multiplied by xⁿ⁻ᵏyᵏ.

Binomial coefficients

The coefficients of the expansion — 1, n, n(n−1)/2, and so on — are the binomial coefficients, which also count combinations and appear as the entries of Pascal's triangle. For example, (x + y)² = x² + 2xy + y² and (x + y)³ = x³ + 3x²y + 3xy² + y³.

History and generalizations

Special cases were known in ancient India, China, and Persia; al-Karaji and Omar Khayyam developed the general method of expansion, and Pascal's triangle is named for Blaise Pascal's 1654 treatise. Isaac Newton generalized the theorem in the 1660s to arbitrary real exponents, producing an infinite series — the binomial series — valid when |y/x| < 1.
