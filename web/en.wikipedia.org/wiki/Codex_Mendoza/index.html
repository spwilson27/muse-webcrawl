Codex Mendoza

The Codex Mendoza is an Aztec codex, believed to have been created around 1541, some twenty years after the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire. Commissioned, it is thought, by Antonio de Mendoza, the first viceroy of New Spain — for whom it is named — possibly for presentation to Emperor Charles V, it was painted by Nahua tlacuiloque (artist-scribes) on European paper and annotated in Spanish by a priest fluent in Nahuatl. It is one of the richest surviving sources on Aztec history, tribute, and daily life.

Contents

The codex consists of 71 folios in three sections. The first records the history of the Mexica rulers from the founding of Tenochtitlan in 1325 (its famous frontispiece shows the eagle on the cactus) through their conquests, reign by reign. The second section lists the towns conquered by the Aztec Empire and the tribute each owed — quantities of warrior costumes, feathers, cacao, gold, jade, and other goods rendered in pictographs. The third is an ethnographic account of Aztec life from birth to old age: child-rearing and punishment, education, food, crafts, priests, and warriors.

History of the manuscript

Sent to Spain, the ship carrying the codex was captured by French privateers, and the manuscript passed into the hands of the French royal cosmographer André Thévet. It later belonged to Richard Hakluyt, was published in part by Samuel Purchas in 1625, and came to rest in the Bodleian Library at the University of Oxford, where it remains today (MS. Arch. Selden. A. 1). Long neglected, its importance was recognized in the 19th and 20th centuries, and a full digital facsimile has been produced by the Bodleian in collaboration with Mexican institutions.
