Great Flood of 1993

The Great Flood of 1993 (or Great Mississippi and Missouri Rivers Flood of 1993) was a flood that occurred in the Midwestern United States, along the Mississippi and Missouri rivers and their tributaries, from April to October 1993. The flood was among the most costly and devastating to ever occur in the United States, with $16 billion in damages (some sources say as high as $20 billion) and 50 people killed. The hydrographic extent of the flooding along the Mississippi River and its tributaries was the largest ever recorded in the United States.

Causes and development

The flood was caused by a persistent weather pattern in which a stationary high-pressure system over the southeastern United States repeatedly steered moist air from the Gulf of Mexico over the upper Midwest, producing record rainfall from the autumn of 1992 through the summer of 1993. Saturated soils from an unusually wet spring left nowhere for the water to go, and river levels rose steadily through June and July.

At St. Louis, Missouri, the Mississippi River crested at 49.58 feet (15.11 m) on August 1, 1993, nearly 20 feet above flood stage. Hundreds of levees failed along both rivers; of the roughly 1,100 federal and non-federal levees in the affected basins, about 1,000 were overtopped or breached. Some 150 major rivers and tributaries were affected across nine states: Illinois, Iowa, Kansas, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, South Dakota, and Wisconsin.

Impact

Approximately 10,000 homes were destroyed and about 15 million acres (61,000 km²) of farmland were inundated, in some places for months. Some 75 towns were completely flooded. More than 50,000 people were evacuated. Barge traffic on the Mississippi and Missouri rivers was halted for nearly two months, and sections of major highways and rail lines, including Amtrak service, were disrupted for weeks.

Aftermath

The flood prompted a major federal review of floodplain management, the "Galloway Report," which recommended greater emphasis on wetland restoration, floodplain buyouts, and non-structural flood mitigation rather than reliance on levees alone. Tens of thousands of flood-damaged properties were purchased and demolished under federal buyout programs. The flood also reshaped thinking about the environmental role of wetlands in absorbing floodwaters.
