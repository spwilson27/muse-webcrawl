Argyll Robertson pupil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Argyll Robertson pupil accommodates but does not react to light.

Argyll Robertson pupils are bilateral small pupils that reduce in size on a near object (they accommodate), but do not constrict when exposed to bright light (they do not react). It is a highly specific sign of neurosyphilis; however, Argyll Robertson pupils may also be a sign of diabetic neuropathy. In general, pupils that accommodate but do not react are said to show light-near dissociation.

History

The sign is named after Douglas Argyll Robertson (1837–1909), a Scottish surgeon and ophthalmologist, who described the abnormal pupillary responses in patients with tabes dorsalis in the 1860s. At the time syphilis was very common, and the sign was one of the classic physical findings of tertiary syphilis affecting the nervous system.

Mechanism

The phenomenon is caused by damage to the pretectal region of the midbrain, interrupting the fibres mediating the light reflex while sparing the more ventrally located fibres of the near (accommodation) reflex. Typically both pupils are small (miotic), irregular in shape, and may dilate poorly with mydriatic drops.

Clinical significance

The classic mnemonic is that the Argyll Robertson pupil is like a "prostitute's pupil" — it accommodates but does not react. Its presence prompts testing for syphilis and evaluation for other features of neurosyphilis, such as tabes dorsalis and general paresis. Light-near dissociation also occurs in other conditions, including Adie's pupil (unilateral, tonic), dorsal midbrain (Parinaud) syndrome, and diabetes mellitus, so the sign must be interpreted alongside pupil size, regularity, and laterality.
