Am star

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (recreated from memory)

An Am star, or metallic-line star, is a type of chemically peculiar star of spectral type A whose spectrum has strong and often variable absorption lines of metals such as zinc, strontium, zirconium and barium, and deficiencies of others such as calcium and scandium. The abbreviation "Am" is written as a prefix in the spectral classification (for example, "kA5hF2mF6" for a star whose classification differs according to whether it is based on the calcium K line, the hydrogen lines, or the metallic lines).

Characteristics

Am stars typically appear to be of spectral type A to early F by their metallic lines, yet their hydrogen lines indicate a later type and their calcium K line an earlier type. The peculiar abundances are understood as the result of atomic diffusion in the star's stable, slowly rotating atmosphere: elements with suitable absorption spectra are pushed upward by radiation pressure (radiative levitation) while others sink under gravity (gravitational settling).

Occurrence

Because rapid rotation mixes the atmosphere and erases the effect, Am stars rotate slowly. Most are members of binary systems, in which tidal braking by a companion has slowed their rotation; a large majority of Am stars are in binaries with orbital periods of a few days. Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, is sometimes discussed in connection with the Am phenomenon, and the class was first systematically described in the 1940s by astronomers including William W. Morgan.

Diversity

Marginal Am stars (Am:) show only mild anomalies. A related but hotter class, the HgMn stars, shows similar diffusion-driven peculiarities in late B-type stars. The study of Am stars provides an important test of diffusion theory in stellar atmospheres.
