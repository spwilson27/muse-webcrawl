DiGeorge syndrome

Other names22q11.2 deletion syndrome, velocardiofacial syndrome
CauseDeletion of a segment of chromosome 22
Frequency1 in 4,000 live births (approx.)

DiGeorge syndrome, also known as 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, is a syndrome caused by a microdeletion on the long arm of chromosome 22. While the symptoms can vary, they often include congenital heart problems, specific facial features, frequent infections, developmental delay, learning problems, and cleft palate. Associated conditions include kidney problems, schizophrenia, hearing loss, and autoimmune disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis or Graves' disease.

Cause

DiGeorge syndrome is caused by the deletion of a small segment of chromosome 22: about 3 million base pairs, comprising some 30 to 40 genes, are missing in most cases. The deletion most often arises as a new mutation during early development, though in around 10 percent of cases it is inherited from an affected parent in an autosomal dominant pattern. The loss of the gene TBX1 is thought to be responsible for many of the characteristic features, because TBX1 directs development of the pharyngeal pouch system from which the thymus, parathyroid glands, and parts of the heart and face derive.

Signs and symptoms

The features are often remembered with the mnemonic CATCH-22: Cardiac defects (especially conotruncal anomalies such as tetralogy of Fallot), Abnormal facies, Thymic hypoplasia (leading to immune deficiency), Cleft palate, and Hypocalcaemia (from underdeveloped parathyroid glands), arising from the chromosome 22 deletion. Immune deficiency results from a small or absent thymus, leaving T-cell production impaired and causing recurrent infections. Individuals also have elevated rates of psychiatric illness in adolescence and adulthood, including schizophrenia, anxiety, and mood disorders.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is confirmed by genetic testing, most commonly fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) or chromosomal microarray, which detects the 22q11.2 deletion. There is no cure; management is directed at the individual problems: heart defects may require surgery, hypocalcaemia is treated with calcium and vitamin D supplementation, immune deficiency may require antibiotic prophylaxis or, rarely, thymus transplantation, and speech, educational, and psychiatric support address developmental and mental-health needs.

History

The syndrome is named for Angelo DiGeorge, the American paediatrician who described the association of absent thymus and parathyroid glands with immunodeficiency in the 1960s. The condition overlaps with velocardiofacial (Shprintzen) syndrome, and the identification of the shared chromosomal deletion in the 1990s unified the diagnoses under 22q11.2 deletion syndrome.
