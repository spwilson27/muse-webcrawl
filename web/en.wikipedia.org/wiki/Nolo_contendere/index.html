Nolo contendere (Latin for "I do not wish to contend"), often shortened to no contest, is a plea used in criminal proceedings in which the defendant accepts conviction and punishment as if guilty, but neither admits nor disputes the charge. For purposes of the case at hand, a nolo plea has the same immediate effect as a guilty plea, and the defendant is sentenced accordingly.

Distinguishing features

The principal advantage of a no-contest plea is that, unlike a guilty plea, it generally cannot be used against the defendant as an admission of liability in a later civil lawsuit arising from the same conduct. In federal court and most states, the plea requires the consent of the court, which must consider the parties' views and the public interest. Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11, a nolo plea may be entered only with the court's consent.

History and use

The plea has roots in English law and an implied confession doctrine dating back centuries. It is commonly used by corporate defendants in antitrust and regulatory prosecutions, and by individuals who wish to resolve a criminal charge without creating an admission usable by private plaintiffs. Some jurisdictions limit the plea: it may be unavailable for certain offenses, and in some states it is treated as a conviction for later sentencing enhancement just as a guilty plea is. To enter the plea, a defendant typically first appears at arraignment.

Comparison with the Alford plea

An Alford plea is formally a guilty plea in which the defendant asserts innocence; a nolo plea neither asserts innocence nor admits guilt. Both result in conviction, but the nolo plea's protection against use as an admission in civil proceedings is its defining practical difference.
