Battle of Jena–Auerstedt

The twin battles of Jena and Auerstedt were fought on 14 October 1806 during the War of the Fourth Coalition in the Napoleonic Wars. Napoleon, believing he faced the main Prussian army, attacked at Jena and routed the Prince of Hohenlohe's force; simultaneously, fourteen miles north, Marshal Davout's single corps met and defeated the actual main Prussian army under the Duke of Brunswick at Auerstedt — one of the most remarkable feats of arms of the era. Together the battles destroyed Prussia as a military power for years.

Course

Prussia, confident in the army inherited from Frederick the Great, had delivered an ultimatum ordering Napoleon behind the Rhine and advanced into Thuringia. Napoleon swung the Grande Armée through the Thuringian Forest onto the Prussian flank and communications. At Jena, fog lifted to reveal his attack crushing Hohenlohe, followed by a ruinous pursuit. At Auerstedt, Davout's III Corps — outnumbered nearly two to one — held off the Prussian main body in fog, counterattacked, and routed it; the Duke of Brunswick was mortally wounded, and with no second-in-command able to take over, the Prussian army dissolved.

Consequences

The pursuit that followed became legendary: fortress after fortress capitulated, and Napoleon entered Berlin on 27 October, famously taking Frederick the Great's sword from Potsdam. The Treaties of Tilsit (1807) halved Prussia's territory. The humiliation provoked the Stein–Hardenberg and Scharnhorst reforms that rebuilt Prussia — intellectually, too: Hegel, finishing his Phenomenology in Jena as the guns sounded, called Napoleon the "world-soul on horseback".
