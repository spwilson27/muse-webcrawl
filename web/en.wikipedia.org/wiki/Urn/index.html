Urn


An ancient Greek funerary urn

An urn is a vase-like vessel, usually with a lid, a narrow neck and a rounded or footed body. The word comes from the Latin urna, a water jar or vessel for drawing lots. Urns are most familiar as funerary urns (cinerary urns) used to hold the cremated ashes of the dead, a practice found from the Bronze Age Urnfield culture of Europe to the present day.

Uses

Beyond funerary use, urns have served to store water, wine, grain and valuables, to draw lots or cast votes (whence probability's "urn model" of drawing balls from an urn), and as decorative garden ornaments. Large metal urns — tea urns and coffee urns, and the Russian samovar — dispense hot drinks. In sport, the most famous urn of all is the small terracotta Ashes urn, symbol of the England–Australia cricket rivalry since 1882–83.

Materials and history

Urns have been made of pottery, bronze, marble, alabaster, glass and biodegradable materials. Notable traditions include Greek lekythoi and loutrophoroi, Etruscan canopic jars, Chinese Ming dynasty porcelain urns, and the face urns of early Iron Age Europe. John Keats immortalised the form in his 1819 "Ode on a Grecian Urn".
