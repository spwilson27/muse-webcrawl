Triangle wave

A triangle wave is a periodic, non-sinusoidal waveform that, as its name suggests, has a triangular shape. It is a piecewise linear, continuous real function that rises and falls with equal slope and equal duration in each direction. Triangle waves are common in signal processing and in music synthesis, where their timbre is softer and more flute-like than a square wave's but richer than a pure sine wave's.

Fourier series

Like the square wave, the triangle wave contains only odd harmonics, but their amplitudes fall off much faster — in proportion to 1/n2 rather than 1/n — and alternate in sign. The fundamental carries most of the energy, which accounts for the mellow sound. A band-limited approximation can be built by summing the series:

tri(t) = (8/π2) Σ (−1)k sin(2k+1)ωt / (2k+1)2

Generation

A triangle wave can be generated by integrating a square wave: the integral of a constant positive value is a rising ramp and of a constant negative value a falling ramp. Analogue function generators exploit this with an integrator and a Schmitt trigger comparator. In digital synthesis it is computed directly from the phase accumulator.

Applications

Triangle waves are used in pulse-width modulation, in CRT and LCD display deflection (as the basis of raster scanning), as test signals, and as a basic oscillator waveform in subtractive synthesisers.
