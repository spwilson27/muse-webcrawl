Burgundy

Burgundy (French: Bourgogne) is a historical territory and former administrative region of east-central France, named after the East Germanic Burgundians who settled there in the 5th century. In 2016 it was merged with Franche-Comté to form the region of Bourgogne-Franche-Comté. Its capital and largest city is Dijon.

History

After the Burgundian kingdom fell to the Franks in 534, the name Burgundy attached to shifting territories: a Merovingian kingdom, a Carolingian duchy, and — most famously — the late medieval Duchy of Burgundy. Under the Valois dukes (1363–1477), including Philip the Bold, John the Fearless, Philip the Good, and Charles the Bold, the duchy became one of Europe's richest and most cultured states, rivaling France itself, until Charles's death at Nancy in 1477 led to its partition between France and the Habsburgs.

Wine

Burgundy is one of the world's great wine regions, renowned for Pinot noir reds and Chardonnay whites from the Côte d'Or (Côte de Nuits and Côte de Beaune), Chablis, the Côte Chalonnaise, and the Mâconnais. Its vineyard climats were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2015.

Geography and culture

The region spans rolling agricultural land drained by the Saône and Yonne rivers, with the Morvan hills in the west. Dijon is famed for mustard and its ducal palace; Romanesque abbeys such as Cluny and Vézelay mark its medieval religious importance.
