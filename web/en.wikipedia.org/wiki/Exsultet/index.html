Exsultet

An 11th-century southern Italian Exsultet roll.

The Exsultet (spelled Exultet in classical Latin), also known as the Easter Proclamation, is a lengthy sung proclamation delivered before the paschal candle during the Easter Vigil in the Roman Rite of the Catholic Church, as well as in some Anglican, Lutheran, and other Western Christian traditions. It is normally sung by a deacon, or if none is available, by a priest or cantor. Its opening words in Latin are Exsultet iam angelica turba coelorum — "Exult, let them exult, the hosts of heaven."

Content

The hymn is a lyrical praise of the night of the Resurrection. It recounts the Exodus of the Israelites from Egypt as a type of Christ's deliverance, blesses the paschal candle and the bees whose wax formed it, and celebrates the paradox of the felix culpa — the "happy fault" of Adam that occasioned so great a Redeemer: O certe necessarium Adae peccatum ("O truly necessary sin of Adam"). The text in its received form dates to roughly the 7th–9th centuries, and has been attributed at various times to St. Ambrose or St. Augustine.

Exsultet rolls

In southern Italy during the 10th–13th centuries, the chant was written on long parchment rolls (Exsultet rolls) bearing illustrations oriented so that, as the deacon unrolled the scroll over the ambo, the congregation saw the images right side up while the text faced the singer. Surviving examples, such as those at Bari and Montecassino, are masterpieces of medieval Italian art.
