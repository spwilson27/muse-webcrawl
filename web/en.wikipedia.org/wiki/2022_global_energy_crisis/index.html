2022 global energy crisis

Crisis details
Period2021 – 2023 (peak in 2022)
Main causesPost-pandemic demand rebound; Russian invasion of Ukraine; cuts to Russian gas supply to Europe
Peak gas price (Europe)Over €340/MWh (TTF, August 2022)
EffectsInflation surge, cost-of-living crises, industrial curtailment, accelerated renewables and LNG investment

The 2022 global energy crisis was a worldwide shortage and price shock in energy markets that began with the post-COVID-19 economic rebound in 2021 and intensified dramatically after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. Natural gas, coal and electricity prices reached record levels, particularly in Europe, which had depended on Russia for roughly 40 per cent of its gas imports. The crisis drove global inflation to multi-decade highs, triggered huge government subsidy programmes, and reshaped energy policy and geopolitics.

Origins

Through 2021, recovering demand, low wind output in Europe, depleted gas storage, droughts affecting hydropower and nuclear outages tightened markets; European benchmark gas prices rose fivefold by the end of the year. Russia's state exporter Gazprom supplied contracted volumes but declined to book extra capacity, which European leaders later described as deliberate pressure.

Invasion and supply shock

Following the invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022, Western sanctions — and Russia's retaliatory cuts — progressively throttled pipeline gas to Europe; flows through Nord Stream 1 stopped entirely in late August, and the pipelines were sabotaged in September 2022. European gas prices peaked above €340 per megawatt-hour in August 2022, roughly fifteen times normal levels. Coal prices also hit records as Europe restarted coal plants, and oil briefly approached $140 a barrel.

Responses

The EU agreed emergency demand-reduction targets, filled storage above 90 per cent before winter, fast-tracked liquefied natural gas terminals (notably floating units in Germany), and capped some energy revenues. Governments spent hundreds of billions of euros shielding households and businesses; the UK introduced an Energy Price Guarantee. A mild winter, demand cuts of about 20 per cent and surging LNG imports from the United States and Qatar averted the feared physical shortages, and prices fell sharply through 2023.

Consequences

The crisis pushed global inflation to its highest since the 1980s, contributing to cost-of-living protests and monetary tightening worldwide; developing countries faced fuel and food shocks compounded by the same war's effects on grain and fertiliser. It also accelerated deployment of renewables and heat pumps, revived interest in nuclear power, and entrenched a lasting reorientation of global gas trade away from Russian pipelines.
