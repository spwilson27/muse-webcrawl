Adie syndrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

SpecialtyNeurology, ophthalmology
SymptomsOne or both pupils abnormally dilated and slow to react to light; absent deep tendon reflexes
OnsetTypically young adults, more often women
CausesDamage to the ciliary ganglion or postganglionic parasympathetic fibers, often idiopathic

Adie syndrome, also known as Holmes–Adie syndrome, is a neurological disorder characterized by a tonically dilated pupil that reacts slowly to light but shows a more normal response to accommodation (near response). It is frequently accompanied by absent or diminished deep tendon reflexes and, in some patients, segmental disturbances of sweating. The condition is named after the British neurologist William John Adie and the neurologist Gordon Holmes, who described aspects of the disorder in the early twentieth century.

Signs and symptoms

The hallmark of Adie syndrome is the tonic pupil: an enlarged pupil that constricts very slowly, or barely at all, in response to bright light, yet constricts more briskly when the patient focuses on a near object — a dissociation between the light and near responses. Once constricted, the pupil redilates slowly. Reading may be blurred when the affected eye is involved. Many patients also show loss of the ankle and knee jerk reflexes. Some develop patches of excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis) alternating with areas of reduced sweating, a variant sometimes called Ross syndrome.

Cause

The pupillary abnormality results from damage to the postganglionic parasympathetic fibers that supply the iris sphincter and ciliary muscle, usually at the level of the ciliary ganglion within the orbit. In most cases no cause is found; proposed triggers include viral or bacterial infection, trauma, and autoimmune processes. The loss of tendon reflexes is thought to reflect involvement of dorsal root ganglia.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical and can be supported by pharmacological testing: a dilute solution of pilocarpine (0.1%) constricts the Adie pupil because of denervation supersensitivity, while barely affecting a normal pupil. Slit-lamp examination often shows segmental palsy of the iris sphincter with slow, vermiform movements of the intact segments.

Treatment and prognosis

No cure exists, and none is usually needed. Reading glasses can compensate for impaired accommodation, and dilute pilocarpine drops may reduce the pupil size and glare sensitivity. Over years the tonically dilated pupil often becomes smaller ("little old Adie pupil"), and the condition may eventually affect the other eye. The overall prognosis is benign, although reflex loss is permanent.
