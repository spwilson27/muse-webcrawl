Hollandaise sauce
TypeSauce
Place of originFrance

Hollandaise sauce (French: sauce hollandaise) is a hot emulsion of egg yolk, melted butter and lemon juice, seasoned with salt and usually a pinch of cayenne or white pepper. It is one of the five "mother sauces" of French haute cuisine codified by Auguste Escoffier, and is best known as the sauce served over eggs Benedict.

Preparation

Yolks are whisked over gentle heat with a little water or lemon juice until they thicken and lighten; melted (often clarified) butter is then drizzled in while whisking continuously, forming a stable emulsion with a consistency like softly whipped cream. The sauce must be kept warm but never boiled, or the emulsion will split. Cooks employ several rescue techniques — a spoonful of cold water, an ice cube, or whisking over a fresh yolk — to repair a broken hollandaise.

History

Despite its name, the sauce is French; the name is generally thought to be a homage to the dairy produce of Holland, or to have arisen when French chefs adopted a Dutch sauce during the seventeenth century. Recipes resembling it appear in French cookbooks from the 1600s, and it was firmly established by the era of Escoffier, who classified it among the mother sauces in Le Guide Culinaire (1903).

Derivatives and uses

  • Béarnaise — hollandaise flavoured with tarragon, shallot and chervil, served with steak.
  • Maltaise — made with blood-orange juice.
  • Mousseline — folded with whipped cream for a lighter texture.
  • Eggs Benedict — poached eggs and ham on an English muffin, coated with hollandaise.

The sauce is also served with asparagus, steamed vegetables and poached fish. Because it contains lightly cooked egg, modern recipes stress careful temperature control and prompt service.
