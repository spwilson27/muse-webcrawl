Self-ownership

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Self-ownership is the concept of property in one's own person — the moral or natural right of an individual to control their own body and life, and to exclude others from doing so without consent. It is a foundational premise of classical liberal and libertarian philosophy: John Locke argued in the Second Treatise (1689) that every man has a property in his own person, from which he derived, by the mixing of labour, property in external things.

From self-ownership, libertarians derive the Non-aggression principle: to coerce a person — by conscription, prohibition or unchosen taxation of their labour's product — is to treat them as a means owned by others. Robert Nozick built his entitlement theory on the idea. The concept also appears, in different form, in defences of bodily autonomy in feminist and medical ethics.

Criticism

Critics, notably G. A. Cohen in Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality (1995), argue that self-ownership combined with unequal external resources yields deep inequality, and that Nozick's conclusions depend on accepting full self-ownership while denying that the world itself is jointly owned; Cohen suggested Marxists should question whether they accept self-ownership at all. Others object to the property metaphor itself — persons, they argue, are not the kind of thing that can be owned, by themselves or anyone — or note that the principle says little about children, incapacity and the obligations that bodies in society owe one another.
