Trifle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A glass bowl of trifle layered with sponge, custard, fruit and cream]

A trifle is an English layered dessert served in a glass bowl: sponge cake (often soaked in sherry or fortified wine), fruit and sometimes jelly, custard, and whipped cream, in strata meant to be dug through together. It appears in print in 1596 (in Thomas Dawson's The Good Huswife's Jewell) as something simpler — thick cream with ginger and rosewater — and took its layered form in the 18th century, when Hannah Glasse's 1747 recipe stacked biscuits, custard, syllabub and decorations into a centrepiece. Its name carries its own defence: a trifle is a light thing, a little of everything, not to be taken too seriously.

Purist wars

Trifle generates England's fiercest dessert correspondence. Jelly: in or out? (Many purists say out — the jelly is a 20th-century commercial intrusion; just as many families would riot without it.) Sherry: essential, or an excuse? Chocolate trifle, Black Forest trifle, and the tinned-fruit-salad school all have partisans. Structurally, though, the agreement holds: soak, fruit, custard, cream, chill well, and let the layers marry — trifle is better on its second day and is the traditional destiny of Christmas leftovers at Boxing Day.

Relatives and fame

Scotland's tipsy laird swaps in whisky and raspberries; Italy's zuppa inglese ("English soup") is the trifle's Alchermes-soaked cousin; the American South layers similar "tipsy cakes". Pop culture keeps the form visible — most notoriously in a 1999 Friends episode in which a cookbook misprint produces a trifle layered with beef, peas and onions, judged, in the show's finest food criticism, to taste "like feet" by everyone except Joey.
