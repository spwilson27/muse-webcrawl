Parallel evolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Parallel evolution is the development of a similar trait in related but distinct species descending from the same ancestor, but from different clades. It is distinguished from convergent evolution, in which unrelated species evolve similar traits independently, and from evolutionary reversal. Parallel evolution suggests that similar developmental or genetic mechanisms in related lineages make the independent origin of the same trait likely.

Definition and distinctions

Because the distinction between parallel and convergent evolution depends on how closely related the species are and how similar their starting conditions were, the two are frequently treated together as "parallel/convergent evolution" or under the broader term homoplasy. Traits that arise in parallel often do so through similar genetic changes, whereas convergent traits in distantly related organisms may be built by different genetic and developmental routes.

Examples

  • The independent evolution of echolocation-related hearing genes (such as Prestin) in bat and dolphin lineages, and of similar antifreeze proteins in Arctic and Antarctic fish
  • Repeated evolution of similar shell shapes in separate lineages of lake snails and of body forms in stickleback fish adapting to freshwater habitats in parallel
  • The repeated evolution of C4 photosynthesis in closely related grass lineages
  • Loss of flight and similar body plans in parallel among related island bird lineages
  • Parallel speciation: similar ecotypes of sticklebacks, Anolis lizards on different Caribbean islands, and whitefish evolving in separate lakes

Significance

Parallel evolution is evidence that evolution can be repeatable and, to some degree, predictable when related organisms face similar selective pressures with similar genetic toolkits. Studies of parallel genetic changes have identified "hotspot" genes repeatedly involved in adaptation, informing debates about the relative roles of chance and determinism in evolution, famously framed by Stephen Jay Gould's question of whether "replaying the tape of life" would give the same outcome.
