Hepburn romanization

Hepburn romanization (Japanese: ヘボン式ローマ字, Hebon-shiki rōmaji) is the most widely used system for transliterating the Japanese language into the Latin alphabet. It is named after the American missionary and physician James Curtis Hepburn, who published it in the third edition of his Japanese–English dictionary in 1886. The commonly used modern version is known as Revised Hepburn.

Features

Hepburn represents Japanese sounds according to English-like phonetic values rather than following the structure of the kana syllabaries strictly. Thus し is written shi, ち is chi, つ is tsu, and じ is ji, where the Japanese government's Kunrei-shiki system writes si, ti, tu, and zi. Long vowels are marked with macrons in Revised Hepburn (Tōkyō, Ōsaka), and the syllabic n is written m before b, p, and m in Traditional Hepburn but n in Revised Hepburn.

Use

Hepburn and its revisions are used in most English-language publications about Japan, on Japanese road signs and station name boards, in personal names on passports, and in the Library of Congress romanization for Japanese. Although the Japanese government officially promulgated Kunrei-shiki in 1954, Hepburn dominates in practice wherever Latin-alphabet text is aimed at foreign readers. A Japanese government panel announced in 2024 that the official standard would shift to a Hepburn-based system, the first formal revision in some seventy years.
