Women's colleges in the United States are institutions of higher education that admit only female students as degree candidates. Founded mainly in the 19th century to give women access to the higher education from which they were excluded, they flourished in the era when most prestigious colleges were all-male. At their peak in the 1960s there were more than 230; after most formerly male institutions became coeducational around 1969–1972, the number fell steadily to roughly thirty today, many of them liberal arts colleges in consortia with coeducational partners.

History

The earliest chartered women's college was Georgia Female College (now Wesleyan College) in 1836. Emma Willard and Mary Lyon pioneered rigorous female education, Lyon founding Mount Holyoke in 1837. The later "Seven Sisters" — Mount Holyoke, Vassar, Wellesley, Smith, Radcliffe, Bryn Mawr, and Barnard — were created as counterparts to the men's Ivy League. Historically Black women's colleges such as Spelman College (1881) served African American women excluded elsewhere.

Coeducation and decline

From the late 1960s, elite men's colleges began admitting women, and many women's colleges closed, merged, or went coeducational — Vassar in 1969 and Radcliffe, which merged fully into Harvard by 1999. Others, including Wellesley, Smith, and Bryn Mawr, reaffirmed their missions. Some have faced financial pressure and controversies over admissions policies for transgender and non-binary students; several, such as Mills College, merged into larger universities in the 2020s.

Present day

Advocates argue that women's colleges produce disproportionate numbers of women leaders, scientists, and PhD recipients, noting graduates such as Hillary Clinton (Wellesley), Madeleine Albright (Wellesley), and Katharine Hepburn (Bryn Mawr). The remaining institutions coordinate through the Women's College Coalition and often share courses with neighbouring universities, as Barnard does with Columbia University and Scripps with the Claremont Colleges.
