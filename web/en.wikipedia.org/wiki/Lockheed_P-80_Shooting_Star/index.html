The Lockheed P-80 Shooting Star was the first jet fighter used operationally by the United States Army Air Forces (later the US Air Force, which redesignated it the F-80). Designed by Clarence "Kelly" Johnson and his Skunk Works team in 1943, it was one of only a handful of Allied jet combat aircraft to see service in the Second World War era, and it fought extensively as a fighter-bomber in the Korean War.

Development

The XP-80 was designed around the British de Havilland Goblin turbojet in an astonishing 143 days, in response to the appearance of German jet fighters. It first flew on 8 January 1944, powered by a Halford H-1B engine, and production aircraft used the General Electric J33 (later built by Allison). Two pre-production YP-80s were sent to Italy in early 1945 and flew reconnaissance sorties, but the P-80 did not see combat before the war ended; several test pilots, including America's top-scoring ace Richard Bong, were killed in early P-80 accidents.

Operational history

Redesignated F-80 in 1948, the Shooting Star became the backbone of early USAF jet squadrons. In Korea from 1950, F-80s flew ground-attack and interdiction missions throughout the war, and on 8 November 1950 an F-80 flown by Lt. Russell J. Brown claimed the first-ever jet-versus-jet victory, against a MiG-15—though the slower, straight-wing F-80 was soon outclassed as a day fighter by the swept-wing F-86 Sabre and shifted mainly to fighter-bomber duties. The two-seat trainer version, the T-33, became one of the most produced jet trainers in history (over 6,500 built) and served with air forces around the world for decades; the F-94 Starfire interceptor was also derived from the design.

Specifications

The F-80C was armed with six 0.50 in (12.7 mm) machine guns, could carry bombs and rockets under its wings, and had a maximum speed of about 956 km/h (594 mph). It was powered by a single Allison J33 turbojet of about 5,400 lbf thrust with water injection.
