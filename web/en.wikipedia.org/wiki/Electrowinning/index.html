Electrowinning

Electrowinning, also called electroextraction, is the electrodeposition of metals from their ores that have been put in solution or liquefied. In this process, a current is passed from an inert anode through a liquid leach solution containing the dissolved metal ions so that the metal is extracted as it is deposited in an electroplating process onto the cathode.

Process

The electrolyte is an aqueous solution of metal salts, usually sulfates. An electric current is applied between an inert anode (often made of lead alloy or coated titanium) and a cathode, typically a stainless steel blank or a "starter sheet" of the metal being produced. Metal ions in the solution are reduced at the cathode surface:

Mn+ + ne− → M

At the anode, water is oxidized, producing oxygen gas and acid:

2H2O → O2 + 4H+ + 4e−

After a period of deposition, typically several days, the cathode is removed and the deposited metal is mechanically stripped from the starter sheet. The harvested metal can be extremely pure, often exceeding 99.99% purity.

Applications

Electrowinning is widely used in hydrometallurgy for the recovery of metals such as copper, zinc, gold, nickel, cobalt, and manganese. In copper production, electrowinning is commonly paired with solvent extraction (SX-EW), in which copper is leached from low-grade oxide ores, concentrated by solvent extraction, and then electrowon.

History

The principles of electrowinning developed alongside 19th-century electrochemistry. Commercial copper electrowinning grew in the early 20th century and expanded greatly with the adoption of heap leaching and solvent extraction in the 1960s and 1970s.
