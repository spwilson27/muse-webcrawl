Pericardial effusion

A pericardial effusion is an abnormal accumulation of fluid in the pericardial cavity, the space between the layers of the pericardium surrounding the heart. The pericardial sac normally contains a small amount of lubricating fluid (roughly 15–50 mL). When fluid collects in larger amounts or accumulates rapidly, it can compress the heart and impair its filling, a life-threatening condition called cardiac tamponade.

Causes

Pericardial effusions have many causes, including inflammation of the pericardium (pericarditis) from viral or other infections, tuberculosis, autoimmune diseases, cancer (either primary or metastatic, or from lymphoma), kidney failure (uraemia), low thyroid function (myxoedema), chest injury, and complications of heart attack or cardiac surgery. In a substantial fraction of cases no cause is identified (idiopathic).

Signs and symptoms

Small, slowly accumulating effusions may cause no symptoms and be found incidentally on imaging. Larger effusions can cause breathlessness, chest discomfort, light-headedness, and reduced exercise tolerance. On examination there may be muffled heart sounds, low blood pressure, and raised jugular venous pressure; the combination of hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and distended neck veins (Beck's triad) suggests tamponade. Echocardiography is the main diagnostic test; chest X-ray may show an enlarged, globular heart shadow, and the ECG may show low-voltage complexes or electrical alternans.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause and on whether the heart is compromised. Effusions causing tamponade require urgent drainage, usually by pericardiocentesis — needle aspiration guided by ultrasound — or by a surgical pericardial window. Inflammatory effusions may respond to anti-inflammatory drugs such as NSAIDs and colchicine; underlying conditions such as infection or malignancy are treated directly. Recurrent effusions may be managed with a pericardial drain, window, or in selected cases removal of part of the pericardium.
