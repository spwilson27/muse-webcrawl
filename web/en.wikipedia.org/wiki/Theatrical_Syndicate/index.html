Theatrical Syndicate

The Theatrical Syndicate was an American organisation that from 1896 exercised a near-monopoly over theatrical bookings in the United States. Formed by six theatre managers and booking agents — Charles Frohman, Al Hayman, Marc Klaw, Abraham Erlanger, Samuel F. Nirdlinger (Nixon) and J. Fred Zimmerman Sr. — the Syndicate controlled the routing of first-class touring productions to hundreds of theatres across the country, dictating terms to theatre owners, producers and actors alike. Its dominance shaped American commercial theatre until opposition led by the Shubert brothers broke its power in the 1910s.

Formation and operation

Before 1896, touring companies booked engagements by negotiating separately with local theatre managers, a chaotic system plagued by overlapping dates and unreliable routes. At a meeting in New York in 1896 the six men pooled control of the principal first-class theatres in the largest American cities and agreed that all their bookings would flow through a common office — effectively the Klaw and Erlanger exchange. Any production wishing to play the Syndicate's houses had to book a full tour through the Syndicate; local managers who dealt with independent attractions risked losing Syndicate shows entirely. Touring routes were standardised into one-night stands and week stands linked by railroad lines, and the Syndicate took commissions from both the theatres and the attractions.

Opposition

Many leading performers and managers resisted. Stars such as Sarah Bernhardt famously performed in tents and skating rinks rather than submit, and independent managers — most consequentially Sam, Lee and Jacob Shubert — built their own circuits of theatres. The Shuberts' open-door policy and their alliance with independent producers gradually eroded the Syndicate's grip. The death of Charles Frohman on the Lusitania in 1915 symbolically marked the Syndicate's decline, and by about 1916 its monopoly was effectively broken, though Klaw and Erlanger's booking business continued.

Legacy

The Syndicate is the classic example of the "trust" era applied to the entertainment business: it rationalised touring, enriched its members, standardised production quality on the road, but also stifled regional theatres — by channelling everything through New York it hastened the decline of local stock companies — and provoked the counter-organisation that created modern Broadway booking.
