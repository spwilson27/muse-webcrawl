Aperitif

An aperitif (French: apéritif) is an alcoholic beverage usually served before a meal to stimulate the appetite, and is usually dry rather than sweet. Common choices for an apéritif are vermouth, champagne, pastis, gin, ouzo, fino sherry, and dry amaro. The word derives from the Latin aperire, "to open," by way of French, and the custom is especially associated with French and Italian dining culture.

Apéritifs are traditionally dry drinks served before a meal

History

The practice of drinking a small amount of alcohol before eating is ancient, with antecedents in flavored wines of the ancient world and in medieval medicinal cordials. The modern apéritif is generally dated to the invention of vermouth in Turin, Italy, in 1786 by Antonio Benedetto Carpano, and to the promotion of Dubonnet in Paris in 1846. In nineteenth-century Italy and France, café culture popularized pre-dinner drinks such as Campari (created in Milan around 1860) and gentian-based bitters. In Italy, the aperitivo developed into a social ritual in which drinks are accompanied by small snacks or buffets.

Types

Apéritifs include aromatized and fortified wines such as vermouth, fino and manzanilla sherry, and quinine-flavored wines like Lillet and Dubonnet; bitter liqueurs and amari such as Campari, Aperol, and Suze; anise-flavored spirits such as pastis, ouzo, and rakı (usually diluted with water); and dry sparkling or still wines, particularly champagne. Apéritif cocktails include the Negroni, the Americano, the Aperol spritz, the martini, and the kir, made with crème de cassis and white wine.

Digestif

The counterpart to the apéritif is the digestif, a drink served after a meal that is traditionally believed to aid digestion; digestifs tend to be stronger or sweeter, such as brandy, grappa, or herbal liqueurs.
