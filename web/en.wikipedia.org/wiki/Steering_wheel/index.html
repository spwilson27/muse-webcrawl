Steering wheel

A steering wheel (also called a driving wheel, a hand wheel, or simply wheel) is a type of steering control in vehicles. It is the part of the steering system manipulated by the driver; the rest of the system responds to these inputs. Steering wheels are used in most modern land vehicles, including all mass-production automobiles, buses, tractors, and trucks, and are turned to change the angle of the steered wheels, usually through a steering column connected to a rack-and-pinion or recirculating-ball mechanism, often with hydraulic or electric power assistance.

History

The earliest automobiles were steered with a tiller, as on a boat. The steering wheel first appeared on a Panhard in 1894, used in the Paris–Rouen race by Alfred Vacheron, and Panhard made it standard within a few years; by the early 1900s the wheel had displaced the tiller almost everywhere. Early wheels were large, thin-rimmed, and made of wood or metal to provide leverage for unassisted steering. As power steering spread after the 1950s, wheels became smaller and thicker-rimmed. Safety designs added collapsible columns and padded hubs, and from the late 1980s the hub commonly houses the driver's frontal airbag. Modern wheels integrate controls for audio, cruise control, telephony, and driver-assistance systems; racing cars use removable, yoke-like or rectangular wheels dense with switches, and some recent production cars have experimented with yoke controls.

Design and use

A typical wheel consists of a circular rim joined by spokes to a central hub mounted on the steering column. Drivers are conventionally taught the "ten and two" or, with airbags, "nine and three" hand positions. The wheel also carries the horn switch and may carry paddle shifters. In right- and left-hand traffic countries the wheel sits on the corresponding side of the cabin. Adjustable columns allow the wheel's rake and reach to be set for the driver. Because it is the driver's constant point of contact, the wheel's feedback — torque buildup and vibration transmitted from the front tires — is an important, and in modern electric systems artificially synthesized, source of information about grip and road conditions.

Other applications

Ship's wheels served the same function on sailing vessels, where the helmsman turned a large spoked wheel connected by ropes and pulleys to the rudder. In video games, racing wheels are popular force-feedback controllers modeled on the automotive original.
