Structural isomer

n-Butane and isobutane (2-methylpropane) share the formula C4H10 but differ in connectivity.

Structural isomers (IUPAC: constitutional isomers) are isomers that have the same molecular formula but different bonding patterns and atomic organization — that is, their atoms are connected in a different order. The concept contrasts with stereoisomers, whose atoms are connected identically but arranged differently in space. Structural isomers have distinct physical and chemical properties: different boiling points, reactivity, and spectra.

Types of structural isomerism

  • Chain (skeletal) isomerism — different carbon skeletons: n-butane vs. isobutane; pentane, isopentane, and neopentane (C5H12).
  • Position isomerism — a functional group or multiple bond at different positions: propan-1-ol vs. propan-2-ol; 1-butene vs. 2-butene; ortho-, meta-, and para-xylene.
  • Functional-group isomerism — different functional groups for the same formula: ethanol vs. dimethyl ether (C2H6O); propanal vs. acetone (C3H6O).
  • Metamerism — different alkyl groups on either side of a functional group, e.g. ethoxyethane vs. 1-methoxypropane.
  • Ring–chain isomerism — an open-chain compound and a ring compound with the same formula, e.g. propene and cyclopropane.

Tautomers

Tautomers are structural isomers that interconvert rapidly, usually by migration of a proton (keto–enol tautomerism, as in acetone and its enol). Because they equilibrate, they are often treated as a single compound in practice, though the separate forms can be trapped and observed spectroscopically.

Counting isomers

The number of structural isomers grows rapidly with molecular size: C10H22 has 75 structural isomers; C20H42 has 366,319. Enumerating them is a classic problem of combinatorics and graph theory (Pólya enumeration), historically important in establishing the structural theory of organic chemistry in the 19th century — the very existence of exactly the predicted numbers of isomers for compounds such as dichlorobenzene (three) confirmed the tetrahedral carbon model.
