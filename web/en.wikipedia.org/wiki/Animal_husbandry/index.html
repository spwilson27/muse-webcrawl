Animal husbandry is the branch of agriculture concerned with animals raised for meat, fibre, milk, eggs or other products. It includes the day-to-day care, breeding and raising of livestock, and has been practised since the Neolithic domestication of animals.

History

Animals were domesticated in the Fertile Crescent and elsewhere from around 11,000 years ago: sheep and goats first, then cattle and pigs. Herding reshaped human societies, providing food security, clothing, transport and traction. Through selective breeding, farmers produced specialised breeds — dairy and beef cattle, wool and meat sheep — a process systematised in the 18th and 19th centuries by pioneers such as Robert Bakewell.

Practices

Husbandry systems range from nomadic pastoralism and extensive grazing on rangeland to intensive indoor production. Core practices include providing feed and water, housing and shelter, breeding management, disease prevention and veterinary care, and record-keeping for pedigree and production. Modern systems add artificial insemination, embryo transfer, formulated rations and climate-controlled housing.

Issues

Livestock farming is a major source of food but also of greenhouse-gas emissions (especially methane), land use and water consumption. Animal welfare — space, transport and slaughter conditions — and the use of antibiotics are subjects of public debate and regulation.
