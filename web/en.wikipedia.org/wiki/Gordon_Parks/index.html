Gordon Parks

Born: Gordon Roger Alexander Buchanan Parks, November 30, 1912, Fort Scott, Kansas, U.S.
Died: March 7, 2006 (aged 93), New York City, U.S.
Occupations: Photographer, film director, author, composer
Known for: American Gothic (Ella Watson); Shaft (1971); The Learning Tree

Gordon Roger Alexander Buchanan Parks (November 30, 1912 – March 7, 2006) was an American photographer, composer, author, poet, and film director, who became prominent in U.S. documentary photojournalism in the 1940s through 1970s — particularly in issues of civil rights, poverty and African Americans — and in glamour photography.

Parks was one of the first black American photographers to achieve prominence. As the first famous pioneer among black filmmakers, he was the first African American to produce and direct major motion pictures — developing the "blaxploitation" genre, with films such as Shaft (1971). He is best remembered for his iconic photos of poor Americans during the 1940s (taken for a federal government project), for his photographic essays for Life magazine, and as the director of Shaft.

Early life

Parks was born in Fort Scott, Kansas, the youngest of fifteen children of a tenant farmer. After his mother's death when he was fourteen, he lived with relatives in Minnesota, where he endured discrimination and poverty, working as a busboy, a janitor's assistant, a brothel pianist, and a semi-professional basketball player. Seeing a magazine story about migrant workers photographed for the Farm Security Administration inspired him to buy a camera from a pawn shop in 1937.

Photography

In 1941 Parks won a fellowship from the Rosenwald Fund and joined the Farm Security Administration / Office of War Information in Washington, D.C. There he made his most famous photograph, American Gothic (1942), showing charwoman Ella Watson holding a mop and broom in front of an American flag — an indictment of racial and gender inequality.

From 1948 to 1972 he was a staff photographer for Life magazine, producing celebrated photo essays on subjects including Harlem gang leader Red Jackson, the civil rights movement, poverty in Brazil (the story of Flávio da Silva), and portraits of figures such as Malcolm X, Martin Luther King Jr., Muhammad Ali, and Duke Ellington.

Film and writing

His semi-autobiographical novel The Learning Tree (1963) was adapted by Parks into a 1969 film for Warner Bros., making him the first African American to direct a major Hollywood studio production. He directed Shaft (1971), a commercial success that launched the blaxploitation era, and its sequel Shaft's Big Score (1972). Parks also composed music, including a ballet, Martin (1989), honoring Martin Luther King Jr.

Legacy

Parks received more than fifty honorary doctorates and numerous awards, including the National Medal of Arts in 1988. The Gordon Parks Foundation in New York preserves his work and supports artists and students.
