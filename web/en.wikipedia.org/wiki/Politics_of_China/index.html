Politics and government of China

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The politics of the People's Republic of China are conducted within a unitary Marxist–Leninist one-party socialist republic led by the Chinese Communist Party (CCP, some 98 million members by recent official counts). The constitution of 1982 vests state power in the National People's Congress (NPC) and its Standing Committee; the president of the PRC is head of state, the premier heads the State Council (the government), and the Central Military Commission commands the armed forces. In practice the Party's organs — the General Secretary, the Politburo and its Standing Committee (seven members since 2012) — stand above the state, and the same leader usually holds the party general secretaryship, the state presidency and the CMC chairmanship (“three-in-one”).

Since 2012, under Xi Jinping, the system has recentralised: term limits on the state presidency were removed by constitutional amendment in 2018; anti-corruption campaigns disciplined millions of cadres; state-owned enterprises and private firms alike were brought under tighter party organisation; and ideological control over media, education and civil society intensified. Scholarship debates the system's resilience (consultative mechanisms, performance legitimacy, meritocratic promotion claims) against its vulnerabilities (information problems, succession opacity, local debt and demographic decline). Sub-national government runs through provinces, prefectures, counties and townships; Hong Kong and Macao are Special Administrative Regions under “one country, two systems” (Hong Kong's autonomy sharply curtailed by the 2020 National Security Law), and Taiwan is claimed by Beijing as a province while being governed by the Republic of China authorities.
