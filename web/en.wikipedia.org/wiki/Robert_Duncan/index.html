Robert Duncan

Born: January 7, 1919, Oakland, California
Died: February 3, 1988, San Francisco, California
Occupation: Poet, essayist
Movement: San Francisco Renaissance; Black Mountain

Robert Edward Duncan (January 7, 1919 – February 3, 1988) was an American poet and a key figure in the San Francisco Renaissance and the Black Mountain school. His work, mystical and syncretic in inspiration, sought to make poetry an act of composition "by field" in which myth, dream, and doctrine interweave.

Life

Duncan's mother died shortly after his birth and he was adopted into a family of theosophists and occultists, an upbringing that shaped his lifelong interest in hermetic traditions. He studied at the University of California, Berkeley, moved in anarchist and avant-garde circles in New York, and in 1944 published the pioneering essay "The Homosexual in Society." Returning to the Bay Area, he became, with Jack Spicer and Robin Blaser, a center of the Berkeley Renaissance; he later taught at Black Mountain College and the New College of California.

Works

Duncan's major collections include The Opening of the Field (1960), which begins with the much-anthologized "Often I Am Permitted to Return to a Meadow"; Roots and Branches (1964); Bending the Bow (1968); and the two Ground Work volumes (1984, 1988). His long prose work The H.D. Book, a meditation on the poet Hilda Doolittle, was published in full only in 2011. Duncan famously declared a fifteen-year moratorium on new books after 1968 to honor his sense that a book should be published only when "the time was right."
