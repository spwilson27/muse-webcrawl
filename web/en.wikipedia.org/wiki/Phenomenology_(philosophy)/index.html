Phenomenology (philosophy)

Phenomenology is a philosophical study and movement that was founded in the first half of the 20th century by Edmund Husserl. It is the study of structures of consciousness as experienced from the first-person point of view. The central structure of an experience is its intentionality — its being directed toward something, as it is experienced by a subject. Phenomenology seeks to describe phenomena as they appear, setting aside theories about their causes and the unexamined assumptions of the "natural attitude".

Husserl's method

Husserl developed phenomenology in the Logical Investigations (1900–01) and deepened it into a transcendental philosophy in Ideas (1913) and the Cartesian Meditations (1931). Key elements include the epoché or "bracketing" — suspending judgement about the existence of the external world in order to study experience itself — and the phenomenological reduction, which turns attention from objects in the world to the acts of consciousness (noesis) and their intended contents (noema) through which objects are given. By varying examples in imagination ("eidetic variation"), the phenomenologist aims to grasp the essential structures of experience. Late Husserl emphasised the lifeworld (Lebenswelt), the pre-theoretical world of everyday experience on which science is founded.

Developments

Husserl's student Martin Heidegger transformed phenomenology into fundamental ontology in Being and Time (1927), centring on Dasein, being-in-the-world, temporality and authenticity. In France, Jean-Paul Sartre fused phenomenology with existentialism, while Maurice Merleau-Ponty developed a phenomenology of embodied perception in Phenomenology of Perception (1945). Emmanuel Levinas redirected it towards ethics and the encounter with the Other. Phenomenology shaped hermeneutics, existentialism, deconstruction and much of 20th-century continental philosophy.

Influence beyond philosophy

Phenomenological methods have been adopted in psychology and psychiatry (exploring lived experience of illness), in qualitative research in nursing, education and sociology, in architecture, and in cognitive science, where "neurophenomenology" (Francisco Varela) seeks to join first-person description with neuroscience. Critics, especially from analytic philosophy, have questioned its claims to rigorous science and the intelligibility of the reduction; defenders see it as an indispensable discipline of describing experience.
