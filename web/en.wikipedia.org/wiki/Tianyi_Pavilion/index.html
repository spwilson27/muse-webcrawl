The Tianyi Pavilion (Chinese: 天一閣, Tiān Yī Gé, "First Pavilion under Heaven") is a library and garden complex in Ningbo, Zhejiang, China. Built in the 1560s by the retired Ming dynasty official Fan Qin, it is the oldest existing private library in China and one of the oldest in Asia, famed both for its collection and for the strict rules by which the Fan family preserved it for over four centuries.

History

Fan Qin, a jinshi graduate who rose to senior office, retired to Ningbo and between 1561 and 1566 built the pavilion to house his collection of some 70,000 volumes, strong in local gazetteers, examination records and Ming editions. The name derives from a Han-dynasty commentary phrase, "heaven begets water" (tiān yī shēng shuǐ), water being the chosen protection against the book collector's great enemy, fire: the two-storey building stands beside a pond, with six rooms above echoed by the water below.

The family rules

The Fans guarded the collection with celebrated strictness: the books were held in common, the key was divided among branches of the family, descendants were forbidden to divide or sell volumes, and outsiders — even high officials — were generally refused entry. Only in 1665 did the scholar Huang Zongxi become the first outsider admitted to read there. Despite the rules, war, theft and decay reduced the holdings over the centuries: British troops took some works during the First Opium War, and further losses followed in the Taiping period and the 20th century.

Today

The pavilion and surrounding gardens are now a museum and a nationally protected cultural site. The surviving original collection numbers around 30,000 volumes, supplemented by later donations, and the complex includes the Fan family ancestral hall, gardens, and exhibition halls devoted to the history of Chinese book collecting. Its design famously influenced later imperial libraries, including the layout traditions associated with the Siku Quanshu repositories.
