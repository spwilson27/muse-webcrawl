Integrated circuit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A black microchip with gold pins on a green circuit board, close-up]

An integrated circuit (IC), also called a microchip, is a set of electronic circuits manufactured on a small plate of semiconductor material, usually silicon. Transistors, resistors and connections are etched photographically in layers, packing billions of components into a fingernail-sized chip.

Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments demonstrated the first working IC in 1958, and Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor independently developed the practical silicon version in 1959; Kilby shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics for his part.

Impact

Integrated circuits made electronics cheap, small and reliable, enabling personal computers, smartphones and the embedding of computation in cars, appliances and medical devices. Their density growth is tracked by Moore's law.

Fabrication is among the most complex manufacturing processes ever devised, with features measured in nanometres and factories costing tens of billions of dollars.
