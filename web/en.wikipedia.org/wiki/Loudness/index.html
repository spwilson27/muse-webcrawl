Loudness

Loudness is the subjective perception of the intensity of a sound — how strong or weak a sound seems to a listener. Unlike sound pressure level, a physical quantity measured in decibels, loudness depends on frequency, duration, bandwidth and the listener's hearing. Psychoacoustics quantifies loudness in phons (matched to a 1 kHz tone) and sones, where a doubling of sones corresponds roughly to a perceived doubling of loudness.

Perception

Equal-loudness contours (the Fletcher–Munson curves, standardised as ISO 226) show that the ear is most sensitive in the 2–5 kHz range and much less sensitive to low and very high frequencies, especially at low levels. Loudness grows as a power function of intensity (Stevens's power law), with a 10 dB increase generally perceived as about twice as loud for mid-frequency tones. Temporal integration, spectral masking and binaural summation also affect perceived loudness.

Measurement and standards

Weighting filters — A-weighting most commonly — approximate the ear's response for environmental noise assessment. For broadcast and streaming, integrated loudness is measured in LUFS under ITU-R BS.1770 and regulated by standards such as EBU R 128 and the US CALM Act, which ended the practice of excessively loud television commercials and underpins platform loudness normalisation.

Health

Prolonged exposure to loud sound causes noise-induced hearing loss; occupational limits are typically set around 85 dB(A) over eight hours. Loudness discomfort levels are also assessed clinically in audiology and hearing-aid fitting.
