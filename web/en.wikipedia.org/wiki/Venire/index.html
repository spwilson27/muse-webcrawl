A venire is the panel or pool of prospective jurors from which a trial jury is selected. The term comes from the Latin venire facias juratores, the name of the writ by which a sheriff was once commanded to summon jurors. Members of the venire are called venirepersons or prospective jurors until they are examined in voir dire and either seated, excused, or removed.

Composition and selection

Modern courts assemble venires from source lists such as voter registrations, driver's license records, and tax rolls, intended to produce a fair cross-section of the community as the Sixth Amendment requires. Summonses direct citizens to report for jury service; those who appear form the venire for the day's cases, and a subset is sent to each courtroom where selection occurs. Systematically excluding a distinctive group from the venire can violate the fair-cross-section requirement and equal protection principles, as in cases where women or racial minorities were underrepresented through the selection method itself.

Process

After random assignment to a courtroom, venire members are questioned about their qualifications and biases. The parties then exercise challenges for cause and peremptory challenges until twelve jurors (and often alternates) remain. If the venire is exhausted before a jury is completed—because of publicity or widespread bias, for example—the court may summon an additional panel or transfer the case to another district.
