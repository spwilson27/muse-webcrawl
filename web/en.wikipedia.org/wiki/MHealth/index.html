mHealth (also written as m-health or mobile health) is a term used for the practice of medicine and public health supported by mobile devices. The term is most commonly used in reference to using mobile communication devices, such as mobile phones, tablet computers and personal digital assistants, and wearable devices such as smart watches, for health services, information, and data collection. The mHealth field has emerged as a sub-segment of eHealth, the use of information and communication technology (ICT), such as computers and the internet, for health services and information.

Applications

mHealth applications include the use of mobile devices in collecting community and clinical health data, delivery of healthcare information to practitioners, researchers, and patients, real-time monitoring of patient vital signs, the direct provision of care (as with mobile telemedicine), and medication adherence reminders. Common examples include SMS-based appointment reminders and treatment support for HIV and tuberculosis patients, smartphone applications for diabetes self-management, decision-support tools for community health workers, and phone-based disease surveillance systems used in outbreak responses.

In low- and middle-income countries, where mobile phone penetration often far exceeds fixed-line or clinic infrastructure, mHealth has been deployed for maternal and child health messaging, vaccination registries, stock monitoring for medicines, and remote consultation connecting rural clinics with specialists. During the COVID-19 pandemic, mobile tools were used at scale for contact tracing, symptom reporting, and vaccination certificates.

Evidence and challenges

Systematic reviews find promising but mixed evidence: text-message interventions show modest benefits for appointment attendance and smoking cessation, while evidence for many commercial wellness apps is thin, and most consumer health apps are not clinically validated. Challenges include data privacy and security, interoperability with electronic health record systems, digital literacy and access inequities, regulatory oversight of apps that function as medical devices, and the difficulty of sustaining pilot projects — a phenomenon sometimes called "pilotitis" — beyond donor-funded trials.
