Treason is the crime of betraying the allegiance one owes to one's sovereign or state — classically by levying war against it, adhering to its enemies and giving them aid and comfort, or (in monarchical law) compassing the death of the sovereign. Historically it was divided into high treason, against the state or monarch, and petty treason, the killing of a social superior such as a master or husband, a distinction abolished in England in the 19th century.

England

English treason law is anchored in the Treason Act 1351, which defined high treason to include compassing the death of the king, queen or heir; violating the king's wife; levying war; adhering to the king's enemies; counterfeiting the great seal or coinage; and killing certain senior officials. The penalty for men was to be hanged, drawn and quartered; the last executions for treason in Britain were of William Joyce ("Lord Haw-Haw") in 1946 and others after the Second World War. Capital punishment for treason was abolished by the Crime and Disorder Act 1998.

United States

Treason is the only crime defined in the U.S. Constitution. Article III, Section 3 restricts it to levying war against the United States or adhering to its enemies, giving them aid and comfort, and requires two witnesses to the same overt act or a confession in open court — safeguards designed by the framers to prevent the abusive treason prosecutions familiar from English history. Convictions have been rare; notable cases include the Whiskey Rebellion trials, the trial of Aaron Burr (acquitted in 1807), and the Second World War cases of the broadcasters Iva Toguri ("Tokyo Rose") and Mildred Gillars ("Axis Sally").

Other countries

Most states retain treason or equivalent offences (such as Hochverrat in Germany), though some have replaced them with modern offences of espionage, terrorism or subversion. Espionage and sedition are related but distinct crimes.
