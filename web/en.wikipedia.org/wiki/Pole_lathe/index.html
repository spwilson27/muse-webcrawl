Pole lathe

A traditional pole lathe in a woodland workshop.

A pole lathe, also known as a spring pole lathe, is a wood-turning lathe that uses a long springy pole as a return spring for a treadle. Pressing the treadle pulls on a cord that is wrapped around the workpiece, causing it to rotate in one direction. When the pressure is released, the pole pulls the cord back, rotating the workpiece in the opposite direction.

Operation

The pole lathe is a simple machine suitable for simple, rough work that does not require great cutting speeds or precision. This type of lathe is frequently used by the "bodgers" of the Chilterns, who made chair legs in the woods surrounding High Wycombe, Buckinghamshire, England. Bodgers also used pole lathes to turn bowls and other items.

Because the workpiece rotates alternately in both directions, the turner must time the application of the cutting tool to the forward (cutting) stroke only. This reciprocal motion distinguishes the pole lathe from later continuous-rotation lathes driven by belts, flywheels or motors.

History

The pole lathe's origins are ancient; forms of reciprocating lathe date back to antiquity. The spring-pole design was common across Europe in the medieval and early modern periods and remained in everyday use by rural craftsmen well into the twentieth century. It survives today in heritage woodworking, green woodworking courses, and traditional chair making.

Construction

  • A sturdy frame or bed holding two centres between which the workpiece is mounted.
  • A treadle connected by a cord to a flexible pole fixed above the lathe.
  • The cord wraps around the workpiece, so treadle pressure spins it.
  • A tool rest across which the turner braces chisels and gouges.

The simplicity of the design means a pole lathe can be built almost entirely from materials found in the woods where it is used.
