The Iroquois Confederacy, known to its members as the Haudenosaunee ("People of the Longhouse"), is a confederacy of Indigenous nations in North America originally comprising five Iroquoian-speaking peoples — the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca — joined by the Tuscarora in 1722 to form the Six Nations. It is one of the oldest participatory democracies in the world, governed by the Great Law of Peace (Gayanashagowa).

Formation

According to Haudenosaunee oral tradition, the confederacy was founded by Dekanawidah, the Great Peacemaker, and his spokesman Hiawatha, who ended generations of warfare among the five nations and bound them in a covenant of peace symbolised by the white pine and the buried weapons. The traditional date is sometimes given as 1142, based on a solar eclipse recorded in the tradition; many historians place the founding in the fifteenth or sixteenth century.

Government

The Great Law of Peace established a Grand Council of fifty rotiyaner (chiefs), apportioned among the nations, who met at Onondaga, the "firekeepers" of the confederacy. Chiefs were nominated by the clan mothers — senior women of the matrilineal clans — who could also remove ("dehorn") a chief who failed in his duties. Decisions required consensus. Women held significant political power through clan structures, controlling land use and nominating leaders.

Colonial era

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the confederacy was the dominant power in the north-east, controlling the fur trade and pursuing the Beaver Wars against French-allied peoples. The Covenant Chain alliance with the British made the Iroquois key players in Anglo-French rivalry. During the American Revolution the confederacy split: most nations sided with the British, while the Oneida and Tuscarora supported the Americans. The 1779 Sullivan Expedition devastated Iroquois towns, and the confederacy's power was broken.

Modern era

Today the Six Nations live in communities in New York State, Wisconsin, Oklahoma and Ontario, including the Six Nations of the Grand River reserve in Ontario — the most populous First Nations reserve in Canada. The Haudenosaunee maintain their own passports and issue lacrosse players under their own flag. The claim that the Great Law of Peace influenced the United States Constitution — advanced by scholars such as Donald Grinde — is widely debated but remains an important part of Haudenosaunee political identity.
