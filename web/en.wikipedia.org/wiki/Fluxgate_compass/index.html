Fluxgate compass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fluxgate compass is an electromagnetic compass that measures the direction of the Earth's magnetic field using fluxgate magnetometer sensors rather than a magnetised needle. Two or three small coils are wound on a highly permeable core that is periodically driven into magnetic saturation by an alternating current; the Earth's field biases the saturation asymmetrically, producing a signal (principally at the second harmonic of the drive frequency) whose amplitude and phase depend on the field component along the coil axis. Electronics convert the signals from orthogonal sensors into a heading.

Applications

Fluxgate compasses are widely used in aircraft, ships' autopilots and electronic navigation systems because they have no moving parts, tolerate vibration, and provide an electrical output that can drive remote repeaters and heading systems. In aviation the sensor (often called a flux valve) is typically mounted in a wingtip or tail to reduce interference from the aircraft's own magnetic fields, and slaved gyro compass systems use it to keep a directional gyro aligned to magnetic north. Related fluxgate magnetometers are used in geophysics, space missions and archaeology for precise field measurement.

Limitations

Like all magnetic compasses, a fluxgate compass indicates magnetic rather than true north and is subject to deviation from nearby ferromagnetic material and electrical currents, requiring compensation or careful installation.
