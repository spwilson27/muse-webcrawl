The French protectorate in Morocco, also known as French Morocco, was the period of French colonial rule in Morocco between 1912 and 1956. It was established by the Treaty of Fes, signed on 30 March 1912, under which Sultan Abd al-Hafid accepted French protection, while a smaller Spanish protectorate was created in the north and south of the country.

History

French influence in Morocco grew during the 19th and early 20th centuries amid rivalry with Germany and Spain, producing the Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911. Following the Agadir Crisis, France and Germany reached an agreement that cleared the way for the protectorate. The first Resident-General, Hubert Lyautey, pursued a policy of ruling through the existing Moroccan monarchy and administration (the Makhzen), preserving traditional institutions while France controlled foreign affairs, the military and much of the economy.

French forces gradually extended control over the interior in campaigns often called the "pacification" of Morocco, which continued into the 1930s and met stiff resistance, most famously in the Rif War (1921–1926), fought primarily against the Spanish zone but involving France as well. The protectorate developed ports, railways, roads and modern cities such as Casablanca, and encouraged European settlement and agricultural colonisation.

End of the protectorate

After the Second World War, Moroccan nationalism grew, centred on Sultan Mohammed V and the Istiqlal Party. France exiled Mohammed V in 1953, a move that intensified resistance and violence. The sultan was allowed to return in 1955, and France recognised Moroccan independence on 2 March 1956; the Spanish protectorate ended shortly afterwards.
