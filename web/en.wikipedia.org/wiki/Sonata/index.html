Sonata

A sonata (from Italian sonare, "to sound") is a large-scale musical composition, typically for a solo instrument or a small ensemble, generally consisting of several movements. The term has been used since the Baroque period, when it simply denoted a piece played on instruments (as opposed to a cantata, which was sung). From the Classical era onward, "sonata" came to describe both a genre — the multi-movement work for piano alone or for piano with one other instrument — and a structural principle, sonata form, governing individual movements.

History

In the Baroque era, composers such as Arcangelo Corelli wrote trio sonatas for two violins and continuo, distinguishing the free sonata da camera (chamber sonata, a suite of dances) from the more serious sonata da chiesa (church sonata). Domenico Scarlatti composed over 500 single-movement keyboard sonatas in binary form.

In the Classical period, Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven established the sonata as the central instrumental genre, typically in three or four movements: a fast movement in sonata form, a slow movement, a minuet or scherzo, and a fast finale. Beethoven expanded the form's expressive range and structural ambition. Romantic composers — Schubert, Chopin, Schumann, Liszt and Brahms — stretched the classical framework, while in the 20th century composers from Prokofiev and Bartók to Boulez reinvented or deliberately subverted it.

Sonata form

Sonata form, or sonata-allegro form, organises a movement into an exposition (presenting thematic material in the tonic and a contrasting key), a development (fragmenting and transforming that material), and a recapitulation (restating the material in the tonic), often framed by an introduction and coda.
