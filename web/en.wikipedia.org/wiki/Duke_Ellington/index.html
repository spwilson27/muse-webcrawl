Duke Ellington

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Duke Ellington at the piano in the 1940s: the bandleader in a light suit, smiling over the keys, orchestra behind him]

Edward Kennedy 'Duke' Ellington (29 April 1899 – 24 May 1974) was an American jazz pianist, composer and bandleader, one of the supreme figures of twentieth-century music. For half a century he led his orchestra — less a band than a compositional instrument whose members (Johnny Hodges, Cootie Williams, Ben Webster, Paul Gonsalves) he wrote for as individuals — producing a body of work from three-minute dance records to sacred concerts.

His compositions include 'Mood Indigo' (1930), 'Sophisticated Lady' (1932), 'Caravan' (with Juan Tizol, 1936), 'Take the 'A' Train' (by his collaborator Billy Strayhorn, 1939), the extended suite Black, Brown and Beige (1943), and hundreds more standards. His 1956 Newport Jazz Festival performance, with Gonsalves's marathon tenor solo, relaunched his career onto the cover of Time.

The Cotton Club and beyond

Washington-born and fastidiously elegant — his mother called him Duke for his manners — Ellington made his name with a residency at Harlem's Cotton Club (1927–31), whose radio broadcasts carried his 'jungle sound' nationwide. He toured Europe in 1933 and 1939 to find himself treated as a great composer, a contrast with segregated America he never forgot.

Long career

Ellington wrote film scores (Anatomy of a Murder, 1959), ballets, a musical, and three Sacred Concerts (1965–73) in his last decade; his memoir Music Is My Mistress appeared in 1973. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1969 and a posthumous Pulitzer Prize special award in 1999. 'Beyond category' was his own favourite verdict on music he admired, and it is the one history has applied to him.
