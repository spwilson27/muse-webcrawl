Familial dysautonomia (FD), also called Riley–Day syndrome and hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy type III (HSAN-III), is a rare genetic disorder that affects the development and survival of neurons in the autonomic and sensory nervous systems. It occurs almost exclusively in people of Ashkenazi Jewish descent, with a carrier frequency of roughly 1 in 30 in that population.

Signs and symptoms

FD impairs involuntary functions such as digestion, breathing, tear production, blood pressure and temperature regulation, as well as sensory functions including pain and taste. Characteristic features include:

  • Absence of overflow tears when crying (alacrima)
  • Difficulty feeding and frequent vomiting in infancy
  • Unstable blood pressure and body temperature
  • "Dysautonomic crises" — episodes of vomiting, racing heart, high blood pressure and blotchy skin triggered by stress or illness
  • Reduced sensitivity to pain and temperature, absent fungiform papillae on the tongue, and absent deep tendon reflexes
  • Scoliosis, poor growth and gastro-oesophageal reflux

Cause

FD is caused by mutations in the ELP1 gene (formerly IKBKAP) on chromosome 9, which encodes a subunit of the elongator complex involved in transcription. In over 99% of cases the mutation is a single splicing defect (IVS20+6T→C) that causes tissue-specific skipping of an exon, most severely in neurons. It is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner.

Diagnosis and management

The disorder was first described in 1949 by Conrad Riley and Richard Day. Diagnosis rests on clinical criteria and genetic testing; carrier screening and prenatal testing are widely available in at-risk populations. There is no cure; treatment is supportive — managing crises, protecting the eyes from dryness, treating reflux and aspiration risk, and physiotherapy. Survival has improved greatly, with many patients now living into middle age; historically, half died before age 30.
