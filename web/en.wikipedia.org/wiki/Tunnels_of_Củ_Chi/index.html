The tunnels of Củ Chi are an immense network of connecting tunnels in the Củ Chi District of Ho Chi Minh City (Saigon), Vietnam, and are part of a much larger network of tunnels that underlie much of the country. The Củ Chi tunnels were the location of several military campaigns during the Vietnam War, and were the Viet Cong's base of operations for the Tết Offensive in 1968.

History

The tunnels were begun in the late 1940s by Viet Minh fighters resisting the French, and were expanded enormously during the 1960s. At their peak the network in Củ Chi stretched over 121 kilometres and contained living quarters, kitchens, hospitals, command centres, weapons caches, and booby-trapped entrances, often on several levels — the deepest around 8–10 metres below ground, proof against tank traffic and most bombs.

American countermeasures

The United States and South Vietnam mounted repeated operations to destroy the tunnels, including Operation Crimp (1966) and Operation Cedar Falls (1967), the latter aimed at the nearby "Iron Triangle". Volunteer soldiers known as "tunnel rats" entered the tunnels armed with pistols, knives, and flashlights; other methods included flooding, gas, demolition charges, and defoliant spraying. Despite these efforts, the tunnels were never eliminated and remained a major thorn for the US base at Củ Chi.

Present day

Two tunnel sites, Ben Dinh and Ben Duoc, are preserved as war memorial parks and are among Vietnam's most visited tourist attractions. Sections have been widened for visitors, and the sites include displays of traps, weapons, and a shooting range.
