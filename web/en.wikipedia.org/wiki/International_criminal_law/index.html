International criminal law

International criminal law (ICL) is a body of public international law designed to prohibit certain categories of conduct commonly viewed as serious atrocities and to make perpetrators of such conduct criminally accountable for their perpetration. The core crimes under international law are genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity, and the crime of aggression.

History

Although antecedents existed in earlier treaties and customs, modern international criminal law emerged after the Second World War with the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals, which prosecuted Axis leaders for crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity. The Nuremberg principles established that individuals, not only states, bear responsibility under international law, and that superior orders are not an absolute defence.

During the Cold War, development stalled, but the 1990s saw the creation of ad hoc tribunals for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY, 1993) and Rwanda (ICTR, 1994). These paved the way for the Rome Statute of 1998, which established the permanent International Criminal Court (ICC) in The Hague; the Court began operating in 2002.

Sources

The sources of international criminal law include treaties such as the Geneva Conventions of 1949 and the Rome Statute, customary international law, general principles of law, and the jurisprudence of international and hybrid tribunals. The principle of legality — nullum crimen sine lege — requires that conduct be criminalised before it is prosecuted.

Enforcement

Enforcement relies heavily on state cooperation, since international courts have no police force. Under the principle of complementarity, the ICC acts only when national courts are unwilling or unable genuinely to prosecute. Universal jurisdiction allows some states to prosecute core crimes regardless of where they occurred.
