Baltic amber


Baltic amber, including a piece with an insect inclusion.

Baltic amber is the fossilised resin found in the Baltic region of northern Europe, the largest known deposit of amber in the world. It is scientifically termed succinite, after its relatively high content of succinic acid (3–8%), which distinguishes it from other fossil resins. Baltic amber accounts for the great majority of the world's amber production and has been traded since prehistory along the so-called Amber Road to the Mediterranean.

Origin and age

The amber derives from the resin of coniferous forests that grew in the region during the Eocene epoch, roughly 44 million years ago (Lutetian); the exact botanical source is debated, with candidates including an extinct pine, Pinus succinifera, and relatives of the modern Japanese umbrella pine. The resin was carried by rivers into a shallow sea and deposited in glauconitic sediments known as "blue earth", centred on the Samland Peninsula near Kaliningrad, where over 90% of the world's extractable amber is found. Storms also wash pieces ashore along the Baltic coasts of Russia, Poland, Lithuania, Latvia, Germany, Denmark, and Sweden.

Properties and inclusions

Baltic amber ranges from nearly transparent yellow and honey tones to milky "bone" or butterscotch varieties coloured by microscopic air bubbles. It is prized scientifically for its inclusions: more than a thousand extinct species of insects, spiders, and other small organisms, as well as plant fragments, have been described from Baltic amber, providing an exceptionally detailed record of the Eocene forest ecosystem.

History and use

Amber beads appear in Mesolithic and Neolithic sites across northern Europe. In antiquity, Tacitus described its collection by the Aestii; it reached Mycenaean Greece and Rome, where it was used for jewellery and medicine. The Teutonic Knights controlled the Samland amber trade from the thirteenth century. The most famous amber artwork, the Amber Room of the Catherine Palace — panels of amber mosaic given by Prussia to Peter the Great in 1716 — was looted by Nazi forces during the Second World War and remains lost; a recreation was completed in 2003.

Today the Kaliningrad Amber Combine mines amber at Yantarny, and centres such as Gdańsk in Poland specialise in amber jewellery and craftsmanship.
