Enjambment

In poetry, enjambment (from the French enjambement, "striding over") is the continuation of a sentence or clause across a line break, so that the meaning runs on into the next line without a pause or terminal punctuation. It is the opposite of an end-stopped line, in which the end of the line coincides with the end of a phrase. Enjambment creates tension between the poet's metre and the natural movement of the syntax, and is a hallmark of much blank verse and free verse.

Examples

John Milton's Paradise Lost is famous for its long enjambed periods, in which sentences stride across many lines of blank verse:

Of Man's first disobedience, and the fruit
Of that forbidden tree whose mortal taste
Brought death into the world, and all our woe...

Shakespeare's later plays increasingly enjamb their verse, giving dialogue a more natural, speech-like flow. In modern poetry, e. e. cummings played with extreme enjambment, splitting even individual words across lines. A celebrated instance is the opening of The Waste Land by T. S. Eliot: "April is the cruellest month, breeding / Lilacs out of the dead land..."

Effects and analysis

Enjambment speeds the reader forward, since the sense is incomplete at the line's end, and it can produce a slight surprise or emphasis when the continuation resolves the phrase in an unexpected way. Critics distinguish degrees of enjambment: a break between clauses is mild, while a break that separates a verb from its object, or splits a tightly bound phrase such as adjective and noun, creates stronger tension. The related device of the caesura — a pause within a line — works in the opposite direction, breaking the line where the verse continues.
