United States Numbered Highway System

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United States Numbered Highway System — often called the U.S. Routes or U.S. Highways — is an integrated network of roads and highways numbered within a nationwide grid in the contiguous United States. It was established in 1926 and is coordinated by the American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials (AASHTO), while the roads themselves are built and maintained by state and local governments.

Numbering grid

In general, even-numbered routes run east–west and odd-numbered routes run north–south. For U.S. Routes, the grid is inverted relative to the later Interstate Highway System: numbers increase from north to south for east–west routes (e.g., US 2 in the north, US 90 in the south) and from east to west for north–south routes (e.g., US 1 on the East Coast, US 101 on the West Coast). Three-digit routes are auxiliary routes of their two-digit "parent".

History

The system replaced the informal named auto trails (such as the Lincoln Highway) with a standardised numbering and the distinctive black-and-white shield. Famous routes include US 66 ("Route 66"), largely decommissioned in 1985, and US 20, the longest U.S. Route. Since the 1950s the Interstates have taken over much long-distance traffic, but U.S. Routes remain vital regional corridors.
