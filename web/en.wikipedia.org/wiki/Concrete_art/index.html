Concrete art

Concrete art is an art movement characterised by purely abstract works that are entirely free of reference to the visible world or symbolic meaning. The term was introduced by the Dutch artist Theo van Doesburg in his "Manifesto of Concrete Art", published in 1930 in the single issue of the journal Art Concret. Van Doesburg argued that a work of art should be completely conceived and formed by the mind before its execution, using only plastic elements — lines, planes, and colours — signifying nothing but themselves.

Development

After van Doesburg's death in 1931, the Swiss artist Max Bill became the movement's leading theorist and practitioner, organising exhibitions and promoting a mathematical-geometric approach. Concrete art influenced the Zurich Concrete artists (Bill, Richard Paul Lohse, Camille Graeser, Verena Loewensberg) and spread internationally.

International reach

The movement took distinctive regional forms: in Argentina the Arte Concreto-Invención group and Madí movement of the 1940s; in Brazil the Grupo Ruptura in São Paulo, which led towards Neo-Concretism; and in post-war Europe groups associated with Galerie Denise René. Concrete art helped lay the groundwork for later movements such as Op art, kinetic art, and minimalism.
