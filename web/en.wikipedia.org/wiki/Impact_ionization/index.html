Impact ionization

Impact ionization is the process in a material by which one energetic charge carrier can, upon collision with the material, create other charge carriers. For example, in semiconductors, an electron (or hole) with enough kinetic energy can knock a bound electron out of its bound state (in the valence band) into a free state (conduction band), creating an additional electron–hole pair. The initiating carrier loses part of its energy in the process and both carriers may be accelerated again by an applied electric field, potentially repeating the process.

Avalanche effect

When impact ionization occurs repeatedly in a high electric field, it produces an exponentially growing cascade of carriers known as avalanche multiplication. This phenomenon is exploited in avalanche photodiodes and Geiger-mode detectors, which amplify tiny photocurrents into measurable signals, and in avalanche transistors. Conversely, uncontrolled avalanche breakdown damages ordinary semiconductor devices, so device designers must keep fields and carrier energies below the ionization threshold in most regions of a device. The energy required for an ionizing collision must exceed the band gap of the material, and in practice carriers must typically accumulate roughly 1.5 times the band-gap energy because of momentum conservation.

In gases

The same concept applies in gases: a free electron accelerated by an electric field can ionize a neutral atom or molecule on impact, freeing another electron. This is the basis of the Townsend discharge, gas-filled detectors such as the Geiger–Müller tube, and ultimately of electrical breakdown and lightning.

Applications

  • Avalanche photodiodes for optical communications and single-photon detection
  • Impact ionization MOSFETs (I-MOS) proposed for low-voltage switching with subthreshold slopes steeper than conventional transistors
  • Gas discharge lamps and plasma generation
