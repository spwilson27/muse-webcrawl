Cinema of the Soviet Union

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The Odessa steps sequence of Battleship Potemkin, a baby carriage bouncing down the stairs]
The Odessa steps sequence of Battleship Potemkin, a baby carriage bouncing down the stairs

The cinema of the Soviet Union — film produced in the Soviet Union (1922–1991) and its Russian predecessor — gave world cinema the theory and practice of montage through Sergei Eisenstein, Dziga Vertov and Lev Kuleshov, and later the poetic cinema of Andrei Tarkovsky. State-controlled through Goskino and its successors, it was simultaneously a propaganda instrument and, repeatedly, a site of formal genius and quiet dissidence.

Montage and the 1920s

Lenin's dictum that cinema was 'the most important of the arts' brought state patronage and avant-garde freedom in the 1920s: Kuleshov's editing experiments, Vertov's Man with a Movie Camera (1929) and Eisenstein's Battleship Potemkin (1925) and October (1928) invented film grammar studied to this day. Vsevolod Pudovkin and Alexander Dovzhenko (Earth, 1930) completed the montage school before Socialist realism clamped down in the 1930s.

Stalinism to thaw

Under Stalin, production shrank to safe classics and musicals (Grigori Aleksandrov), Eisenstein's Ivan the Terrible Part II was banned, and the industry served the cult — yet wartime and post-war masters emerged. The Khrushchev thaw freed a generation: Mikhail Kalatozov's The Cranes Are Flying (Palme d'Or 1958), Grigori Chukhrai, Elem Klimov's generation, the comedies of Leonid Gaidai (the USSR's most popular films), and Tarkovsky's Andrei Rublev (1966) and Stalker (1979) — shelved or cut at home, canonised abroad.

End and legacy

Glasnost uncorked shelved films (Repentance, 1987, became a sensation) before the industry collapsed with the Union in 1991 into the chaos of post-Soviet cinema. The Soviet archive — from Potemkin to Tarkovsky — remains foundational to film education worldwide.
