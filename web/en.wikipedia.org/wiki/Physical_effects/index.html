Physical effects

A miniature model photographed for a physical effect shot.

Physical effects, also known as practical effects, are special effects produced physically, without computer-generated imagery or other artificial post-production techniques. In filmmaking, physical effects include mechanical effects — such as explosions, weather effects, breakaway props, miniatures and animatronics — and physical prosthetic makeup effects applied directly to performers.

Types of physical effects

  • Pyrotechnics and explosions — controlled detonations, fire and smoke effects.
  • Weather effects — rain towers, wind machines, snow and fog.
  • Miniatures and models — scale models of vehicles, buildings and landscapes photographed as if full-size.
  • Animatronics and puppetry — mechanically operated creatures and characters.
  • Breakaways and rigging — furniture and sets designed to smash safely, wire work for stunts.
  • Prosthetic makeup — appliances, masks and wounds applied to actors.

History

Physical effects are as old as cinema itself: pioneers such as Georges Méliès used substitution splices, miniatures and pyrotechnics in the earliest years of film. For most of the 20th century they were the only way to realise spectacular events on screen. Although computer-generated imagery (CGI) displaced many physical techniques from the 1990s onward, physical effects remain widely used, often in combination with digital work; many directors argue that tangible effects give actors something real to react to and age more gracefully on screen. Modern blockbuster productions frequently blend miniatures, full-scale set pieces and prosthetics with CGI enhancement.
