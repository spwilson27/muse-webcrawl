Suburbanization

Suburbanization (American English), or suburbanisation (British English), is a population shift from central urban areas into suburbs, resulting in the formation of (sub)urban sprawl. It is a demographic and spatial process in which residents and businesses move outward from a city core to lower-density residential communities at the metropolitan periphery, often while remaining economically tied to the city through commuting.

History

Early suburbs grew along streetcar and railway lines in the 19th century, allowing affluent households to live outside crowded industrial cities. Mass suburbanization followed the Second World War, above all in the United States, where automobile ownership, the Interstate Highway System, federally backed mortgages, and mass-produced housing developments such as Levittown made suburban homeownership available to a broad middle class. Similar, if less extreme, decentralization occurred in Canada, Australia, and parts of Europe and later in rapidly urbanizing countries worldwide.

Causes

Drivers commonly cited include cheaper land and larger homes at the periphery, preferences for detached houses and gardens, avoidance of congestion, pollution, and crime associated with central cities, school quality, zoning rules that separate land uses and mandate low densities, and transportation investments focused on cars. In the United States, suburbanization was also shaped by racial segregation, discriminatory lending ("redlining"), and so-called "white flight," which concentrated poverty in many inner cities.

Effects and criticism

Suburbanization produces urban sprawl, with consequences debated by planners: increased car dependence and traffic, loss of farmland and habitat, higher infrastructure costs per resident, and social segregation, balanced against homeownership, space, and perceived quality of life. Counter-movements include New Urbanism, transit-oriented development, and the reurbanization or gentrification of some central districts since the late 20th century.
