Truss

Truss
Type:Structural framework
Components:Chords, web members, joints (nodes)
Used in:Bridges, roofs, towers, cranes

A truss is an assembly of members, such as beams or bars, connected at joints (nodes) to form a rigid structure, typically arranged in interconnected triangles. Within a truss, members are assumed to carry load primarily in tension or compression along their axes, so a truss converts bending loads on the whole structure into axial loads in its members, using material efficiently over long spans.

Design

A planar truss lies in a single plane and is commonly used for bridges and roofs; a space truss has members in three dimensions, as in transmission towers and large stadium roofs. The upper and lower longitudinal members are called chords, and the diagonal and vertical members between them form the web. Common configurations include the Pratt truss (diagonals in tension under vertical load), the Howe truss (diagonals in compression), and the Warren truss (equilateral triangles). The truss bridge made of a series of such panels was a mainstay of 19th-century railway engineering.

Analysis

Classical analysis treats the joints as pins and applies equilibrium at each joint (the method of joints) or to sections of the truss (the method of sections). Modern practice uses matrix structural analysis and finite-element methods. Gustav Eiffel's tower in Paris and roof trusses in timber and steel trusses in industrial buildings are prominent examples of the form.

Other uses

The term also refers to a medical device: a hernia truss is a supportive belt used to hold a reducible hernia in place, and in horticulture a "truss" is a cluster of flowers or fruit, as on a tomato plant.
