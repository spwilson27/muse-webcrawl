USS Lexington (CV-2), nicknamed "Lady Lex", was the name ship of her class of two aircraft carriers built for the United States Navy during the 1920s. Originally designed as a battlecruiser, she was converted into one of the Navy's first aircraft carriers during construction to comply with the terms of the Washington Naval Treaty of 1922. The ship entered service in 1928 and was assigned to the Pacific Fleet for her entire career.

Interwar service

Together with her sister Saratoga (CV-3), Lexington was used to develop and refine carrier tactics in a series of annual exercises before World War II. In one celebrated episode in December 1929, her turbo-electric propulsion plant supplied emergency electric power to the city of Tacoma, Washington, for about a month after drought disabled the city's hydroelectric supply.

World War II

After the attack on Pearl Harbor, Lexington conducted raids in the South Pacific, including the Lae–Salamaua raid of March 1942. In May 1942, with Yorktown, she fought the Battle of the Coral Sea — the first naval battle in history fought entirely by aircraft, in which opposing ships never sighted each other. Her aircraft sank the Japanese light carrier Shōhō and damaged the fleet carrier Shōkaku, but Lexington was struck by torpedoes and bombs. Hours later, leaking aviation gasoline ignited in a massive internal explosion, and the ship was abandoned and scuttled by torpedoes from the destroyer Phelps. Her loss nonetheless helped blunt the Japanese advance toward Port Moresby and Australia.

The wreck of Lexington was located in March 2018 by the research vessel Petrel at a depth of nearly 3,000 metres in the Coral Sea.
