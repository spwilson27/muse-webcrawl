Maltese (Maltese: Malti) is a Semitic language spoken by the Maltese people. It is the national language of Malta and the only Semitic official language of the European Union. Maltese is a Latinised variety of spoken historical Arabic, descended from Siculo-Arabic, the Arabic dialect spoken in Sicily and Malta between the end of the 9th century and the end of the 12th century. Over the following centuries it absorbed large amounts of vocabulary from Sicilian, Italian, and later English, and it is the only Semitic language written in the Latin alphabet in its standard form.

History

After the Norman conquest of Malta in 1091 severed the islands from the Arabic-speaking world, the local Arabic dialect evolved independently, increasingly influenced by the Romance languages of successive rulers. The oldest known text in Maltese is the Cantilena, a poem attributed to Pietru Caxaro (late 15th century). Maltese became an official language of Malta in 1934, replacing Italian alongside English, and was recognised as an official EU language on Malta's accession in 2004.

Vocabulary and structure

Estimates vary, but roughly a third of Maltese vocabulary — including most core words and grammatical structures — derives from Arabic, about half from Sicilian and Italian, and a growing share (around 6–20% by various counts) from English. Semitic morphology, including root-and-pattern derivation and broken plurals, survives alongside Romance-style sound plurals. The alphabet has 30 letters, including ċ, ġ, għ, ħ, and ż.

Phonology

Maltese retains several distinctive Semitic consonants, though the historical Arabic emphatics have been lost and the letter għ has largely fallen silent or acts as a vowel lengthener in many dialects. It has five short and five long vowels.
