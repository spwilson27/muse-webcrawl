Boogie-woogie

Stylistic originsBlues, ragtime, barrelhouse piano

Boogie-woogie is a genre of American music that became popular during the late 1920s, developed in African-American communities from the 1870s. It was extended from piano to piano duo and trio, guitar, big band, country and western, and gospel, and it influenced rhythm and blues and rock and roll. The music is characterized by a strong, repetitive ostinato bass figure in the left hand — played in eighth notes, often called the "eight to the bar" — over which the right hand plays syncopated melodies and riffs.

History

Boogie-woogie originated among pianists in the lumber and turpentine camps and barrelhouses of Texas and the Deep South, evolving from fast blues piano styles. Clarence "Pinetop" Smith's 1928 recording "Pinetop's Boogie Woogie" gave the style its name on record. The genre became a national craze after producer John Hammond's 1938 From Spirituals to Swing concert at Carnegie Hall featured Meade Lux Lewis, Albert Ammons, and Pete Johnson. During the Second World War, boogie-woogie bands scored hits such as the Andrews Sisters' "Boogie Woogie Bugle Boy" (1941), and the style permeated jump blues and country music.

Musical characteristics

Boogie-woogie is typically built on the twelve-bar blues progression. The left hand plays a driving repeated bass pattern — walking figures, broken octaves, or "rolling" patterns — at a fast tempo, while the right hand improvises melodic figures, trills, and tremolos. Classic bass patterns include the "Chicago" and "dipping" figures used by Jimmy Yancey, often called the father of boogie-woogie.

Legacy

Boogie-woogie directly influenced rhythm and blues, rockabilly, and rock and roll pianists such as Little Richard, Jerry Lee Lewis, and Fats Domino. The term also names a competitive swing dance (the European counterpart of East Coast swing) danced to boogie-woogie and rock and roll music.
