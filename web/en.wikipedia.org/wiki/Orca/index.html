The orca (Orcinus orca), or killer whale, is a toothed whale that is the largest member of the oceanic dolphin family. It is the only extant species in the genus Orcinus. Orcas are recognizable by their black-and-white patterned body. A cosmopolitan species, they are found in diverse marine environments, from Arctic to Antarctic regions to tropical seas.

Orcas are apex predators with a diverse diet; individual populations often specialize in particular types of prey. They feed on fish, sharks, rays, marine mammals such as seals and other dolphins and whales, and seabirds. They are highly social animals; some populations are composed of highly stable matrilineal family groups (pods). Their sophisticated hunting techniques and vocal behaviours, which are often specific to a particular group and passed across generations, are considered examples of animal culture.

Taxonomy and ecotypes

Although the IUCN currently lists a single species, researchers recognize several distinct forms or "ecotypes" that differ in appearance, prey specialisation, social structure and genetics. In the North Pacific, fish-eating "residents", mammal-eating "transients" (Bigg's killer whales), and "offshores" are distinguished; in the Antarctic, types A–D have been described. In 2024, a study proposed elevating the resident and Bigg's forms to full species (Orcinus ater and Orcinus rectipinnus), though the taxonomy remains debated.

Behaviour and intelligence

Orcas live in stable pods led by older females; offspring of both sexes may remain with their mothers for life. Females can live 80–90 years in the wild and undergo one of the longest menopauses known in any animal, continuing to lead and provision their adult sons after reproduction ceases. Pods have distinctive dialects of clicks, whistles and pulsed calls used in echolocation and communication. Hunting strategies include wave-washing seals off ice floes, beaching to snatch sea lions, and carousel feeding on herring.

Conservation

The species is listed as Data Deficient because the taxonomy is unresolved and populations vary greatly in status. Threats include depletion of prey (notably Chinook salmon for the endangered Southern Resident population of the Pacific Northwest, reduced by the US to 74 individuals in recent years), chemical contamination by PCBs, noise pollution, and capture for marine mammal parks, a practice now banned or restricted in many countries.
