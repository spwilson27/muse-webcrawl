Ionic bond

An ionic bond is a chemical bond formed when one atom transfers electrons to another, producing oppositely charged ions that attract each other electrostatically. It typically forms between a metal and a nonmetal — as when sodium reacts with chlorine to give sodium chloride.

Ionic compounds do not form discrete molecules but giant crystal lattices, which explains their high melting points, brittleness, and ability to conduct electricity when molten or dissolved (when the ions can move) but not as solids.

[Image: A sodium chloride crystal lattice model of alternating purple and green spheres]

Formation

A sodium atom loses its outer electron (becoming Na⁺) and a chlorine atom gains one (becoming Cl⁻); the energy balance of ion formation plus lattice energy decides whether the compound forms. Electron transfer is really the extreme end of the polar-bond continuum rather than a wholly separate phenomenon.

Properties in solution

Dissolved ionic compounds dissociate into free ions, making solutions electrolytes. The ions are surrounded (hydrated) by water molecules, which is why salts dissolve and why their solutions conduct.
