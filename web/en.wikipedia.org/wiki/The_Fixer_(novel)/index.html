The Fixer (novel)

The Fixer is a 1966 novel by American writer Bernard Malamud. Inspired by the real-life blood libel trial of Menahem Mendel Beilis in Tsarist Kyiv, it tells the story of Yakov Bok, a Jewish handyman imprisoned without trial on a fabricated charge of ritual murder. The novel won both the National Book Award and the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction.

Plot

Yakov Bok, a luckless Jewish "fixer" (handyman) from a shtetl, moves to Kyiv seeking a better life, concealing his Jewishness in a city closed to Jews without permits. Rescuing a drunken antisemitic official earns him work managing a brick factory. When a Russian boy is found murdered near the factory, Bok is accused of ritual murder, arrested, and held in brutal conditions for more than two years without trial. Though offered freedom in exchange for a confession and despite the authorities' forged evidence, he refuses to confess to a crime he did not commit, insisting on a public trial. The novel ends as he is taken to court amid turmoil: "there is no such thing as an apolitical man", he reflects, "especially a Jew".

Background and reception

Malamud drew on Maurice Samuel's account of the Beilis affair, Blood Accusation (1966), and on Beilis's own memoir, though the novel is a work of imaginative fiction rather than a retelling. Critics praised its bleak depiction of injustice and Bok's transformation from a passive sufferer into a man of moral resistance.

Film adaptation

The novel was adapted into the 1968 film The Fixer, directed by John Frankenheimer and starring Alan Bates as Bok, for which Bates received an Academy Award nomination.
