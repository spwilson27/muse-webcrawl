The Chicago "L" (short for "elevated") is the rapid transit system serving the city of Chicago and some of its surrounding suburbs in the U.S. state of Illinois. It is the third-busiest rapid transit system in the United States, after the New York City Subway and the Washington Metro. Operated by the Chicago Transit Authority (CTA), it is officially known as the 'L', with the "L" standing for elevated.

Overview

The system includes 146 stations on eight lines with 102.8 miles (165.4 km) of track, of which roughly half is elevated, with the remainder at grade or in subways. In a typical year the "L" carries more than 200 million passenger trips. The two downtown loops — the Union Loop in the subway and the elevated Loop that gives the district its name — are among the most recognisable transit landmarks in America.

History

The "L" began operation on 6 June 1892, when the Chicago and South Side Rapid Transit Railroad ran steam-powered trains on elevated tracks. Competing elevated companies consolidated over the following decades, and electrification arrived in the early twentieth century. The State Street subway opened in 1943 and the Dearborn subway in 1951. The CTA was created in 1947 to unify operations. Extensions in the 2000s and 2010s, including the Orange Line to Midway and the Pink Line, expanded service, and the system continues to be extended and modernised.

Rolling stock

Trains draw 600-volt direct current from a third rail. The fleet includes the 5000-series cars and the newer 7000-series cars, whose first units entered service in 2024. Fares are collected at turnstiles; transfers to CTA buses are integrated through the Ventra fare system.
