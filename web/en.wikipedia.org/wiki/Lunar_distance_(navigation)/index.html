Lunar distance (navigation)

[Image: Measuring the lunar distance with a sextant — the angle between the Moon and a star]

Overview

In celestial navigation, lunar distance (also called a lunar) is the angular distance between the Moon and another celestial body — the Sun or a bright star near the ecliptic. Navigators measured it with a sextant and, using tables predicting the angle for Greenwich time (in Maskelyne's Nautical Almanac, from 1767), computed Greenwich time and hence their longitude — the principal rival to the marine chronometer in the 18th and early 19th centuries.

Method and decline

The method demands precise measurement and laborious "clearing" of the distance for parallax and refraction (with published shortcut tables by Borda, Thompson and others). Lunars were accurate to perhaps 0.3–0.5° of longitude in skilled hands and cost nothing like a chronometer, so they served as the chronometer's check well into the 1800s. As chronometers cheapened and almanacs dropped lunar tables (the British Nautical Almanac last included them in 1906), the art faded — revived today mainly by celestial-navigation enthusiasts.
