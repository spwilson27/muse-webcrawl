Incremental reading

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Incremental reading interleaves reading with spaced-repetition review.

Incremental reading is a method for learning and retaining information from electronic texts, integrating reading with a spaced repetition system. It was developed as part of the SuperMemo software by Piotr Woźniak and introduced around 2000. Instead of reading a text once through, the reader processes many texts in small portions, extracting the most valuable fragments and converting them into flashcards that are then reviewed on an expanding schedule.

Process

The learner imports articles — from the web, e-books, or documents — into a queue sorted by priority. During a session, each article is shown briefly; the reader skims or reads a portion, highlights key passages, and presses a key to extract them as separate items. Extracts are themselves scheduled for later revisits, and over successive passes are shortened and finally converted into question–answer or cloze-deletion cards. Reading is thus "incremental": texts are consumed in small pieces over days or months, and nothing depends on finishing a source in one sitting.

Rationale

The method rests on the spacing effect, active recall, and the idea that reading and memorisation should not be separate activities. Priorities let the learner manage far more reading material than could be processed linearly, discarding low-value items as they sink in the queue.

Criticism

Users note a steep learning curve, the proprietary nature of SuperMemo, and the risk of accumulating unprocessed extracts. Related open-source implementations and plug-ins (for example in Anki) reproduce parts of the workflow.
