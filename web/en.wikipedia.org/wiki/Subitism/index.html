Subitism

Subitism, also known as sudden enlightenment, is a doctrine found especially in Chan and Zen Buddhism holding that awakening (bodhi) is attained suddenly and all at once, rather than through a long, gradual process of cultivation. It stands in contrast to gradualism, the view that enlightenment is approached step by step over many lifetimes or years of practice.

Origins

The sudden/gradual distinction became prominent in Chinese Buddhism. The Indian tradition already contained both tendencies, but in China the debate sharpened around the interpretation of texts such as the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra and the teachings attributed to Bodhidharma. The Tiantai and Huayan schools developed schemes reconciling sudden and gradual aspects, often speaking of sudden enlightenment followed by gradual cultivation.

The Southern school

Subitism is most famously associated with the "Southern school" of Chan and its legendary sixth patriarch, Huineng, whose Platform Sūtra contrasts his sudden teaching with the supposedly gradual teaching of his rival Shenxiu of the "Northern school." The celebrated verse contest in the Platform Sūtra — Shenxiu's poem comparing the mind to a mirror that must be kept polished, against Huineng's rejoinder that there is no mirror and nowhere for dust to settle — dramatizes the subitist position that awakening is not produced by accumulating gradual effort, because Buddha-nature is already present and complete.

Later developments and criticism

Later teachers such as Guifeng Zongmi criticized a crude subitism that rejected practice altogether, advocating instead "sudden enlightenment followed by gradual cultivation": insight dawns suddenly, but ingrained habits must still be worn away afterward. Sudden-awakening rhetoric became central to the Linji (Rinzai) school and its use of kōans, while the Caodong (Sōtō) school is often — somewhat misleadingly — associated with gradualism. Modern scholarship notes that the sudden/gradual polarity is partly a rhetorical and polemical construction of the Chan tradition itself.
