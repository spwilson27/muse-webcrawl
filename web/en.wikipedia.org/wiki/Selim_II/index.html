Selim II (Ottoman Turkish: سليم ثانى; 28 May 1524 – 15 December 1574), also known as Selim the Blond and, less flatteringly, Selim the Drunkard, was the eleventh sultan of the Ottoman Empire, reigning from 1566 until his death in 1574. He was a son of Suleiman the Magnificent and his wife Hürrem Sultan.

Path to the throne

Selim's succession was shaped by the fierce rivalry among Suleiman's sons. His elder half-brother Mustafa was executed in 1553 and his full brother Bayezid rebelled and was executed in 1561 after fleeing to Persia, leaving Selim the sole surviving heir. Unlike his campaigning father, Selim never led armies in person; he was the first sultan to remain in the capital throughout his reign, delegating military and administrative affairs to his grand vizier.

Reign

The dominant figure of the reign was the Grand Vizier Sokollu Mehmed Pasha, a Bosnian devshirme recruit who had also served Suleiman and would serve Murad III, and who effectively governed the empire with Selim's consort Nurbanu Sultan. Under Sokollu, the Ottomans concluded a favourable peace with the Habsburgs (1568), conquered Yemen, and captured Cyprus from Venice in 1570–1571 — the conquest that provoked the formation of the Holy League. At the Battle of Lepanto (7 October 1571), the League's fleet destroyed the Ottoman navy, though the Ottomans rebuilt their fleet within a year and retained Cyprus, while also taking Tunis in 1574. Sokollu's ambitious projects — a Don–Volga canal (attempted in 1569 against Russia at Astrakhan, which failed) and a planned Suez canal — testify to the empire's still-expansive strategic horizon.

Assessment and death

Selim commissioned the Selimiye Mosque in Edirne, the masterpiece of the architect Sinan, completed shortly after his death and regarded as the crowning achievement of Ottoman classical architecture. He died in December 1574, reportedly after a fall in the palace baths, and was succeeded by his son Murad III. Traditional historiography treated Selim as the first of the "decline" sultans — indolent and fond of wine — but modern historians note that the empire remained at its territorial and institutional height under the competent administration of Sokollu.
