Stapes

The stapes (from Latin stapes, "stirrup") is the smallest and lightest bone in the human body, and the innermost of the three ossicles in the middle ear. It is shaped like a stirrup, about 3 mm in size, and transmits sound vibrations from the incus to the oval window of the cochlea.

Anatomy

The stapes consists of a head, which articulates with the incus; two limbs (anterior and posterior crura); and a flat base, the footplate, which sits in the oval window (fenestra vestibuli) and is sealed there by the annular ligament. The stapedius muscle, the smallest skeletal muscle in the body, attaches to the neck of the stapes and dampens its movement in response to loud sounds as part of the acoustic reflex.

Function

Sound waves vibrate the tympanic membrane, moving the chain of ossicles — malleus, incus, then stapes. The footplate of the stapes pushes in and out of the oval window, creating pressure waves in the fluid of the inner ear. Because the footplate is much smaller than the eardrum, the ossicular chain increases pressure substantially, efficiently coupling airborne sound to the fluid-filled cochlea.

Clinical relevance

In otosclerosis, abnormal bone growth fixes the footplate in the oval window, producing progressive conductive hearing loss; it is commonly treated by stapedectomy or stapedotomy, replacing the fixed stapes with a small prosthesis. The stapes develops from the second pharyngeal arch and was first described in the 16th century, credited to Giovanni Filippo Ingrassia.
