Supermoon

A supermoon appears slightly larger and brighter than an average full moon.

A supermoon is a full moon or a new moon that nearly coincides with perigee — the closest that the Moon comes to the Earth in its elliptic orbit — resulting in a slightly larger-than-usual apparent size of the lunar disk as viewed from Earth. The technical name is a perigee syzygy.

Appearance

A full moon at perigee appears roughly 14 percent larger in diameter and up to 30 percent brighter than a full moon at apogee (sometimes informally called a "micromoon"). The difference is subtle to the untrained eye, and the Moon illusion — the apparent enlargement of the Moon near the horizon — often dominates popular impressions of the phenomenon.

Origin of the term

The term "supermoon" was coined in 1979 by astrologer Richard Nolle, who defined it as a new or full moon occurring within 90 percent of the Moon's closest approach to Earth in a given orbit. The term entered mainstream media coverage in March 2011, when a full moon coincided closely with perigee shortly after the Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami, prompting unfounded speculation about a causal connection.

Effects

Perigean full moons produce slightly larger tides, known as perigean spring tides, which can add a few centimetres to tidal ranges and occasionally contribute to minor coastal flooding. There is no scientific evidence linking supermoons to natural disasters.
