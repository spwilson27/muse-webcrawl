Source textsBook of Exodus, Numbers; Qur'an

Manna (Hebrew: מָן, mān; Arabic: المنّ, al-mann) is, according to the Hebrew Bible and the Qur'an, a miraculous food provided by God to the Israelites during their journey through the desert after the Exodus from Egypt. The Book of Exodus describes it appearing each morning with the dew as fine, flake-like frost upon the ground, white like coriander seed and tasting like wafers made with honey; the Book of Numbers adds that it arrived at night, could be ground, baked into cakes, and tasted like fresh oil.

Biblical account

Exodus 16 relates that the Israelites were to gather an omer per person daily; manna gathered in excess bred worms and stank, except on the sixth day, when a double portion kept fresh for the Sabbath, on which no manna fell. The supply continued for forty years, ceasing when Israel entered Canaan and ate its produce (Joshua 5:12). A golden pot of manna was preserved in the Ark of the Covenant as a memorial. In the New Testament, Jesus refers to himself as the "true bread from heaven" in John 6, contrasting his teaching with the manna eaten in the wilderness.

Proposed natural explanations

Scholars have proposed natural candidates: the sweet honeydew excretions of scale insects feeding on tamarisk trees in the Sinai, which dry into white flakes and are still collected by Bedouin as "manna"; wind-borne lichen (Lecanora esculenta) reported falling in "rains" in southwest Asia; and sap secretions of other desert plants. The word has passed into general usage for any unexpected, welcome provision — "manna from heaven."

In Islam

The Qur'an mentions al-mann among the blessings given to the Children of Israel (e.g. 2:57, 7:160, 20:80), alongside quails (al-salwā), which Exodus also says accompanied the manna.
