Church cantata

A Baroque church cantata performance in a German church.

A church cantata or sacred cantata is a cantata intended to be performed during a liturgical service. The genre developed from the earlier sacred vocal concerto and became a central form of music in the Lutheran Church of Baroque Germany, where a cantata — often based on the prescribed readings for the Sunday or feast day — was sung as part of the service, typically in the morning communion service.

The church cantata combined biblical texts, free poetry, and stanzas of chorales (Lutheran hymns) and set them in several movements: typically choruses, accompanied recitatives, arias, duets, and a concluding chorale. Composers such as Dieterich Buxtehude, Georg Philipp Telemann, and above all Johann Sebastian Bach made the church cantata a vehicle for both musical invention and theological reflection.

History

The term cantata originated in seventeenth-century Italy for a secular solo vocal work, but in Lutheran Germany the sacred cantata took on a distinct character. Early examples by Johann Pachelbel, Johann Kuhnau, and members of the Bach family drew on the older geistliches Konzert (sacred concerto). Erdmann Neumeister, pastor at Hamburg, published cycles of cantata texts beginning in 1700 and introduced the alternation of recitative and da capo aria borrowed from opera, complaining that the new form sounded "like a piece from an opera."

Neumeister's librettos shaped the mature Baroque cantata. Bach composed complete cycles of cantatas for every Sunday and feast day of the church year during his tenure as Thomaskantor in Leipzig (1723–1750); some three hundred are attributed to him, of which about two hundred survive. After 1750 the genre declined as liturgical practice changed and the galant style favoured simpler music, though church cantatas continued to be written into the Classical era and were revived in the nineteenth century following the Bach revival associated with Felix Mendelssohn.

Structure

A typical Baroque church cantata contains:

  • An opening chorus, often on a biblical dictum
  • Recitatives (both secco and accompanied) and arias for solo voices
  • A chorale movement, usually four-part, closing the work
  • Occasionally an instrumental sinfonia or ritornello

Scoring ranged from small ensembles with continuo to full orchestras with trumpets and timpani for festive occasions. The cantata was performed after the Gospel reading and before the sermon, which it was understood to interpret musically; in Leipzig, longer cantatas were sung in two parts, before and after the sermon.
