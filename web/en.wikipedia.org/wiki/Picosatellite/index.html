A picosatellite is a small artificial satellite with a wet mass between 0.1 and 1 kilogram. Such satellites are typically built using commercial off-the-shelf electronics, launched as secondary payloads, and used for low-cost technology demonstration, education and simple communications or Earth observation missions in low Earth orbit.

Classification

Small satellites are classified by mass: minisatellites (100–500 kg), microsatellites (10–100 kg), nanosatellites (1–10 kg), picosatellites (0.1–1 kg) and femtosatellites (below 0.1 kg). The boundaries are conventions rather than strict engineering definitions, and the picosatellite class overlaps with the smallest CubeSats.

CubeSats

The CubeSat standard, proposed in 1999 by Jordi Puig-Suari (Cal Poly) and Bob Twiggs (Stanford), defined a 10 cm cube "1U" unit of about 1 kg, bringing picosatellite-class craft within reach of universities and small companies. Standardised deployers (P-POD and successors) made launches routine, and thousands of CubeSats have flown since 2003, including interplanetary missions such as MarCO.

Applications

Picosatellites are used for student training, amateur radio relay, technology tests of miniaturised components, and scientific measurements such as ionospheric sounding. Their low cost comes with short design lives, limited power and pointing capability, and an increasing contribution to orbital debris concerns, mitigated by low orbits that decay within a few years.
