Plants are the eukaryotes that form the kingdom Plantae. They are predominantly photosynthetic: they obtain energy from sunlight using the green pigment chlorophyll, stored in chloroplasts, and release oxygen as a by-product. Most plants are multicellular, rooted in place, and have cell walls made of cellulose. The plant kingdom includes trees, grasses, flowers, ferns, mosses and green algae by some definitions. Plants are essential to almost all life on Earth: they produce most of the world's oxygen and form the base of most food chains.

Diversity and evolution

Plants evolved from aquatic algal ancestors and colonised land roughly 470 million years ago, transforming Earth's atmosphere and soils. Living groups range from tiny duckweeds — the smallest flowering plants — to giant sequoias among the largest organisms on Earth. Flowering plants (angiosperms), which arose in the Cretaceous, now dominate most terrestrial ecosystems, with roughly 300,000 known species; conifers and other gymnosperms, ferns and mosses make up most of the remainder.

Biology

Photosynthesis converts carbon dioxide and water into sugars using light energy: 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2. Roots absorb water and minerals, stems transport them through xylem and phloem vascular tissues, and leaves exchange gases through stomata. Plants reproduce sexually through flowers and seeds, or asexually by runners, bulbs and cuttings. Plant hormones such as auxins coordinate growth responses to light and gravity.

Importance to humans

Agriculture depends on a small number of plant species — wheat, rice, maize, potato and soybean provide most of humanity's calories. Plants also supply timber, fibres (cotton, linen), medicines (from willow-bark aspirin to foxglove digitalis), fuels and ornamentals, and they regulate climate by storing carbon. The scientific study of plants is botany.
