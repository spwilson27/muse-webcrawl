Black Boy (1945) is a memoir by the American author Richard Wright detailing his upbringing. It describes his early life in Roxie, Mississippi, and his fraught relationships with family members, his experiences with religion, and his move north to Chicago, where he joined the Communist Party and later the Federal Writers' Project. The book is considered a classic of African-American literature.

Content

The narrative follows Wright from early childhood — marked by his father's abandonment, poverty, hunger and a strict, religious grandmother — through a series of jobs and encounters with Jim Crow racism in the South, to his decisive migration to Chicago as part of the Great Migration. Wright portrays reading and writing as his escape route, forging notes to borrow library books reserved for whites.

Publication history

Wright's original manuscript, titled American Hunger, also covered his disillusioning involvement with the Communist Party in Chicago. As published in 1945, only the Southern childhood portion appeared; the second part was published posthumously as American Hunger in 1977, and the complete text was restored in a 1991 Library of America edition. The 1945 book was a best seller and a Book-of-the-Month Club selection.

Reception

Black Boy was praised for its unflinching depiction of racism and poverty, and it is frequently taught in American schools, though it has also faced challenges and bans over its language and themes.
