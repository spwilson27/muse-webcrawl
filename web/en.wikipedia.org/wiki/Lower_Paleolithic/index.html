The Lower Paleolithic (or Lower Palaeolithic) is the earliest subdivision of the Paleolithic, or Old Stone Age. It spans the time from around 3.3 million years ago, when the first evidence for stone tool production and use by hominins appears in the archaeological record, until around 300,000 years ago, encompassing the Oldowan (and Lomekwian) and Acheulean lithic industries.

Stone tool industries

The earliest known tools, from Lomekwi in Kenya (about 3.3 million years old), precede the genus Homo. The Oldowan industry (from about 2.6 million years ago, named after Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania) consists of simple cores, choppers and sharp flakes struck with hammerstones, used for butchery and processing plants. The Acheulean, appearing about 1.76 million years ago, is characterised by large bifacial tools — handaxes and cleavers — shaped to a planned, symmetrical form, and persisted for well over a million years in Africa, Europe and western Asia.

Hominins and behaviour

Lower Paleolithic tools are associated with early Homo (including Homo habilis and Homo erectus) and later archaic humans such as Homo heidelbergensis. During this period hominins first dispersed out of Africa into Asia and Europe, mastered the controlled use of fire (securely documented by several hundred thousand years ago), built simple shelters, and hunted or scavenged large game. The period ends with the emergence of prepared-core (Levallois) technology and the Middle Paleolithic, around 300,000 years ago.
