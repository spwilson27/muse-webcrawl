Bayberry

Bayberry (Myrica / Morella)
FamilyMyricaceae
Well-known speciesMyrica cerifera (southern wax myrtle), M. pensylvanica (northern bayberry), M. rubra (yangmei)

Bayberry is a common name applied to several species of shrubs and small trees in the wax-myrtle family, Myricaceae, principally in the genera Myrica and Morella. The name most often refers to the North American species whose waxy grey-blue berries have traditionally been used to make fragrant candles and soap.

Species and distribution

The northern bayberry, Morella pensylvanica (syn. Myrica pensylvanica), is a deciduous shrub native to the Atlantic coast and Great Lakes shores of North America, valued for its tolerance of poor, sandy soils. The southern bayberry or wax myrtle, Morella cerifera, ranges through the southeastern United States, the Caribbean and Central America and is semi-evergreen or evergreen. The Asian Myrica rubra, known in China as yangmei ("bayberry" in commerce), is grown commercially for its edible red fruit.

Uses

The fruits of the North American species are coated with a brittle, aromatic wax. Boiling the berries in water skims off this wax, traditionally used for bayberry candles — noted for their clean burn and spicy fragrance — and for soap; the custom dates to the colonial era, when bayberry wax supplemented scarce tallow. The root bark was used in folk herbal medicine as an astringent. In the garden, bayberries are planted for erosion control, hedging and wildlife value, and their roots fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiosis with Frankia bacteria, allowing them to colonise infertile ground.

Ecology

The berries are eaten by many birds, notably the yellow-rumped warbler ("myrtle warbler"), which can digest the wax. Because the shrubs tolerate salt spray and nutrient-poor soils, they are characteristic plants of dunes, barrens and coastal thickets.
