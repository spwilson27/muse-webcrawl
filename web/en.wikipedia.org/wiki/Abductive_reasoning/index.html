Abductive reasoning

Abductive reasoning (also called abduction, abductive inference, or retroduction) is a form of logical inference that seeks the simplest and most likely conclusion from a set of observations. It was formulated and advanced by American philosopher and logician Charles Sanders Peirce beginning in the last third of the 19th century.

Form

Peirce characterised abduction with the schema: "The surprising fact, C, is observed. But if A were true, C would be a matter of course. Hence, there is reason to suspect that A is true." Unlike deduction, abduction does not guarantee its conclusion, and unlike induction it generates a new hypothesis rather than generalising from instances. In modern philosophy of science the closely related notion is inference to the best explanation: selecting, among competing hypotheses, the one that best explains the evidence.

Applications

Abductive reasoning is pervasive in medical diagnosis (inferring a disease from symptoms), fault diagnosis in engineering, detective and legal reasoning, scientific discovery, and natural language understanding. In artificial intelligence and logic programming, abduction is implemented as the search for hypotheses which, together with a knowledge base, entail a given observation, and it is used in plan recognition and diagnosis systems.

Criticism

Because the "best" explanation is judged by virtues such as simplicity, scope and fit with background knowledge, critics question whether abduction is a distinct form of inference or a heuristic stage in inquiry; Peirce himself treated it as the first, hypothesis-generating stage, to be followed by deduction of consequences and inductive testing.
