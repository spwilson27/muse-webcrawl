Protagoras (Greek: Πρωταγόρας) is a dialogue by Plato in which Socrates debates the great Sophist Protagoras on whether virtue (aretē) can be taught. Set in the house of Callias during Protagoras's visit to Athens, the dialogue features an unusually large cast of famous intellectuals, including the sophists Prodicus and Hippias and Pericles' sons, and it is prized both for its philosophical arguments and for its drama: it is one of the few dialogues narrated by Socrates himself, to an unnamed companion. The dramatic date is generally placed around 433 BC.

Plot

Before dawn, the young Hippocrates wakes Socrates with exciting news: Protagoras has arrived in Athens. Hippocrates wants an introduction so that he can become the sophist's pupil. Socrates warns him that one should be more careful buying nourishment for the soul than food for the body, since teachings, unlike food, cannot be carried away in a separate vessel—one absorbs them immediately, for better or worse. The two go to the house of Callias, where Protagoras is pacing the portico with a train of admirers who part and wheel behind him "like a chorus". Prodicus is still in bed, wrapped in blankets, in a storeroom; Hippias sits enthroned fielding questions about astronomy.

Asked what Hippocrates will learn, Protagoras answers without embarrassment that he teaches the art of politics and makes men good citizens. Socrates objects that virtue does not appear to be teachable: the Athenians let anyone speak on politics but demand experts on technical subjects; great men such as Pericles fail to transmit their virtue to their sons; and there are no acknowledged teachers of virtue as there are of music or medicine.

The Great Speech

Protagoras replies with a celebrated set speech. First he tells a myth: Epimetheus distributed powers among the creatures but left human beings naked and defenceless, so Prometheus stole fire and the practical arts from Hephaestus and Athena. Humans, lacking the political art, wronged one another and were scattered by wild beasts, until Zeus sent Hermes to give all human beings a share in aidōs (respect, shame) and dikē (justice). Then, in logos, Protagoras argues from practice: cities punish wrongdoers to improve them, not merely to avenge the past; children are educated in virtue from the cradle by nurses, parents, teachers and laws; and the fact that even the worst citizen is a paragon of justice compared with men who had no laws shows that virtue is indeed cultivated by everyone. The speech is one of the most important surviving defences of democracy's underlying assumptions.

The poem of Simonides

Protagoras challenges Socrates's method of short questions; Socrates threatens to leave, complaining of a poor memory, but the company negotiates a compromise in which each man leads in turn. The argument stalls over an ode by Simonides—"hard is it to become a good man"—which Protagoras accuses of contradicting the poet's own criticism of Pittacus's saying "hard is it to be noble". Socrates, aided by Prodicus's distinctions among synonyms, delivers a virtuoso exegesis arguing that Simonides is consistent: becoming good is hard, but being good is impossible for a human being; only a god can possess excellence unconditionally. Socrates then disowns the whole exercise—discussing poets, he says, is like entertainment at drinking parties of the uneducated, who hire flute-girls because they cannot converse in their own voices; educated men should test each other directly.

The unity of virtue and the hedonism passage

The discussion returns to the opening question through the problem of whether the virtues—justice, temperance, holiness, wisdom, courage—are parts of one thing, like parts of a face (each with its own function) or parts of a lump of gold (differing only in size). Protagoras chooses the face. Socrates argues that justice is holy and holiness just, and drives at wisdom and temperance being one. The hardest case is courage: Protagoras insists it differs from the rest, since men can be courageous yet unjust. Socrates then explores the thesis that the pleasant is the good: if the many are "overcome by pleasure", that can only mean they choose a smaller near pleasure over a larger distant one through bad measurement—an art of measurement (metrētikē technē) would save us, making knowledge, not pleasure, master. Protagoras refuses to identify the good with pleasure outright, and the argument that courage is knowledge of what is and is not to be feared is left pressing against his earlier position.

Ending

The dialogue ends in a famous reversal. Socrates, who began by doubting that virtue is teachable, has argued in a way implying that virtue is knowledge—and knowledge is eminently teachable. Protagoras, who began by claiming to teach virtue, has resisted identifying virtue with knowledge. Socrates wryly proposes that they investigate what virtue is before asking how it is acquired, and compliments Protagoras's performance. The two part in mutual respect, with the argument unresolved.

Themes and influence

The Protagoras is a principal source for the thought of the historical Protagoras—especially the Great Speech—and for sophistic educational theory. Its hedonism passage (351b–358d) is among the most debated in Plato: whether Socrates endorses the equation of good and pleasure or adopts it only to argue with "the many" remains disputed. The dialogue's dramatic portrait of the sophists' world—part seminar, part celebrity tour—has made it a favourite for readers interested in fifth-century Athenian intellectual life as well as in ethics.
