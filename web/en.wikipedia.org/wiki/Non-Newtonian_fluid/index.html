Non-Newtonian fluid


Labelled placeholder: oobleck demonstration

A non-Newtonian fluid is a fluid that does not follow Newton's law of viscosity, that is, it has variable viscosity dependent on stress. In particular, the viscosity of non-Newtonian fluids can change when subjected to force, becoming either more liquid or more solid. Ketchup, for example, becomes runnier when shaken and is thus a non-Newtonian fluid. Many salt solutions and molten polymers are non-Newtonian fluids, as are many commonly found substances such as custard, toothpaste, starch suspensions (such as cornstarch in water, sometimes called "oobleck"), paint, blood, melted butter and shampoo.

Most commonly, the viscosity (the gradual deformation by shear or tensile stresses) of non-Newtonian fluids is dependent on shear rate or shear rate history. Some non-Newtonian fluids with shear-independent viscosity, however, still exhibit normal stress-differences or other non-Newtonian behaviour.

In a Newtonian fluid, the relation between the shear stress and the shear rate is linear, passing through the origin, the constant of proportionality being the coefficient of viscosity. In a non-Newtonian fluid, the relation between the shear stress and the shear rate is different. The fluid can even exhibit time-dependent viscosity. Therefore, a constant coefficient of viscosity cannot be defined.

Types

Shear thickening (dilatant)

In a shear-thickening (dilatant) fluid, viscosity increases with the rate of shear strain. A well-known example is a suspension of cornstarch in water ("oobleck"), which can support a running person's weight but yields to slow pressure.

Shear thinning (pseudoplastic)

In shear-thinning (pseudoplastic) fluids, viscosity decreases with increasing shear rate. Paint, ketchup, blood and many polymer solutions behave this way, flowing more easily when stirred, shaken or spread.

Thixotropic and rheopectic

Thixotropic fluids show a time-dependent decrease in viscosity under constant shear (e.g., some gels and drilling muds); rheopectic fluids show the opposite, time-dependent increase in viscosity.

Bingham plastic

A Bingham plastic behaves as a rigid body at low stresses but flows as a viscous fluid at high stress; toothpaste and mayonnaise are common examples.

Examples

Examples include oobleck, quicksand, silly putty, blood, ketchup, paint, shampoo, toothpaste and many polymer melts and suspensions.
