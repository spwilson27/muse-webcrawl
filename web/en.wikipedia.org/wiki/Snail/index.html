Snail

A snail is a shelled gastropod. The name is most often applied to land snails, terrestrial pulmonate gastropod molluscs. However, the common name snail is also used for most of the members of the molluscan class Gastropoda that have a coiled shell that is large enough for the animal to retract completely into. When the word "snail" is used in this most general sense, it includes not just land snails but also numerous species of sea snails and freshwater snails.

Gastropods that lack a conspicuous shell are commonly called slugs rather than snails.

Overview

Snails that respire using a lung belong to the group Pulmonata; those with gills form a paraphyletic group. Most land snails are pulmonates. They are found across the world in a wide range of habitats, from deserts to the deep sea. The largest known land gastropod is the African giant snail Achatina achatina, whose shell can measure up to 30 cm in length, while some marine snails (such as the Australian trumpet, Syrinx aruanus) have shells approaching 1 metre.

Anatomy

The body of a snail consists of a head, a mantle enclosing the internal organs and a mantle cavity functioning as a lung in pulmonates, and a muscular ventral foot used for locomotion. The head carries one or two pairs of tentacles; in land snails the eyes are at the tips of the upper pair. The mouth contains a radula, a ribbon of tiny teeth used to scrape or cut food. Most of the digestive and reproductive organs are contained in the visceral hump, protected by the coiled shell.

Life cycle

Most land snails are hermaphrodites, possessing both male and female reproductive organs, though they usually exchange sperm with a partner. They lay eggs in soil or under leaf litter; the young hatch with a small, translucent shell that grows with them. Lifespans range from a few years in the wild to a decade or more in captivity.

Human relevance

Snails feature in cuisine — most famously as French escargot — and have been eaten since prehistoric times. They are kept as pets, studied in science, can damage crops and gardens, and some freshwater species transmit parasites such as schistosomes. Snail mucus (mucin) and slime are used in cosmetics.
