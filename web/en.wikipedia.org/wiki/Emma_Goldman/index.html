Emma Goldman (June 27, 1869 – May 14, 1940) was an anarchist political activist and writer. Born in Kaunas in the Russian Empire (present-day Lithuania), she emigrated to the United States in 1885 and became a leading figure in the American anarchist movement. Known to her detractors as "Red Emma," she was a celebrated orator who lectured widely on anarchism, women's emancipation, free speech, and birth control.

Early life

Goldman was born into a Jewish family in Kaunas and spent part of her childhood in Königsberg and St. Petersburg. At sixteen she emigrated with a sister to Rochester, New York, where she worked in a garment factory. The execution of the Haymarket defendants in 1887 pushed her toward anarchism. In 1889 she moved to New York City, where she met the anarchist Alexander Berkman, who became her lifelong comrade and, for a time, lover.

Activism

In 1892, during the Homestead Strike, Berkman attempted to assassinate industrialist Henry Clay Frick; Goldman was implicated in the plot's planning and her notoriety grew. She was imprisoned in 1893 for inciting unemployed workers to riot, and repeatedly thereafter: for distributing birth-control literature (1916) and for opposing conscription during the First World War (1917).

From 1906 she published the anarchist journal Mother Earth. Her essays were collected in Anarchism and Other Essays (1910), and she wrote extensively on drama, sexuality, and women's rights, arguing that marriage and religion constrained women's freedom.

"If I can't dance, I don't want to be part of your revolution." — a sentiment widely attributed to Goldman, summarizing her insistence that liberation include joy and everyday life.

Deportation and later years

In December 1919, during the First Red Scare, Goldman was deported with Berkman and hundreds of others to the Soviet Union aboard the USAT Buford. Initially hopeful about the Bolshevik Revolution, she became disillusioned by its repression, recorded in My Disillusionment in Russia (1923). She later lived in England, France, and Canada, supported the anarchist cause in the Spanish Civil War, and published her autobiography, Living My Life, in 1931. She died in Toronto in 1940 and was buried near the Haymarket martyrs in Chicago.
