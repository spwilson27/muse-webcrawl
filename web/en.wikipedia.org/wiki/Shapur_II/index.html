Shapur II (Middle Persian: 𐭱𐭧𐭯𐭥𐭧𐭥𐭩, Šābuhr; 309–379), also known as Shapur the Great, was the tenth Sasanian King of Kings of Iran. His reign of seventy years — the longest in Iranian history — began, uniquely, with his coronation before birth or in infancy, and saw the Sasanian Empire reach a golden age: he reversed territorial losses, defeated the Roman emperors Constantius II and Julian, expanded into Arabia and Central Asia, and presided over a strengthening of Zoroastrian orthodoxy accompanied by the persecution of Christians.

Early life and accession

According to later tradition, Shapur was crowned in the womb: after the death of Hormizd II and the brief reigns and depositions of his elder sons, the nobles and priests placed the crown on the belly of Hormizd's pregnant wife, and the infant Shapur was king from birth. Real power during his minority lay with the nobility and his mother. At sixteen he assumed personal rule, first campaigning against Arab tribes that raided southern Persia, earning the epithet "Zu'l-Aktaf" ("he of the shoulders") in Arabic sources for his alleged treatment of prisoners.

Wars with Rome

Shapur's great project was the recovery of the five provinces ceded to Rome by the Treaty of Nisibis (299). From 337 he waged war on Constantius II, winning battles in Mesopotamia and besieging Nisibis three times without taking it. In 359 he captured the fortress of Amida after a famous siege described by Ammianus Marcellinus, and in 363 confronted the invasion of the emperor Julian, who advanced to Ctesiphon but was killed during the Roman retreat. His successor Jovian surrendered Nisibis, Singara and the trans-Tigris provinces, restoring Persia's eastern frontier with Rome for a generation.

Eastern and Arabian campaigns

In the east, Shapur subdued the Chionites and other Hunnic peoples, some of whom later fought in his armies, and maintained Sasanian authority in Bactria and the Kabul valley. In Arabia he campaigned deep into the peninsula, deporting Arab tribes and fortifying the "Wall of the Arabs" against desert raiders.

Religion and administration

A devout Zoroastrian, Shapur II supported the priesthood under mobads such as Adurbad-i Mahraspandand, and under his reign the Avesta was further codified. After the Roman Empire's turn to Christianity, Persian Christians fell under suspicion of Roman sympathies, and from about 340 Shapur imposed double taxation and episodes of persecution in which many Christians, including the Catholicos Simeon bar Sabba'e, were executed — remembered as the Great Persecution in Syriac martyrologies.

Legacy

Shapur II died in 379 and was succeeded by his brother Ardashir II. He is remembered in Iranian tradition as one of the greatest Sasanian monarchs, restorer of the empire's frontiers and military prestige after a period of dynastic instability.
