Phase diagram

A phase diagram in physical chemistry, engineering, mineralogy, and materials science is a type of chart used to show conditions (pressure, temperature, volume, composition) at which thermodynamically distinct phases (such as solid, liquid, and gaseous states) occur and coexist at equilibrium. For a pure substance, a pressure–temperature diagram divides the plane into regions of solid, liquid, and gas separated by phase-boundary lines: the fusion curve, vaporisation curve, and sublimation curve, which meet at the triple point; the vaporisation curve ends at the critical point, beyond which liquid and gas become indistinguishable as a supercritical fluid.

Features

Water's phase diagram is anomalous: because ice is less dense than liquid water, its fusion curve slopes to the left (negative dP/dT), so pressure can melt ice. The triple point of water (273.16 K, 611.657 Pa) formerly defined the kelvin. Carbon dioxide's triple point lies above atmospheric pressure, so solid CO2 ("dry ice") sublimes rather than melts at 1 atm.[1]

Binary and materials diagrams

For two-component systems, composition replaces one axis, yielding temperature–composition diagrams with liquidus and solidus curves, eutectic and peritectic points, and two-phase fields interpreted with the lever rule. Iron–carbon diagrams govern steel metallurgy, and liquid-crystal, superconducting, and polymer phase diagrams follow the same logic. The number of degrees of freedom at any point follows the Gibbs phase rule.
