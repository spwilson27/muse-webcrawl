The Armagh Observatory is an astronomical research institute in Armagh, Northern Ireland. It was founded in 1789 by Archbishop Richard Robinson, Baron Rokeby, as part of his vision for a university in the city, and is one of the oldest observatories in the United Kingdom and Ireland still operating on its original site. Since 1947 it has shared its grounds with the Armagh Planetarium; the two merged in 2016 as the Armagh Observatory and Planetarium.

History and research

Under directors including Romney Robinson, the observatory built a reputation for positional astronomy and meteorology. It houses the Armagh catalogue of stars and the New General Catalogue's predecessor work by J. L. E. Dreyer, who compiled the New General Catalogue of Nebulae and Clusters of Stars (1888) while director. Its weather station has recorded daily meteorological data since 1795, giving one of the longest continuous climate records in the British Isles. Modern research covers stellar astrophysics, the Sun, Solar System bodies, and the history of astronomy.

Instruments and grounds

Historic instruments preserved at the observatory include a Troughton equatorial telescope and the Armagh refractor. The grounds form an astropark with scale models of the Solar System and the Universe, and the Human Orrery, in which visitors themselves act as planets.
