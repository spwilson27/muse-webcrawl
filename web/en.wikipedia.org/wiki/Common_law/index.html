Common law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Common law (also known as judicial precedent, judge-made law or case law) is the body of law primarily developed through decisions of courts and similar tribunals, as opposed to statutes adopted through the legislative process or regulations issued by the executive. A common law system gives great weight to precedent, on the principle that similar facts should be treated alike (stare decisis). It is the legal system of England and Wales and of most former British territories, including the United States, Canada, Australia and India, often in mixed forms.

History

The system originated after the Norman Conquest as royal courts standardised ("made common") the law of England, developing writs and, later, the parallel jurisdiction of equity to soften the common law's rigidity. William Blackstone's Commentaries (1765–69) systematised it for a wide audience. Received throughout the British Empire, common law interacted with local customs and, after independence, with written constitutions and codifying statutes.

Operation

Judges decide cases by identifying the binding ratio decidendi of earlier decisions from higher courts, distinguishing or following them; the adversarial procedure, with party-led presentation of evidence and oral argument, is characteristic. Modern common-law courts also interpret statutes intensively, and statute law now dominates fields such as taxation and regulation; yet judge-made law remains central in contract, tort and much of criminal law. The flexibility of precedent — its capacity for incremental adaptation — is praised as evolutionary wisdom and criticised as undemocratic and uncertain, sometimes in the same judgment.
