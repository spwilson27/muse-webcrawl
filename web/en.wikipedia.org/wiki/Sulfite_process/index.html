Sulfite process

The sulfite process is a method of extracting wood pulp using sulfurous acid and bisulfite ions to break the bonds between cellulose and lignin in wood. Invented by the American chemist Benjamin Chew Tilghman, who patented it in 1867, and developed industrially by Carl Daniel Ekman in Sweden in the 1870s, the sulfite process was the dominant method of chemical pulping from the late nineteenth century until the 1940s, when it was overtaken by the alkaline kraft process. It survives today in specialty grades and in the production of dissolving pulp.

Chemistry

Wood chips are cooked under pressure in large digesters with a solution of sulfurous acid and a bisulfite of calcium, magnesium, sodium, or ammonium. Under acidic conditions (pH roughly 1.5–5 depending on the variant), the bisulfite sulfonates the lignin polymer, converting it into water-soluble lignosulfonates that wash away from the cellulose fibers. Cooking temperatures reach about 130–160 °C over several hours. Because the acidic liquor attacks cellulose as well, sulfite pulp is generally weaker than kraft pulp, but it is brighter and easier to bleach, and the process is more suitable for resinous softwoods than earlier methods were.

Variants

Depending on the base chemical and pH, the process takes several forms: the original acid (calcium) sulfite process; bisulfite processes at moderate acidity; neutral sulfite semichemical (NSSC) pulping, widely used for corrugating medium; and alkaline sulfite variants. The choice of base also determines whether the spent liquor can be burned for chemical recovery — magnesium- and sodium-based systems permit recovery, whereas the original calcium system did not.

By-products

The spent cooking liquor ("red liquor") contains lignosulfonates, which are used as dispersants, binders, concrete admixtures, and drilling-mud additives, and sugars that can be fermented to ethanol or used to grow torula yeast. Historically, sulfite mills were notorious water polluters; discharge of spent liquor into rivers caused severe oxygen depletion, one reason the recoverable kraft process displaced sulfite on both economic and environmental grounds.

Uses of sulfite pulp

Sulfite pulps are valued for brightness, cleanliness, and ease of refining, and are used for fine papers, tissue, and as dissolving pulp for rayon, cellophane, cellulose acetate, and nitrocellulose.
