Single-stage-to-orbit

A single-stage-to-orbit (SSTO) vehicle reaches orbit from the surface of a body without jettisoning hardware such as rocket stages, expendable tanks or booster rockets. No SSTO vehicle has yet operated from Earth, although several designs have been proposed and a few subscale prototypes tested.

Advantages

In principle, an SSTO vehicle would be fully reusable, avoiding the cost and operational complexity of recovering, refurbishing or discarding stages. Advocates argue that a mature SSTO could operate more like an airliner, with rapid turnaround and greatly reduced cost per flight.

Challenges

The rocket equation makes SSTO from Earth extremely demanding. Reaching low Earth orbit requires a delta-v of roughly 9.4 km/s including losses. A chemical rocket must therefore devote around 90% of its lift-off mass to propellant, leaving very little mass budget for structure, engines, thermal protection and payload. The vehicle must also carry engines efficient both at sea level and in vacuum, a difficulty that has motivated aerospike and tripropellant designs.

Notable projects

  • DC-X (Delta Clipper) — McDonnell Douglas prototype that demonstrated vertical take-off and landing in the 1990s, though it was not designed to reach orbit.
  • X-33 / VentureStar — NASA and Lockheed Martin programme cancelled in 2001 after composite fuel tank failures.
  • HOTOL and its successor Skylon — British air-breathing concepts using the SABRE engine.
  • Roton — a rotary-rocket concept from Rotary Rocket Company.

SSTO on other bodies

Because the Moon and Mars have weaker gravity and (for the Moon) no atmosphere, single-stage ascent vehicles are practical there; the Apollo Lunar Module ascent stage was effectively an SSTO vehicle on the Moon.
