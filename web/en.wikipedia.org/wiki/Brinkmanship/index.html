Brinkmanship

Brinkmanship (also spelled brinksmanship) is the practice of trying to achieve an advantageous outcome by pushing dangerous events to the brink of active conflict. The tactic is used in international politics, foreign policy, labour relations, contemporary military strategy and litigation, and involves the threat of extreme measures to intimidate an opponent into giving in.

Origin

The term is associated with John Foster Dulles, United States Secretary of State under President Dwight D. Eisenhower, who in a 1956 Life magazine interview described the ability to get to the verge of war without getting into war as "the necessary art". Critics, including Adlai Stevenson, turned the description into the pejorative "brinkmanship". The strategy formed part of the doctrine of massive retaliation during the early Cold War.

Mechanics

In game-theoretic terms, brinkmanship works by deliberately creating a shared risk of disaster — a situation in which events may slip out of control — so that the opponent, fearing catastrophe, backs down first. It resembles the game of chicken but adds the possibility that neither side can fully control the escalation. The Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 is frequently cited as both the classic example of brinkmanship and a demonstration of its dangers.
