Morpheme

A morpheme is the smallest meaningful constituent within a linguistic expression, as distinct from a phoneme, which is the smallest unit of sound. A morpheme cannot be divided into smaller meaningful parts. For example, the word unbreakable consists of three morphemes: un- (a prefix meaning "not"), break (a root), and -able (a suffix meaning "able to be"), while the single word break is itself one morpheme. The study of morphemes is called morphology.

Classification

Free and bound morphemes

Free morphemes can stand alone as words (break, dog, quick). Bound morphemes must attach to another form: these include prefixes and suffixes (affixes) such as un-, -ness, -ed, as well as bound roots like -ceive in receive and perceive.

Derivational and inflectional

Derivational morphemes create new words or change a word's category, e.g. happy → happiness. Inflectional morphemes mark grammatical information — tense, number, case, comparison — without changing the core word: English has only eight inflectional suffixes, including plural -s, possessive -'s, past tense -ed, and comparative -er.

Allomorphs

The variant pronunciations or spellings of a single morpheme are its allomorphs. The English plural morpheme, for instance, is realised as /s/ in cats, /z/ in dogs, and /ɪz/ in horses, conditioned by the final sound of the stem.

Other concepts

Some forms, like the cran- in cranberry, occur in only one word and are called "cranberry morphemes". A zero morpheme is a posited meaningful unit with no phonetic form, sometimes used to analyse words like sheep used as a plural.
