Variable-sweep wing

A variable-sweep wing, colloquially known as a swing wing, is an airplane wing, or set of wings, that may be swept back and then returned to its original straight position during flight. It allows the aircraft's shape to be modified in flight, combining the low-speed benefits of a straight wing with the high-speed benefits of a highly swept wing.

Principle

Unswept wings give high lift and efficient low-speed handling for take-off and landing. Swept wings reduce drag at transonic and supersonic speeds. A swing wing pivots, usually on bearings set in or outside the fuselage, driven by hydraulic or electric actuators, with seals and glove vanes managing airflow.

History

Experiments date to the 1940s–50s, including the Bell X-5. Operational types proliferated in the 1960s–70s: the General Dynamics F-111, Grumman F-14 Tomcat, Panavia Tornado, Rockwell B-1 Lancer, Mikoyan-Gurevich MiG-23 and MiG-27, Sukhoi Su-24, and Tupolev Tu-22M and Tu-160 bombers.

Advantages and drawbacks

Benefits include good performance across a wide speed range and short take-off. Drawbacks are weight, mechanical complexity, maintenance cost, and the shift of the centre of lift as wings sweep. Modern flight-control, materials and fixed-wing designs with high-lift devices reduced interest in new swing-wing designs after the 1980s.
