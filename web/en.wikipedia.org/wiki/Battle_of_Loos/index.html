Battle of Loos

The Battle of Loos (25 September – 8 October 1915) was a British offensive in the Artois region of northern France during the First World War, forming part of the French autumn offensive (the Third Battle of Artois). The British First Army under Sir Douglas Haig attacked at Loos-en-Gohelle with the first British use of poison gas, gained ground on the opening day, but failed to achieve a breakthrough; the battle cost some 50,000 British casualties and is remembered for the destruction of several Kitchener's Army battalions and the death of many notable figures, including Rudyard Kipling's son John.

Background

To support the great French offensive in Champagne, Sir John French's BEF attacked in the unpromising coal-mining country around Loos, where the terrain — flat pithead villages, slag heaps and miners' cottages — favoured the defence. Because artillery and shells were short, Haig relied on 5,500 cylinders releasing 140 tons of chlorine gas to compensate, despite the risk of unfavourable wind. The reserves (XI Corps) were held far back under French's control, the central grievance of the battle's aftermath.

The battle

On 25 September 1915 the gas drifted unevenly — in places it hung in no man's land or blew back on the attackers — but in the south the assault broke through: the 47th (London) Division took Loos village, and troops stormed the Hohenzollern Redoubt and Hill 70, coming within sight of Lens. In the north the attack failed against intact wire and machine guns around Hulluch. The decisive reserves, the inexperienced 21st and 24th Divisions of Kitchener's Army, marched up exhausted through clogged roads and attacked on 26 September without adequate artillery support across open ground swept by German fire; they were cut down in thousands in what German accounts called the Leichenfeld von Loos — the Corpse Field of Loos. German counterattacks, including the fighting around the Hohenzollern Redoubt that continued to 8 October, recovered most of the lost ground.

Aftermath

Loos cost the British roughly 50,000 casualties for no strategic gain and claimed the lives of three major-generals and many of the "Pals" of the New Armies. The recriminations over the handling of the reserves contributed to Sir John French's replacement by Haig as Commander-in-Chief in December 1915. The Loos Memorial at Dud Corner commemorates over 20,000 British soldiers with no known grave.
