Fermentation in winemaking

The process of fermentation in winemaking turns grape juice into an alcoholic beverage. During fermentation, yeasts transform the sugars in the juice into ethanol and carbon dioxide (as a by-product). The temperature and speed of fermentation are important considerations, as is the management of oxygen levels. Fermentation may be carried out in stainless steel tanks, open wooden vats, oak barrels, or inside the wine bottle itself, as in the production of many sparkling wines.

Role of yeast

The principal yeast is Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Fermentation may begin with wild yeasts naturally present on the grapes, or winemakers may inoculate with cultured strains selected for flavour, alcohol tolerance, and reliability. Yeast also produces flavour compounds, including esters and higher alcohols, that shape the wine's aroma.

Secondary fermentation

Malolactic fermentation, carried out by lactic acid bacteria such as Oenococcus oeni, converts sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid and is used for most red wines and many whites. A distinct secondary fermentation in the bottle, the méthode traditionnelle, produces the carbonation in Champagne and other sparkling wines.

Risks

Stuck fermentations, excessive temperatures, and spoilage organisms such as Brettanomyces can spoil wine; sulphur dioxide and temperature control are standard safeguards.
