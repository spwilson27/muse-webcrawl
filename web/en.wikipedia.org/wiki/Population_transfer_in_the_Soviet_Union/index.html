Population transfer in the Soviet Union

Population transfer in the Soviet Union refers to the forced migration of ethnic groups and other categories of people within the territory of the Soviet Union between the 1930s and the early 1950s, carried out on the orders of Joseph Stalin and administered chiefly by the NKVD under Lavrentiy Beria. Whole nationalities — including Soviet Koreans, Volga Germans, Crimean Tatars, Chechens, Ingush, Kalmyks, Karachays, Balkars, Meskhetian Turks and others — were deported from their homelands, usually to Central Asia and Siberia, in operations that often took only a few days.

Background

Forced resettlement had precedents in the Tsarist era and in the dekulakization campaigns of collectivization (1929–1933), in which roughly 1.8 million kulaks were exiled to special settlements. From the mid-1930s, deportation policy acquired an explicitly ethnic character: entire peoples were treated as collectively suspect because of their cross-border ethnic ties or alleged collaboration.

Major deportations

The first total ethnic deportation was that of the Soviet Koreans of the Far East in 1937, moved to Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan on suspicion of Japanese espionage. After the German invasion in 1941, the Volga German Autonomous Republic was abolished and its population deported east. In 1943–1944, following the recapture of territory from German occupation, the Karachays, Kalmyks, Chechens, Ingush, Balkars and Crimean Tatars were deported en masse, accused of collaboration with the enemy; their autonomous republics were dissolved. In November 1944 the Meskhetian Turks and other groups were deported from southwestern Georgia. Deportees travelled in freight trains for weeks, with high mortality from hunger, cold and disease.

Special settlements

Deportees were placed in "special settlements" (spetsposeleniya) under NKVD commandants, required to report regularly, forbidden to leave, and used as forced labour in agriculture, forestry and mining. Conditions were harsh, especially in the first winters, and mortality was severe — for some groups, demographic losses are estimated in the range of a fifth to a quarter of the population.

Rehabilitation and return

After Stalin's death in 1953 and Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" of 1956, most deported peoples were formally rehabilitated and allowed to return home; the Chechens, Ingush, Karachays, Balkars and Kalmyks regained their autonomies in 1957. The Crimean Tatars, Volga Germans and Meskhetian Turks, however, were not permitted to return for decades. In 1989 the Supreme Soviet declared the deportations illegal and repressive, and the Russian RSFSR law of 1991 "On the Rehabilitation of Repressed Peoples" formally recognized them as victims.
