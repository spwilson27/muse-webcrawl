The Vienna Awards were two arbitral decisions imposed by Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy in 1938 and 1940 that transferred territories from Czechoslovakia and Romania to Hungary. They were a product of Hungary's revisionist campaign against the post-First World War Treaty of Trianon, and of the Axis powers' policy of exploiting ethnic tensions in Eastern Europe on the road to the Second World War.

First Vienna Award (1938)

After the Munich Agreement (September 1938) dismembered Czechoslovakia, Hungary pressed claims to the Hungarian-inhabited southern strip of Slovakia and Carpathian Ruthenia. When bilateral talks at Komárno failed, Germany and Italy imposed an arbitration at the Belvedere Palace in Vienna on 2 November 1938, conducted by foreign ministers Joachim von Ribbentrop and Galeazzo Ciano. Czechoslovakia ceded about 11,900 km² of southern Slovakia and southern Carpathian Ruthenia, with some 870,000 inhabitants, to Hungary. Hungary annexed the remainder of Carpathian Ruthenia in March 1939 as Czechoslovakia collapsed.

Second Vienna Award (1940)

Following the Soviet annexation of Bessarabia and northern Bukovina from Romania in June 1940, Hungary demanded Transylvania, and Bulgaria demanded Southern Dobruja. To prevent war between its prospective allies, Germany imposed the Second Vienna Award on 30 August 1940: Romania ceded Northern Transylvania — about 43,000 km² with roughly 2.6 million inhabitants — to Hungary. The award embittered Romanian politics, contributed to the fall of King Carol II, and left both countries dissatisfied; Hungary also participated in the Axis invasion of Yugoslavia and the USSR partly to retain favour in Berlin.

Aftermath

Both awards were annulled after the Second World War. The 1947 Paris Peace Treaties restored the pre-1938 frontiers: southern Slovakia returned to Czechoslovakia, and Northern Transylvania returned to Romania. The treaties thus reversed the awards' territorial changes in full.
