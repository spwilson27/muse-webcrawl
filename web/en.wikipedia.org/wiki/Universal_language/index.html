Universal language

A universal language is a language intended, historically or hypothetically, to be spoken and understood by all people. The idea appears in religious traditions — such as the single language of humanity before the Tower of Babel in the Book of Genesis — and in modern projects for a common international auxiliary language.

The Tower of Babel, after Pieter Bruegel the Elder

Historical projects

In the seventeenth century, scholars such as John Wilkins (An Essay towards a Real Character, and a Philosophical Language, 1668) and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (with his dream of a characteristica universalis) attempted to design "philosophical languages" that would express all concepts unambiguously through a logical classification of ideas. Latin long served as a de facto universal language of scholarship, religion, and diplomacy in Europe.

Constructed auxiliary languages

The modern era produced planned languages meant for universal adoption. Volapük (1879) briefly attracted a large following before being eclipsed by Esperanto, published by L. L. Zamenhof in 1887, which remains the most widely spoken constructed international language, with a continuous community of speakers and a body of original literature. Later projects include Ido, Interlingua, and Latino sine flexione. None achieved universal official adoption, though Esperanto was considered by the League of Nations and is recognized by UNESCO resolutions.

Natural lingua francas

In practice, the role of a global language has fallen to a natural language: English, the most widely learned second language and the dominant tongue of science, aviation, shipping, and the internet. Regional lingua francas such as French, Spanish, Arabic, Russian, Mandarin Chinese, and Swahili serve similar bridging functions in their spheres. Some linguists argue that machine translation may eventually make a single universal language unnecessary.
