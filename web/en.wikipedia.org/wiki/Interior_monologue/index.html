Interior monologue

Interior monologue
TypeNarrative device
Related conceptsStream of consciousness, free indirect discourse
Notable practitionersÉdouard Dujardin, James Joyce, Virginia Woolf, William Faulkner

In literature, an interior monologue is a narrative device that presents a character's thoughts directly to the reader, as though the reader were overhearing the character's inner speech. It is closely associated with the stream of consciousness technique of modernist fiction, though the two terms are not identical: interior monologue refers to the device of rendering thought, while stream of consciousness describes the style of writing that imitates the flow of mental processes.

Characteristics

Interior monologue typically dispenses with a narrating intermediary, presenting thought in the first person and present tense, often with loose syntax, abrupt transitions and private associations that mimic the movement of the mind. Critics distinguish the direct interior monologue, in which the author's presence is minimal, from the indirect form, in which a third-person narrator renders a character's thoughts while retaining narrative control — a technique closely related to free indirect discourse.

History

The French novelist Édouard Dujardin is generally credited with the first sustained use of the device in Les Lauriers sont coupés (1888). It was taken up and transformed by the modernists: James Joyce's Ulysses (1922), and above all Molly Bloom's closing monologue, is the canonical example, while Virginia Woolf, Dorothy Richardson and William Faulkner made the technique central to their fiction. Earlier antecedents include the soliloquies of Shakespearean drama and the psychological narration of Fyodor Dostoevsky.

In other media

Equivalent devices appear in theatre (the soliloquy and aside), film (voice-over narration of a character's thoughts) and comics, where thought balloons and caption boxes serve a similar function.
