P-glycoprotein 1 (permeability glycoprotein, abbreviated as P-gp or Pgp), also known as multidrug resistance protein 1 (MDR1) or ATP-binding cassette sub-family B member 1 (ABCB1) or cluster of differentiation 243 (CD243), is an important protein of the cell membrane that pumps many foreign substances out of cells. More formally, it is an ATP-dependent efflux pump with broad substrate specificity. It exists in animals, fungi, and bacteria, and it likely evolved as a defense mechanism against harmful substances.

P-gp is extensively distributed and expressed in the intestinal epithelium, where it pumps xenobiotics (such as toxins or drugs) back into the intestinal lumen; in liver cells, where it pumps them into bile ducts; in the cells of the proximal tubule of the kidney, where it pumps them into the urine-conducting ducts; and in the capillary endothelial cells composing the blood–brain barrier and blood–testis barrier, where it pumps them back into the capillaries. Some cancer cells also express large amounts of P-gp, further amplifying that effect and rendering these cancers multidrug resistant.

Discovery

P-gp was first characterized in 1976 by Victor Ling and colleagues, who showed that Chinese hamster ovary cells selected for resistance to colchicine displayed cross-resistance to a wide range of unrelated drugs and had an altered cell-surface glycoprotein of about 170 kDa. The protein was named "P" for permeability, reflecting the hypothesis that it modified the permeability of the cell membrane to drugs.

Structure and mechanism

Human P-gp is a 170 kDa transmembrane glycoprotein of 1,280 amino acids. It consists of two homologous halves, each containing six transmembrane domains and a nucleotide-binding domain that binds and hydrolyzes ATP. Substrates are recognized within a large, flexible drug-binding pocket formed by the transmembrane helices when the transporter faces the cytoplasm; ATP hydrolysis then drives a conformational change that expels the substrate to the extracellular side. Many chemically diverse compounds — including anthracyclines, vinca alkaloids, taxanes, HIV protease inhibitors, immunosuppressants, and many others — are substrates.

Clinical significance

Because P-gp limits the oral absorption and brain penetration of many drugs and promotes their elimination into bile and urine, it is a major determinant of drug pharmacokinetics. Inhibitors of P-gp such as verapamil, cyclosporine, ritonavir, and elacridar can raise plasma and tissue concentrations of substrate drugs, while inducers such as rifampicin and St John's wort can lower them. In oncology, overexpression of ABCB1 in tumor cells is a classic mechanism of multidrug resistance; three generations of P-gp inhibitors have been tested clinically, though none has yet become a standard part of cancer therapy.

Polymorphisms in the ABCB1 gene have been studied in relation to drug response and susceptibility to diseases including inflammatory bowel disease, Parkinson's disease, and epilepsy, with often inconsistent results. In veterinary medicine, a deletion mutation in ABCB1 in collies and related dog breeds makes them exquisitely sensitive to ivermectin and other substrate drugs.
