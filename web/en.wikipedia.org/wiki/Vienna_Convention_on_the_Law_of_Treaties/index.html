Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) is an international agreement that regulates treaties between states, establishing comprehensive rules, procedures and guidelines for how treaties are drafted, defined, amended and interpreted. Adopted in 1969 and in force from 1980, it is often called the "treaty on treaties". Although some significant states (notably the United States) have signed but not ratified it, most of its provisions are widely accepted as codifying customary international law, and courts cite it as such.

Main rules

Part III's pacta sunt servanda (Article 26: every treaty in force binds the parties and must be performed in good faith) and the bar on invoking internal law to justify non-performance (Article 27) are foundational. The interpretation rules (Articles 31–33: ordinary meaning in context and in light of object and purpose, supplemented by preparatory work) are the working method of every international tribunal. Grounds of invalidity are narrow and codified (error, fraud, corruption, coercion of a representative or of the state, and conflict with a peremptory norm — jus cogens), as are termination and suspension, including material breach (Article 60) and the tightly limited fundamental change of circumstances (Article 62, rebus sic stantibus — rejected by the ICJ in the cases that invoked it). Reservations are regulated by the object-and-purpose test (Articles 19–23).
