Fargo is a 1996 black comedy crime film written, produced, and directed by Joel Coen, and co-written and produced by Ethan Coen. Set in the snow-covered American Upper Midwest, it stars Frances McDormand as Marge Gunderson, a pregnant Minnesota police chief investigating a triple homicide, and William H. Macy as Jerry Lundegaard, a desperate car salesman whose scheme to have his own wife kidnapped for ransom unravels into violence.

Plot

In 1987, Minneapolis car salesman Jerry Lundegaard, deep in debt, hires two criminals, Carl Showalter and Gaear Grimsrud, to kidnap his wife Jean so that her wealthy father will pay a ransom Jerry intends to skim. The plan disintegrates: a state trooper and two witnesses are murdered near Brainerd, and police chief Marge Gunderson patiently follows the evidence back through Jerry's dealership and his lies to the killers' lakeside hideout, arresting Grimsrud as he feeds his accomplice's body into a wood chipper.

Production and style

Though titled Fargo, the film is set mostly in Minneapolis and Brainerd, Minnesota, and was filmed around the Twin Cities and in North Dakota. Its famous opening claim — "This is a true story" — is a deliberate fiction, part of the Coens' play with vernacular realism, reinforced by exaggerated regional dialect ("Minnesota nice", "you betcha") and Roger Deakins's bleached, white-out cinematography.

Reception and legacy

Fargo premiered at the 1996 Cannes Film Festival, where Joel Coen won Best Director. It won two Academy Awards — Best Actress for McDormand and Best Original Screenplay — from seven nominations. In 2006 it was selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry. The film inspired the acclaimed FX anthology television series Fargo (2014– ), created by Noah Hawley.
