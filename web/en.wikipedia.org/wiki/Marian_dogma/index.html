Marian dogma

Illustration: The Virgin and Child (placeholder image)

Marian dogmas are the doctrines concerning Mary, mother of Jesus, that the Roman Catholic Church holds to be divinely revealed and binding on the faithful. There are four doctrines that have been formally defined as dogma: the Divine Motherhood (Mary as Theotokos, "God-bearer"), her perpetual virginity, the Immaculate Conception, and her Assumption into heaven. Other churches accept some of these teachings, or hold them as pious opinion, while Protestant traditions generally reject those lacking explicit scriptural warrant.

The four dogmas

Divine Motherhood. The Council of Ephesus (431) affirmed Mary as Theotokos — Mother of God — against Nestorius, on the ground that the one born of her is truly God the Son.

Perpetual virginity. The Church teaches that Mary was a virgin before, during and after the birth of Jesus (virginitas ante partum, in partu, post partum), affirmed at the Lateran Council of 649 and held also by the Orthodox and by many early Reformers including Luther and Calvin.

Immaculate Conception. Defined by Pope Pius IX in Ineffabilis Deus (1854), this dogma holds that Mary, from the first moment of her conception, was preserved free from original sin by a singular grace in view of the merits of Christ.

Assumption. Defined by Pope Pius XII in Munificentissimus Deus (1950), this dogma holds that Mary, "having completed the course of her earthly life, was assumed body and soul into heavenly glory." It is the most recent Marian dogma and the only one defined by a pope exercising papal infallibility since that doctrine's own definition in 1870 (the Immaculate Conception definition of 1854 preceded but anticipated it).

Other Marian titles

Beyond the four dogmas, Catholic devotion also honours Mary as Mediatrix, Queen of Heaven, and — in proposals sometimes advanced by the faithful — Co-Redemptrix, though these have not been dogmatically defined. The Second Vatican Council's constitution Lumen Gentium (1964) devoted a chapter to Mary, presenting her as Mother of the Church, a title proclaimed by Pope Paul VI and later extended by Pope Francis, who instituted a memorial of Mary, Mother of the Church.
