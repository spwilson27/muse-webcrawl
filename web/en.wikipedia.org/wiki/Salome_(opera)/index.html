Salome (opera)

Composer: Richard Strauss
Libretto: Hedwig Lachmann (after Oscar Wilde)
Language: German
Premiere: 9 December 1905, Semperoper, Dresden

Salome, Op. 54, is an opera in one act by Richard Strauss. The libretto is Hedwig Lachmann's German translation of Oscar Wilde's French play Salomé, as edited and adapted by the composer. The opera is famous for its "Dance of the Seven Veils" and for its final scene, in which Salome kisses the severed head of John the Baptist.

Background

Strauss saw Wilde's play in Berlin in 1902 and began composing in 1903. The opera premiered at the Dresden Semperoper on 9 December 1905 and caused a sensation: audiences and critics were divided by its dissonant, intensely chromatic score and its decadent subject matter. It was initially banned or censored in several cities—performances in London and at New York's Metropolitan Opera were withdrawn after protests—yet it quickly made Strauss wealthy and established his reputation as an operatic composer.

Synopsis

At the court of Herod Antipas in Galilee, the princess Salome becomes fascinated by the imprisoned prophet Jochanaan (John the Baptist), who denounces her mother Herodias. When Jochanaan rejects her advances, Salome agrees to dance for Herod in return for a promise of anything she wishes. After the Dance of the Seven Veils, she demands the head of Jochanaan on a silver charger. Horrified but bound by his oath, Herod complies. Salome sings a final, ecstatic monologue to the head and kisses it; disgusted, Herod orders his soldiers to kill her.

Music

The score calls for a very large orchestra and is notable for its use of polytonality, extended chromaticism, and leitmotifs associated with Salome, Herod, and Jochanaan. The Dance of the Seven Veils is an orchestral interlude drawing on exoticized "oriental" colour.
