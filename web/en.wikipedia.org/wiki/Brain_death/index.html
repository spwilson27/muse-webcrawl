Brain death

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cerebral angiography demonstrating absent intracranial blood flow.

Brain death is the permanent, irreversible, and complete loss of brain function, including the involuntary activity necessary to sustain life. It is used as an indicator of legal death in many jurisdictions, even though a mechanically ventilated body may retain a heartbeat and some spinal reflexes for a time. Brain death is distinct from a persistent vegetative state, in which some brain functions—such as sleep–wake cycles and brainstem reflexes—remain, and from coma, from which recovery is sometimes possible.

History

The concept emerged with advances in intensive care in the 1950s, when mechanical ventilation made it possible to sustain the bodies of patients whose brains had been irreversibly destroyed. French neurologists described the state as coma dépassé ("a state beyond coma") in 1959. In 1968 the Ad Hoc Committee of the Harvard Medical School published influential criteria for "irreversible coma" as a new definition of death, and in 1981 the United States President's Commission proposed the Uniform Determination of Death Act, which most U.S. states adopted: an individual is dead who has sustained either irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions or irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem. The British tradition instead centres on brainstem death, confirmed by clinical tests of brainstem reflexes.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires a known, catastrophic, and irreversible cause of brain injury; exclusion of confounders such as hypothermia, sedative or paralysing drugs, intoxication, and severe metabolic derangement; and a clinical examination demonstrating coma, absent brainstem reflexes (pupillary, corneal, oculocephalic, gag, and cough), and apnoea—no respiratory effort despite an adequate carbon dioxide stimulus on formal apnoea testing. Some spinal reflex movements, including the dramatic Lazarus sign, may persist and do not preclude the diagnosis. Where the clinical examination cannot be completed, ancillary tests such as four-vessel cerebral angiography, radionuclide cerebral blood-flow studies, transcranial Doppler, or EEG may be used, depending on jurisdiction. Criteria in children require particular care and repeated examinations.

Prerequisites and details vary by country: the United Kingdom tests brainstem function (without routinely requiring EEG), the United States generally follows the American Academy of Neurology guidelines for "death by neurologic criteria", and some jurisdictions require two physicians or specified observation periods.

Organ donation and controversies

Most organs for transplantation are recovered from donors declared dead by neurologic criteria, because circulation continues to perfuse the organs until procurement (the "beating-heart donor"). Brain death law has been contested in a number of well-known cases, in which families have disputed diagnoses on religious or philosophical grounds, and scholars continue to debate whether the whole-brain criterion perfectly tracks the biological death of the organism, since some hypothalamic and hormonal functions may persist and bodies declared brain dead have, with support, gestated fetuses or undergone puberty. Legislatures have responded in different ways—for example, New Jersey law explicitly allows a religious exemption from neurologic criteria.
