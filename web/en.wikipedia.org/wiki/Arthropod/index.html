Arthropod

[Image: a stag beetle, a spider and a crab arranged on a natural-history museum tray with labels]

Arthropods are invertebrate animals with an exoskeleton, a segmented body and paired jointed appendages. They form the phylum Arthropoda, by far the largest in the animal kingdom: over 1.2 million species have been described — about 80% of all known animal species — including insects, arachnids (spiders, scorpions, mites), crustaceans (crabs, shrimp, barnacles) and myriapods (centipedes and millipedes), and total species are estimated in the millions.

Arthropods live in virtually every habitat, from deep oceans and deserts to the air, and are ecologically indispensable as pollinators, decomposers, prey and predators.

Body plan and growth

The rigid external skeleton, made of chitin, protects and supports the body but cannot grow, so arthropods moult (ecdysis) — shedding the old cuticle and expanding before a new one hardens; this vulnerable process is a defining feature of their lives and a limit on their size, most being small.

Segmentation allows specialisation: in insects the body resolves into head, thorax and abdomen with three pairs of legs and usually wings; arachnids have two body parts and four pairs of legs; crustaceans are mostly aquatic with gills and two pairs of antennae.

Evolution

Arthropods evolved in the Cambrian seas over 520 million years ago; trilobites, an extinct group, dominated early oceans and left an unmatched fossil record. Arthropods were among the first animals on land, and insects later became the first animals to fly — advantages that underlie their unmatched diversity.
