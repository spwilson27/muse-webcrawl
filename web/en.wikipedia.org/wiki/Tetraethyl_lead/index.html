FormulaPb(CH₂CH₃)₄
UseAntiknock additive in petrol ("ethyl")

Tetraethyl lead (TEL) is an organolead compound once widely blended into petrol as an antiknock agent, sold under names such as "Ethyl". Chemist Thomas Midgley Jr., working for General Motors research under Charles Kettering, discovered its effectiveness in 1921: tiny amounts raised fuel octane, preventing engine knock and enabling higher-compression engines.

Controversy and toxicity

TEL is acutely toxic — several workers died of lead poisoning at early production plants in 1924, prompting a temporary suspension and a Surgeon General's conference — yet production resumed and leaded petrol spread worldwide. Exhaust from leaded fuel dispersed fine lead particles everywhere; scientists, prominently geochemist Clair Patterson, later showed that environmental lead burdens had risen far above natural levels. Epidemiological research linked childhood lead exposure to reduced IQ, behavioural problems and, in ecological studies, later violent-crime trends.

Phase-out

The United States began phasing down lead in the 1970s, both for health and because lead poisons catalytic converters; leaded road fuel was banned there in 1996, and the European Union followed in 2000. The UN Environment Programme's campaign ended global sales of leaded petrol in July 2021, when Algeria, the last holdout, exhausted supplies. TEL remains in use in aviation gasoline (avgas 100LL) for piston-engine aircraft, for which replacements have been long sought.
