Japanese garden

Japanese gardens (日本庭園, nihon teien) are traditional gardens created in the aesthetics of Japan, shaped by Shinto reverence for nature, Buddhist ideas transmitted from China and Korea, and native ideals of miniaturised landscape. Rather than displaying flowers in beds, the Japanese garden composes rocks, water, sand, trees and borrowed scenery into scenes designed to evoke natural landscapes — often in miniature — and to reward contemplation from changing viewpoints.

History

Garden-making in Japan began under Chinese influence in the Asuka and Nara periods, when aristocratic estates featured ponds and artificial hills modelled on Chinese cosmology. In the Heian period (794–1185), the manual Sakuteiki codified the art of "setting stones", and Pure Land Buddhism inspired "paradise gardens" centred on a pond and an Amida hall, as at Byōdō-in. Under Zen influence in the Muromachi period came the austere dry landscape (karesansui) gardens of raked gravel and rock — supremely the garden of Ryōan-ji (c. 1500) — and the moss garden of Saihō-ji. The tea ceremony of Sen no Rikyū produced the rustic tea garden (roji), while the Edo period developed the great daimyō stroll gardens, such as Kenroku-en in Kanazawa, Koraku-en in Okayama and Kairaku-en in Mito, the celebrated "Three Great Gardens of Japan".

Principles and elements

  • Miniaturisation and symbolism — a few rocks may stand for mountains or the Isles of the Immortals; raked sand suggests water and waves.
  • Asymmetry and concealment (miegakure) — paths curve so scenes are revealed gradually, never all at once.
  • Borrowed scenery (shakkei) — distant mountains or temple roofs are incorporated into the composition.
  • Key elements — carefully placed rocks, ponds and waterfalls, islands, arched or zigzag bridges, stone lanterns, water basins (tsukubai), clipped pines and maples, moss, bamboo fences and stepping-stone paths.
  • Seasonality — plantings are chosen for a sequence of cherry blossom, fresh green, autumn colour and snowy silhouettes.

Influence abroad

Japanese gardens at international expositions from 1876 onward captivated Western visitors, influencing Arts and Crafts and modernist landscape design. Major examples abroad include the Japanese Garden in San Francisco's Golden Gate Park (1894), the Brooklyn Botanic Garden's Hill-and-Pond Garden (1915) and the Portland Japanese Garden, while the style's vocabulary of stone, gravel and restrained planting remains a global idiom of contemplative garden design.
