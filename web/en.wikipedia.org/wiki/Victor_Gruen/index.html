Victor Gruen (born Victor David Grünbaum; July 18, 1903 – February 14, 1980) was an Austrian-born American architect and urban planner best known as a pioneer of the modern shopping mall. An advocate of pedestrian-oriented urban design, Gruen intended the mall to recreate the dense, mixed-use centres of European cities in the American suburbs, but he became disillusioned with how developers stripped his concept of its civic elements.

Life and career

Gruen studied architecture at the Vienna Academy of Fine Arts and was active in socialist political cabaret in interwar Vienna. After the Anschluss in 1938 he fled Austria for the United States, arriving in New York with little money and limited English. He found work designing shopfronts, including the Fifth Avenue boutique of the leather-goods company Lederer, whose innovative recessed entryway attracted notice.

His firm designed the open-air Northland Center near Detroit (1954) and then Southdale Center in Edina, Minnesota (1956), the first fully enclosed, climate-controlled shopping mall, which combined two department stores, dozens of shops, and a garden court intended as a community gathering space. Gruen's later work included downtown revitalisation plans, such as the 1956 Fort Worth plan and the pedestrianisation of Kalamazoo, Michigan (1959), the first American city to close a downtown street to create a pedestrian mall.

Legacy

Although malls became ubiquitous, Gruen repudiated the industry late in life, saying in a 1978 speech that he refused to "pay alimony for those bastard developments". The "Gruen transfer" — the moment when a shopper's purposeful walk gives way to impulsive browsing in a confusingly designed retail space — is named after him, though the effect arose from developers' designs rather than his own intentions. He returned to Vienna in 1968 and died there in 1980.
