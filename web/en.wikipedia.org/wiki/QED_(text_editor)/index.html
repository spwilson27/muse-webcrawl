QED is a line-oriented text editor, originally written by Butler Lampson and L. Peter Deutsch for the Berkeley Timesharing System running on the SDS 940 computer, in 1965–66. Its name is said to stand for "quick editor" (a pun on the Latin quod erat demonstrandum). QED is notable as a foundational influence on the Unix editors ed, ex and vi.

Features

For its time QED was unusually powerful. It introduced regular expressions as an editing tool — generalising them from the theory of automata — and allowed commands to be applied conditionally across a file, stored in multiple buffers, and combined into macros: users could write QED command sequences to perform complex, repeatable transformations of text. Editing was line-based, as terminals of the day were teletypes rather than screens.

Influence

Ken Thompson encountered QED and reimplemented versions for CTSS and Multics, extending it with his own regular-expression engine; when he created Unix, its standard editor ed was modelled on QED's command language — the familiar single-letter commands, address ranges and the s/regexp/replacement/ substitution syntax all descend from it. Through ed, QED's design passed into ex and vi, and its regular-expression notation — later formalised in Thompson's 1968 CACM paper — underlies grep and much subsequent Unix text processing.
