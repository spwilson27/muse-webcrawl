General systems theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

General systems theory (GST) is the interdisciplinary study of systems — cohesive sets of interrelated, interdependent components — whether natural or artificial. It seeks to identify principles common to all complex entities, at all levels of nesting, and applicable to all fields of research: physical, biological, social, and technological.

The theory was founded by the Austrian biologist Ludwig von Bertalanffy, who proposed it in the 1930s and developed it after the Second World War, most fully in his book General System Theory: Foundations, Development, Applications (1968). Bertalanffy reacted against reductionism and vitalism alike, arguing for an "organismic" conception of biology in which the properties of the whole emerge from the interactions of the parts and cannot be explained by the parts alone.

Key concepts

  • Open and closed systems — an open system exchanges matter and energy with its environment; organisms are open systems maintaining a steady state (Fliessgleichgewicht) far from thermodynamic equilibrium.
  • Equifinality — in open systems, the same final state may be reached from different initial conditions and by different paths, unlike in closed physical systems.
  • Feedback — circular causal processes, positive (amplifying) or negative (stabilising, as in homeostasis), a concept developed in parallel by cybernetics.
  • Hierarchy — systems are nested within systems, from cells to organisms to ecosystems and societies, each level exhibiting emergent properties.
  • Holism and emergence — "the whole is more than the sum of its parts."

History and influence

Bertalanffy first lectured on the theory in 1937 and published on it in German during the 1940s; his 1950 paper "An Outline of General System Theory" in the British Journal for the Philosophy of Science brought it to the English-speaking world. In 1954 he co-founded the Society for General Systems Research with Kenneth Boulding, James G. Miller, and Ralph Gerard. Related movements arose simultaneously: Norbert Wiener's cybernetics, Claude Shannon's information theory, and John von Neumann's game theory and cellular automata.

General systems theory influenced family therapy, management science, ecology (notably the ecosystem ecology of Eugene and Howard Odum), and later systems engineering and systems thinking. Its legacy continues in contemporary fields such as complexity science and systems biology, though critics have argued that GST remained more a programme or worldview than a rigorous unified theory with testable laws.
