Canadian identity refers to the culture, characteristics, and shared experience that define the people of Canada and distinguish the country in the world. It has been shaped by Indigenous peoples, French and British colonial foundations, waves of immigration, the country's northern geography, and its long, often anxious relationship with the United States. Debates over what it means to be Canadian — a "perennial Canadian debate" — are themselves often cited as a defining feature of the identity.

Foundations: two nations and Indigenous peoples

Canada's identity rests on multiple founding peoples: First Nations, Inuit, and Métis peoples with millennia of history; French settlers of New France; and British loyalists who arrived after the American Revolution, bringing a counter-revolutionary, monarchical tradition. The coexistence of English and French Canada — "two founding nations" — produced institutional bilingualism (official since 1969) and recurring constitutional tensions, especially over Quebec's status as a distinct society and its sovereignty movement.

Multiculturalism

In 1971 Canada became the first country to adopt multiculturalism as official policy, later entrenched in the Multiculturalism Act (1988) and the Charter of Rights and Freedoms. Rather than a "melting pot", Canada describes itself as a "cultural mosaic", in which immigrant communities retain distinct identities within a shared citizenship — a self-image central to modern Canadian identity, alongside increasingly prominent efforts at reconciliation with Indigenous peoples over the legacy of residential schools and colonial policy.

Distinction from the United States

Canadian identity is frequently defined in contrast to the United States: public health care, parliamentary government and the Crown, gun control, peacekeeping traditions, and a self-deprecating, collectivist ethos are commonly invoked as differentiators, captured in the famous formulation of "peace, order, and good government" versus "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness". Polls consistently show Canadians rank their health-care system, the Charter, and the flag among their proudest symbols.

Regional identities and symbols

Strong regional identities — Atlantic Canada, Quebec, Ontario, the Prairies, British Columbia, and the North — coexist with, and sometimes rival, the national one. Cultural touchstones include ice hockey (the national winter sport), lacrosse (the national summer sport), Tim Hortons, CBC broadcasting, maple syrup, the beaver (official animal symbol since 1975), the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, and the northern landscape itself, summarised in the anthem's phrase "the True North strong and free".
