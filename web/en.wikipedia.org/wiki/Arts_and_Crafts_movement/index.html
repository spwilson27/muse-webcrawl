Arts and Crafts movement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A Morris & Co. textile in the 'Strawberry Thief' pattern (1883): birds and strawberries woven into dense, scrolling foliage]

The Arts and Crafts movement was an international trend in the decorative and fine arts, flourishing in Britain from about 1880 to 1920 and spreading to Europe and North America. Inspired by the writings of John Ruskin and led by William Morris, it reacted against industrial mass production, advocating traditional craftsmanship, honest materials, and the unity of design and making — 'art made by the people and for the people' — and it reshaped architecture, furniture, textiles, metalwork and book design.

Key organisations included Morris & Co., the Art Workers' Guild (1884) and the Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society (1887), whose exhibitions gave the movement its name; C. R. Ashbee's Guild of Handicraft carried the ideal to the Cotswolds.

Philosophy

Morris held that industrial capitalism had degraded both the worker and the object, separating designer from maker; his workshops revived hand weaving, natural dyeing, hand-block printing and medieval building crafts. The movement's style favoured nature-derived pattern, exposed construction and vernacular forms — the 'honest' cottage aesthetic that fed the Garden City movement and English domestic revival architecture.

International spread and legacy

In America the movement shaped Stickley's Craftsman furniture, the Roycrofters and California bungalow architecture; in Europe it fed the Wiener Werkstätte and, indirectly, the Bauhaus's craft-based foundation course. Its paradox — hand-made goods only the rich could afford — was one Morris himself lamented, but its insistence on design quality for everyday things underlies modern design education.
