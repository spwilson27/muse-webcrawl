Fractional crystallization (chemistry)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fractional crystallization, or crystal fractionation, is one of the most important geochemical and physical processes operating within crust and mantle of a rocky planetary body, such as the Earth. In chemistry more broadly, it is a method of refining substances based on differences in their solubility: a mixture of two or more substances dissolved in a solvent is allowed to crystallise out gradually, and because the substances generally have different solubilities — and crystallise at different temperatures — the composition of the crystals that form (and of the remaining liquid) changes progressively.

Principle

As a solution cools, the least soluble component reaches saturation first and begins to crystallise. If these crystals are removed (by filtration or decanting) and the process repeated, each successive crop of crystals is richer in one component than the last, so that repetition of the cycle eventually separates the mixture into nearly pure fractions. The technique is used when ordinary recrystallization is insufficient, for example when the components have very similar solubilities.

Applications

Fractional crystallization was historically important in the separation of the rare-earth elements, whose chemical similarity made them exceptionally difficult to purify; thousands of successive crystallizations were sometimes required. It remains used in the purification of chemicals and pharmaceuticals, in sugar refining, and in winterization of oils.

In geology

In igneous petrology, fractional crystallization describes the removal of early-formed crystals from a cooling magma. Because the first minerals to crystallise (such as olivine and pyroxene in Bowen's reaction series) differ in composition from the melt, their removal drives the residual liquid toward more silica-rich, evolved compositions — the process largely responsible for the diversity of igneous rocks, from basalt through andesite to rhyolite, and for the formation of layered intrusions and many ore deposits.
