Transient global amnesia (TGA) is a neurological disorder whose key defining characteristic is a temporary but almost total disruption of short-term memory with a range of problems accessing older memories. A person in a state of TGA exhibits no other signs of impaired cognitive functioning but recalls only the last few moments of consciousness, as well as potentially a few deeply encoded facts of the individual's past, such as their childhood, family, or home.

Signs and symptoms

During an episode, the person is unable to form new memories (anterograde amnesia) and has difficulty recalling events of the recent past (retrograde amnesia), while identity, language and procedural skills remain intact. A characteristic feature is repetitive questioning — the person asks the same questions, such as "How did I get here?", every few minutes, forgetting the answers given. Episodes typically last between one and ten hours, with most resolving in four to six hours, and by definition within 24 hours.

Causes and triggers

The cause of TGA remains unknown. Episodes are often preceded by precipitating events such as sudden immersion in cold or hot water, physical exertion, sexual intercourse, emotional stress, pain, or medical procedures. Hypotheses include transient dysfunction of the hippocampus (particularly the CA1 region), venous congestion, migraine-related mechanisms and, less commonly, transient ischaemia. Delayed MRI often shows small punctate lesions in the hippocampus on diffusion-weighted imaging.

Diagnosis and prognosis

Diagnosis is clinical, based on criteria such as those proposed by Hodges and Warlow: the attack must be witnessed, there must be clear anterograde amnesia, no clouding of consciousness or focal neurological signs, and resolution within 24 hours. Differential diagnoses include transient epileptic amnesia, stroke, and psychogenic amnesia. TGA has a benign prognosis: it does not increase the risk of stroke or mortality, older memories return as the episode resolves (though a permanent gap for the episode itself remains), and recurrence occurs in a minority of patients, roughly 5–25%.
