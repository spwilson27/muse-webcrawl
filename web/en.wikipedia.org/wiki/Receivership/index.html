Receivership

Receivership is a remedy available under equity and common-law systems in which a neutral third party, the receiver, is appointed to take custody and control of a person or entity's property or business. Receivers are appointed by courts, by government regulators, or in some circumstances by secured creditors under the terms of a security agreement. Receivership is used in corporate insolvency, in disputes between parties, in regulatory enforcement, and — in the United States — by agencies such as the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation when a bank fails.

In a receivership, a court- or creditor-appointed receiver takes control of an entity's assets.

Functions of a receiver

A receiver's powers depend on the appointing instrument or court order, but commonly include taking possession of assets, operating or winding down a business, collecting debts, selling property, and distributing proceeds to creditors or other entitled parties. Unlike a company's own directors, the receiver owes duties defined by the appointment and by fiduciary law, and generally acts as an officer of the court when court-appointed.

Types and jurisdictions

In the United Kingdom, administrative receivership historically allowed a floating-charge holder to appoint a receiver-manager over a company's whole undertaking; the Enterprise Act 2002 largely abolished the procedure for new charges in favour of administration, though fixed-charge (Law of Property Act) receiverships over real property remain common. In the United States, receiverships arise under state law, in federal equity proceedings, and under specific statutes — the FDIC acts as receiver for failed insured banks, while the SEC may seek receivers over entities accused of fraud. Equity receiverships of insolvent railroads were a major feature of 19th-century American finance and a precursor of modern bankruptcy reorganization.

Relation to bankruptcy

Receivership is distinct from bankruptcy: it is not necessarily a collective insolvency proceeding for all creditors, and the entity in receivership may survive. A receiver may also be appointed for limited purposes, such as preserving a disputed asset pending litigation, collecting rents, or completing a project.
