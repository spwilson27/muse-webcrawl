Musikalisches Würfelspiel

A Musikalisches Würfelspiel (German for "musical dice game") is a system for composing music using dice to randomly select pre-written measures of music. Such games were popular in Western Europe during the late 18th and early 19th centuries, marketed as a way for amateurs to compose minuets, waltzes, and other dances without knowledge of composition. They are early examples of aleatoric and generative music.

A typical dice-game table: two dice are rolled for each bar, and the sum selects a measure from the chart.

Method

A typical game provides enough pre-composed bars to assemble a complete dance — for example, 16 bars of a minuet and 16 of a trio. For each bar position, the player rolls two dice (giving outcomes 2–12) or consults a table of random numbers, and the result points to one of several numbered alternative measures printed in the collection. Because every alternative fits the same harmonic scheme, any combination yields a grammatically correct piece. With eleven alternatives for each of sixteen positions, the number of possible minuets runs into the trillions (1116 ≈ 4.6 × 1016), though surviving games differ in their exact arithmetic.

History

The earliest known example is Johann Philipp Kirnberger's Der allezeit fertige Polonoisen- und Menuettencomponist ("The Ever-Ready Minuet and Polonaise Composer", 1751, revised 1783). Maximilian Stadler and Franz Joseph Haydn also had dice games published under their names. The most famous example is the Musikalisches Würfelspiel published in Berlin in 1792 and attributed to Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart (K. 294d/516f), though the attribution to Mozart is uncertain. C. P. E. Bach and others contributed to the fashion, which faded as musical taste moved toward the idea of the composer's unique genius in the 19th century.

Legacy

Dice games are frequently cited as precursors of 20th-century chance music, including the work of John Cage, and of modern algorithmic and generative composition systems, from computer-generated music to contemporary generative audio software.
