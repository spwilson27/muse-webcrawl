Deciduous teeth

Deciduous teeth, also known as baby teeth, milk teeth, or primary teeth, are the first set of teeth in the growth and development of humans and other diphyodont mammals. They develop during the embryonic stage of development and erupt (become visible in the mouth) during infancy. They are usually lost and replaced by permanent teeth, but in the absence of their permanent replacements, they can remain functional for many years into adulthood.

Description

Humans normally have 20 deciduous teeth: ten in the upper jaw and ten in the lower jaw, consisting of incisors, canines, and molars, but no premolars. They are smaller and whiter than permanent teeth, with thinner enamel and proportionally larger pulp chambers. The deciduous dentition divides into:

Eruption and exfoliation

The first teeth typically erupt around six months of age — usually the lower central incisors — and the full set is generally present by about two and a half to three years of age. Eruption of teeth ("teething") may be accompanied by mild irritability, drooling, and a desire to chew. Children begin to lose their deciduous teeth at about age six, as the roots resorb and the permanent successors erupt; the process continues until about age twelve. In many cultures, lost baby teeth are associated with rituals involving a tooth fairy or similar figure.

Function and care

Deciduous teeth are essential for chewing, speech development, and maintaining space in the jaws for the permanent teeth. Premature loss from decay or injury can cause crowding and misalignment of the permanent dentition. Because their enamel is thinner, they are especially susceptible to tooth decay, including "baby bottle tooth decay" from prolonged exposure to sugary liquids.
