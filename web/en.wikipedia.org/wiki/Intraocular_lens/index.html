An intraocular lens (IOL) is a lens implanted in the eye, usually replacing the existing crystalline lens because it has been clouded over by a cataract, or as a form of refractive surgery to change the eye's optical power. It usually consists of a small plastic lens with plastic side struts, called haptics, to hold the lens in place in the capsular bag of the eye.

History

The first intraocular lens was implanted by Sir Harold Ridley on 29 November 1949 at St Thomas' Hospital, London, in a 45-year-old cataract patient. Ridley had observed that fragments of PMMA (Perspex) canopy from Spitfire aircraft lodged in the eyes of injured pilots during the Second World War did not provoke rejection, suggesting the material was inert in ocular tissue. His innovation was initially met with strong opposition from the ophthalmological establishment, but lens implants gradually became standard practice from the 1970s onward.

Types

Monofocal lenses provide clear vision at a single focal distance. Multifocal and accommodating lenses aim to give useful vision at several distances and reduce dependence on spectacles. Toric lenses correct corneal astigmatism. Most modern lenses are foldable acrylic or silicone designs inserted through an incision of roughly 2–3 mm during phacoemulsification. Phakic IOLs are implanted in front of the natural lens, without removing it, to correct high refractive errors such as severe myopia.

Surgery

During cataract surgery the clouded natural lens is emulsified and removed, and the IOL is placed in the emptied capsular bag. Lens power is calculated beforehand from measurements of axial length and corneal curvature (biometry). Common complications include posterior capsule opacification, which can be treated with a Nd:YAG laser capsulotomy, lens dislocation, and, rarely, endophthalmitis.
