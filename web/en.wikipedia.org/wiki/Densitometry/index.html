Densitometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Densitometry is the quantitative measurement of optical density in light-sensitive materials, such as photographic paper and film, or in semi-transparent samples. The instrument used is a densitometer, which shines light of known intensity through (or reflects it from) the sample and measures how much is transmitted or reflected; the optical density is the logarithm of the ratio of incident to transmitted light. Densitometry underlies the science of sensitometry, developed by Hurter and Driffield in the late 19th century to characterise photographic emulsions.

Applications

  • Photography and printing: densitometers measure film density, monitor photographic processing, and calibrate printing processes — checking ink density, dot gain, and colour balance in prepress and pressrooms.
  • Biochemistry and molecular biology: densitometry quantifies bands on gels and blots (for example in western blotting and gel electrophoresis), estimating the amount of protein or DNA in each band relative to standards.
  • Medicine: bone densitometry — most commonly dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA/DXA) — measures bone mineral density to diagnose osteoporosis and assess fracture risk.
  • Planetary and materials science: transmitted-light densitometry is used to analyse autoradiographs, X-ray films, and thin sections.

Types of densitometers

Transmission densitometers measure light passing through transparent material such as film; reflection densitometers measure light reflected from opaque surfaces such as photographic prints. Scanning densitometers move a small light spot across a sample — such as a chromatography plate or electrophoresis gel — to produce a profile of density versus position. Modern digital imaging software performs the same quantification from scanned images.
