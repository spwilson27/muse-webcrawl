André Bazin

Born: 18 April 1918, Angers, France
Died: 11 November 1958, Nogent-sur-Marne
Occupation: Film critic and theorist
Known for: Co-founding Cahiers du cinéma

André Bazin (1918–1958) was a French film critic and theorist, arguably the most influential figure in the history of film criticism. A co-founder in 1951 of the journal Cahiers du cinéma, he championed cinematic realism, mentored the future directors of the French New Wave, and developed theories of film that still structure film studies today.

Theory

In essays such as "The Ontology of the Photographic Image" (1945), Bazin argued that photography and cinema possess a unique realism: the photographic image is produced automatically, sharing a being with its model, and cinema adds the dimension of duration — "change mummified". Against the montage-centred aesthetics of Soviet film theory, Bazin praised directors such as Orson Welles, William Wyler and the Italian neorealists, who used deep focus and the long take to preserve the ambiguity and spatial unity of reality, leaving interpretation to the viewer.

Influence

As editor and mentor at Cahiers, Bazin guided young critics including François Truffaut — whom he effectively adopted and rescued from military prison — Jean-Luc Godard, Jacques Rivette, Éric Rohmer and Claude Chabrol, who became the core of the Nouvelle Vague. He championed directors such as Jean Renoir, Roberto Rossellini and Alfred Hitchcock. His writings were collected posthumously as Qu'est-ce que le cinéma? (What Is Cinema?). He died of leukaemia in 1958, aged forty, on the day Truffaut began shooting The 400 Blows, which is dedicated to him.
