Vergina (Greek: Βεργίνα) is a small town in northern Greece, in the regional unit of Imathia, Central Macedonia. It is famous as the site of ancient Aigai (Aegae), the first capital of the kingdom of Macedon, and for the royal tombs excavated there from 1977, one of which is widely identified as the tomb of Philip II, father of Alexander the Great.

Ancient Aigai

Aigai was the original capital of the Macedonian kingdom and remained its sacred burial ground even after the court moved to Pella. The city contained a palace — among the largest buildings of classical Greece — a theatre, sanctuaries and a necropolis of Macedonian tombs. It was at Aigai, during the wedding of his daughter Cleopatra in 336 BC, that Philip II was assassinated.

The royal tombs

In 1977 the archaeologist Manolis Andronikos excavated the Great Tumulus at Vergina and uncovered several unlooted tombs. Tomb II contained a marble sarcophagus holding a golden larnax (casket) bearing the Vergina Sun emblem and cremated remains, together with rich grave goods including a gold wreath, armour and a gilded silver diadem. Andronikos identified the tomb as that of Philip II; the attribution has been widely accepted, though some scholars have argued for Philip III Arrhidaeus. The site museum, opened in 1993, displays the tombs in situ beneath a reconstructed tumulus, alongside the celebrated finds, including painted tomb façades such as the Abduction of Persephone fresco.

Legacy

The discovery transformed knowledge of Macedonian art and burial practice. The Vergina Sun symbol from the larnax became a prominent — and politically contested — emblem in the naming dispute between Greece and North Macedonia. The archaeological site of Aigai was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1996.
