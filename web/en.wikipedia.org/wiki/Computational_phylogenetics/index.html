Computational phylogenetics

Computational phylogenetics (also phylogenetic computation) is the application of computational algorithms, methods, and programs to phylogenetic analyses, with the aim of assembling a phylogenetic tree representing the evolutionary relationships among a set of genes, species, or other taxa. Because the number of possible trees grows super-exponentially with the number of taxa, exact optimisation is generally infeasible for large datasets, and heuristic search methods are used.

Types of methods

Methods are commonly divided into distance-matrix methods (such as neighbour joining and UPGMA), which first convert aligned sequences into pairwise distances, and character-based methods, including maximum parsimony, maximum likelihood, and Bayesian inference of phylogeny. Bayesian methods use Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling to estimate posterior probabilities of trees under a model of sequence evolution.

Sequence alignment and models

Analyses typically begin from a multiple sequence alignment. Substitution models such as Jukes–Cantor, HKY, and the general time reversible (GTR) model describe how nucleotides or amino acids change over time; among-site rate variation is often modelled with a gamma distribution. Software packages such as RAxML, IQ-TREE, MrBayes, and BEAST are widely used.

Applications

Computational phylogenetics underpins studies of molecular evolution, epidemiology and phylodynamics (for example tracing viral outbreaks), conservation biology, and comparative genomics.
