McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), is a landmark U.S. Supreme Court decision that defined the scope of federal power and the relationship between the federal government and the states. Writing for a unanimous Court, Chief Justice John Marshall upheld Congress's power to charter the Second Bank of the United States and struck down Maryland's tax on the bank's notes.

Background

In 1818 Maryland imposed a tax on banknotes issued by banks not chartered by the state — in practice only the Baltimore branch of the Bank of the United States. James McCulloch, the branch's head cashier, issued notes without paying the tax. Maryland argued the Constitution was a compact of sovereign states that never granted Congress power to create a bank; the United States, argued by Daniel Webster and Attorney General William Wirt, countered that the Constitution emanated from "the people".

Holding and reasoning

Marshall resolved four questions: the Constitution derives from the people, not the states; Congress may charter a bank because the Necessary and Proper Clause grants implied powers — "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate... are constitutional"; the people intended the Constitution to endure — "a constitution intended to endure for ages to come"; and under the Supremacy Clause Maryland's tax was void, since "the power to tax involves the power to destroy" and states cannot burden the operations of the Union.

Legacy

McCulloch remains the foundational precedent for a broad reading of federal implied powers and for intergovernmental tax immunity, cited in debates from the New Deal to the Affordable Care Act case, NFIB v. Sebelius (2012).
