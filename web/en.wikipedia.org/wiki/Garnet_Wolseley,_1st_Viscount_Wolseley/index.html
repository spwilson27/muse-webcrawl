Field Marshal Garnet Joseph Wolseley, 1st Viscount Wolseley (4 June 1833 – 25 March 1913) was an Anglo-Irish officer in the British Army. He became one of the most influential and admired British generals of the Victorian era, famous for the efficiency of his expeditionary commands, and served as Commander-in-Chief of the Forces from 1895 to 1900. The phrase "all Sir Garnet", meaning that everything is in order, entered the language from his reputation.

Early career

Wolseley was born at Golden Bridge near Dublin, the son of a retired officer. Unable to afford Sandhurst, he obtained an ensign's commission without purchase in 1852. He was severely wounded in the Second Anglo-Burmese War and again in the Crimean War, where he lost an eye at the siege of Sevastopol. He served with distinction in the Indian Mutiny, including the relief of Lucknow, and in the Second Opium War in China in 1860, afterwards writing a narrative of the war and the influential Soldier's Pocket-Book (1869).

Major commands

In 1870 Wolseley commanded the Red River Expedition in Canada, a model logistical operation that reached Fort Garry (Winnipeg) without a shot fired. In 1873–74 his expedition against the Ashanti kingdom captured Kumasi in the Third Anglo-Ashanti War. After service as the first British High Commissioner of Cyprus and as commander in Natal during the aftermath of the Zulu War, he won his greatest success in 1882, defeating Urabi's forces at the Battle of Tel el-Kebir in Egypt in a surprise night attack. In 1884–85 he commanded the Nile Expedition sent to relieve General Gordon at Khartoum, which arrived two days after the city fell.

Later career

Wolseley was raised to the peerage as Baron Wolseley in 1882 and created Viscount Wolseley in 1885. He served as Adjutant-General and then Commander-in-Chief of the Forces (1895–1900), succeeding the Duke of Cambridge. A tireless reformer at the War Office alongside his rival Lord Roberts, he promoted the creation of a general staff and improved mobilisation planning, though he quarrelled with the government over army reform and retired in 1900. He died at Menton, France, in 1913 and was buried in St Paul's Cathedral.

Legacy

Wolseley's reputation for meticulous preparation made him the archetype of the "very model of a modern Major-General" — he is often cited as Gilbert's inspiration for the character in The Pirates of Penzance. The circle of able officers he patronised was known as the "Wolseley ring". His memoirs, The Story of a Soldier's Life (1903), remain a major source for Victorian military history.
