Richard III (2 October 1452 – 22 August 1485) was King of England from 1483 until his death at the Battle of Bosworth Field. He was the last king of the House of York and the last of the Plantagenet dynasty. His defeat and death at Bosworth — the last decisive battle of the Wars of the Roses — marked the end of the English Middle Ages and the beginning of the Tudor dynasty under Henry VII.

Early life

Richard was the youngest surviving son of Richard, Duke of York, and Cecily Neville. After his father's death at the Battle of Wakefield in 1460, he was raised in the household of his cousin Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick. When his brother became Edward IV in 1461, Richard was created Duke of Gloucester and became one of the king's most loyal and capable commanders, fighting at Barnet and Tewkesbury, governing the north of England, and leading the 1482 campaign into Scotland that recovered Berwick. He married Anne Neville, Warwick's younger daughter, in 1472.

Accession

On Edward IV's death in April 1483, Richard became Lord Protector for his twelve-year-old nephew, Edward V. Within weeks, the marriage of Edward IV to Elizabeth Woodville was declared invalid on grounds of a prior contract, rendering the young king and his brother illegitimate — a claim enacted by Parliament in the document known as Titulus Regius. Richard accepted the crown on 26 June 1483 and was crowned on 6 July. The two princes, the "Princes in the Tower", were last seen that summer and were never seen again; their fate remains one of history's most debated mysteries, and Richard has traditionally been blamed for their deaths.

Reign

Richard's single Parliament of 1484 abolished benevolences, reformed abuses in bail law, and passed measures favourable to English trade; he also founded the Council of the North and the College of Arms. His only legitimate son, Edward of Middleham, died in 1484, followed by Queen Anne in March 1485.

Death and rediscovery

On 22 August 1485 Richard was killed at the Battle of Bosworth Field fighting Henry Tudor; he was the last English king to die in battle. His body was buried at Greyfriars in Leicester. In 2012 his remains were excavated beneath a Leicester car park — on the site of the Greyfriars church — and identified by DNA comparison, radiocarbon dating, and evidence of scoliosis and battle wounds. He was reburied in Leicester Cathedral in March 2015.

Reputation

Tudor writers, most famously William Shakespeare in Richard III, portrayed him as a deformed, murderous tyrant. Modern historians debate how much of this was propaganda; the Richard III Society, founded in 1924, campaigns for a reassessment of his reign.
