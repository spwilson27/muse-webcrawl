Real exchange rate
DefinitionNominal exchange rate adjusted for relative price levels
FormulaRER = E × (P / P*)
VariantsBilateral; real effective exchange rate (REER)

The real exchange rate (RER) between two currencies is the nominal exchange rate adjusted for the difference in price levels between the two countries. It measures the relative price of foreign goods in terms of domestic goods — that is, the rate at which goods can actually be traded across borders — and is a key indicator of international price competitiveness. (Convention varies: the rate is often defined so that a rise denotes a real appreciation of the home currency.)

Definition

For the bilateral rate, with E the nominal exchange rate (domestic currency per unit of foreign currency), P the domestic price level and P* the foreign price level:

RER = E × (P* / P)

Under this convention a rise in the RER (a real appreciation) makes domestic goods more expensive relative to foreign goods, tending to worsen the trade balance, other things equal. The real effective exchange rate (REER) is a trade-weighted average of bilateral real rates against many partners, published as an index by institutions such as the Bank for International Settlements.

Purchasing power parity and deviations

If purchasing power parity (PPP) held exactly, the real exchange rate would be constant at unity, since nominal movements would offset price-level differences. In practice the RER varies substantially and persistently — a puzzle in international economics — because of transport costs, non-traded goods, imperfect competition and sticky prices. The Balassa–Samuelson effect predicts that countries with faster productivity growth in traded goods will experience sustained real appreciation, which is why price levels are systematically higher in richer countries (the Penn effect).

Uses

Policymakers and analysts use real exchange rates to assess currency misalignment and over- or undervaluation (as in the IMF's external balance assessments), competitiveness, and the transmission of monetary policy and terms-of-trade shocks.
