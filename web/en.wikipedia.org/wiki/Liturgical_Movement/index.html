Liturgical Movement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Benedictine monastic liturgy, cradle of the movement

The Liturgical Movement was a movement of scholarship and pastoral reform in the Roman Catholic Church, and later in other Christian churches, during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. It sought a renewal of Christian worship through a return to the sources of the early Church, a deeper study of the history of the liturgy, and the "full, conscious and active participation" of the laity in the Church's public prayer — a phrase that became central to its programme.

Origins and development

The movement is usually traced to the work of Dom Prosper Guéranger, who refounded the Benedictine abbey of Solesmes in France in 1833 and campaigned for the restoration of the Roman liturgy and Gregorian chant against local neo-Gallican uses. In the early twentieth century the focus shifted from restoration to participation: in 1903 Pope Pius X's motu proprio Tra le sollecitudini called active participation in the sacred mysteries the foremost source of the Christian spirit. Landmark moments included Dom Lambert Beauduin's address at Malines in 1909 and his founding of Mont César abbey in Belgium, and the scholarly work of German and Austrian centres such as Maria Laach and Klosterneuburg, where figures like Romano Guardini and Pius Parsch promoted the people's missal and vernacular explanation of the rites. Pope Pius XII's encyclical Mediator Dei (1947) gave the movement official encouragement and theological shape.

Outcome

The movement's principles were taken up by the Second Vatican Council, whose Constitution on the Sacred Liturgy, Sacrosanctum Concilium (1963), mandated a general reform of the Roman Rite: greater use of the vernacular, restored prayers and readings, communal celebration and simplified rites. Parallel currents in Anglican, Lutheran and other churches produced similar renewals and a notable convergence in eucharistic texts. Critics from traditionalist quarters have argued that the reforms went beyond what the movement's pioneers intended, while supporters regard them as its legitimate fruit.
