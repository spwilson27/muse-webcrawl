Coumarin

[Image: Coumarin]
FormulaC9H6O2
Molar mass146.14 g/mol
AppearanceColourless crystals
OdourSweet, like new-mown hay

Coumarin is a fragrant organic chemical compound in the benzopyrone chemical class, found in many plants, notably in high concentration in the tonka bean, sweet woodruff, and sweet clover. It has a sweet odour, readily recognised as the scent of newly mown hay, and has been used in perfumes since 1882.

History and synthesis

Coumarin was first isolated from tonka beans in 1820 by A. Vogel, and first synthesised in 1868 by William Henry Perkin via the reaction that now bears his name. It is prepared industrially from salicylaldehyde by the Perkin reaction, and by other routes from phenol.

Uses

Coumarin is used in perfumes, fabric softeners and as a precursor to anticoagulant drugs. It is the parent compound of the coumarin anticoagulants, including warfarin and dicoumarol, which forms when coumarin in spoiled sweet clover is converted by fungi. Coumarin derivatives are also used as laser dyes and fluorescent probes.

Regulation

Because of evidence of hepatotoxicity in animal studies, coumarin was banned as a food additive in the United States in 1954, and its levels in foods such as cinnamon (especially cassia cinnamon, which is rich in coumarin) are regulated in the European Union.
