Siege of Corinth

The Siege of Corinth (also known as the First Battle of Corinth) was fought from April 29 through May 30, 1862, in Corinth, Mississippi, during the American Civil War. A large Union army under Major General Henry Halleck advanced with extreme caution against the vital Confederate rail junction at Corinth, compelling the outnumbered Confederate force under General P. G. T. Beauregard to evacuate the town without a major battle.

Background

Corinth was strategically vital because two railroads — the Memphis and Charleston Railroad, the Confederacy's only east–west line, and the Mobile and Ohio Railroad — crossed there. Following the Union victory at the Battle of Shiloh in April 1862, Halleck concentrated the combined Armies of the Tennessee, the Ohio and the Mississippi, over 120,000 men, and advanced on Corinth from Pittsburg Landing, only about 22 miles away.

The siege

Rather than risk a costly assault, Halleck advanced at a crawl, ordering his men to entrench after each short advance — a methodical approach derided as "siege" operations in open country. His army took nearly a month to cover the distance. Beauregard, weakened by disease in his camps (contaminated water caused widespread sickness) and heavily outnumbered, decided to withdraw. On the night of May 29–30 he evacuated his army by rail to Tupelo, using elaborate deception — cheering as if reinforcements were arriving, dummy guns, and campfires left burning — to conceal the retreat. Union forces occupied Corinth on May 30.

Aftermath

The fall of Corinth opened the way for Union operations against Memphis and along the Mississippi River, and divided the Confederacy's rail network. Halleck's caution, however, was widely criticised, and his army was soon dispersed. The town changed hands in fighting again at the Second Battle of Corinth in October 1862, when the Confederates unsuccessfully attempted to retake it.
