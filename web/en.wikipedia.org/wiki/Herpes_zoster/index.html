Shingles

Shingles, also known as herpes zoster, is a viral disease characterised by a painful skin rash with blisters in a limited area on one side of the body, typically in a stripe following a dermatome. It results from reactivation of the varicella zoster virus (VZV), the same virus that causes chickenpox, which remains dormant in nerve ganglia after the initial infection.

Signs and symptoms

Early symptoms include tingling, localised pain, fever and headache. The rash appears two to five days later as red patches that develop into fluid-filled blisters, which dry out and crust over seven to ten days. Pain may persist long after the rash heals as postherpetic neuralgia, the most common complication, particularly in older adults. Involvement of the eye (herpes zoster ophthalmicus) can threaten vision.

Cause and risk factors

Risk increases with age as cell-mediated immunity to VZV declines; about half of cases occur in people over 60. Immunosuppression, including from HIV or chemotherapy, also raises risk. A person with shingles can transmit VZV to a susceptible contact, who would develop chickenpox rather than shingles.

Prevention and treatment

The recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix) is more than 90% effective and is recommended for adults over 50 in many countries, superseding the older live vaccine Zostavax. Early treatment with antivirals such as aciclovir can shorten the course and reduce severity; analgesics are used for pain.
