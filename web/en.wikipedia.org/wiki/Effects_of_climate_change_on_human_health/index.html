Effects of climate change on human health

The effects of climate change on human health are wide-ranging and increasingly well documented. Climate change affects health directly through heatwaves, floods, storms, and droughts, and indirectly through changes in air quality, the spread of infectious diseases, food and water security, and social and economic disruption. The World Health Organization has described climate change as one of the greatest threats to global health in the twenty-first century.

Direct effects

Rising temperatures increase the frequency and intensity of heatwaves, which cause heatstroke and excess deaths, particularly among the elderly and people with chronic illness. Extreme weather events such as floods, wildfires, and storms cause injuries, deaths, displacement, and mental health problems.

Infectious diseases

Changing patterns of temperature and rainfall alter the distribution of disease vectors such as mosquitoes and ticks, expanding the range of malaria, dengue, and Lyme disease. Flooding and water scarcity can increase the transmission of waterborne diseases such as cholera.

Food, water, and air

Climate change threatens crop yields and nutritional quality in many regions, increasing the risk of undernutrition. Air pollution, including ozone formed in hot weather and smoke from wildfires, aggravates respiratory and cardiovascular disease. Longer pollen seasons can worsen allergies and asthma.

Vulnerable populations

The health burdens of climate change fall disproportionately on people in low-income countries, children, the elderly, outdoor workers, and communities with weak health systems. Adaptation measures include heat-health action plans, early warning systems, resilient health infrastructure, and reductions in the emissions driving climate change.
