Monogenesis (linguistics)

In linguistics, monogenesis is the theory that all human languages descend from a single ancestral language. The opposite view, polygenesis, holds that language arose independently in multiple human populations. Monogenesis in a narrower sense is also used for theories that a particular class of languages — for example, pidgins and creoles — derive from a single source.

Monogenesis of language

The idea that all languages share a common origin is ancient, reflected in traditions such as the Tower of Babel, and was a commonplace of pre-modern etymology. In modern linguistics, the hypothesis connects to the proposal of a Proto-Human (or Proto-World) language spoken by early modern humans, possibly in Africa tens of thousands of years ago. Because the comparative method can reliably reconstruct languages only several thousand years deep, claims for Proto-World vocabulary — such as those of Merritt Ruhlen and other proponents of "mass comparison" — remain controversial and are not accepted by most historical linguists. The scientific question is also entangled with the single-origin model of modern human evolution ("Out of Africa"), which is consistent with, but does not prove, a single origin of language.

Monogenesis of pidgins and creoles

In creole studies, the monogenetic theory (associated with scholars such as Hugo Schuchardt and developed in the 20th century) proposed that all Atlantic creole languages derive from a single early Portuguese-based pidgin used on the West African coast from the 15th century, whose vocabulary was later relexified with words from English, French, Dutch, or Spanish while retaining its grammatical structure. Evidence cited includes shared grammatical features and nautical vocabulary across creoles. The theory competes with polygenetic and universalist accounts, such as Derek Bickerton's language bioprogram hypothesis, and with substratist explanations emphasising African language influence.
