The prefrontal cortex (PFC) is the cerebral cortex covering the front part of the frontal lobe. This brain region has been implicated in planning complex cognitive behaviour, personality expression, decision making, and moderating social behaviour. The basic activity of this brain region is considered to be the orchestration of thoughts and actions in accordance with internal goals.

The most typical psychological term for functions carried out by the prefrontal cortex is executive function: abilities that enable a person to distinguish between conflicting thoughts, determine good and bad, better and best, and future consequences of current activities, work toward a defined goal, predict outcomes, and modulate behaviour according to social expectations.

Structure

The prefrontal cortex is conventionally divided into the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (involved in working memory and executive control), the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (response inhibition and language-related processing), the orbitofrontal cortex (reward valuation and decision making), and the medial prefrontal cortex (self-referential thought, social cognition, and the default mode network). In humans the PFC is disproportionately large and is among the last cortical regions to mature, with myelination and synaptic pruning continuing into the third decade of life.

Connections

The PFC receives input from virtually all cortical sensory and motor systems and from subcortical structures including the mediodorsal nucleus of the thalamus — whose connections largely define its boundaries — the amygdala, hippocampus, and basal ganglia. Dopaminergic projections from the ventral tegmental area strongly modulate prefrontal function, following an inverted-U relationship in which both too little and too much dopamine impair working memory.

Function

Lesion and neuroimaging studies associate the PFC with working memory, cognitive flexibility, planning, inhibition, abstract reasoning, and the integration of emotion with decision making. The famous case of Phineas Gage, who survived an iron rod through his frontal lobes in 1848 with marked personality changes, remains a classic illustration of its role in social conduct.

Clinical significance

Prefrontal dysfunction is implicated in schizophrenia, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, depression, and obsessive–compulsive disorder. Bilateral damage can produce either apathy and abulia (medial lesions) or disinhibition and impulsivity (orbitofrontal lesions). The prefrontal leucotomy (lobotomy), developed in the 1930s–40s, deliberately severed prefrontal connections and was abandoned as antipsychotic medication arrived.
