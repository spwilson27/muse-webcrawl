The Third Dynasty of ancient Egypt (c. 2686 – 2648 BC) is the first dynasty of the Old Kingdom. It is best known for the reign of Pharaoh Djoser and his vizier Imhotep, who together inaugurated monumental stone architecture with the Step Pyramid at Saqqara — the earliest colossal stone building in Egypt and the ancestor of the true pyramids.

Kings

The dynasty's king list, reconstructed from the Turin Canon, later lists and contemporary monuments, usually comprises Sanakht, Djoser (Netjerikhet), Sekhemkhet, Khaba, and Huni. Djoser, the most famous, ruled some 19–29 years; his Horus name Netjerikhet ("divine of body") appears throughout his Saqqara complex. Sekhemkhet began a second step pyramid (the "Buried Pyramid") at Saqqara, and Khaba is associated with the Layer Pyramid at Zawyet el-Aryan; Huni, the last king, is credited by tradition with building projects across Egypt and is the father of Sneferu, founder of the Fourth Dynasty.

The Step Pyramid and Imhotep

Djoser's funerary complex at Saqqara, designed by his chancellor Imhotep, translated the royal tomb from mudbrick into cut stone: a mastaba enlarged in stages into a six-stepped pyramid roughly 60 metres high, set within a walled ceremonial city of chapels, courtyards and dummy temples in stone. Imhotep — healer, sage and architect — was later deified, one of the very few non-royal Egyptians so honoured. The Third Dynasty also saw the consolidation of the Memphis-centred state, an effective scribal administration, and expeditions for turquoise and copper in Sinai.
