Quetzalcoatl is a feathered-serpent deity of ancient Mesoamerican religion and mythology, worshipped in various forms from Teotihuacan (where the great Feathered Serpent Pyramid stands) through the Toltecs to the Aztecs, with Maya counterparts Kukulkan and Gucumatz. His Nahuatl name combines quetzal — the brilliant green bird and its precious feathers — and cōātl, serpent.

Myths and roles

To the Aztecs, Quetzalcoatl was god of wind (in his aspect Ehecatl, who sweeps the path for the rain), of learning, books and the priesthood, and a creator: he descended to the underworld to retrieve the bones of previous humanity, sprinkled them with his own blood, and so made the people of the present age, the Fifth Sun. Legend also told of a priest-king of Tula, Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl, who opposed human sacrifice, was tricked and exiled by Tezcatlipoca, and sailed east promising to return — a story later, controversially, connected with the Spanish arrival under Cortés.

Worship and legacy

His temples were characteristically round (to offer no corners to the wind), and he was patron of the calmecac schools where noble youths were educated. The equation of Cortés with the returning Quetzalcoatl, long repeated, is now regarded by many historians as a post-conquest construction rather than a contemporary Aztec belief. The feathered serpent remains among the most recognisable symbols of pre-Columbian Mexico, from the murals of Teotihuacan to modern Mexican iconography.
