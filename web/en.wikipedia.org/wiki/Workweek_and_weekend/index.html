The workweek and weekend are the complementary parts of the week devoted to labour and rest respectively. Days devoted to work are called weekdays or workdays; the remaining days form the weekend, which may consist of one or two days. In most of the world the workweek runs from Monday to Friday, and the weekend is Saturday and Sunday.

History

The seven-day week with a single weekly day of rest has religious roots in the Jewish Sabbath and the Christian and Muslim days of worship; the word "weekend" emerged in Britain in the late 19th century, when industrial workers in northern England campaigned for Saturday afternoon off, creating the "half-day" Saturday. In the United States, mills began closing on Saturdays from 1908 so that Jewish workers could observe the Sabbath, and in 1926 Henry Ford adopted the five-day, 40-hour week at his plants, reasoning that leisure boosted consumption. The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 institutionalised overtime beyond 40 hours, and the two-day weekend spread internationally after the Second World War.

Variations around the world

Most Muslim-majority countries long observed a Friday–Saturday weekend because of Friday congregational prayer; several have since shifted to Saturday–Sunday to align with global markets (the United Arab Emirates moved to a Saturday–Sunday weekend in 2022, with a half day on Friday for public sector workers). Israel's weekend is Friday–Saturday. Nepal traditionally keeps a one-day weekend on Saturday. In most European countries and the Americas, Saturday–Sunday prevails, with a standard week of 35–40 hours; France famously legislated a 35-hour week in 2000.

Length of the workweek

Actual weekly hours vary widely: the Netherlands has among the shortest average working weeks (around 30 hours including part-time work), while workers in parts of Asia and Latin America average considerably more than 40. Campaigns for a four-day week revived in the 2010s–2020s, with trials in Iceland, the United Kingdom, and elsewhere reporting maintained productivity and improved wellbeing.

Effects

The weekly cycle structures schooling, commerce, transport, and broadcasting. Research links clear rest days with health benefits, while "Sunday scaries" and boundary-blurring remote work have prompted laws such as France's "right to disconnect".
