3,4-Methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA), commonly known as ecstasy (in tablet form) and molly (in crystalline form), is a synthetic drug with stimulant and empathogenic (entactogenic) effects. It increases activity of the neurotransmitters serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine, largely by reversing their transporters and promoting release, producing euphoria, increased energy, emotional closeness, and altered perception.

History

MDMA was first synthesised in 1912 by Anton Köllisch at Merck and patented in 1914, though it attracted little attention for decades. Chemist Alexander Shulgin resynthesised it in the 1970s and introduced it to psychotherapists, some of whom used it as an adjunct to therapy. Its popularity as a recreational drug in dance and rave culture led the United States to place it in Schedule I in 1985; it is prohibited in most countries.

Effects and risks

Effects begin within about 30–60 minutes of oral use and last roughly 3–6 hours. Adverse effects include jaw clenching, increased heart rate and blood pressure, hyperthermia (especially dangerous in hot, crowded settings), hyponatraemia from overhydration, and a post-use low mood sometimes called a "comedown". Serious harm and death, though comparatively uncommon, are documented; chronic heavy use is associated with serotonergic neurotoxicity in animal studies.

Medical research

Clinical trials of MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), sponsored by the Multidisciplinary Association for Psychedelic Studies (MAPS/Lykos), reported positive phase 3 results, though in 2024 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration declined to approve the therapy and requested further study. In 2023, Australia became the first country to permit authorised psychiatrists to prescribe MDMA for PTSD under controlled conditions.
