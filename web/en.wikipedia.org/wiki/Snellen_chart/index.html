Snellen chart

A Snellen chart is an eye chart used by eye care professionals to measure visual acuity. Snellen charts are named after the Dutch ophthalmologist Herman Snellen, who developed the chart in 1862 as a companion instrument for his optotypes. Although many types of early vision charts had existed, Snellen standardised the letters and the concept of the fraction.

Description

The traditional chart consists of rows of capital letters (optotypes) — traditionally the letters C, D, E, F, L, N, O, P, T and Z — decreasing in size down the page. The letters are not ordinary typeface characters but are drawn on a 5×5 grid so that the whole letter subtends 5 minutes of arc, and each stroke 1 minute of arc, at its designated distance. The patient covers one eye and reads the smallest line possible from a standard distance, usually 6 metres (or 20 feet in the United States).

Snellen fraction

Visual acuity is expressed as a fraction: the numerator is the testing distance, and the denominator is the distance at which a person with normal vision could read that line. Thus "6/6" (or "20/20") means the patient reads at 6 m what a normal eye reads at 6 m. A result of 6/12 (20/40) means the patient reads at 6 m what the normal eye reads at 12 m. In the US, 20/200 or worse in the better eye after correction defines legal blindness. The fraction is not a percentage of "perfect" vision, a common misconception.

Variants and limitations

Variants include the tumbling E chart (for children and illiterate patients, who indicate which way the "legs" of the E point), Landolt C rings, picture charts, and illuminated or digital charts. The logMAR chart (ETDRS and Bailey–Lovie designs) corrects known flaws of the Snellen chart — uneven numbers of letters per line, irregular size progression and non-standard spacing — and is preferred in research.
