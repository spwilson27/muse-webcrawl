Standardization

Standardization is the process of developing and implementing technical standards based on the consensus of different parties that include firms, users, interest groups, standards organizations, and governments. Standardization can help maximize compatibility, interoperability, safety, repeatability, efficiency, and quality. It can also facilitate the commoditization of formerly custom processes.

History

Early examples include standardized weights and measures in ancient civilizations, uniform coinage, and the gauge standards of early railways. Modern industrial standardization grew with interchangeable parts in manufacturing and with electrical engineering in the late 19th century. The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) was founded in 1947; other bodies include the IEC, the ITU, and national bodies such as ANSI and BSI.

Types

Standards may be de facto (arising from market practice, such as the QWERTY keyboard) or de jure (formalized by a standards organization). Open standards are publicly available and developed by consensus; the standardization process in computing also involves bodies like the W3C and the IETF.

Benefits and criticism

Standardization reduces costs and barriers to trade and enables mass production, but critics note it can entrench inferior technologies (lock-in) and stifle innovation when standards become rigid.
