Giacomo Meyerbeer (born Jacob Liebmann Beer; 5 September 1791 – 2 May 1864) was a German opera composer, the most frequently performed operatic composer during the nineteenth century and the central figure of French grand opera. Working primarily in Paris, he fused German orchestral technique, Italian vocal melody and French theatrical spectacle on an unprecedented scale.

Career

Born into a wealthy Berlin Jewish banking family, Meyerbeer was a piano prodigy who studied in Italy, where his early Italian operas culminated in Il crociato in Egitto (1824). Moving to Paris, he collaborated with librettist Eugène Scribe to create grand opera: Robert le diable (1831) was a sensation, followed by Les Huguenots (1836), one of the century's greatest successes, Le prophète (1849) and the posthumously premiered L'Africaine (1865). From 1842 he also served as Prussian Generalmusikdirektor in Berlin.

Reputation

Meyerbeer's spectacular stage effects and historical dramas dominated European opera for decades and profoundly influenced Verdi and Wagner — the latter of whom later attacked him bitterly in an infamous antisemitic essay. Partly under the effect of such criticism and changing taste, his reputation collapsed in the 20th century, and his operas became rarities, though they have seen increasing scholarly attention and revivals.
