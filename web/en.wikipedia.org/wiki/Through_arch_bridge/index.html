Through arch bridge

The Sydney Harbour Bridge (1932), the classic steel through arch.

A through arch bridge is an arch bridge in which the deck does not rest on top of the arch, nor pass entirely beneath it, but runs through the arch: the arch ribs rise above the deck, and the road or railway is carried between them, suspended from the ribs by hangers (in the upper portion) and supported by columns where the arch descends below deck level near the springings. The type combines the visual drama of a tall arch with a level, unobstructed roadway passing between the ribs.

Structural action

Loads on the deck are transferred partly upward through vertical hangers into the arch ribs, which carry them in compression to the abutments. Where the outward thrust of the arch is resisted by massive foundations, the bridge is a "true" through arch; where the thrust is instead taken by tension ties in the deck itself, the structure is a tied-arch (or bowstring) bridge, a closely related arrangement that allows the bridge to be built where foundations cannot resist large horizontal forces. Many twentieth-century steel bridges of very long span use the through arch because the rib above deck stiffens the structure against wind and buckling while giving a clear span below for navigation.

Notable examples

  • Sydney Harbour Bridge, Australia (1932) — steel through arch of 503 m span; at completion the longest steel arch span in the world, nicknamed "the Coathanger".
  • Hell Gate Bridge, New York City (1916) — steel through arch of 298 m, the longest of its type when opened, and the model in spirit for Sydney's design.
  • Bayonne Bridge, between New Jersey and Staten Island (1931) — briefly the longest arch span in the world.
  • Tyne Bridge, Newcastle upon Tyne, England (1928) — steel through arch of 161 m.
  • New River Gorge Bridge, West Virginia (1977) — a long-span steel arch of 518 m, although of deck-arch configuration it is often grouped in discussions of great steel arches.
  • Lupu Bridge and Chaotianmen Bridge, China — modern through arch bridges among the longest arch spans ever built.

Comparison with other arch forms

In a deck arch bridge the deck sits wholly above the arch on columns (spandrel construction), and the arch stays below deck level. In a half-through arch, the deck passes part way up the rib. The through arch, by carrying traffic between two ribs, needs the deck to be suspended wherever the rib is overhead, which demands careful detailing of the hangers and their connections, but it allows the maximum clearance beneath the centre of the span — an essential quality for harbour and river crossings.
