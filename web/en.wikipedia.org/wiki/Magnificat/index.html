Magnificat

TextLuke 1:46–55
SpeakerMary, mother of Jesus
OccasionThe Visitation to Elizabeth
Liturgical useVespers / Evening Prayer; Anglican Evensong

The Magnificat (Latin for "[My soul] magnifies") is a canticle from the Gospel of Luke (1:46–55), spoken by Mary during her visit to her cousin Elizabeth shortly after the Annunciation. Also known as the Song of Mary and, in the Byzantine tradition, the Ode of the Theotokos, it is one of the oldest Christian hymns and has been part of daily worship since the early Church.

"My soul doth magnify the Lord, and my spirit hath rejoiced in God my Saviour. For he hath regarded the low estate of his handmaiden: for, behold, from henceforth all generations shall call me blessed."

Content and themes

The canticle is a song of praise modelled closely on the Song of Hannah (1 Samuel 2) and saturated with echoes of the Psalms and prophets. Mary rejoices in God's favour to the lowly and proclaims a dramatic reversal of worldly order: the proud are scattered, rulers are brought down from their thrones, the humble are exalted, the hungry are filled with good things and the rich sent away empty. These verses have made the Magnificat a favourite text of liberation theology and, at times, a politically charged one — it has reportedly been restricted or suppressed by authorities in various eras, including during the British Raj in India and by military regimes in Latin America.

Musical settings

The Magnificat is sung daily at Vespers in the Roman Catholic Church and at Evensong in the Anglican tradition, usually in Latin or in translation (in the Book of Common Prayer, "My soul doth magnify the Lord"). Composers have set it in every era: notable settings include those by Monteverdi (in the 1610 Vespers), Vivaldi, J. S. Bach (the Magnificat in D major, BWV 243), and in the 20th century by Vaughan Williams, Rutter and Arvo Pärt. In Orthodox worship, the text is woven into Matins, interlaced with the refrain "More honourable than the Cherubim…".
