Multistage rocket

A multistage rocket (or step rocket) is a launch vehicle that uses two or more rocket stages, each of which contains its own engines and propellant. A stage is mounted on top of another stage (tandem or serial staging) or attached alongside it (parallel staging). As each stage exhausts its propellant it is jettisoned, so that the remaining stages no longer have to accelerate the empty tanks and engines of the spent stage.

Rationale

The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation shows that the velocity change a rocket can achieve depends on its mass ratio. Because a single stage must carry its empty structure all the way to orbit, reaching orbital velocity with one stage is extremely difficult. Discarding empty structure in stages dramatically improves the total achievable delta-v, and in practice all orbital launch vehicles use staging. The idea was developed theoretically by Konstantin Tsiolkovsky and implemented by pioneers such as Robert Goddard and Hermann Oberth.

Examples

The Saturn V that carried Apollo astronauts to the Moon had three stages. The Soyuz family uses parallel strap-on boosters as a first stage. Modern vehicles such as Falcon 9 use two stages, the first of which can be recovered and reused. Upper stages are optimised for vacuum operation, while first stages are optimised for atmospheric flight and high thrust.

Staging events

Stage separation is a critical flight event, accomplished with pyrotechnic devices, springs or small ullage motors that ensure the spent stage clears safely. Hot staging, in which the next stage's engines ignite before separation, has been used on Russian vehicles and on SpaceX's Starship.
