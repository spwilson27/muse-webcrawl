Ricercar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A ricercar (also spelled ricercare, from Italian ricercare, "to seek out") is a type of late Renaissance and mostly early Baroque instrumental composition. The term was used for two rather different kinds of piece: an improvisatory, prelude-like work, and a serious contrapuntal composition in imitative counterpoint that is an important precursor of the fugue.

The two types

In its earliest usage, in lute and keyboard music of the early 16th century, a ricercar was a short, improvisatory prelude that explored ("sought out") the mode or the tuning of the instrument, often through chords and scale passages. Examples appear in the lute books of Francesco Spinacino (1507) and in the works of Marco Antonio Cavazzoni.

By the middle of the 16th century, the term increasingly denoted a motet-like piece in strict imitation, in which a theme is developed through successive points of imitation. Such ricercars were written for organ and for instrumental ensembles by composers including Adrian Willaert, Girolamo Cavazzoni, Andrea Gabrieli and Claudio Merulo. These imitative ricercars, together with the fantasia and canzona, fed directly into the development of the Baroque fugue.

Later usage

Johann Sebastian Bach used the term deliberately in its older contrapuntal sense in The Musical Offering (1747), whose two ricercars on Frederick the Great's theme are in fact learned fugues; the title's letters were also read as an acrostic, Regis Iussu Cantio Et Reliqua Canonica Arte Resoluta ("the theme given by the king's command, with the rest resolved according to canonic art"). In the 20th century, Igor Stravinsky used the word (Latinised) for a movement of his cantata in honour of the earlier tradition.
