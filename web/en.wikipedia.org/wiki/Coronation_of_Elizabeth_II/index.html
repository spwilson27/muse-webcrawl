Coronation of Elizabeth II

Coronation of Elizabeth II
Date2 June 1953
LocationWestminster Abbey, London, United Kingdom
ParticipantsElizabeth II; Geoffrey Fisher, Archbishop of Canterbury; over 8,000 guests

The coronation of Elizabeth II as queen of the United Kingdom and the other Commonwealth realms took place on 2 June 1953 at Westminster Abbey in London. Elizabeth acceded to the throne at the age of 25 upon the death of her father, George VI, on 6 February 1952, and was proclaimed queen by her various privy and executive councils shortly afterwards. The coronation was held more than a year later because of the tradition of allowing an appropriate length of time to pass after a monarch dies; it also gave the planning committees adequate time to make preparations.

Ceremony

During the service, conducted by Geoffrey Fisher, Archbishop of Canterbury, Elizabeth took an oath, was anointed with holy oil, was invested with robes and regalia, and was crowned St Edward's Crown by the archbishop. The anointing — the most sacred part of the service — was not televised. She processed to the Abbey in the Gold State Coach, wore the George IV State Diadem, and changed into the Robe of Estate over a coronation dress designed by Norman Hartnell and embroidered with the floral emblems of the Commonwealth realms. Over 8,000 guests attended, and processions and services were held throughout the Commonwealth.

Broadcast and celebrations

The coronation was the first British coronation to be fully televised; cameras had not been allowed inside the Abbey for her father's coronation in 1937. Against the advice of Winston Churchill and the Cabinet, Elizabeth insisted on television coverage, and the event became a landmark in broadcasting history, watched by an estimated 277 million people worldwide and spurring television sales in Britain. A public holiday was declared, street parties were held across the country, and news that Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay had summited Mount Everest reached London on coronation morning, widely taken as a good omen for the new reign.
