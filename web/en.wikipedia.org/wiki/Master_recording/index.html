Master recording

A master recording is the original recording of a musical performance — historically recorded on magnetic tape, and in modern times stored as a digital audio file — from which all commercial copies are produced. The holder of the master therefore controls reproduction and distribution of that recording.

Ownership and rights

Music recordings are subject to two distinct copyrights: the copyright in the underlying song (the composition, usually owned by the songwriter or publisher) and the copyright in the sound recording itself (the master). Under traditional recording contracts, record labels typically acquired ownership of the masters in exchange for funding the recording and promotion of an artist's work. This arrangement has been a longstanding source of dispute in the music industry, as ownership of the master entitles its holder to royalty income from sales and streaming.

Notable disputes

The issue gained wide public attention during Taylor Swift's dispute with Big Machine Records over ownership of the masters of her first six albums, including their 2019 sale to Scooter Braun's Ithaca Holdings — a deal that prompted Swift to re-record the albums, releasing "Taylor's Version" editions. Earlier disputes include Prince's battles with Warner Bros. Records over control of his catalogue, and George Michael's 1992 lawsuit against Sony Music.

Remastering

Historically, the master referred to the most complete version of a release (the original stereo master tape) from which copies were made; its format could limit later reissues. Contemporary reissues are often "remastered" from the original recording using modern mixing and equalisation technology, and the term "digital remaster" became a marketing staple during the CD era.
