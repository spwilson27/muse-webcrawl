Chemically peculiar star

In astrophysics, a chemically peculiar star (CP star) is a hot main-sequence star of spectral type A or late B whose spectrum shows abnormal abundances of certain elements — overabundances of silicon, chromium, strontium, europium and other heavy elements in its surface layers, often alongside underabundances of calcium and scandium.

Classes

Chemically peculiar stars are usually divided into four main classes: metallic-line (Am) stars, magnetic Ap stars (subdivided into silicon and strontium–chromium–europium types), mercury–manganese (HgMn) stars, and helium-weak stars. The peculiarities are generally attributed not to the star's overall composition but to vertical stratification and diffusion of elements in a stable atmosphere, often stabilised by a strong magnetic field. Many Ap stars are also slow rotators and show periodic spectral and light variations described by the oblique rotator model.

History

The unusual spectra of these stars were noted as early as 1897 by Antonia Maury in her classification work at Harvard. The field was systematised in the mid-20th century, and Preston's 1974 review established the modern classification.
