Prehistoric music

Prehistoric music (previously called primitive music) is a term in the history of music for all music produced in preliterate (prehistoric) cultures, beginning somewhere in very late geological history. Prehistoric music is followed by ancient music in different parts of the world, but still exists in some isolated areas. However, it is more commonly referred to as folk, indigenous or traditional music. Prehistoric music is studied in the fields of music archaeology and ethnomusicology.

The origin of music is bound up with the origin of language and vocal behaviour. Since music leaves almost no material trace, evidence for it comes from the earliest surviving instruments and from inferences drawn from the acoustic and social behaviour of other primates and of contemporary hunter-gatherer peoples.

Origins

Charles Darwin proposed that music, like bird song, evolved through sexual selection as a courtship display, while others have argued it developed as a means of social bonding, group cohesion, and infant care (in the form of lullaby). Singing and rhythmic group activity would have required neither instruments nor notation, and most scholars assume vocal music and percussion — hand-clapping, sticks, stones — long preceded crafted instruments.

Archaeological evidence

Bone flutes

The oldest widely accepted musical instruments are bone and ivory flutes from the Swabian Jura caves of southwestern Germany — Geissenklösterle, Hohle Fels, and Vogelherd — dated to roughly 42,000–40,000 years ago, in the early Upper Paleolithic. The Hohle Fels flute, carved from a griffon vulture's wing bone, has five finger holes and, in reconstruction, can produce a range of notes consistent with familiar scales. A still older claimed instrument, the Divje Babe flute from Slovenia (c. 43,000 years ago), is attributed by some researchers to Neanderthals, though others argue its holes were made by carnivore teeth.

Other instruments

Other early sound-producers include bullroarers, rasps, and lithophones — resonant stones such as those found in Vietnam and India. Whistles made from reindeer toe bones are known from Aurignacian sites. By the Neolithic and Bronze Age, instruments multiplied: the Chinese Jiahu bone flutes (c. 7000–6600 BC) were still playable when excavated, and drums, lyres, and ritual bells appear in early farming societies.

Interpretation

Because the instruments that survive are a biased sample — bone and stone endure, while wood, skin drums, and the human voice do not — the true antiquity of music is certainly greater than the archaeological record shows. Rock-art depictions of dancers and apparent musicians, and the resonant acoustic properties of some painted caves, suggest music accompanied ritual life deep in the Stone Age.
