Hunger artist

A hunger artist or starvation artist is a person who fasts for long periods as a public performance or spectacle. Hunger artists were a popular form of entertainment in Europe and North America from the late 19th to the early 20th century, performing extended fasts — commonly of 40 days or more — in cages, glass cases or theatre venues while audiences paid to observe them.

History

The phenomenon is usually traced to performers such as Henry S. Tanner, who fasted 40 days in New York in 1880 under medical observation, and Giovanni Succi, who undertook a series of celebrated fasts across Europe in the 1880s and 1890s. Impresarios promoted the artists, sold refreshments to watching crowds, and employed watchers to guard against secret eating. Some performers were exposed as frauds; others suffered lasting damage to their health. The fashion faded with the First World War and the rise of cinema.

In literature

The hunger artist is best remembered through Franz Kafka's short story "A Hunger Artist" (Ein Hungerkünstler, 1922), in which an ageing fasting performer, his art no longer understood or appreciated, wastes away in a circus cage and confesses on his deathbed that he fasted only because he never found food he liked. The story is widely read as a parable of art, asceticism and neglect.
