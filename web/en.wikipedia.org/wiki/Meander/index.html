Meander

A meander is one of a series of regular sinuous curves in the channel of a river or other watercourse. It is produced as a watercourse erodes the sediments that make up an outer, concave bank (the cut bank) and deposits sediments on an inner, convex bank, the point bar. The zone within which a meandering stream periodically shifts its channel is known as a meander belt.

Formation

Once a channel begins to curve, flow is thrown toward the outer bank, where velocity and erosion are greatest, while slower water on the inside deposits sediment. The bend therefore grows and migrates both outward and downstream across the floodplain. The term derives from the winding Maeander River (now the Büyük Menderes) of southwestern Turkey, famed in antiquity for its tortuous course.

Cutoffs and oxbow lakes

Continued migration can bring two bends together: in a neck cutoff the stream breaks through the narrow neck between bends, and in a chute cutoff it carves a new channel across a point bar. The abandoned bend, isolated when sediment seals its ends, becomes an oxbow lake, which slowly silts into a meander scar. On valley sides, entrenched and incised meanders record a history of uplift or falling base level, as in the goosenecks of the San Juan River in Utah.

Geometry

Meander geometry is strikingly regular: meander wavelength is typically about ten to fourteen times the channel width, and the radius of curvature of a bend is usually two to three times the width. Meander patterns occur not only in rivers but in ocean currents such as the Gulf Stream, in supraglacial streams on ice, and in the trails of some burrowing organisms preserved as trace fossils.
