Japan–Korea Treaty of 1876

The Japan–Korea Treaty of 1876 (also known as the Treaty of Ganghwa Island or Treaty of Kanghwa) was made between representatives of the Empire of Japan and the Kingdom of Joseon (Korea) in 1876. Concluded after the Ganghwa Island incident, it was an unequal treaty that opened Korea to Japanese trade and recognised Korea as independent of Qing China's tributary system.

Treaty of Ganghwa
Signed: 26 February 1876, Ganghwa Island
Parties: Empire of Japan; Joseon (Korea)
Type: Unequal treaty, trade and amity

Background

Korea under the regency of the Heungseon Daewongun pursued isolation, repelling French (1866) and American (1871) expeditions. Meiji Japan, itself opened by the United States and party to unequal treaties with the West, debated a punitive expedition (seikanron) in 1873. In 1875 the Japanese warship Un'yō was fired on by Korean batteries at Ganghwa Island while surveying coastal waters — the Ganghwa Island incident — giving Japan a pretext to demand a treaty.

Terms

Negotiated by Kuroda Kiyotaka with a fleet at Ganghwa, the treaty declared Korea an independent state enjoying the same sovereign rights as Japan (Article 1, aimed at loosening Qing suzerainty), opened Busan, and later Wonsan and Incheon, to Japanese trade, permitted Japanese coastal surveying, and granted Japanese residents extraterritoriality and duty-free trade provisions.

Consequences

The treaty began Korea's forced opening — followed by treaties with the United States (1882) and European powers — and intensified rivalry among Japan, Qing China and Russia over Korea, leading through the First Sino-Japanese War and Russo-Japanese War to annexation in 1910. In South Korea it is remembered as the start of Japanese encroachment. See Joseon, Meiji Restoration.
