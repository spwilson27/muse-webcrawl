Brent Crude

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Type: Sweet light crude oil
Origin: North Sea
Benchmark: ICE Brent futures

Brent Crude may refer to one of four crude oil streams produced in the North Sea, or to the futures contract based on these streams. It is one of the major classifications of crude oil and serves as a benchmark price for purchases of oil worldwide, alongside West Texas Intermediate (WTI) and Dubai Crude.

The name "Brent" comes from the Brent oilfield in the East Shetland Basin, discovered by Royal Dutch Shell in 1971 and named, following Shell's practice of naming fields after birds, after the brent goose. Production from the field began in 1976. The original Brent field has largely been decommissioned, and the benchmark blend—known as Brent Blend or London Brent—now combines crude from several North Sea fields, including Forties, Oseberg, Ekofisk, Troll and, since 2022, WTI Midland from the United States.

Characteristics

Brent is described as a light and sweet crude: it has an API gravity of around 38–39 degrees and a sulphur content of roughly 0.37–0.45 percent. These properties make it relatively easy to refine into petrol (gasoline) and middle distillates such as diesel and jet fuel.

Trading

Brent futures are traded on the Intercontinental Exchange (ICE) in London. Because about two-thirds of the world's internationally traded crude supplies are priced relative to Brent, it is often called the world's most important oil benchmark. The dated Brent assessment, published by price reporting agencies, is used to price physical cargoes.

Price history

Brent prices rose above US$140 per barrel in mid-2008 before collapsing during the financial crisis. In April 2020, pandemic-driven demand destruction briefly pushed Brent below $20, while in 2022 prices spiked toward $130 following Russia's invasion of Ukraine.
