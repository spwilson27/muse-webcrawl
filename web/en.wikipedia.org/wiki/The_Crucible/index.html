The Crucible

The Crucible is a 1953 play by the American playwright Arthur Miller. A fictionalised account of the Salem witch trials of 1692–93 in colonial Massachusetts, it was written as an allegory of McCarthyism — the anti-communist investigations then sweeping the United States — and has become one of the most performed and studied American plays. It won the Tony Award for Best Play.

Plot

In Salem, a group of girls led by Abigail Williams is caught dancing in the woods with the enslaved Tituba and, to escape punishment, begins accusing townspeople of witchcraft. Abigail, who has had an affair with the farmer John Proctor, accuses Proctor's wife Elizabeth so she can take her place. The court, presided over by the inflexible Deputy Governor Danforth, accepts the girls' "spectral evidence"; hysteria spreads, fuelled by old grudges and land disputes, and the accused can save themselves only by confessing and naming others. Proctor confesses his adultery to discredit Abigail, but Elizabeth, questioned separately, lies to protect his name, dooming him. In the final act Proctor signs a false confession, then tears it up, choosing execution over the loss of his integrity: "Because it is my name!"

Historical basis and licence

Miller researched the trial records at Salem but freely altered the material: he aged Abigail Williams from about 11 to 17, invented her affair with Proctor (who was in his sixties), combined several judges into Danforth, and reduced the number of accusers. He considered such changes justified to reveal the essential truth of the events. Miller wrote the play as the House Un-American Activities Committee and Senator Joseph McCarthy were compelling testimony about alleged communists; Miller himself was called before HUAC in 1956 and convicted of contempt for refusing to name others (the conviction was overturned on appeal).

Reception and legacy

The Broadway premiere received mixed reviews amid the charged political climate, but the play's reputation grew steadily; it is now a staple of school curricula and stages worldwide, frequently revived at moments of political persecution or moral panic. Adaptations include the 1996 film with a screenplay by Miller, starring Daniel Day-Lewis and Winona Ryder, and Robert Ward's Pulitzer Prize-winning opera (1961). The phrase "witch hunt" owes much of its modern political currency to the play's allegory.
