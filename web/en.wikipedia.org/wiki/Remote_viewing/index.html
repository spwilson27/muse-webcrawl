Remote viewing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A viewer's sketch of a target

Remote viewing (RV) is the practice of seeking impressions about a distant or unseen target, purportedly using extrasensory perception (ESP) or "sensing" with the mind. Remote viewing experiments have historically been criticized for lack of proper controls and repeatability, and there is no scientific evidence that remote viewing exists. The practice is generally regarded as pseudoscience.

History

Remote viewing was popularized in the 1990s upon the declassification of certain documents related to the Stargate Project, a US$20 million research program sponsored by the U.S. government from 1975 to 1995 to determine whether psychics could be used for military intelligence. Earlier research was conducted at the Stanford Research Institute (SRI) in the 1970s by physicists Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff with subjects including Ingo Swann and Pat Price.

Scientific reception

A 1995 evaluation commissioned by the CIA, conducted by the American Institutes for Research, concluded that remote viewing had not produced actionable intelligence and the program was terminated. Psychologists such as Ray Hyman have pointed to methodological flaws including sensory leakage and subjective validation. Meta-analyses claiming positive results remain contested, and controlled studies with adequate blinding have failed to demonstrate the phenomenon.

In popular culture

Remote viewing has been the subject of numerous books, the 2004 comic film Suspect Zero, and the 2009 film The Men Who Stare at Goats, adapted from Jon Ronson's book about the U.S. military's psychic research programs.
