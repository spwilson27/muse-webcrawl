Annual percentage rate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The term annual percentage rate of charge (APR), corresponding sometimes to a nominal APR and sometimes to an effective APR (EAPR), is the interest rate for a whole year (annualized), rather than just a monthly fee/rate, as applied on a loan, mortgage loan, credit card, etc. It is a finance charge expressed as an annual rate. In many countries and jurisdictions, lenders are required by law to disclose the "cost" of borrowing in some standardized way, so that consumers can compare borrowing costs.

Definitions

The nominal APR is the simple-interest rate for a year: for example, a monthly rate of 1% corresponds to a nominal APR of 12%. The effective APR, or EAR, compounds the periodic rate over the year, so the same loan has an effective APR of about 12.68%. In the United States, the calculation and disclosure of APR is governed by the Truth in Lending Act (implemented by Regulation Z), which requires APR to include certain fees and additional costs associated with the transaction.

Use in comparison

APR is intended to make it easier for borrowers to compare loans with different fee structures: a loan with a low headline rate but high upfront fees may have a higher APR than a fee-free loan with a higher nominal rate. However, APR has limitations — for adjustable-rate mortgages the disclosed APR assumes no rate changes, and for closed-ended loans the figure spreads upfront fees over the full term, understating their effect if the loan is repaid early.

APR and the European Union

In the EU, consumer credit directives define the annual percentage rate of charge to include the total cost of credit to the consumer, expressed as an annual percentage of the total amount of credit, and require it in advertising and contracts.
