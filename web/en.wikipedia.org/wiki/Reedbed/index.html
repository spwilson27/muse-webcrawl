Reedbed

Reedbed
Habitat type: Wetland
Dominant plant: Common reed (Phragmites australis)
Key species: Eurasian bittern, reed warbler, bearded reedling, marsh harrier

A reedbed is a natural habitat found in wetlands — floodplains, river edges, lake margins, and estuaries — dominated by stands of reed, most commonly the common reed Phragmites australis, sometimes with reedmace (bulrush), sedges, and reed sweet-grass. Young and old stems stand through the year, growing in shallow water or waterlogged soil.

Ecology

Reedbeds are among the most important habitats for birds in Europe. Specialist species include the Eurasian bittern, which booms from within dense reed; the marsh harrier, which nests on platforms of reed; the bearded reedling (bearded tit); and summer visitors such as the reed warbler and sedge warbler. The beds also shelter invertebrates, fish fry, harvest mice, and otters, and they filter silt and nutrients from water passing through them.

Management and decline

Left unmanaged, reedbeds dry out and succeed to scrub and woodland through the accumulation of litter; traditional management by rotational cutting or grazing — historically for thatching reed — kept them open. Drainage for agriculture destroyed most reedbeds in lowland Europe, making surviving and newly created beds a conservation priority; in Britain, sites such as RSPB Minsmere and Leighton Moss are managed expressly for bitterns.

Reedbed treatment systems

Engineered reedbeds (constructed wetlands) imitate the natural filtering action: sewage or industrial effluent percolates slowly through a shallow bed of reeds planted in gravel, where microbial communities on roots and substrate break down pollutants. They are widely used for small communities and for polishing mine and road runoff.
