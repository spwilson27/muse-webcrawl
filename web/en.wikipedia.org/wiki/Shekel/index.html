Shekel

A shekel or sheqel (Akkadian: 𒂖, romanized: šiqlu or siqlu; Hebrew: שֶׁקֶל, sheqel, plural Hebrew: שקלים, romanized: sheqalim) is an ancient Mesopotamian coin and unit of weight used in trade and commerce, and later adopted in ancient Israel and Judah. In the ancient Near East, the shekel began as a unit of weight — roughly 9–17 grams depending on region and period — before becoming a coin struck to a standard weight of precious metal. The word derives from a Semitic root meaning "to weigh."

A silver shekel of Tyre, the type often identified with the "thirty pieces of silver" of the New Testament.

Unit of weight

In Mesopotamia the shekel was one sixtieth of a mina, and sixty minas made a talent. Barley was an early standard, and the weight of a shekel was defined by a number of grains. Regional standards varied: the Babylonian shekel was about 8.3 g, the Phoenician about 14.2 g, and the Hebrew common shekel around 11.4 g. The Hebrew Bible repeatedly specifies "the shekel of the sanctuary," a standard used for temple dues and valuations; the half-shekel tax supported the Tabernacle and later the Temple.

Coinage

From the 5th century BCE, coins denominated in shekels were struck in the Levant. The most famous are the silver tetradrachms ("shekels") of Tyre, struck from about 126 BCE to 65 CE, which, because of their purity, were required for payment of the Temple tax in Jerusalem; money changers in the Temple exchanged other currency for them. During the First Jewish–Roman War (66–70 CE) and the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135 CE), Jewish authorities struck silver shekels bearing Hebrew inscriptions such as "Shekel of Israel" and images of the Temple facade and ritual objects.

Modern use

The name was revived for the currency of the modern State of Israel: the (old) shekel, introduced in 1980, and the new shekel (NIS, symbol ₪) introduced in 1985, which remains Israel's currency. "Shekels" is also slang in several languages for money in general.
