The Umayyad conquest of Hispania, also known as the Muslim conquest of the Iberian Peninsula, was the invasion and subjugation of the Visigothic Kingdom of Hispania by the Umayyad Caliphate between 711 and 718. It established Muslim rule over most of the peninsula, known in Arabic as al-Andalus, which would last in some form for nearly eight centuries.

Background

The Visigothic kingdom was weakened by succession disputes: King Roderic's accession was contested by the partisans of the previous dynasty, and factional strife left the kingdom exposed when a Muslim expedition crossed from North Africa. Muslim forces under Musa ibn Nusayr, governor of Ifriqiya, had consolidated control of the Maghreb by about 709.

The invasion of 711

In April 711, Tariq ibn Ziyad landed near the rock that still bears his name — Jabal Tariq, Gibraltar — with a force composed mainly of Berbers, perhaps 7,000–12,000 strong. On 19 July 711 his army destroyed Roderic's army at the Battle of Guadalete, where Roderic was killed. Tariq then advanced rapidly, taking Córdoba, Málaga, Granada, and the Visigothic capital Toledo, while Musa followed with reinforcements and captured Seville and Mérida.

Consolidation

By 718 most of the peninsula was under Muslim control, governed from Córdoba. Local elites were often left in place under treaties, such as the Treaty of Tudmir (713) in the southeast. Raids continued north of the Pyrenees into Frankish territory, culminating in the defeat at the Battle of Tours (732). The far north of Iberia, where Pelayo's victory at Covadonga (c. 722) founded the Kingdom of Asturias, remained outside Muslim control.

Aftermath

The conquered territory became the province of al-Andalus, first of the Umayyad Caliphate and after 756 an independent emirate under Abd al-Rahman I. The conquest began nearly 800 years of Muslim political presence in Iberia and inaugurated a civilization notable for urban prosperity and interfaith interaction, ending only with the Christian Reconquista and the fall of Granada in 1492.
