Thomas Chrowder Chamberlin

Thomas Chrowder Chamberlin (September 25, 1843 – November 15, 1928) was an American geologist and educator. He is best known for the planetesimal hypothesis of the origin of the Solar System, developed with astronomer Forest Ray Moulton, and for his method of multiple working hypotheses.

Thomas Chrowder Chamberlin, American geologist.

Early life and education

Chamberlin was born in Mattoon, Illinois, and grew up in Wisconsin. He graduated from Beloit College in 1866 and undertook graduate study at the University of Michigan. After teaching for a time at Beloit, he served as Wisconsin's state geologist (1873–1882), directing the geological survey of the state.

Career

Chamberlin taught geology at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, where he became president (1887–1892). He then moved to the newly founded University of Chicago, where he headed the geology department and founded the Journal of Geology in 1893. He also directed the glacier division of the U.S. Geological Survey and made influential studies of continental glaciation, including the classification of the ice ages.

Contributions

In 1890 Chamberlin published "The Method of Multiple Working Hypotheses," an influential essay on scientific reasoning that argued against attachment to a single ruling theory. Around 1900, working with Moulton, he proposed that the planets formed by accretion of small solid bodies — planetesimals — drawn from material ejected from the Sun by a passing star. The Chamberlin–Moulton planetesimal hypothesis dominated cosmogony for several decades and its accretion concept survives in modern planetary science. Chamberlin also questioned Lord Kelvin's young age for the Earth and argued early for the role of atmospheric carbon dioxide in climate change.

Honours

Chamberlin received the Penrose Medal of the Geological Society of America in 1927. Craters on the Moon and Mars are named for him.
