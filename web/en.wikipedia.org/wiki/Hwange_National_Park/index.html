Hwange National Park

LocationMatabeleland North, Zimbabwe
Area14,651 km² (5,657 sq mi)
Established1928 (as Wankie Game Reserve)
Governing bodyZimbabwe Parks and Wildlife Management Authority

Hwange National Park (formerly Wankie National Park) is the largest natural reserve in Zimbabwe, covering about 14,600 square kilometres (5,600 sq mi) in the northwest of the country, just south of Victoria Falls. It was founded in 1928 as the Wankie Game Reserve by the game ranger Ted Davison. The park lies in the Kalahari sandveld region and is known for one of the largest elephant populations in Africa and for its populations of lions, African wild dogs, and gemsbok.

Elephants gather at a waterhole during the dry season.

Geography and ecology

The park's vegetation ranges from Kalahari sand teak woodland in the north to mopane woodland and grassland vleis in the south. Natural surface water is scarce in the dry season, so since the 1930s water has been pumped into artificial pans, which concentrate game and support the park's large herbivore herds. Hwange hosts over 100 mammal species and 400 bird species; its elephant population, estimated in the tens of thousands, is among the largest of any single park in the world. The Presidential Herd of elephants, habituated near Main Camp, is a well-known conservation and tourism symbol.

History and issues

The area was once the hunting ground of the Ndebele king Mzilikazi. Poaching has been a recurring threat: in 2013 poachers poisoned waterholes with cyanide, killing more than 300 elephants. In July 2015 the killing of Cecil, a well-studied collared lion, by an American trophy hunter just outside the park provoked worldwide outrage and led to changes in hunting and wildlife-export rules in several countries. Hwange forms part of the Kavango–Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area, the world's largest transfrontier conservation area.
