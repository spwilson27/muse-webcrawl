Marian procession

Illustration: A Marian procession with a statue of Our Lady (placeholder image)

A Marian procession is a public religious procession in the Roman Catholic (and some Orthodox and Anglican) tradition in which an image or statue of the Virgin Mary is carried through the streets, accompanied by clergy and faithful praying and singing hymns, most commonly the rosary and Marian antiphons. Such processions are a central feature of popular Catholic devotion, held on Marian feast days such as the Assumption (15 August), the Immaculate Conception (8 December), Our Lady of Fátima (13 May and 13 October), and during the month of May, traditionally dedicated to Mary.

Practices

The statue is typically carried on a litter or float (andor in Portuguese, paso in Spanish) by members of confraternities, often in distinctive dress. Candlelight processions are characteristic of the great pilgrimage shrines — Lourdes in France, where the evening torchlight procession has been held since the nineteenth century, and Fátima in Portugal, where hundreds of thousands carry candles on the nights of 12–13 May and October. In Spain and Latin America, Holy Week and local patronal feasts feature elaborate processions of images such as Our Lady of Sorrows and Our Lady of Guadalupe; in the Philippines, the flores de mayo and the procession of Our Lady of Peñafrancia are major events.

History and significance

Processions with sacred images date to late antiquity; in Rome, Pope Sergius I (late seventh century) instituted processions on the four principal Marian feasts. The defeat of the Ottoman fleet at Lepanto in 1571, attributed to the intercession of Our Lady of the Rosary, spurred processions across Catholic Europe. Theologically, Marian processions express communal prayer, public witness, penance and thanksgiving, and they often function as focal points of local identity and heritage, some being inscribed on UNESCO lists of intangible cultural heritage.
