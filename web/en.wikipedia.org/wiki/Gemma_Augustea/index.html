Gemma Augustea

TypeCameo (engraved gem)
MaterialArabian onyx / sardonyx, two layers
Size19 × 23 cm
Createdc. 9–12 CE (Augustan or early Tiberian)
LocationKunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna

The Gemma Augustea is a large ancient Roman low-relief cameo cut in two layers of sardonyx, measuring about 19 × 23 centimetres. It is among the most famous works of imperial Roman glyptic art and is now displayed in the Kunsthistorisches Museum in Vienna.

Description

The cameo is arranged in two registers. In the upper register, the emperor Augustus sits enthroned in the guise of Jupiter, half-draped and holding a sceptre and augur's staff, his feet resting on a shield and the Capricorn — his birth sign — beside him. The goddess Roma sits with him, while the personification Oikoumene (the inhabited world) crowns him with the civic crown, and Oceanus and Italia/Tellus look on. Before the throne, Tiberius descends from a chariot driven by Victory, returning from a campaign, accompanied by the young Germanicus.

In the lower register, Roman soldiers erect a trophy of captured arms while barbarian prisoners — usually identified with the defeated peoples of the northern frontier campaigns in Illyricum or Germany — sit in dejection or are dragged by their hair. The scene grounds the divine triumph above in a concrete military victory.

History and interpretation

Scholars generally date the gem to the last years of Augustus (around 9–12 CE) or the opening of Tiberius's reign, and it may have been a court gift celebrating the dynasty. Like the Gemma Claudia and the Grand Camée de France, it presents the imperial family within a cosmic order sanctioned by the gods. The gem is documented in the treasury of the Basilica of Saint-Sernin in Toulouse in the Middle Ages, was later in French royal hands and those of the Emperor Rudolf II in Prague, and passed to Vienna, where it has been since the 17th century.
