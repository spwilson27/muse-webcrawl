Gau (territory)

Gaue of the Frankish and early medieval German lands
LanguageGerman
MeaningShire, district, region

Gau (plural Gaue) is a Germanic term for a region within a country, often a former or current province. It was used in the Middle Ages, when it can be seen as roughly corresponding to an English shire. The administrative use of the term was revived in Nazi Germany as the main administrative subdivision of the party and, increasingly, of the state.

Middle Ages

In the Frankish Empire and the early medieval German kingdom, a Gau was a territorial district administered by a count (Graf), who exercised military, judicial, and fiscal authority on behalf of the king. The names of many medieval Gaue survive in modern German landscape and regional names, such as the Rheingau, Allgäu, Breisgau, Kraichgau, and Thurgau. The concept is cognate with the Frankish pagus and similar district terms used across early medieval Europe.

Nazi Germany

The Nazi Party divided Germany into regional party districts called Gaue, each headed by a Gauleiter. After 1933 these party districts increasingly overlapped with and displaced the traditional state (Länder) administration; in 1934 the Gaue became administrative units of the Reich, and new Reichsgaue were created in annexed territories such as Austria and the Sudetenland. By the end of the Second World War there were more than 40 Gaue. The system ended with Germany's defeat in 1945.

Modern use

Today the word survives mainly in regional and landscape names and in the names of some sporting and administrative associations (for example Turn-Gau and wine-growing districts), without its former administrative significance.
