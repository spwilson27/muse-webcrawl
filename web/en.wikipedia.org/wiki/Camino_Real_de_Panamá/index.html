Camino Real de Panamá

Camino Real de Panamá
Type: colonial mule road
Length: about 65 km
Between: Panama City (Pacific) and Nombre de Dios / Portobelo (Caribbean)
Active: 16th–18th centuries

The Camino Real ("Royal Road") was the principal trans-isthmian route of the Spanish Empire in Panama, a cobbled mule road crossing the isthmus from Panama City on the Pacific to Nombre de Dios, and later Portobelo, on the Caribbean. Together with the Camino de Cruces, it formed the corridor along which Peruvian silver and other treasure reached Spain's Atlantic fleets.

History

Developed from the 1520s after the founding of Panama City (1519), the road carried the riches of Peru north by mule train in the dry season; the fairs of Nombre de Dios and Portobelo timed their markets to the arrival of the treasure fleet. The route's wealth attracted pirates and privateers, including Francis Drake and Henry Morgan, whose 1671 raid destroyed Panama City. The road declined as trade shifted to the Cape Horn route and Portobelo's fairs ended in the 18th century.

Today

Surviving cobbled stretches cross Chagres National Park; hikers can still walk parts of the road. The Camino Real and the fortifications of Panama are recognised in UNESCO's tentative listing for the colonial trans-isthmian routes of Panama.
