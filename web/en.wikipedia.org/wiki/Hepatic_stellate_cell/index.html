Hepatic stellate cell

Hepatic stellate cells (HSC), also known as perisinusoidal cells or Ito cells (earlier lipocytes or fat-storing cells), are pericytes found in the perisinusoidal space of the liver, also known as the space of Disse (a small area between the sinusoids and hepatocytes). The stellate cell is the major cell type involved in liver fibrosis, which is the formation of scar tissue in response to liver damage; in addition this cell type may be involved in the storage of vitamin A.

Structure

Hepatic stellate cells can be selectively stained with gold chloride, but their distinguishing feature in the normal liver is the presence of multiple vitamin A–rich lipid droplets in their cytoplasm. In the quiescent state they make up roughly 5–8% of the total number of liver cells. The droplets give the cells a foamy appearance under the microscope, and the cells extend long cytoplasmic processes that wrap around the sinusoids.

Function

In the normal liver, stellate cells are in a quiescent state and store most of the body's reserves of vitamin A as retinyl palmitate. They also produce extracellular matrix components in small amounts, secrete growth factors, and may help regulate sinusoidal blood flow through their contractile processes, acting as liver-specific pericytes.

Activation and fibrosis

In response to liver injury — from viral hepatitis, alcohol, metabolic disease, or toxins — quiescent stellate cells undergo activation, transdifferentiating into proliferative, contractile myofibroblast-like cells. Activated stellate cells lose their vitamin A droplets, express alpha-smooth muscle actin, migrate to sites of injury, and secrete large amounts of extracellular matrix, particularly type I and type III collagen, as well as tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs). This response is driven by signals including transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), released by Kupffer cells, damaged hepatocytes, and inflammatory cells.

Persistent activation of hepatic stellate cells is the central mechanism of liver fibrosis, which can progress to cirrhosis. Because of this role, stellate cells are a major target of antifibrotic drug development, and strategies to induce their apoptosis, senescence, or reversion to quiescence are under investigation.

History

The cells were first described by Karl Wilhelm von Kupffer in 1876, who observed star-shaped ("Sternzellen") cells in the liver using gold chloride staining — ironically, the cells Kupffer described were the stellate cells, while the phagocytic macrophages now bearing his name were distinguished later. Toshio Ito described the fat-storing cells in 1951, and their identity with Kupffer's stellate cells was established by Kenjiro Wake in 1971.
