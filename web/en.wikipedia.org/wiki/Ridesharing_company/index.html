Ridesharing company

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A ridesharing company (also called a transportation network company, TNC, or ride-hailing service) is a company that matches passengers with drivers of vehicles for hire through a website or mobile app. Unlike traditional taxis, the drivers are generally independent contractors using their own cars, and payment, dispatch, rating and navigation are all handled by the platform.

History

The model emerged in the early 2010s. Uber, founded in 2009 as UberCab in San Francisco, launched its app-based black-car service in 2010 and its lower-cost UberX service in 2012, the year Lyft also launched in the United States with its community-oriented branding. Similar companies spread worldwide, including Didi Chuxing in China, Grab in Southeast Asia, Ola in India, Bolt in Europe and Africa, and Cabify in Spain and Latin America. Within a decade, ride-hailing had reshaped urban transport in hundreds of cities and attracted enormous venture-capital investment.

Business model and features

Platforms typically charge passengers a fare computed from distance, time and demand — including "surge" or dynamic pricing at peak times — and retain a percentage commission from the driver. GPS-based matching, cashless payment, two-way ratings and estimated arrival times are standard features. Many companies have expanded into food delivery, freight and micromobility.

Criticism and regulation

Ridesharing companies have faced sustained controversy: regulators and taxi industries accused them of operating unlicensed taxi services; labour advocates and courts have disputed the classification of drivers as independent contractors rather than employees (an issue addressed by measures such as California's Proposition 22 and the UK Supreme Court's 2021 ruling that Uber drivers are "workers"); and studies have examined effects on congestion, public transit ridership and driver earnings. Many jurisdictions now license TNCs under bespoke regulatory categories.
