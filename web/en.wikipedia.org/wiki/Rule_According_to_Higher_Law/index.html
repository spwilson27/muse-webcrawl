Rule according to higher law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The rule according to a higher law is the principle that no law — however duly enacted — is valid if it contradicts fundamental principles held to stand above positive law: natural law, divine law, or the substantive commitments of a constitution as understood by its interpreters. It is the intellectual root of constitutional supremacy and judicial review: Marbury-era reasoning, German Basic Law jurisprudence (including the eternity clauses and the Radbruch formula that extreme injustice is no law), and natural-rights readings of the US Ninth Amendment all draw on it.

Tension with legal positivism

The doctrine answers to legal horror — legally enacted atrocity from slavery to apartheid to the Nazi system — by preserving a moral court of appeal within law. Legal positivists counter that conflating law and morality corrupts both: citizens and officials should be able to identify law clearly the better to resist it morally (the disobedient's honesty), and "higher law" has licensed judicial subjectivity and even insurrectionist rhetoric (abolitionists and, later, opponents of civil-rights law invoked Seymour's "higher law" in opposite causes). The Radbruch formula's post-1945 use in German courts, and the Nuremberg tribunals' reliance on supra-positive norms, are the doctrine's canonical vindications; its indeterminacy is the standing objection.
