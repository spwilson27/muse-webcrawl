Cognitive therapy

Cognitive therapy (CT) is a type of psychotherapy developed by American psychiatrist Aaron T. Beck in the 1960s. It is based on the theory that psychological problems are partly a product of faulty or unhelpful ways of thinking and of learned patterns of unhelpful behaviour. Treatment focuses on identifying and changing distorted cognitions, beliefs and attitudes, and on developing personal coping strategies that target current problems.

History

Beck originally trained as a psychoanalyst and set out to test psychoanalytic theories of depression empirically. His clinical observations instead led him to conclude that depressed patients experienced streams of negative automatic thoughts about the self, the world and the future — the so-called negative cognitive triad. He published the approach in Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders (1976).

Principles

Cognitive therapy is structured, time-limited, present-focused and collaborative. The therapist and patient work together to identify cognitive distortions — such as all-or-nothing thinking, catastrophising and overgeneralisation — and to test them against evidence, a process sometimes called collaborative empiricism. Techniques include Socratic questioning, thought records, behavioural experiments and activity scheduling.

Relationship to CBT

Cognitive therapy is one of the therapeutic approaches within the larger group of cognitive behavioural therapies (CBT), and the terms are often used interchangeably. Beck's model has been adapted for conditions including anxiety disorders, insomnia, eating disorders, substance use and personality disorders.

Effectiveness

Numerous controlled trials and meta-analyses support the efficacy of cognitive therapy, particularly for depression and anxiety disorders, and it is recommended in many national treatment guidelines.
