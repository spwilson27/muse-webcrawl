The one gene–one enzyme hypothesis is the idea that genes act through the production of enzymes, with each gene responsible for producing a single enzyme that in turn affects a single step in a metabolic pathway. It was proposed by George Wells Beadle and Edward Lawrie Tatum in 1941 from their studies of mutations in the bread mould Neurospora crassa, and it founded the field of biochemical genetics.

Background

The concept built on earlier hints, notably Archibald Garrod's 1902 notion of "inborn errors of metabolism", in which inherited diseases such as alkaptonuria were attributed to a missing enzyme. Beadle and Tatum sought a system in which gene action could be studied biochemically.

The Neurospora experiments

Beadle and Tatum exposed Neurospora to X-rays to induce mutations, then screened for strains unable to grow on minimal medium but rescued by specific supplements such as vitamins or amino acids. Each mutant strain proved defective in a single metabolic step — and the defect behaved as a single-gene mutation — leading them to conclude that each gene controls the production of one enzyme.

Impact and refinement

The work, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 1941, earned Beadle and Tatum half of the 1958 Nobel Prize. As knowledge grew, the hypothesis was refined to "one gene–one polypeptide", since many proteins are not enzymes and many proteins consist of chains encoded by different genes; modern findings on alternative splicing and RNA genes qualify the idea further, but its core insight — genes specify proteins — remains central to molecular biology.
