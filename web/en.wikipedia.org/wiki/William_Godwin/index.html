William Godwin (3 March 1756 – 7 April 1836) was an English journalist, political philosopher and novelist, regarded as one of the first exponents of utilitarianism and modern anarchism. His Enquiry Concerning Political Justice (1793) argued that government corrupts society and that humanity would progress toward a stateless condition through reason and free discussion.

Life and work

Godwin was educated for the dissenting ministry at Hoxton Academy and preached for several years before losing his faith and turning to writing. Political Justice, published at the height of debate over the French Revolution, made him famous overnight; its price of three guineas is said to have saved him from prosecution, since Pitt's government judged a book so expensive could do little harm among the poor. His novel Things as They Are; or, The Adventures of Caleb Williams (1794) dramatised his critique of aristocratic power and the legal system.

In 1797 he married the feminist writer Mary Wollstonecraft, author of A Vindication of the Rights of Woman; she died days after giving birth to their daughter, the future Mary Shelley. Godwin's frank Memoirs of Wollstonecraft (1798) scandalised readers and damaged both their reputations. He later ran a children's publishing business, wrote histories, biographies and novels, and remained a central figure in London's radical literary circles, influencing poets including Shelley and Coleridge.

Philosophy

Godwin argued for perfectibility through private judgement, opposed marriage and property as institutions in his early work, and famously held that in a fire one should save the person of greatest benefit to society — even over one's own parent — a conclusion he later softened.
