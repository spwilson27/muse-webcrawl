Al-Falaq (Arabic: الفلق, "The Daybreak" or "The Dawn") is the 113th chapter (surah) of the Qur'an, consisting of 5 verses (ayat). Together with the following surah, An-Nas, it forms the pair known as al-Mu'awwidhatayn ("the two protectors"), short chapters traditionally recited to seek God's protection from evil. Most authorities classify it as Meccan, though some hold it to be Medinan.

Text and meaning

The surah reads, in one common translation: "Say: I seek refuge with the Lord of the Dawn, from the evil of what He has created, and from the evil of the darkness when it settles, and from the evil of the blowers in knots, and from the evil of the envier when he envies." The "blowers in knots" are generally understood by exegetes as sorcerers — in the traditional image, witches who blow on knotted cords while casting spells — and the final verse seeks refuge specifically from envy (ḥasad).

Context and use

Traditions relate that the Mu'awwidhatayn were revealed in connection with an episode in which Muhammad was believed to have been affected by sorcery attributed to Labid ibn al-A'sam; the historical details are debated among scholars. In devotional practice, Al-Falaq and An-Nas are recited together with Al-Ikhlas in morning and evening supplications and before sleep, and they appear frequently in protective amulets (ruqyah).
