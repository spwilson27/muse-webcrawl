Principles of war

The principles of war are concepts governing the conduct of warfare that are intended to serve as general guidance for military commanders. They are not prescriptive rules but distilled principles, developed over centuries of military thought, that are taught in staff colleges and embodied in national military doctrines.

Origins

Ideas that became the modern principles appear in Sun Tzu's The Art of War, in Carl von Clausewitz's On War (1832), and in the writings of Antoine-Henri Jomini. They were first formally codified in the early 20th century, notably in British Field Service Regulations (influenced by J. F. C. Fuller's lists) and in United States Army regulations of 1921.

Common formulations

The British Army's ten principles are: selection and maintenance of the aim; maintenance of morale; offensive action; security; surprise; concentration of force; economy of effort; flexibility; cooperation; and sustainability. The United States armed forces traditionally list nine principles — objective, offensive, mass, economy of force, maneuver, unity of command, security, surprise, and simplicity — remembered by the acronym MOOSEMUSS, later expanded with restraint, perseverance, and legitimacy for joint operations.

Although the lists differ between nations, they share a common core derived from classical military theory, and officers are expected to balance the principles against each other in context rather than apply them mechanically.
