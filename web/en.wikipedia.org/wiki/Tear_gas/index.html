Tear gas

Tear gas (lachrymator)

Common agentsCS, CN, CR, OC (pepper spray)
EffectsTearing, burning pain, coughing, blepharospasm
UsesRiot control; banned in warfare (CWC)
PersistenceEffects usually resolve in 15–30 minutes

Tear gas, formally known as a lachrymator agent or lachrymator, is a class of chemical compounds that stimulate the corneal nerves of the eyes, causing tearing, pain and sometimes temporary blindness. Despite the name, tear agents are usually solids or liquids dispersed as aerosols rather than true gases. They are used worldwide for riot control and in self-defence sprays, but their use in warfare is prohibited by the Chemical Weapons Convention.

Agents

The most widely used agent is CS (2-chlorobenzalmalononitrile), developed in the 1920s and adopted by police from the late 1950s for its potency and relative safety compared with the older CN (chloroacetophenone, "Mace"). OC (oleoresin capsicum, pepper spray) is an inflammatory agent derived from chilli peppers. These compounds activate TRPA1 or TRPV1 pain receptors, producing intense burning, copious tears, runny nose, coughing and involuntary closing of the eyes.

Effects and risks

Effects begin within seconds and usually subside within half an hour of leaving the contaminated area. Most exposures cause no lasting harm, but heavy exposure in enclosed spaces, or direct impacts from canisters, can cause serious injury, chemical burns, respiratory damage and occasionally death; people with asthma are at particular risk. Medical guidance emphasises moving to fresh air and irrigating eyes with water or saline.

Legality

Tear gas was used on a large scale in the First World War, leading to prohibition of chemical weapons in war under the 1925 Geneva Protocol and the 1993 Chemical Weapons Convention. The CWC expressly permits use for "law enforcement including domestic riot control", a dual status that has drawn criticism from human-rights groups during mass protests in many countries.
