Acadiana (French: L'Acadiane) is the region of southern Louisiana that is home to the state's distinctive Francophone Cajun culture. It comprises the 22 parishes in the southwestern and south-central part of the state, an area of roughly 36,000 square kilometres (14,000 square miles) stretching from the Texas border eastward to the Atchafalaya Basin and south to the Gulf of Mexico. Its cultural and economic centre is the city of Lafayette.

Name and official recognition

The name combines "Acadia" with the suffix "-ana" from Louisiana, and derives ultimately from Acadia, the former French colony in present-day Atlantic Canada whose French-speaking inhabitants — the Acadians — were expelled by the British beginning in 1755 and many of whom settled in Spanish Louisiana. The word is said to have entered print through a typographical error in a New Orleans newspaper in the early 1960s. In 1971 the Louisiana legislature formally recognised Acadiana as the official name of the Cajun region, specifying its 22 parishes.

Geography

Acadiana divides naturally into three landscapes: the coastal marshes along the Gulf, the bayou country of slow-moving waterways lined with moss-draped live oaks, and the open Cajun Prairie in the northwest of the region. The Atchafalaya River and its basin form the region's eastern edge. Major cities besides Lafayette include Lake Charles, Baton Rouge's western suburbs, Houma, New Iberia, Opelousas, Crowley — billed as the "Rice Capital of America" — and Eunice.

Culture

The region is known for Cajun and zydeco music, for a cuisine built on rice, seafood, and the "holy trinity" of onions, bell peppers and celery — dishes such as gumbo, jambalaya, étouffée and boudin — and for French-language heritage maintained by organisations such as CODOFIL (the Council for the Development of French in Louisiana). Annual events include the Festival International de Louisiane and Festivals Acadiens et Créoles in Lafayette, and rural Mardi Gras runs (courir de Mardi Gras) distinct from the urban parades of New Orleans. The population also includes Louisiana Creoles of African, French, Spanish and Native American descent, whose culture overlaps with and differs from that of the Cajuns.

Economy

Oil and natural gas extraction and services, centred on Lafayette and the offshore industry of the Gulf, long dominated the economy, alongside fishing and shrimping, rice and sugarcane farming, and a growing tourism sector drawn by the region's food and music.
