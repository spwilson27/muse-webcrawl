Jordan–Hölder theorem

In abstract algebra, the Jordan–Hölder theorem states that any two composition series of a given group (or, more generally, of a module) are equivalent: they have the same length and the same composition factors, up to permutation and isomorphism. It is one of the fundamental results of group theory, first proved for groups by Camille Jordan and for the uniqueness part in general by Otto Hölder.

Statement

A composition series of a group G is a finite chain of subgroups

1 = N0 ⊲ N1 ⊲ ⋯ ⊲ Nk = G

in which each Ni is a normal subgroup of Ni+1 and each quotient group (factor) Ni+1/Ni is simple. The theorem asserts that if G admits one composition series, then any two composition series have the same length k, and their multisets of composition factors coincide up to reordering.

History and proof

Jordan proved in 1870 that the orders of the composition factors are invariant; Hölder showed in 1889 that the factors themselves are invariant up to isomorphism. The standard modern proof proceeds via the Schreier refinement theorem, which states that any two subnormal series of a group have equivalent refinements; the Jordan–Hölder theorem follows by refining two composition series, which cannot be properly refined further.

Generalisations

The same result holds for modules of finite length over a ring, and in the general setting of modular lattices. The composition factors of a finite group play a role analogous to the prime factorisation of an integer: the classification of finite simple groups can thus be viewed as describing the "atoms" from which all finite groups are built.
