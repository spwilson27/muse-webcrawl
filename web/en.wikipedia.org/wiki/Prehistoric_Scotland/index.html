Prehistoric Scotland

Archaeology and geology continue to reveal the secrets of prehistoric Scotland, uncovering a complex past before the arrival of written records. Events of prehistoric Scotland are relative to the arrival of Romans in the 1st century AD, and comprise the era of renouncing the use of written records. From the Pleistocene occupation of the land by hunter-gatherers through the Mesolithic and Neolithic periods, the Bronze Age and Iron Age, Scotland has a rich diversity of archaeological sites and artefacts.

Palaeolithic

Scotland was intermittently covered by ice sheets during the Pleistocene. Between glacial phases the climate moderated, and humans first colonised the land. The earliest known evidence of human presence comes from the Howburn Farm site near Biggar in South Lanarkshire, where flint tools of the Hamburgian culture dated to around 14,000–12,000 BC were found. The Creswellian site at Kilmory on Arran also produced tools of a similar age.

Mesolithic

As the ice retreated around 9600 BC, Mesolithic hunter-gatherers settled the coastline and river valleys. Important sites include the hazelnut shell pits at Staosnaig on Colonsay, evidence of large-scale food processing, and the site at Cramond near Edinburgh, one of the earliest dated Mesolithic settlements in Scotland (c. 8500 BC). Mesolithic people exploited fish, shellfish, deer and wild plants, and moved seasonally across the landscape.

Neolithic

Farming arrived around 4000 BC. Neolithic Scotland produced some of Europe's most remarkable monuments. In Orkney, the "Heart of Neolithic Orkney" includes the stone-built village of Skara Brae, the chambered cairn of Maeshowe, the Stones of Stenness and the Ring of Brodgar. The Callanish Stones on Lewis date from the later Neolithic. Chambered cairns, cursus monuments, henges and carved stone balls are widespread, and polished stone axes from factories such as Creag na Caillich near Killin were traded over long distances.

Bronze Age

The Bronze Age (c. 2500–800 BC) brought metalworking, new burial customs including cist burials and cremations, and artefacts such as the gold lunulae and jet necklaces. Hillforts began to appear in the later Bronze Age. Climatic deterioration in the later 2nd millennium BC may have contributed to social change and the abandonment of upland settlements.

Iron Age

The Iron Age (c. 800 BC – AD 100) saw the construction of over 500 brochs, tall drystone towers concentrated in the north and west, along with duns, crannogs and wheelhouses. In the south, large hillforts such as Traprain Law and the Eildon Hills dominated tribal territories. The peoples recorded by the Romans — including the Caledonians and the Votadini — emerged from these Iron Age societies.
