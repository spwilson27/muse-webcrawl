Thomas Harold Flowers (22 December 1905 – 28 October 1998) was an English engineer with the British General Post Office who designed and built Colossus, the world's first programmable electronic digital computer, used at Bletchley Park to break the German Lorenz cipher during the Second World War.

Early career

Flowers was born in Poplar, in London's East End, the son of a bricklayer. Apprenticed as a mechanical engineer, he studied electrical engineering at night and joined the Post Office research station at Dollis Hill, where he pioneered the use of thermionic valves (vacuum tubes) in telephone exchange equipment — experience that convinced him equipment containing thousands of valves could run reliably.

Colossus

In 1942 Flowers was brought in to assist Bletchley's codebreakers, working with Max Newman and cryptanalyst Bill Tutte on mechanising the attack on the Lorenz "Tunny" cipher. His earlier Heath Robinson machine was slow and unreliable; Flowers proposed an all-electronic machine with 1,500 valves. Scepticism at Bletchley led him to fund much of the project initially at Dollis Hill. Colossus Mark 1 was working by December 1943 and in service by February 1944; the 2,400-valve Mark 2 followed in time for D-Day. Ten Colossi were built, and their intelligence product materially shortened decrypt times.

Secrecy and recognition

After the war the machines were dismantled and Flowers was bound by the Official Secrets Act for decades; colleagues' wartime careers benefited while his own contribution went publicly unrecognised until the 1970s. He received an MBE in 1943 and later awards including the Martlesham Medal. Colossus is now celebrated as a landmark of computing history, and a working rebuild stands at The National Museum of Computing at Bletchley Park.
