End-of-life care

End-of-life care

Field: Palliative medicine, nursing, ethics

Settings: Hospice, hospital, care home, patient's home

Focus: Symptom control, comfort, dignity, support for patient and family

Related: Palliative care, hospice, advance healthcare directive

End-of-life care is health care provided to people who are in the final stages of a terminal illness or nearing the end of life. Its aim is not to cure but to relieve pain and other symptoms, preserve dignity and quality of life, and support the psychological, social, and spiritual needs of patients and their families. It is closely related to — and often delivered through — palliative care and the hospice movement founded by Cicely Saunders, who opened St Christopher's Hospice in London in 1967.

Components

  • Symptom management: control of pain (frequently with opioids such as morphine), breathlessness, nausea, agitation, and terminal secretions.
  • Communication and planning: honest discussion of prognosis, goals of care, and advance care planning, including advance directives, lasting powers of attorney, and do-not-attempt-resuscitation decisions.
  • Psychosocial and spiritual support: for the patient and for carers, extending into bereavement care after death.

Settings and provision

Care may be delivered in hospices, hospitals, nursing and care homes, or the person's own home by community teams, often coordinated through frameworks intended to ensure that people can die in their preferred place. In the United Kingdom, standards are set by bodies such as NICE; the Liverpool Care Pathway, once widely used, was phased out after an independent review (2013) found it had sometimes been applied poorly, and replaced by individualised care plans.

Ethical issues

End-of-life care raises ethical and legal questions about withdrawing or withholding life-sustaining treatment, clinically assisted nutrition and hydration, palliative sedation, and the doctrine of double effect (relieving symptoms with foreseen but unintended risks). Debates over assisted dying legislation continue in many jurisdictions and are distinct from, though often discussed alongside, standard end-of-life practice.
