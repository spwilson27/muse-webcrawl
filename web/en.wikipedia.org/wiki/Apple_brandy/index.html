Apple brandy is a fruit brandy distilled from fermented apple juice or cider. It has been produced for centuries in apple-growing regions of Europe and North America. The best-known protected style is Calvados, from Normandy in France, made in pot stills or column stills depending on the appellation and aged in oak casks for a minimum of two years. In the United States, the traditional form is applejack, historically concentrated by freeze distillation ("jacking") and later by conventional distillation; American apple brandy producers such as Laird's, founded in New Jersey in the eighteenth century, supplied a spirit recorded in George Washington's own recipe requests.

Oak ageing gives aged apple brandies their amber colour and vanilla and spice notes.

Production

Producers ferment apple juice — often from bittersweet and bittersharp cider apple varieties chosen for tannin and acidity — into cider of about 5–6% alcohol, then distil it. Calvados from the Pays d'Auge must be double-distilled in copper pot stills, a slower method valued for richness; other areas may use column stills for a lighter spirit. The distillate is aged in oak, where it gains colour and flavours of vanilla, spice, and dried fruit, and premium bottlings may be aged for decades. Age statements and blends follow appellation rules in France; American apple brandy bottled "in bond" or labelled straight apple brandy follows federal standards of identity.

Styles and serving

Young apple brandies are fruity and fiery and may be used in cocktails such as the Jack Rose (applejack, lemon or lime juice, and grenadine). Aged Calvados is sipped neat, served in the Norman trou normand tradition between courses, and used in cooking — flambéing, sauces for pork and poultry, and desserts. Unaged or lightly aged apple eaux-de-vie are produced in Germany (Apfelbrand), Switzerland, Austria, and Quebec, where ice cider distillation is also practised.

History

Distillation of cider is documented in Normandy from the sixteenth century; the name Calvados derives from the département created after the French Revolution. Colonial American farmers, facing abundant apple harvests and poor roads, converted cider to durable, transportable applejack, which served as currency in some communities and formed part of soldiers' rations. Prohibition curtailed legal production in the United States, but the category revived with the craft-distilling movement of the twenty-first century.
