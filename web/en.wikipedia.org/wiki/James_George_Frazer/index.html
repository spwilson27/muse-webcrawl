Life

Sir James George Frazer (1 January 1854 – 7 May 1941) was a Scottish social anthropologist and folklorist, a fellow of Trinity College, Cambridge, and one of the founding figures — though an "armchair" practitioner who rarely did fieldwork — of the comparative study of myth and religion. Educated at Glasgow and Cambridge in classics and law, he was drawn to anthropology by E. B. Tylor's Primitive Culture and by his friend William Robertson Smith.

The Golden Bough

Frazer's masterwork, The Golden Bough: A Study in Comparative Religion, began from a single puzzle: why the priest of Diana at Nemi — the "King of the Wood," guardian of a sacred golden bough — had to be a runaway slave who won office by killing his predecessor. First published in two volumes (1890), the work swelled to twelve volumes (1906–1915) of worldwide parallels in myth, ritual, taboo, sacrifice, and dying-and-rising gods; Frazer's own one-volume abridgement (1922) became one of the most influential books of the century.

Theory of magic and religion

Frazer proposed an evolutionary sequence of human thought: magic (the mistaken application of the "laws of sympathy" — similarity and contagion), superseded by religion (propitiation of conscious powers), in turn superseded by science. He systematised concepts such as taboo, totemism (in his four-volume Totemism and Exogamy, 1910), the scapegoat, and the divine king killed to renew fertility.

Influence and criticism

The Golden Bough profoundly shaped modernist literature — T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land, Joyce, Yeats, and D. H. Lawrence drew on it — and psychoanalysis via Freud's Totem and Taboo. Later anthropologists (Malinowski, Evans-Pritchard, Wittgenstein in his Remarks) rejected Frazer's evolutionism and his decontextualised comparison, though his collections of ethnographic material remain valuable and his literary power unmatched in the field.
