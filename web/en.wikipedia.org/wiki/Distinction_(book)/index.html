Distinction (book)

Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste
AuthorPierre Bourdieu
Original titleLa Distinction: Critique sociale du jugement
Published1979 (Éditions de Minuit); English translation 1984 (Harvard University Press)
SubjectSociology of culture, taste, social class
TranslatorRichard Nice

Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste (French: La Distinction: Critique sociale du jugement, 1979) is a sociological book by French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu. Based on extensive empirical research conducted in France in the 1960s and 1970s, it argues that judgements of taste — in art, music, food, furniture and lifestyle — are not innocent personal preferences but are deeply shaped by social class, and function as markers and weapons in struggles for status. The International Sociological Association named it one of the ten most important sociology books of the twentieth century.

Argument

Bourdieu surveyed over a thousand French respondents about their preferences — for works of art, music, food, home decoration, leisure activities and more — and analysed the results statistically, famously using correspondence analysis to map a "social space" of lifestyles. He found systematic correspondences between people's class position and their tastes. To explain them, he deployed and developed his core theoretical toolkit:

  • Habitus — the durable, embodied set of dispositions, acquired through upbringing and class conditions, that generates perceptions and preferences that feel natural but are socially produced.
  • Capital — people hold differing volumes and compositions of economic capital (wealth) and cultural capital (education, knowledge, credentials and familiarity with legitimate culture); the "space of social positions" is structured by both.
  • Taste as classification — "taste classifies, and it classifies the classifier": choosing what is "good taste" also displays and legitimises one's social rank. The dominant class's "aesthetic disposition" — valuing form over function, abstraction and distance — is set against the working-class "taste for necessity", which prizes function, substance and participation.

Thus cultural consumption helps reproduce social hierarchy: familiarity with legitimate culture is rewarded in education and careers, while working-class tastes are stigmatised, making inequality appear as a matter of natural refinement rather than privilege.

Reception and influence

Distinction made Bourdieu one of the most cited social scientists of the late twentieth century and founded much of the modern sociology of culture and consumption. Critics have questioned how far findings from 1960s France generalise to other societies and eras; subsequent research proposed modifications such as the "cultural omnivore" thesis (high-status groups in the US displaying breadth rather than snobbish exclusivity of taste). The book remains a canonical text in sociology, cultural studies and education research, and its concepts of habitus and cultural capital are standard vocabulary across the social sciences.
