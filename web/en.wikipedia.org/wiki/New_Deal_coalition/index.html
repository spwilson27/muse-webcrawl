The New Deal coalition was an American political coalition that supported the Democratic Party and its New Deal programs from the 1932 presidential election onward. Assembled by President Franklin D. Roosevelt, it united groups that had often been at odds — the white South, big-city political machines, labor unions, African Americans, intellectuals, farmers, and ethnic and religious minorities — and made the Democrats the majority party in American politics for a generation.

Composition

  • The Solid South: White Southerners, loyal Democrats since Reconstruction, provided the coalition's most reliable electoral base.
  • Organized labor: Industrial unions, especially the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO), mobilized workers after the Wagner Act (1935) protected collective bargaining.
  • Big-city machines: Urban organizations in cities such as Chicago, New York, and Pittsburgh delivered immigrant and working-class votes.
  • African Americans: Black voters, historically Republican since the Civil War, shifted decisively to the Democrats in 1936 in response to New Deal relief programs.
  • Farmers and the West: Agricultural subsidies and rural programs won support in farming regions.
  • Ethnic and religious minorities: Catholics, Jews, and Southern and Eastern European immigrants were heavily represented.
  • Intellectuals and liberals: Reformers and academics staffed and supported the New Deal agencies.

Political dominance

The coalition powered Democratic victories in five consecutive presidential elections (1932–1948) and long periods of congressional control. Even Republican president Dwight D. Eisenhower governed largely within the New Deal framework in the 1950s, accepting Social Security and much of the regulatory state.

Decline and collapse

Strains appeared early: conservative Southern Democrats and Republicans formed the "conservative coalition" in Congress from 1937, blocking further domestic reforms. The coalition fractured in the 1960s over civil rights — the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965 accelerated the defection of white Southerners to the Republican Party, a shift consolidated by Richard Nixon's "Southern strategy" in 1968. Conflicts over the Vietnam War, urban unrest, and cultural issues further splintered the alliance. By the election of Ronald Reagan in 1980, the New Deal order had largely given way; many former coalition voters became "Reagan Democrats" before realigning permanently.

Legacy

Historians treat the coalition as the foundation of the "Fifth Party System" in American politics. Its breakdown reshaped both parties and remains a central reference point in analyses of American political realignment.
