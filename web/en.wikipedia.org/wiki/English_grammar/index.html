English grammar

English grammar is the set of structural rules of the English language, including its morphology (the structure of words) and syntax (the structure of sentences). English is a relatively analytic language: it has largely lost the inflectional case system of its ancestor Old English, relying instead on word order and auxiliary verbs to express grammatical relations. Its basic word order is subject–verb–object (SVO).

English relies on word order and auxiliaries where older stages of the language used inflections.

Nouns and pronouns

English nouns inflect only for number (regular plural -s) and for the genitive (possessive 's), a clitic that can attach to whole phrases ("the Queen of England's hat"). Articles distinguish definiteness (the) and indefiniteness (a/an), but nouns carry no grammatical gender. Personal pronouns preserve remnants of case — I/me, he/him, she/her, we/us, they/them — and third-person singular pronouns mark natural gender (he, she, it), with singular they in wide and growing use.

Verbs

The English verb has only two morphologically distinct tenses, present and past (walk/walked, sing/sang), plus a third-person singular present -s and the participles used with auxiliaries. Future time is expressed with modal or semi-modal constructions (will, be going to). Aspect is built periphrastically: progressive be + -ing, perfect have + past participle, and their combination. A rich system of modal auxiliaries (can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would) expresses possibility, permission, obligation and futurity, and negation and questions typically require do-support ("Do you know?", "I do not know").

Sentences and clauses

English syntax is strongly configurational: subjects generally precede verbs and objects follow them, giving the language a fixed order compared with its Germanic relatives. Word order changes mark sentence type — subject–auxiliary inversion forms most questions ("Are you coming?"), and relative clauses use the relative pronouns who, which, that or zero ("the book I read"). Subordination uses complementisers such as that, if, whether and a large inventory of prepositions, which carry much of the relational load lost with the old case endings.

History and description

The first grammar of English was William Bullokar's Pamphlet for Grammar (1586); the eighteenth century produced influential prescriptive works by Robert Lowth (1762) and Lindley Murray (1795), which codified rules such as the ban on double negatives and split infinitives. Modern scholarship is dominated by large descriptive grammars, notably A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language (Quirk et al., 1985) and The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002), which describe the grammar of Standard English as actually used.
