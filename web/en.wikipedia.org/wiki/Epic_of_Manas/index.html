The Epic of Manas is a vast oral epic cycle of the Kyrgyz people of Central Asia and the centrepiece of Kyrgyz literature and national identity. It recounts the deeds of the hero Manas, who unites the scattered Kyrgyz tribes against their enemies, and continues with his son Semetey and grandson Seytek, forming a trilogy. In its fullest recorded version, dictated by the singer Sayakbai Karalaev in the 20th century, the cycle runs to more than 500,000 lines — over sixteen times the length of the Odyssey and among the longest epic poems in the world.

Content

The first part tells of Manas's miraculous birth, his gathering of forty warrior companions (kırk çoro), his campaigns against the Kalmyks and other foes, his marriage to the wise Kanykei, and his death. Semetey follows his son, who grows up in exile and returns to reclaim his father's legacy, while Seytek completes the reconciliation of the dynasty. The poems blend myth, legendary history, and Kyrgyz customary life, preserving vocabulary, place names, and traditions across centuries.

Performance and transmission

The epic was transmitted orally by professional bards called manaschi, who recite for hours in a distinctive trance-like chant, traditionally claiming to have learned the epic through a dream calling. Performances vary with each singer, who improvises within a stock of formulas and episodes. Written recording began with collectors such as Wilhelm Radloff in the 1860s; Chokan Valikhanov recorded an episode in 1856 and called the epic an encyclopedia of Kyrgyz life.

Modern significance

After Kyrgyzstan's independence from the Soviet Union in 1991, Manas became the supreme national symbol: his name graces the country's main airport, its highest-order state decoration, and a major university, and a monumental statue stands in Bishkek. In 1995 Kyrgyzstan celebrated the officially designated 1000th anniversary of the epic. In 2013 the trilogy was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
