Water table

The water table is the surface between the unsaturated zone above and the saturated zone below.

The water table is the upper surface of the zone of saturation (the phreatic zone) in the ground, at which the soil or rocks are permanently saturated with water. More precisely, it is the surface where the water pressure head is equal to the atmospheric pressure — the level to which water rises in a shallow well. Above the water table lies the vadose (unsaturated) zone, where pore spaces contain both air and water, aside from a thin capillary fringe where water is drawn upward by surface tension.

Behavior

The water table typically follows, in subdued form, the shape of the land surface, standing higher beneath hills and intersecting the surface at streams, lakes and springs, where groundwater discharges. Its depth fluctuates seasonally with recharge from precipitation and with losses to evapotranspiration and pumping. Where an impermeable layer traps water above the main table, a perched water table forms. Heavy pumping near a well draws the table down into a cone of depression; long-term over-abstraction of aquifers such as the Ogallala has lowered water tables by tens of meters.

Importance

Water table depth determines how deep wells must be drilled, influences soil drainage and agriculture, controls foundation and septic-system design, and affects how readily surface contaminants can reach groundwater. In arid regions it may lie hundreds of meters deep; in wetlands it is at or above the surface.
