Tephrochronology

Tephrochronology
FieldGeochronology, Quaternary science, archaeology
Term coinedSigurdur Thorarinsson (1944), from Greek tephra, "ashes"
MethodCorrelation of volcanic ash layers by composition and age
ApplicationsDating sediments, ice cores and archaeological sites; eruption histories

Tephrochronology is a geochronological technique that uses discrete layers of tephra — volcanic ash and other ejecta from a single eruption — as time-parallel marker horizons to date and correlate sedimentary and archaeological sequences. Because an ash fall is geologically instantaneous and can blanket thousands of square kilometres, a chemically fingerprinted ash layer forms an isochron: the same "date line" wherever it is found.

Method

The Icelandic geologist Sigurdur Thorarinsson coined the term in his 1944 dissertation on the 1943-area eruptions of Hekla and their tephra layers in Icelandic soils. Practitioners identify layers by field characteristics and by laboratory analysis: the major- and trace-element composition of volcanic glass shards, measured by electron microprobe, is usually distinctive for each eruption. Where ash is present only as microscopic shards — cryptotephra — in lake sediments, peat, marine cores or ice cores, extraction and counting techniques extend correlation over continental distances. Independent ages come from radiocarbon dating of bracketing material, argon–argon dating, or historically recorded eruptions.

Applications

Famous marker tephras include the Mazama ash from the eruption that formed Crater Lake (~7,600 years ago) across western North America, Icelandic ashes such as Hekla 1104 and Öræfajökull 1362 used to date Norse settlement, and the Laacher See tephra in late-glacial Europe. In archaeology, tephra layers bracket occupation horizons — for example at sites buried by the eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79 — and in climate science they synchronise ice-core and marine records.
