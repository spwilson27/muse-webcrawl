American Scene painting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

American Scene painting celebrated everyday life in the United States, especially during the 1930s.

American Scene painting is a term for a movement in American art of the 1920s and 1930s in which artists depicted recognizably American subjects — small towns, farms, industrial cities, and ordinary working people — in a naturalistic, representational style. It arose partly in reaction against European modernist abstraction and partly out of a desire, sharpened by the Great Depression, to create an art rooted in national experience.

Regionalism and Social Realism

The movement is usually divided into two broad tendencies. Regionalism, centered in the Midwest, idealized rural and small-town life; its best-known practitioners were Grant Wood, Thomas Hart Benton, and John Steuart Curry, sometimes called the Regionalist triumvirate. Social Realism, more urban and politically engaged, documented poverty, labor conflict, and city life; artists associated with it include Ben Shahn, Reginald Marsh, and the Mexican-influenced muralists working in the United States.

Context and patronage

American Scene painting flourished under New Deal patronage. The Public Works of Art Project, the Section of Painting and Sculpture, and the Works Progress Administration's Federal Art Project employed thousands of artists and filled post offices, schools, and courthouses with murals of American life. Magazine covers, calendar art, and the popular prints of artists such as Norman Rockwell carried the style to a mass audience.

Decline and legacy

By the end of World War II the movement had lost momentum, displaced by Abstract expressionism and criticized as provincial or propagandistic. Later scholarship has reassessed American Scene painting as a complex record of national identity during a period of economic crisis, and major works remain central to collections of American art.
