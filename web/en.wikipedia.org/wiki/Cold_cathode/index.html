Cold cathode

A cold cathode is a cathode that is not electrically heated by a filament. A cathode is an electrode from which electrons are emitted, or through which conventional current flows out of a polarized electrical device. In vacuum tubes and gas-discharge lamps, a cold cathode may emit electrons by field electron emission, photoelectric effect, or secondary emission caused by ion bombardment, rather than by thermionic emission from a hot filament.

Cold cathode

Type: Electrode / discharge device
Related: Neon lamp, cathode-ray tube, Nixie tube

Principle

In a hot-cathode device, heating lowers the effective work function and electrons boil off the surface. In a cold cathode, a strong electric field at a sharp or specially coated surface pulls electrons out, or positive ions striking the cathode knock electrons free. Once discharge begins, it can be sustained at a relatively steady voltage.

Applications

Cold cathodes are used in neon signs and neon indicator lamps, fluorescent lamps started without filament preheating, Nixie tubes, voltage-regulator tubes, flash lamps, cold-cathode fluorescent lamps (CCFLs) formerly used for laptop backlights, and some X-ray tubes. Their advantages include instant starting, ruggedness, and long life; disadvantages can include higher starting voltage and sputtering of cathode material.

Cold-cathode fluorescent lamps

A CCFL is a gas-discharge lamp using a cold cathode, commonly with mercury vapour and a phosphor coating. It was widely used as a backlight for liquid-crystal displays before light-emitting diodes became dominant.
