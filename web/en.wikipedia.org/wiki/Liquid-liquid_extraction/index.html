Liquid–liquid extraction

Liquid–liquid extraction, also known as solvent extraction and partitioning, is a method to separate compounds or metal complexes, based on their relative solubilities in two different immiscible liquids, usually water (polar) and an organic solvent (non-polar). There is a net transfer of one or more species from one liquid into another liquid phase, generally from aqueous to organic.

Principles

The extent of extraction is governed by the distribution coefficient of the solute between the two phases, which follows the Nernst distribution law. Extraction can be enhanced by adjusting pH (for ionisable compounds), by complexation with chelating agents such as dithizone or oxines, or by salting out. In the laboratory the process is carried out in a separatory funnel; industrial plants use mixer-settlers, pulsed columns or centrifugal contactors, often in countercurrent cascades of many stages.

Applications

  • Hydrometallurgy: recovery of copper, uranium, rare-earth elements and other metals.
  • Nuclear fuel reprocessing, notably the PUREX process for uranium and plutonium.
  • Pharmaceutical and fine-chemical purification, and removal of impurities from process streams.
  • Recovery of organic acids and antibiotics, such as penicillin, from fermentation broths.
  • Decaffeination of coffee and tea, and production of flavour extracts.
