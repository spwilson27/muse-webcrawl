Combinatorial chemistry

Combinatorial chemistry comprises chemical synthesis methods that make it possible to prepare a large number (tens to thousands or even millions) of compounds in a single process. These compound libraries can be made as mixtures, sets of individual compounds, or chemical structures generated in silico. Combinatorial chemistry is used principally in drug discovery, together with high-throughput screening, and in materials science.

History

The roots of the field lie in solid-phase peptide synthesis developed by Robert Bruce Merrifield in the 1960s. In the 1980s, researchers such as H. Mario Geysen developed parallel synthesis on pins and multi-component methods, and by the 1990s the pharmaceutical industry had widely adopted library synthesis for lead discovery. Early enthusiasm met with criticism that libraries were often too large, too lipophilic and structurally repetitive; modern practice emphasizes smaller, high-quality, drug-like libraries and diversity-oriented synthesis.

Methods

  • Split-and-pool (split-mix) synthesis: resin beads are divided, reacted with different building blocks, pooled and repeated, so that each bead carries a single pure compound and the number of products multiplies at each step.
  • Parallel synthesis: compounds are made individually in separate wells of microplates, often with robotic automation, giving known structures in known locations.
  • Dynamic combinatorial chemistry: building blocks combine reversibly under thermodynamic control, and a target molecule can amplify the best-binding library member.
  • DNA-encoded libraries: each compound is tagged with a DNA barcode recording its synthesis history, enabling selection against a target and decoding by sequencing.

Applications

Combinatorial methods have produced screening libraries underpinning many drug discovery programmes and are also used to search for new catalysts, polymers, dyes and ligands. Coupled with high-throughput screening and computational triage, they remain a standard tool of medicinal chemistry.
