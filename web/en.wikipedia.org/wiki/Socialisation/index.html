Socialisation

Socialisation (American English: socialization) is the lifelong process through which individuals learn the norms, values, language, skills and behaviours of their society, enabling them to function as members of it. Sociologists distinguish primary socialisation in early childhood, mainly within the family, from secondary socialisation through schools, peer groups, workplaces, media and other institutions.

Theories

George Herbert Mead explained the emergence of the self through play and role-taking, culminating in understanding the "generalised other". Charles Horton Cooley proposed the "looking-glass self". Sigmund Freud described development through the resolution of instinctual conflicts, while Jean Piaget and Lawrence Kohlberg focused on cognitive and moral development. Erving Goffman studied "total institutions" such as asylums and prisons, where resocialisation can strip and rebuild identity. Pierre Bourdieu emphasised the transmission of habitus and cultural capital, often reproducing class inequality.

Agents and forms

Key agents include family, education, peers, mass and social media, religion and the workplace. Gender socialisation channels children toward culturally approved masculine and feminine roles; political socialisation forms civic attitudes; organisational socialisation inducts new employees. Sociologists debate the balance between socialisation and individual agency, and how far the process reproduces or transforms social order.
