Torque converter

A torque converter is a type of fluid coupling that transfers rotating power from a prime mover, such as an internal combustion engine, to a rotating driven load. In modern form it is used in automatic transmissions in automobiles to replace the mechanical clutch of a manual transmission. Its principal characteristics are that it transmits power hydrodynamically, allows the engine to keep running while the driven load is stationary, and multiplies torque as rotational speed difference between input and output increases.

Construction

A torque converter consists of three rotating elements inside a sealed housing filled with transmission fluid: an impeller (pump) driven by the engine, a turbine connected to the transmission input shaft, and a stator mounted on a one-way freewheel clutch between them. Fluid thrown outward by the impeller strikes the turbine blades and drives it; the stator redirects the returning fluid so that it assists the impeller, producing torque multiplication — typically up to about 2:1 at stall. As turbine speed approaches impeller speed, the stator begins to freewheel.

Lock-up and efficiency

Because hydrodynamic drive always involves some slip, which wastes fuel, most modern torque converters incorporate a lock-up clutch that mechanically locks the turbine to the impeller at cruising speed, eliminating slip. Developments include multi-plate lock-up clutches and damper systems that allow lock-up at lower speeds.

History

The torque converter derives from the fluid coupling and the work of Hermann Föttinger in the early 20th century, originally for marine turbines. It entered mass automotive use with General Motors' Hydramatic and, in true torque-converter form, the Buick Dynaflow of 1948.
