Arthur Scargill

Arthur Scargill, president of the NUM during the 1984–85 strike

Arthur Scargill (born 11 January 1938) is a British trade unionist who was President of the National Union of Mineworkers (NUM) from 1982 to 2002. He led the union through the miners' strike of 1984–85, the defining industrial confrontation of Margaret Thatcher's premiership, which ended in the miners' defeat and the rapid contraction of the British coal industry.

Early life and rise

Scargill was born in Worsbrough Dale near Barnsley, Yorkshire, the son of a miner. He left school at 15 and began work at Woolley Colliery in 1953. Radicalised by poverty and by the Young Communist League, which he joined as a teenager, he rose through union office in the Yorkshire NUM, becoming a full-time official and then president of the Yorkshire Area in 1973. He gained national prominence as the organiser of the mass picket at Saltley Gate coke depot in Birmingham during the 1972 miners' strike, which forced the depot's closure and became a landmark of trade-union power.

NUM presidency and the 1984–85 strike

Elected national president in 1981 on a strongly left-wing platform, Scargill confronted the Thatcher government's plans to close uneconomic pits. When the National Coal Board announced the closure of Cortonwood colliery in March 1984, strike action spread from Yorkshire across most coalfields. Scargill led the strike without the national ballot many miners and much of the public expected, a decision that split the union and allowed a breakaway, the Union of Democratic Mineworkers, to form in Nottinghamshire where most miners kept working.

The year-long strike saw mass picketing, violent clashes with police — most famously at Orgreave in June 1984 — and great hardship in mining communities. It ended in March 1985 with a return to work without agreement. Pit closures accelerated, and the industry was privatised in 1994.

Later years

Scargill remained NUM president until 2002, by then leading a much diminished union. In 1996 he broke with Labour and founded the Socialist Labour Party, which he continues to lead, standing unsuccessfully in several parliamentary elections. He has remained a polarising figure: a hero to many on the left for his resistance to Thatcherism, and blamed by critics for the strategy that led the miners to defeat.
