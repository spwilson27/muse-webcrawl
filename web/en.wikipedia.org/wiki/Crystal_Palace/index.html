Crystal Palace

The Crystal Palace was a cast-iron and plate-glass structure in Hyde Park, London, built to house the Great Exhibition of 1851. Designed by Joseph Paxton, the enormous prefabricated building — 1,851 feet long — was constructed in less than nine months and contained over 14,000 exhibits from around the world. Its name, coined in the satirical magazine Punch, came from its unprecedented expanses of glass.

Relocation and destruction

After the exhibition, the building was dismantled and re-erected, enlarged, on Sydenham Hill in south London in 1854, where it stood at the centre of gardens designed by Paxton and gave its name to the surrounding district of Crystal Palace. It hosted concerts, exhibitions and sporting events, including early FA Cup finals. On the night of 30 November 1936 the palace was destroyed by fire; despite the efforts of 89 fire engines and nearly 400 firefighters, the blaze — visible across London — gutted the building, and the ruins were demolished in 1941.

Legacy

The Crystal Palace became a symbol of Victorian engineering and the possibilities of industrial prefabrication. The site is now Crystal Palace Park, home to the Crystal Palace Dinosaurs and the Crystal Palace National Sports Centre. See also Great Exhibition and Joseph Paxton.
