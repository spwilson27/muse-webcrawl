Algebraic number

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An algebraic number is a real or complex number that is a root of a non-zero polynomial equation with integer (equivalently, rational) coefficients. Numbers that are not algebraic, such as π and e, are called transcendental numbers.

Examples and properties

All rational numbers are algebraic, being roots of linear equations. Irrational numbers may or may not be algebraic: √2 is algebraic (it satisfies x² − 2 = 0), as is the imaginary unit i and every root of unity, while π was proved transcendental by Lindemann in 1882. The algebraic numbers form a field: sums, products and quotients of algebraic numbers are algebraic. They are also countable, which implies that almost all real and complex numbers are transcendental.

Degree and minimal polynomial

Every algebraic number has a unique monic minimal polynomial with integer coefficients; its degree is called the degree of the number. Rationals have degree 1, quadratic irrationals degree 2, and so on. Algebraic integers are those algebraic numbers whose minimal polynomial has leading coefficient 1 with all coefficients integral, a subclass central to algebraic number theory.

History

The distinction between algebraic and transcendental numbers was articulated in the 19th century; Liouville constructed the first explicit transcendental numbers in 1844, and the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem later settled the ancient problem of squaring the circle by proving π transcendental.
