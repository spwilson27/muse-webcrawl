Caracol is a large ancient Maya archaeological site, located in what is now the Cayo District of Belize. It is situated approximately 40 kilometres (25 mi) south of Xunantunich and the town of San Ignacio, and 15 km (9.3 mi) from the Macal River. Resting on the Vaca Plateau at an elevation of 500 m (1,640 ft) above sea-level, in the foothills of the Maya Mountains, Caracol was one of the most important regional political centres of the Maya Lowlands during the Classic period.

At its height around AD 650, Caracol's urban core covered some 200 square kilometres — an area larger than modern Belize City — and supported an estimated population of more than 100,000 people, maintained by an extensive system of agricultural terraces and connected by raised causeways (sacbeob). The site's name is Spanish for "snail" or "conch shell," given by early explorers because of the winding access road; its ancient emblem glyph has been read as K'uhul Kaanal Ajaw, "Divine Lord of Kaanal" ("Snake Place") — a connection that has fuelled debate about Caracol's relationship to the powerful Kaanul ("Snake") dynasty.

History

Caracol was occupied as early as 600 BC, in the Middle Preclassic period. It rose to prominence in the Early Classic, and its fortunes were transformed by war: in AD 556 (the "Star War" era of Maya politics) Caracol was drawn into the orbit of Calakmul's alliance against Tikal. In AD 562, under Lord Yajaw Te' K'inich II, Caracol defeated the great city of Tikal, killing its king (the "Double Bird" Wak Chan K'awiil) and helping to plunge Tikal into its century-long "hiatus." In AD 599 and again in AD 619 Caracol attacked and defeated Naranjo, and epigraphic records document its further victories, including a star war against Naranjo in 680 tempered by a Naranjo resurgence thereafter.

The Late Classic was Caracol's golden age. Its kings raised the enormous Caana pyramid, laid out a ring of causeways radiating up to 20 km to satellite settlements, and supervised a prosperous, densely settled landscape of terraced fields and reservoirs. Construction essentially ceased in the mid-ninth century, though occupation lingered into the Terminal Classic; the last dated monument was erected in AD 859, and the final recorded date at the site is AD 895. A final occupation phase around AD 1050, evidenced by burials and offerings in abandoned structures, marks the site's end as the Classic Maya world collapsed.

Architecture

The dominant structure is Caana ("Sky Palace"), a triadic acropolis 43 m (141 ft) high — still among the tallest human-made structures in Belize — containing temples, palaces, shrines and tombs, including that of Te' K'ab Chaak, the dynasty's founder, whose burial was announced by the excavators. The core also includes two ballcourts, an astronomically aligned E-Group complex, the plaza groups A and B, and hundreds of mapped residential groups among more than 35,000 structures estimated across the city. Famous carved monuments include Altar 21, which records the Tikal defeat, and the giant ajaw altars marking calendrical period endings.

Investigation

The site was reported in 1937 by logger Rosa Mai, who found monuments while searching for mahogany. Archaeological Commissioner A. H. Anderson named it Caracol and recorded its stelae; Linton Satterthwaite of the University of Pennsylvania mapped and excavated in 1950–53. Since 1985 the Caracol Archaeological Project, directed by Arlen and Diane Chase of the University of Nevada, Las Vegas (now University of Houston), has conducted continuous research, including a landmark LiDAR survey (2009) that revealed the city's full urban sprawl, terraces and causeways beneath the forest canopy.
