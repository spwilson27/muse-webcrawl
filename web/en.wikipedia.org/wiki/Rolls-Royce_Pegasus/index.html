Rolls-Royce Pegasus


Type: Vectored-thrust turbofan
Manufacturer: Bristol Siddeley, later Rolls-Royce
First run: 1959
Major applications: Hawker Siddeley Harrier, Harrier II, Sea Harrier
Thrust: up to 23,800 lbf (Pegasus 11-61)

The Rolls-Royce Pegasus is a turbofan engine originally designed by Bristol Siddeley and manufactured by Rolls-Royce. It powers all versions of the Harrier family of vertical/short takeoff and landing (V/STOL) jets by directing its exhaust through four rotating nozzles that can be swivelled from horizontal, for forward flight, to vertical, for hover, and even slightly forward for braking or reverse thrust in flight.

Development

In the 1950s, Bristol's technical director Stanley Hooker championed a design by Gordon Lewis based on the Bristol Orpheus and Olympus engines. The concept put a large front fan and a core engine on a common shaft, with the fan's cold air discharged through two side nozzles and the hot exhaust through two more, so that the plenum chamber of the earlier Wibault concept was replaced by a practical single engine. The prototype BE.53 led to the Pegasus, first run in 1959, which powered the Hawker P.1127 prototype that made its first hovering flights in 1960 and evolved into the Harrier.

Design

The Pegasus is a two-shaft turbofan with a three-stage fan and an eight-stage high-pressure compressor, the two spools contra-rotating to reduce gyroscopic effects in the hover. The four vectoring nozzles are linked mechanically and driven by air motors fed from the compressor. Because the engine's thrust must balance the aircraft's weight in the hover, the Harrier's reaction control system bleeds compressor air to nozzles at the nose, tail, and wingtips for attitude control at low speed.

Service

Successive marks raised thrust from about 11,000 lbf in early versions to 21,500 lbf in the Pegasus 11 of the Sea Harrier and AV-8B, and 23,800 lbf in the Pegasus 11-61 (F402-RR-408) of late Harrier IIs. The engine saw combat in the Falklands War (1982) with the Sea Harrier and in later conflicts with US Marine Corps and allied Harriers. The Pegasus remained in production and service for over half a century, and its vectored-thrust concept made it one of the most distinctive aero engines ever built.
