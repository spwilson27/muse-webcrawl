Semisimple Lie algebra

In mathematics, a Lie algebra is semisimple if it is a direct sum of simple Lie algebras; that is, if it contains no non-zero abelian ideals. Throughout the article, unless otherwise stated, a Lie algebra is finite-dimensional over a field of characteristic zero, usually the real or complex numbers.

The Dynkin diagrams classifying the simple Lie algebras.

Semisimplicity is a very important concept in the representation theory of Lie algebras: by Weyl's theorem on complete reducibility, every finite-dimensional representation of a semisimple Lie algebra is semisimple, and the finite-dimensional irreducible representations can be classified by their highest weights. The classical theory was developed by Élie Cartan and Wilhelm Killing in the late nineteenth century and completed by Hermann Weyl in the 1920s.

Definitions and equivalent characterisations

Several conditions are equivalent for a finite-dimensional Lie algebra g over a field of characteristic zero:

  • g is a direct sum of simple ideals.
  • The Killing form of g is non-degenerate (Cartan's criterion).
  • g has no non-zero solvable ideals; that is, its radical is zero.
  • Every finite-dimensional representation of g is completely reducible.

The radical of a Lie algebra is its unique maximal solvable ideal; the algebra is semisimple precisely when this ideal vanishes.

Examples

The standard examples of simple — hence semisimple — Lie algebras are the classical matrix algebras: the special linear algebras sln, the orthogonal algebras son, and the symplectic algebras sp2n. By contrast, the Lie algebra of upper-triangular matrices is solvable and not semisimple, and the general linear algebra gln is reductive rather than semisimple: it decomposes as the direct sum of sln and a one-dimensional centre.

Classification

The finite-dimensional simple Lie algebras over the complex numbers are classified into four infinite families — An, Bn, Cn and Dn, corresponding to sln+1, so2n+1, sp2n and so2n — together with five exceptional algebras, of dimensions 14, 52, 78, 133 and 248, associated with the root systems G2, F4, E6, E7 and E8. The classification proceeds by associating to each semisimple Lie algebra a root system in a maximal abelian (Cartan) subalgebra and showing that the root system determines the algebra.

Structure theory

The structure of a complex semisimple Lie algebra is governed by the choice of a Cartan subalgebra h — a maximal abelian subalgebra consisting of semisimple elements. The algebra decomposes into h and one-dimensional root spaces indexed by the roots, a finite set of non-zero linear functionals on h forming a root system. Choosing a set of simple roots yields a Borel subalgebra and a triangular decomposition g = n− ⊕ h ⊕ n+, from which the representation theory is built.

Representation theory

Finite-dimensional representations of a complex semisimple Lie algebra are classified by the theorem of the highest weight: irreducible representations correspond bijectively to dominant integral weights. The dimensions of irreducible representations are given by Weyl's dimension formula, and their characters by the Weyl character formula, both expressed in terms of the root system.
