Tycho Brahe (1546–1601) was a Danish astronomer known for his accurate and comprehensive astronomical observations. Working before the invention of the telescope, he built the finest observatory of his age, Uraniborg, on the island of Hven, and accumulated two decades of positional data accurate to about one arcminute. His assistant Johannes Kepler used those observations to formulate the laws of planetary motion.

Life

Born into the Danish high nobility, Tycho was raised by his uncle and studied at Copenhagen and German universities. In 1566 he lost part of his nose in a sword duel with a fellow student and thereafter wore a prosthetic nose. A solar eclipse in 1560 and the approach of astronomy through ephemerides turned him against his family's intended legal career.

The new star and the comet

In November 1572 Tycho observed a brilliant new star (the supernova SN 1572) in Cassiopeia and showed that it had no measurable parallax, placing it in the supposedly changeless celestial realm and undermining Aristotelian cosmology. His observations of the Great Comet of 1577 similarly placed comets beyond the Moon, refuting the idea of solid celestial spheres.

Uraniborg

In 1576 Frederick II granted Tycho the island of Hven and funds to build Uraniborg ("castle of Urania"), an elaborate observatory and alchemical laboratory with a staff of assistants, large quadrants and sextants, a paper mill and printing press. Tycho and his team recorded star positions and planetary motions with unmatched precision from 1576 to 1597.

The Tychonic system

Tycho proposed a geo-heliocentric compromise: the Sun and Moon orbit the stationary Earth, while the other planets orbit the Sun. It preserved a stationary Earth (which he believed physics and scripture required) while matching Copernican predictions, and was the leading rival to Copernicus into the seventeenth century.

Death and legacy

After falling out with Christian IV, Tycho left Denmark and settled in Prague as Imperial Mathematician to Rudolf II, where Kepler joined him in 1600. He died in 1601; legend says of a burst bladder after refusing to leave a banquet, though modern analysis of exhumed remains suggests other causes. His data, published in the Rudolphine Tables (1627), underpinned Kepler's elliptic orbits and Newtonian gravitation research.
