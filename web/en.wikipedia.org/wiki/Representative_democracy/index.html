Representative democracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Representative democracy is a type of democracy where elected persons represent a group of people, in contrast to direct democracy. Nearly all modern Western-style democracies function as some type of representative democracy: the United Kingdom is a constitutional monarchy with a parliamentary system and a sovereign Parliament; the United States is a federal presidential republic; Germany is a federal parliamentary republic.

Features

Elections under universal (or near-universal) suffrage choose representatives for fixed terms; representatives deliberate and vote in legislatures, are expected to be accountable through re-election, recall (rare), a free press and public scrutiny; and constitutional limits, rights protections and independent courts usually qualify majority rule — the full package often termed liberal democracy. Representatives may understand themselves as delegates bound by constituents' wishes, trustees exercising independent judgement (in Edmund Burke's famous formulation), or agents of their party's programme; real politics mixes all three.

Criticisms and developments

Critics point to representation gaps (descriptive under-representation of women and minorities), party discipline overriding constituency views, money in politics, and declining turnout and party membership — a "crisis of representation" motivating proportional electoral reform, quotas, citizens' assemblies and digital participation experiments. Comparative research generally finds representative democracies with strong institutions score highest on rights, stability and policy responsiveness.
