Gunboat diplomacy

Gunboat diplomacy (or big stick diplomacy in American usage) is the pursuit of foreign policy objectives with the aid of a conspicuous display of naval power, implying or constituting a direct threat of warfare should terms not be agreeable to the superior force. The term derives from the nineteenth century, when imperial powers used small but heavily armed warships — gunboats — to coerce weaker states into concessions.

A gunboat on station, the classic instrument of coercive diplomacy

History

Classic episodes include Commodore Matthew Perry's expedition to Japan in 1853–54, which used American warships to press Japan into opening trade; British and French naval pressure during the Opium Wars with Qing China; and the Venezuela crisis of 1902–03, when British, German and Italian warships blockaded Venezuela to collect debts. The practice was closely associated with European imperialism in Africa and Asia and with American policy in the Caribbean and Latin America under the Monroe Doctrine and its Roosevelt Corollary.

Modern usage

Although the age of the literal gunboat has passed, the term remains in use for coercive naval diplomacy: port visits, exercises and deployments intended to signal resolve or intimidate an adversary. Modern "naval diplomacy" also includes benign uses such as humanitarian relief and presence missions, but the coercive sense of gunboat diplomacy persists in political commentary, including in disputes over maritime claims in the South China Sea.
