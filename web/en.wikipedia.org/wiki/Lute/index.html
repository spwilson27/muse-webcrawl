A lute (or luth) is any plucked string instrument with a neck and a deep round back enclosing a hollow cavity, usually with a sound hole or opening in the body. The European lute — the instrument most commonly meant by the word — descends from the Arab oud brought to medieval Europe, and was the pre-eminent domestic and court instrument of the Renaissance and Baroque eras. Its body is built of thin wooden ribs forming a teardrop-shaped bowl, with a flat spruce soundboard, a carved rosette ("rose") soundhole, and a pegbox bent back at a sharp angle; the strings, historically of gut and arranged in double courses, are plucked with the fingertips.

History

The word "lute" derives (via French luth and Spanish laúd) from Arabic al-ʿūd, "the wood". The instrument reached Europe through Moorish Spain and via the Crusades, and by the 16th century it dominated European art music: more music was published for the lute than for any other solo instrument in the Renaissance. Composers such as John Dowland in England and Francesco da Milano in Italy expanded its repertoire, and large tablature collections preserve thousands of intabulations, dances and fantasias. In the Baroque era the lute grew additional bass strings, spawning larger variants — the theorbo and archlute — used chiefly for basso continuo accompaniment.

Decline and revival

By the mid-18th century the lute had been eclipsed by the harpsichord and the emerging piano, and its complex tuning and quiet voice fell from fashion. The 20th-century early music revival restored it to the concert stage; performers such as Julian Bream, Konrad Ragossnig, Hopkinson Smith and Paul O'Dette helped re-establish the Renaissance and Baroque lute repertoire, and modern makers build instruments after surviving historical examples.

Construction and playing

Renaissance lutes typically had 6–10 courses (pairs) of strings in the tuning g–c–f–a–d′–g′, with tied gut frets; Baroque lutes standardised on 11 or 13 courses in a D-minor tuning. Music was written in tablature, which shows finger placement rather than pitch. Modern "lute family" also includes the mandora, gittern, cittern and related instruments.
