The Cooper test is a physical fitness test devised by Kenneth H. Cooper in 1968 for use by the United States military. In its original form, the point of the test is to run as far as possible within 12 minutes. The distance covered is used to estimate the subject's maximal oxygen uptake (VO2 max) and aerobic fitness.

Procedure

The test is intended to be run at a steady, maximal sustainable pace. The subject should warm up, and the test is best performed on a standard 400 m running track so that the distance covered can be measured easily. The test is self-administered in many school and military settings, although Cooper recommended medical clearance for older or untrained participants.

Estimation of VO2 max

An approximate VO2 max in ml/(kg·min) can be calculated from the distance d in metres covered in 12 minutes by the formula VO2 max ≈ (d − 504.9) / 44.73. Results are also compared against normative tables by age and sex; for example, a distance above roughly 2,800 m in 12 minutes rates "excellent" for men aged 20–29, while under 1,600 m rates "very poor".

Use

The Cooper test is widely used by schools, the military, police forces and football referees to assess aerobic endurance, and forms part of fitness testing in many countries. Variations include the Cooper 1.5-mile run test. Criticisms include its dependence on pacing skill and motivation, and the fact that laboratory-measured VO2 max correlates only approximately with test results.
