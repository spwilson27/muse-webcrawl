Bloom syndrome (congenital telangiectatic erythema) is a rare autosomal recessive genetic disorder characterised by short stature, a sun-sensitive red rash over the nose and cheeks, genomic instability and a greatly increased predisposition to cancer. It is caused by mutations in the BLM gene, which encodes a RecQ DNA helicase involved in maintaining genome stability during replication and repair.

Signs and symptoms

Affected individuals are small before and after birth and remain well below average height as adults. The characteristic facial rash (telangiectatic erythema) appears in infancy on sun-exposed areas. Other features include a high-pitched voice, learning difficulties in some patients, recurrent infections due to reduced immunoglobulin levels, insulin-resistant diabetes, and male infertility. Cancers of many types develop at unusually young ages and are the leading cause of death, with average life expectancy around the late twenties to thirties, though this varies.

Genetics and diagnosis

Without functional BLM helicase, chromosomes show a dramatically elevated rate of sister chromatid exchange and quadriradial figures, a cytogenetic signature long used in diagnosis; today testing is by sequencing of the BLM gene. The disorder is more common in people of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry, in whom a single founder mutation accounts for most cases, but it occurs worldwide.

Management

There is no cure; management involves rigorous cancer surveillance, prompt treatment of infections and diabetes, sun protection and avoidance of unnecessary radiation and certain chemotherapies that damage DNA.
