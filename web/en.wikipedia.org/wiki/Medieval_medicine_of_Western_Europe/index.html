Medieval medicine of Western Europe

Medieval medicine of Western Europe was a mixture of existing ideas from antiquity and spiritual influences. In the Early Middle Ages, following the fall of the Western Roman Empire, standard medical knowledge was based chiefly upon surviving Greek and Roman texts preserved in monasteries. Medieval medicine is widely misunderstood, having been judged harshly by later observers; in fact, practitioners combined observation, herbal remedies, and theoretical frameworks inherited from Galen and Hippocrates.

Humoral theory

The dominant framework was the theory of the four humours — blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile — corresponding to the four elements and four temperaments. Health was a balance of the humours; disease, an imbalance. Treatments such as bloodletting, dietary regulation, and purging aimed to restore balance. Diagnosis relied heavily on uroscopy (inspection of urine), pulse-taking, and astrological considerations.

Monastic and scholastic medicine

Monasteries preserved and copied medical texts, maintained herb gardens, and cared for the sick in infirmaries. The medical school at Salerno (the Schola Medica Salernitana), flourishing from the 9th to 13th centuries, became famous throughout Europe, and from the 11th century the translation movement rendered Arabic medical works — notably Avicenna's Canon of Medicine and works of Rhazes and Al-Zahrawi — into Latin, enriching the Western tradition.

Universities and practitioners

From the 12th century, universities at Bologna, Montpellier, Paris, and Oxford taught medicine as a learned profession based on Galen, Hippocrates, and Avicenna. Below university physicians stood surgeons, barber-surgeons, apothecaries, midwives, and folk healers, who provided most day-to-day care. Anatomy teaching advanced with Mondino de' Liuzzi's Anathomia (1316), the first modern anatomy manual based on human dissection.

Plague and public health

The Black Death (1347–1351) killed a large fraction of Europe's population and spurred public health measures such as quarantine (first institutionalised at Ragusa in 1377), plague orders, and the founding of hospitals and pesthouses. Leprosaria and hospitals run by religious orders, such as the Order of St John, provided institutional care.
