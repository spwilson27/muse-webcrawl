Mask of Agamemnon

The Mask of Agamemnon is a gold funeral mask discovered at the ancient Greek site of Mycenae in 1876 by the German archaeologist Heinrich Schliemann. Schliemann believed that he had discovered the tomb of the legendary Mycenaean king Agamemnon, hero of the Trojan War, and famously reported that he had "gazed upon the face of Agamemnon". The mask is now displayed in the National Archaeological Museum of Athens.

Discovery

Schliemann excavated the shaft graves of Grave Circle A at Mycenae in 1876 under the supervision of the Greek Archaeological Society. Among the five gold funerary masks recovered from the graves, the finest — found in Grave V — came to be known as the Mask of Agamemnon. The mask depicts a bearded man with distinctive features, including a moustache, and is made of a thicker sheet of gold than the other masks, worked in repoussé with cut-out eyes.

Dating

Modern scholarship dates the mask to around 1550–1500 BC, several centuries before the period in which a historical Agamemnon would have lived (the Trojan War is traditionally placed in the 13th or 12th century BC). It therefore cannot have belonged to Agamemnon himself, though it remains one of the finest surviving artefacts of Mycenaean civilisation.

Authenticity debate

The mask's authenticity was questioned in the 20th century, most pointedly by the scholars William Calder III and David Traill, who noted Schliemann's history of embellishment and suggested the mask might have been forged or "improved". However, the prevailing view among archaeologists is that the mask is a genuine Bronze Age artefact, stylistically consistent with the other Mycenaean masks, and its authenticity is accepted by the National Archaeological Museum.
