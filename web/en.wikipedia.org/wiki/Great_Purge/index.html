The Great Purge, or the Great Terror (Russian: Большой террор), was a political purge in the Soviet Union that occurred from 1936 to 1938 and was ordered by Joseph Stalin to consolidate his power. It involved a large-scale repression of wealthy peasants (kulaks), a series of show trials and executions of Communist Party officials, a purge of the Red Army high command, and widespread police surveillance, suspicion of saboteurs, imprisonment, and arbitrary executions by the NKVD.

Course of the terror

The assassination of Leningrad party chief Sergei Kirov in December 1934 supplied the pretext for escalating repression. Three great Moscow show trials (1936, 1937, 1938) condemned Old Bolsheviks including Zinoviev, Kamenev, Bukharin, and Rykov on fabricated charges of espionage and wrecking, secured through torture and threats against their families. In 1937 the purge consumed the military: Marshal Tukhachevsky and most of the senior officer corps were shot, a decapitation of the Red Army on the eve of the Second World War.

The mass operations of 1937–38 went far beyond the Party. Under NKVD Order No. 00447, regional "troikas" processed quotas of "former kulaks" and other "anti-Soviet elements"; the "national operations" targeted Poles, Germans, Koreans, and other diaspora nationalities. Archival research after 1991 documented roughly 1.5 million arrests in 1937–38 and at least 681,692 executions — a figure historians treat as a floor. NKVD chief Nikolai Yezhov, who presided over the bloodiest phase (the Yezhovshchina), was himself arrested and shot in 1940 after being replaced by Lavrentiy Beria, under whom the mass operations wound down.

Aftermath

The terror was first acknowledged officially in Nikita Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" of 1956, and victims were rehabilitated in waves during the Khrushchev thaw and again under glasnost. Memorial societies documented execution sites such as Butovo and Kommunarka near Moscow. Historians continue to debate Stalin's motives — paranoia, preparation for war, and the logic of totalitarian control all feature in explanations — as well as the total death toll, including deaths in custody and in the Gulag.
