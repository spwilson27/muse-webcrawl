Library of Alexandria

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Artistic reconstruction of the Great Library's reading hall, lined with scroll niches]

The Great Library of Alexandria, in Egypt, was one of the largest and most significant libraries of the ancient world, part of the larger research institution known as the Mouseion (Museum) founded under Ptolemy I and Ptolemy II in the 3rd century BC. Dedicated to the Muses, it aimed to collect all the world's knowledge — its scrolls, said in later sources to number in the hundreds of thousands, were gathered by royal purchase, confiscation from ships docking at Alexandria, and an aggressive programme of copying and translation (including, by tradition, the Septuagint Greek Bible). Scholars such as Euclid, Eratosthenes (its chief librarian), Aristarchus and Herophilus worked in its orbit. Its decline was gradual — fires (notably during Caesar's Alexandrian War, 48 BC), Roman-era neglect and religious violence (the destruction of its daughter library in the Serapeum, 391 AD) — and its 'loss' became the West's great symbol of vanished knowledge.

The Mouseion and its scholars

The Library was not a public library but a royal research institute: scholars received salaries, lodging and tax exemption to edit, catalogue and surpass the classics. Callimachus' Pinakes (120 scrolls) was the first systematic library catalogue; Zenodotus and Aristarchus produced the critical editions of Homer from which our text descends; Eratosthenes measured the Earth's circumference to within a few per cent; Herophilus founded human anatomy on dissection. Ships entering Alexandria were searched for books, which were copied — the copies, by one proud account, sometimes returned to their owners while the originals stayed, marked 'from the ships'.

Destruction — and what actually happened

No single catastrophe destroyed the Library. Caesar's fire in 48 BC burned warehouses of scrolls near the harbour (how many, and whether of the Library itself, is unclear); the palace quarter decayed under Rome; Aurelian's reconquest (270s AD) devastated the Brucheion district where the Mouseion stood; and in 391 AD the Serapeum — housing a 'daughter' collection — was demolished by a Christian mob with imperial sanction. The later story that Caliph Omar ordered the books burned in 642 AD is a 13th-century fabrication. The truth — slow strangulation as patronage withered — is less dramatic and more typical of how libraries die.
