Albert Kahn (architect)

Born: March 21, 1869, Rhaunen, Germany
Died: December 8, 1942, Detroit, Michigan, US
Occupation: Architect

Albert Kahn (March 21, 1869 – December 8, 1942) was an American architect regarded by many as the foremost industrial architect of his time. Sometimes called the "architect of Detroit", he designed industrial plant complexes such as the Ford River Rouge automobile complex, and office buildings, churches, and homes in the Detroit area.

Early life and career

Kahn was born in Rhaunen, in the Rhineland of Germany, and emigrated with his family to Detroit in 1880. He trained in the office of Mason & Rice, won a scholarship to study in Europe, and in 1902 formed a partnership that grew into Albert Kahn Associates. His younger brother Julius Kahn, an engineer, developed the "Kahn system" of reinforced concrete construction, which the firm used to build factories with large, fireproof, open floor spans flooded with natural light.

Industrial architecture

Kahn's firm designed factories for Packard, Ford, General Motors and Chrysler, including Highland Park Ford Plant, where the moving assembly line was introduced, and the vast River Rouge complex in Dearborn. By the late 1920s the office employed hundreds and produced a significant share of all architect-designed industrial buildings in the United States. Between 1929 and 1932, Kahn's firm also designed hundreds of factories in the Soviet Union under contract with Amtorg, training Soviet engineers in modern industrial design methods.

Other works

Beyond factories, Kahn designed the Fisher Building and the General Motors Building in Detroit, the Clements Library and Hill Auditorium at the University of Michigan, and numerous residences and synagogues. During the Second World War his firm converted to defence work, designing plants such as the Willow Run bomber factory.

Legacy

Kahn died in Detroit in 1942. His firm, Albert Kahn Associates, continues to practise, and his daylight factory designs influenced industrial architecture worldwide, including the work of European modernists who admired their functionalism.
