Shadow cabinet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A shadow cabinet is a feature of the Westminster system of government. It consists of a senior group of opposition spokespeople who, under the leadership of the Leader of the Opposition, form an alternative cabinet to that of the government, with each member "shadowing" or mirroring a corresponding minister. Shadow ministers scrutinise their counterparts' departments, develop alternative policies and present their party as a government-in-waiting.

Practice

The institution is formalised in the UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, where shadow ministers receive some official recognition and (for the leader and certain officeholders) salaries. It originated in 19th-century British practice and the term dates from the early 20th century. Shadow cabinets train future ministers, discipline opposition messaging, and structure accountability through question time and select committees; their internal politics (election by caucus in Australian Labor practice, appointment by the leader in the UK Conservative and Labour traditions) shape leadership contests. In most non-Westminster systems the functions are performed less formally by party spokespeople and parliamentary group leaders.
