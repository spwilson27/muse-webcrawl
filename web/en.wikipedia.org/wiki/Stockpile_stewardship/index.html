Stockpile stewardship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stockpile stewardship refers to the United States programme of reliability testing and maintenance of its nuclear weapons without the use of live nuclear testing. Because no new nuclear weapons have been produced by the United States since 1992, and the existing weapons are ageing, stockpile stewardship is the mechanism by which the safety, security, and reliability of the arsenal are certified.

Background

The United States observed a moratorium on nuclear explosive testing beginning in 1992, the year of the last test at the Nevada Test Site, and later signed the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty in 1996 (which the Senate declined to ratify in 1999). In response, the Department of Energy established the Stockpile Stewardship and Management Program in 1995 to maintain the stockpile using science-based methods instead of explosive testing.

Methods

The programme relies on:

  • Supercomputing — large-scale simulations of weapon physics run by the Advanced Simulation and Computing Program at the three weapons laboratories (Los Alamos, Lawrence Livermore, and Sandia).
  • Experimental facilities — such as the National Ignition Facility (NIF) at Livermore, the Z machine at Sandia, and the Dual-Axis Radiographic Hydrodynamic Test Facility (DARHT) at Los Alamos, which study materials and implosion dynamics at extreme conditions without nuclear yield.
  • Surveillance — routine disassembly and inspection of sampled weapons to detect ageing and defects in components such as plutonium pits, high explosives, and electronics.
  • Life extension programmes — refurbishment of warheads (such as the W76 and B61 programmes) to extend service life while replacing ageing components.

Annual certification

Each year the directors of the three laboratories formally assess and certify to the President whether the stockpile remains safe and reliable without nuclear testing.
