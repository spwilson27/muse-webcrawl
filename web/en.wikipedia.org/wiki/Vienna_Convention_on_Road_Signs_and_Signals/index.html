Vienna Convention on Road Signs and Signals

The Vienna Convention on Road Signs and Signals, adopted on 8 November 1968, is a multilateral treaty designed to increase road safety and aid international road traffic by standardising the signing system for road traffic in use internationally. The convention was agreed upon by the United Nations Economic and Social Council at its Conference on Road Traffic in Vienna from 7 October to 8 November 1968, and came into force on 6 June 1978.

The convention builds on the 1949 Geneva Protocol on Road Signs and Signals and the 1931 Convention concerning the Unification of Road Signals. It is complemented by the Vienna Convention on Road Traffic, concluded the same day, which standardises traffic laws.

Contents

The treaty classifies road signs into categories including danger warning signs, priority signs, prohibitory or restrictive signs, mandatory signs, information and service signs, direction and position signs, and additional panels. It prescribes standard shapes and colours: for example, warning signs are generally triangular with red borders, prohibitory signs circular, and mandatory signs circular with blue backgrounds. It also standardises traffic lights, road markings, and signals used at level crossings.

Parties

As of recent years, the convention has been ratified by around 70 countries, mostly in Europe, Central Asia, and parts of Africa and Latin America. Notable non-parties include the United States, China, and Japan, which use their own signing systems, although many of their signs follow similar principles.
