Judicial review in English law

Judicial review in English law is a procedure by which the High Court supervises the decisions and actions of public bodies to ensure they act lawfully, fairly, and reasonably. It is a cornerstone of administrative law in England and Wales and a key mechanism of the rule of law where there is no codified constitution.

Grounds

In Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service [1985] (the GCHQ case), Lord Diplock classified the grounds as:

  • Illegality — the decision-maker misdirected itself in law, acted ultra vires, or fettered its discretion.
  • Irrationality — Wednesbury unreasonableness: a decision so unreasonable no reasonable authority could have made it.
  • Procedural impropriety — failure to follow required procedures or the rules of natural justice, including the right to a fair hearing and the rule against bias.

Since the Human Rights Act 1998, proportionality has become an additional ground where Convention rights are engaged.

Procedure and remedies

Claims are brought in the Administrative Court under CPR Part 54, normally within three months, and require permission. Remedies are discretionary and include quashing orders, prohibiting orders, mandatory orders, declarations, and injunctions. Judicial review concerns the lawfulness of a decision, not its merits, though the boundary is debated in cases such as R (Miller) v Prime Minister [2019] on prorogation.

Scope

Only public bodies — and private bodies exercising public functions — are amenable to review. Parliamentary sovereignty means courts cannot strike down primary legislation, though they may issue a declaration of incompatibility under the Human Rights Act.
