First Bank of the United States

The First Bank of the United States was a national bank chartered by the United States Congress in 1791 and signed into law by President George Washington. Proposed by Secretary of the Treasury Alexander Hamilton as the centrepiece of his financial programme, the bank was intended to stabilise the young republic's finances, manage the federal government's funds, issue a uniform paper currency, and provide credit to commerce.

Establishment

Hamilton's proposal, set out in his Report on a National Bank (1790), met fierce opposition from Thomas Jefferson and James Madison, who argued that the Constitution did not authorise Congress to charter a bank. Hamilton countered with the doctrine of implied powers, grounded in the Necessary and Proper Clause. Washington, after soliciting written opinions from both sides, signed the bill on 25 February 1791. The bank was capitalised at $10 million, of which the federal government subscribed one fifth, and it opened in Philadelphia in December 1791, later establishing branches in other major ports.

Operations

The bank acted as the government's fiscal agent, holding federal deposits, facilitating tax collection and transferring public funds. Its banknotes, redeemable in specie, circulated nationally and helped discipline the notes of state-chartered banks, since the First Bank could demand payment in gold or silver from banks whose notes it had accumulated. It also lent to merchants and to the government itself.

Expiry of the charter

When the twenty-year charter came up for renewal in 1811, opposition from state banks, agrarian interests and strict constructionists prevailed. The recharter bill was defeated by a single vote in the House of Representatives, and in the Senate Vice President George Clinton broke a tie against renewal. The bank's assets were liquidated. The financial chaos of the War of 1812 persuaded Congress to reverse course, chartering the Second Bank of the United States in 1816.

Building

The bank's Philadelphia headquarters, completed in 1797 on South Third Street, is one of the earliest American examples of monumental Greek Revival design and is preserved today as part of Independence National Historical Park.
