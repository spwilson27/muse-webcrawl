The Emergency Broadcast System (EBS), sometimes called the Emergency Action Notification System (EANS), was an emergency warning system used in the United States. It was implemented in 1963 to replace the CONELRAD system, and was itself replaced by the Emergency Alert System (EAS) in 1997.

The EBS was designed to allow the President of the United States to address the American public in the event of a national emergency, particularly a nuclear attack. Although the system was never used for its intended purpose of a wartime emergency, it was activated hundreds of times for civil emergencies and severe weather warnings at the state and local level.

History

The system grew out of Cold War civil defense planning. Under CONELRAD (Control of Electromagnetic Radiation), radio stations were required to go off the air or operate at reduced power to prevent enemy bombers from using broadcast signals as navigation aids. The EBS, established in 1963, shifted the emphasis toward rapid presidential communication and local emergency information.

Stations were required to conduct weekly tests of the system. These tests became a familiar part of American broadcasting: a distinctive two-tone attention signal (combining 853 Hz and 960 Hz sine waves) followed by an announcer reading a script beginning "This is a test. This station is conducting a test of the Emergency Broadcast System. This is only a test." The script closed with the reassurance that, had this been an actual emergency, listeners would have been instructed where to tune for news and official information.

Activation procedure

An Emergency Action Notification was initiated by the White House and transmitted through a chain of primary stations. Broadcasters authenticated the message using sealed code words kept in an envelope at each station. Upon a genuine activation, stations would either carry the presidential message or shut down, depending on their role in the plan.

False alarms

The most famous EBS incident occurred on February 20, 1971, when an erroneous Emergency Action Notification was transmitted nationwide after a teletype operator at NORAD's Cheyenne Mountain facility accidentally played the wrong tape during a test. Many stations failed to react, exposing weaknesses in the system; the mistake was cancelled about forty minutes later using a code word that, ironically, was "hatefulness".

Legacy

The EBS was replaced on January 1, 1997, by the Emergency Alert System, which uses digital SAME (Specific Area Message Encoding) technology to target alerts geographically. The phrase "This is a test" and the system's attention tone remain embedded in American popular culture.
