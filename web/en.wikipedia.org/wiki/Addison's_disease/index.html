Addison's disease, also known as primary adrenal insufficiency, is a long-term endocrine disorder in which the adrenal glands do not produce enough of the steroid hormones cortisol and often aldosterone. It is named after Thomas Addison, who described it in 1855.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms develop gradually and are non-specific: fatigue, muscle weakness, weight loss, nausea and abdominal pain. More distinctive features include darkening of the skin (hyperpigmentation, from excess ACTH), low blood pressure — especially on standing — and craving for salt. An acute deterioration, an adrenal crisis, can occur under physical stress such as infection or surgery and is a medical emergency.

Causes and diagnosis

In developed countries the commonest cause is autoimmune adrenalitis; historically tuberculosis was the leading cause and remains important worldwide. Diagnosis is by blood tests showing low cortisol with high ACTH, confirmed by the ACTH stimulation test, along with low aldosterone and often low sodium and high potassium.

Treatment

Treatment is lifelong hormone replacement: hydrocortisone (or another glucocorticoid) for cortisol and fludrocortisone for aldosterone, with increased doses during illness. With treatment, life expectancy is near normal; US President John F. Kennedy is a well-known historical case.
