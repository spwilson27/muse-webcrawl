Legionnaires' disease

Legionnaires' disease is a severe form of pneumonia — lung inflammation usually caused by infection with bacteria of the genus Legionella, most commonly Legionella pneumophila. It is one of two diseases caused by Legionella, the other being the milder Pontiac fever. The disease acquired its name from the July 1976 outbreak at a convention of the American Legion in Philadelphia, where 182 attendees became ill and 29 died.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually develop 2 to 10 days after exposure and include high fever, chills, cough, muscle aches and headaches; some patients develop diarrhoea, confusion and kidney or liver dysfunction. Without treatment the disease can be fatal, particularly in older adults, smokers and people with weakened immune systems.

Transmission

Legionella bacteria occur naturally in freshwater and proliferate in warm artificial water systems such as cooling towers, hot tubs, large plumbing systems and air-conditioning equipment. People are infected by inhaling fine droplets of contaminated water; it does not usually spread from person to person.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is most often made by a urinary antigen test or culture of respiratory secretions. Treatment is with antibiotics such as levofloxacin or azithromycin. Prompt treatment substantially reduces the risk of death.

History

The causative organism was isolated in 1977 by scientists at the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention investigating the 1976 Philadelphia outbreak. Retrospective testing linked earlier unexplained outbreaks, including one at a 1965 psychiatric hospital in Washington, D.C., to the same bacterium.
