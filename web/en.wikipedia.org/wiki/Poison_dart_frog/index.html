Poison dart frog (also known as dart-poison frog, poison frog or formerly known as poison arrow frog) is the common name of a group of frogs in the family Dendrobatidae which are native to tropical Central and South America. These species are diurnal and often have brightly colored bodies. This bright coloration is correlated with the toxicity of the species, making them aposematic. Some species of the family Dendrobatidae exhibit extremely bright coloration along with high toxicity — a feature known as aposematism. The frogs secrete alkaloid toxins through their skin, which they obtain from their diet of mites, ants, and other arthropods.

History and taxonomy

The family Dendrobatidae contains over 200 species distributed among some 13–17 genera, including Dendrobates, Oophaga, Phyllobates, Ranitomeya and Epipedobates. Indigenous Emberá people of Colombia have used the skin secretions of the golden poison frog (Phyllobates terribilis) to tip blow darts for hunting, giving the group its common name; the secretions of that species include the potent neurotoxin batrachotoxin.

Characteristics

Most species of poison dart frogs are small, sometimes less than 1.5 cm (0.59 in) in adult length, although a few grow up to 6 cm (2.4 in). They weigh about 2 grams, depending on the size of the frog. Unusually for frogs, many dendrobatids show elaborate parental care: males or females carry tadpoles on their backs to small pools of water in bromeliads or leaf axils, and in Oophaga species the mother returns to feed the tadpoles with unfertilised eggs.

Toxicity

Many poison dart frogs secrete lipophilic alkaloid toxins such as batrachotoxin, epibatidine, histrionicotoxin, and pumiliotoxin through their skin. These chemical defenses can protect them from predators. Captive-bred frogs lose their toxicity because the alkaloids are sequestered from dietary arthropods that are absent in captivity.

Conservation

Many species are threatened by habitat loss, the pet trade, and the fungal disease chytridiomycosis. Numerous species are listed by the IUCN, and the family is included in CITES Appendix II.
