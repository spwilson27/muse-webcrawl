Dynastic union

The Iberian Union (1580–1640) joined the crowns of Spain and Portugal in one monarch.

A dynastic union is a form of political union in which two or more states are linked only by sharing the same monarch—a personal union created by dynastic marriage or inheritance. Unlike a real union or a federation, the states remain legally separate, each keeping its own laws, institutions, courts and often its own coinage and army; the sovereign is simply king (or queen) of each of them.

Examples

Dynastic unions shaped much of European history. The marriage of Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile in 1469 joined the two crowns that became Spain. The Iberian Union (1580–1640) placed Portugal and its empire under the Spanish Habsburgs. England and Scotland shared a monarch from the Union of the Crowns in 1603 until the political union of 1707, and Great Britain and Hanover were joined from 1714 to 1837, when different succession laws separated them. The Polish–Lithuanian union began dynastically with the marriage of Jogaila and Jadwiga in 1386 before deepening into the Commonwealth of 1569.

Instability

Because the bond rests on one person, dynastic unions are inherently fragile: a succession dispute, differing inheritance laws (for instance, Salic law excluding female heirs) or a monarch's death without a common heir can dissolve them, and wars of succession—such as the War of the Spanish Succession—often resulted. Many such unions nevertheless hardened over time into lasting composite states or full political unions.
