War of the Fourth Coalition

The War of the Fourth Coalition (1806–1807) was fought between Napoleonic France and the Fourth Coalition of Prussia, Russia, Saxony, Sweden and the United Kingdom during the Napoleonic Wars. Prussia, alarmed by France's reorganisation of Germany after the Treaty of Pressburg — culminating in the dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire and the creation of the Confederation of the Rhine — issued an ultimatum in October 1806 demanding that all French forces withdraw east of the Rhine. Napoleon invaded instead, and in one of the most famous campaigns in military history destroyed the Prussian army in six weeks at the twin battles of Jena and Auerstedt (14 October 1806) and occupied Berlin. The war continued through the winter in East Prussia and Poland against Russia, through the carnage of Eylau (February 1807) to the decisive French victory at Friedland (June 1807), and ended with the Treaties of Tilsit, which made France master of Europe west of Russia and reduced Prussia to a second-rate power.

The Jena–Auerstedt campaign

The Prussian army of 1806 still basked in the reputation of Frederick the Great, but its command, tactics and staff work were obsolete; its senior officers were old (the Duke of Brunswick was 71) and its councils divided. Napoleon marched into Saxony in three great columns and turned the Prussian flank through the Thuringian Forest. On 14 October he defeated Hohenlohe's army at Jena — believing he faced the Prussian main body — while at Auerstedt, 15 km away, Davout's single corps of some 27,000 men defeated the actual Prussian main army of over 50,000 under Brunswick, who was mortally wounded. The double defeat unhinged the Prussian state: the retreat became a rout, fortresses capitulated with scarcely a shot (Erfurt, Spandau, Stettin — the latter to a handful of French hussars), Blücher surrendered at Lübeck, and on 27 October Napoleon entered Berlin, sending Frederick the Great's sword and watch to Paris as trophies. From Berlin he issued the decrees inaugurating the Continental System, his economic blockade of Britain.

The winter campaign in Poland

Prussia's remnants fell back behind the Vistula and joined the Russians under Bennigsen. Napoleon advanced into Poland, welcomed by Polish nationalists hoping for a restored state, and fought a miserable winter campaign of mud and snow: an indecisive action at Pułtusk in December, then, on 7–8 February 1807, the battle of Eylau — fought in a blizzard, the bloodiest battle of the era to that date, with perhaps 30,000–50,000 casualties on the two sides, Augereau's corps destroyed in the snow, and Murat's great cavalry charge saving the French army. Both sides claimed victory; the Russians withdrew, and the armies went into winter quarters, shaken.

Friedland and Tilsit

After a spring refit and the costly siege of Danzig, Napoleon returned to the offensive in June 1807. Bennigsen's attack on Ney at Guttstadt was beaten off, Heilsberg was indecisive, and on 14 June 1807 — the anniversary of Marengo — Napoleon caught the Russian army with its back to the river Alle at Friedland and destroyed it, Lannes pinning the Russians while the French main body crushed their left in an afternoon's fighting. Russian losses were perhaps 20,000 or more.

Alexander I sued for peace. The two emperors met on a raft in the Niemen at Tilsit on 25 June; the treaties signed there in July divided Europe between them: Russia joined the Continental System, recognised Napoleon's arrangements in Germany and Italy, and received a free hand against Sweden and the Ottomans; Prussia, whose queen Luise pleaded in vain with Napoleon, lost all its territory west of the Elbe and its Polish lands (from which the French client Duchy of Warsaw was created), was reduced to about half its population, saddled with a huge indemnity and French occupation until it was paid, and its army later capped at 42,000 men.

Consequences

Tilsit marked the zenith of Napoleon's power — master of the Continent from the Niemen to the Pyrenees, allied with Russia, with only Britain undefeated at sea. For Prussia the catastrophe provoked the Stein–Hardenberg reforms: the abolition of serfdom, municipal self-government, and under Scharnhorst and Gneisenau the rebuilding of the army that would return in 1813. The Franco-Russian alliance, meanwhile, rested on the Continental System, whose burdens on Russian trade would drive the two empires to war in 1812.
