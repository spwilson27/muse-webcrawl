Genetic testing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genetic testing, also known as DNA testing, is the analysis of DNA, chromosomes, or proteins to identify changes associated with inherited disorders, ancestry, or other traits. It is used to diagnose genetic conditions, predict disease risk, guide treatment, establish biological relationships, and identify individuals in forensic investigations. More than 77,000 genetic tests have been registered, and the field has expanded rapidly with the advent of low-cost genome sequencing.

Types

  • Diagnostic testing — used to identify or rule out a specific genetic or chromosomal condition in a symptomatic individual.
  • Predictive and presymptomatic testing — detects mutations associated with disorders that appear later in life, such as Huntington's disease or BRCA1/2-related cancers.
  • Carrier testing — identifies people who carry one copy of a recessive disease-causing variant.
  • Prenatal testing and newborn screening — testing during pregnancy or shortly after birth; non-invasive prenatal testing analyses fetal DNA in maternal blood.
  • Pharmacogenomic testing — predicts response to medications, e.g. drug metabolism variants.
  • Forensic and genealogical testing — DNA profiling for identification, criminal investigation, paternity, and ancestry inference, including direct-to-consumer services.

Methods

Techniques range from karyotyping and fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) for chromosomal abnormalities, through targeted variant assays and gene panels, to exome and whole-genome sequencing. Molecular methods include PCR, Sanger sequencing, SNP arrays, and next-generation sequencing; results are interpreted against databases of known pathogenic and benign variants, a process complicated by variants of uncertain significance.

Ethics and regulation

Genetic testing raises issues of informed consent, privacy, psychological impact, and discrimination. In the United States, the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 (GINA) prohibits the use of genetic information in health insurance and employment decisions, though it does not cover life insurance. Clinical testing is regulated and laboratory quality overseen (e.g. under CLIA), while direct-to-consumer tests have attracted regulatory scrutiny over the medical claims they may make. Testing of children for adult-onset conditions without medical benefit in childhood is generally discouraged by professional guidelines.
