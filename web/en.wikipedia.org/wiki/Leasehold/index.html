Leasehold is a form of land or property tenure in which one party buys the right to occupy land or a building for a fixed, usually long, period of time while another party — the freeholder — retains ultimate ownership. It is distinguished from freehold, which is ownership in perpetuity. Leasehold arrangements are especially common in England and Wales, where many flats, and historically many houses, are sold on long leases.

How it works

The leaseholder pays a premium for the lease and typically an annual ground rent plus service charges for maintenance of common parts. The lease sets out rights and obligations — repair, alterations, subletting, insurance — and the property reverts to the freeholder when the lease expires. Because a shortening lease loses value (and becomes hard to mortgage below about 80 years unexpired, when "marriage value" starts to apply), leaseholders commonly extend their leases or band together to buy the freehold (enfranchisement).

Reform debates

Leasehold has been controversial in England and Wales: escalating ground rents on new-build houses, opaque service charges, and the cost of remediating unsafe cladding after the Grenfell Tower fire prompted government reform. Legislation in 2022 removed ground rent on most new residential leases, and further leasehold and commonhold reform has been debated. Other systems — Scottish feuhold (largely abolished), strata title in Australia, and condominium in North America — provide alternatives that give apartment owners outright ownership of their unit plus a share of common parts.
