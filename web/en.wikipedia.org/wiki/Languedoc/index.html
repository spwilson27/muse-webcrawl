Languedoc

Languedoc (French: Languedoc; Occitan: Lengadòc) is a former province of France, now continued in the modern region of Occitanie in the south of the country. Its name derives from the langue d'oc, the Romance language in which òc means "yes", in contrast to the northern langue d'oïl.

History

The region was Romanised early as part of Gallia Narbonensis, with Narbonne as a major port. In the Middle Ages it was divided among the counts of Toulouse and other lords and was a centre of the Cathar heresy, suppressed by the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229), after which the territory was progressively annexed to the French crown. The provincial Estates met regularly, and the Canal du Midi (opened 1681) linked the region's Atlantic and Mediterranean trade.

Geography and wine

Languedoc stretches from the Rhône to the Pyrenees along the Mediterranean coast, with cities including Montpellier, Nîmes, Narbonne, Béziers and Carcassonne. It is one of the world's largest wine-producing areas, long known for volume production and increasingly for quality appellations such as Corbières, Minervois and Picpoul de Pinet.
