Thames Tunnel

The Thames Tunnel is an underwater tunnel beneath the River Thames in London, connecting Rotherhithe on the south bank with Wapping on the north bank. Built between 1825 and 1843 by the engineers Marc Brunel and his son Isambard Kingdom Brunel, it was the first tunnel known to have been constructed successfully underneath a navigable river. It measures 35 feet (11 m) wide, 20 feet (6 m) high and 1,300 feet (396 m) long, running at a depth of about 75 feet (23 m) below the river surface at high tide.

Construction

Marc Brunel's key invention was the tunnelling shield, inspired by the shipworm Teredo navalis, whose boring action he observed in a naval dockyard. The shield divided the working face into cells in which miners dug forward a few inches at a time while the shield was jacked ahead and bricklayers lined the exposed earth behind it. Work began in 1825 but was plagued by floods, fires, methane and hydrogen sulphide, and financial crises. The tunnel was flooded catastrophically on 18 January 1828, killing six men; Isambard Kingdom Brunel narrowly escaped drowning. Work stopped for seven years before resuming with a government loan in 1835.

Opening and use

The tunnel opened to pedestrians on 25 March 1843 and was an immediate sensation, attracting two million visitors in its first year, who paid a penny to descend the grand entrance shafts and walk beneath the river among stalls and entertainers. It never carried the horse-drawn carriage traffic for which it was designed, because the approach ramps were never funded. In 1869 it was sold to the East London Railway and converted for trains. Today it forms part of the London Overground network between Rotherhithe and Wapping stations on the East London line, and is the oldest section of any underground railway system in the world still in use.

The Rotherhithe entrance shaft now houses the Brunel Museum, which commemorates the tunnel and its engineers.
