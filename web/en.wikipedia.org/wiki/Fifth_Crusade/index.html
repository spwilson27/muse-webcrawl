Fifth Crusade
Dates: 1217–1221
Theatre: Egypt (Damietta, Nile Delta)
Called by: Pope Innocent III / Honorius III
Result: Ayyubid victory; crusaders withdraw

The Fifth Crusade (1217–1221) was a campaign launched by Western European Christians to recover Jerusalem and the Holy Land by first conquering the Ayyubid sultanate of Egypt, whose wealth and armies were seen as the key to Muslim power in the region. Called by Pope Innocent III at the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 and carried forward by Honorius III, it drew forces from Hungary, Austria, France, the Empire, the Low Countries, and the Italian maritime cities.

The campaign

King Andrew II of Hungary and Duke Leopold VI of Austria campaigned in Syria in 1217–18 with little result. The main effort fell on Egypt: in 1218 a crusader fleet attacked Damietta at the mouth of the Nile, capturing its harbour chain-tower after a long siege — during which Frisian and German crusaders used an ingenious floating siege engine — and taking the city itself in November 1219 after famine and disease had emptied it. Cardinal Pelagius, the papal legate, exercised command, frequently overruling John of Brienne, king of Jerusalem. St. Francis of Assisi famously crossed the lines in 1219 to preach to Sultan al-Kamil, who received him courteously.

Failure and aftermath

Al-Kamil repeatedly offered to trade Jerusalem for the crusaders' withdrawal from Egypt, but Pelagius refused, expecting the arrival of Emperor Frederick II, who never came. In July 1221 the army advanced on Cairo but was trapped between the Nile's floodwaters and the sultan's forces near Mansurah; forced to surrender, the crusaders gave up Damietta in exchange for safe passage and an eight-year truce. The failure was widely blamed on Pelagius's leadership and Frederick's absence, setting the stage for Frederick's own crusade in 1228.
