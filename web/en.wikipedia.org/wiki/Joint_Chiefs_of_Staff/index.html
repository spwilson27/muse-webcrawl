The Joint Chiefs of Staff (JCS) is the body of the most senior uniformed leaders within the United States Department of Defense, which advises the president of the United States, the secretary of defense, the Homeland Security Council, and the National Security Council on military matters. The composition of the Joint Chiefs of Staff is defined by statute and consists of a chairman, a vice chairman, the service chiefs of the Army, Marine Corps, Navy, Air Force, and Space Force, and the chief of the National Guard Bureau.

History

The body originated in the Second World War, when American and British leaders formed the Combined Chiefs of Staff in 1942 and the American service chiefs began meeting informally as a joint body, chaired in practice by Admiral William D. Leahy as Chief of Staff to the Commander in Chief. The National Security Act of 1947 gave the Joint Chiefs a statutory basis; amendments in 1949 created the office of chairman, first held by General Omar Bradley. The Goldwater–Nichols Act of 1986 strengthened the chairman's authority, making him the principal military advisor to the president and designating the vice chairman as the second-ranking officer in the armed forces.

Roles and responsibilities

Under Goldwater–Nichols, the operational chain of command runs from the president through the secretary of defense to the commanders of the unified combatant commands; the Joint Chiefs themselves hold no operational command authority. The chairman functions as the principal military adviser, while the individual service chiefs are responsible for organizing, training, and equipping their forces and for advising on matters concerning their services. The Joint Staff, approximately 1,200 personnel drawn from all services, supports the chairman at the Pentagon. Meetings of the chiefs are traditionally held in a secure conference room known as "the Tank."

Membership

Members serve at the pleasure of the president and are appointed with the advice and consent of the Senate. Notable chairmen have included George C. Marshall (as wartime army chief and de facto leader of the wartime JCS), Maxwell Taylor, Colin Powell — who became the first African American chairman in 1989 — and, more recently, Mark Milley. The chairman is by law the highest-ranking officer in the U.S. armed forces, though he exercises no command over the combatant commands.
