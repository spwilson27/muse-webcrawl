The Nasrid dynasty (Arabic: بنو نصر, Banū Naṣr) was the last Muslim dynasty in the Iberian Peninsula, ruling the Emirate of Granada from 1232 until 1492. Twenty-three sultans of the dynasty ruled from the Alhambra palace complex in Granada, surviving for two and a half centuries as a tributary vassal of the Christian kingdom of Castile while the rest of al-Andalus fell to the Reconquista.

History

The dynasty was founded by Muhammad I ibn Nasr ("Ibn al-Ahmar", "son of the Red"), an Arjona sayyid who seized Jaén, then Granada (1238), and accepted Castilian vassalage in 1246, even assisting Ferdinand III in the conquest of Seville. His successors balanced tribute payments to Castile with alliances with the Marinids of Morocco, and intervened in Castilian civil wars.

Under Yusuf I (1333–1354) and Muhammad V (1354–1391, with interruptions), the emirate enjoyed a cultural golden age: the great palaces of the Alhambra — the Comares Palace and the Court of the Lions — were built, and Granada attracted scholars such as the statesman-historian Ibn al-Khatib and the traveller Ibn Battuta. In the 15th century dynastic feuding (notably between the Abencerrages and rivals) weakened the state. In 1482 the Catholic Monarchs launched the Granada War; city after city fell, and on 2 January 1492 Muhammad XII ("Boabdil") surrendered Granada, ending Muslim rule in Iberia.

Culture

Nasrid art and architecture — carved stucco, muqarnas vaulting, glazed tilework (zellij) and the dynasty's motto "There is no conqueror but God" (wa lā gāliba illā Allāh) repeated across the Alhambra — represent the final flowering of Andalusi Islamic civilisation.
