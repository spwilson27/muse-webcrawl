Heat index

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The heat index (also called the apparent temperature, or humidex in a Canadian variant) is an index that combines air temperature and relative humidity to determine the human-perceived equivalent temperature — how hot it feels. High humidity slows the evaporation of sweat from the skin, impairing the body's cooling and making the air feel hotter than it is. For example, an air temperature of 36 °C (97 °F) with 70% relative humidity produces a heat index of about 50 °C (122 °F).

Basis and formula

The index rests on the work of R. G. Steadman (1979), who modelled heat transfer between a clothed human body and the environment. The formula used by the US National Weather Service is a multiple-regression fit (the Rothfusz regression) to Steadman's table, with adjustments for extremes of temperature and humidity. Canada's humidex, introduced in 1979, applies a similar idea using dew point.

Limitations

The heat index assumes a person in shade with light wind, average clothing and no unusual exertion; direct sun can add up to about 8 °C (15 °F) to the perceived value. It is defined for temperatures above about 27 °C (80 °F) and is not meaningful for cool conditions. Related measures include the wet-bulb globe temperature used in occupational safety and the wind chill index for cold weather.

Records and trends

The highest heat-index values are recorded around the Persian Gulf and in South Asia, where extreme humidity combines with extreme heat; a heat index of 81 °C (178 °F) was reported at Dhahran, Saudi Arabia, in July 2003 (based on a dew point of 35 °C). Such extreme apparent temperatures are expected to become more common with climate warming.
