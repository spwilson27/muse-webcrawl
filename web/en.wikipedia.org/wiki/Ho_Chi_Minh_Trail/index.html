The Ho Chi Minh Trail (Vietnamese: Đường mòn Hồ Chí Minh), also called the Truong Son Road, was a logistical network of roads, trails, waterways and pipelines running from North Vietnam through Laos and Cambodia into South Vietnam. It provided manpower and matériel to the National Liberation Front (Viet Cong) and the People's Army of Vietnam during the Vietnam War, and is regarded as one of the great feats of military engineering of the twentieth century.

Development

In May 1959 Hanoi created Group 559, commanded by Võ Bầm, to open a supply route through the Annamite (Trường Sơn) mountains. Initially porters and bicycles carried loads along jungle paths; over time the network grew into thousands of kilometres of graded roads with way stations (binh tram), truck parks, fuel pipelines, hospitals and anti-aircraft defences, largely hidden beneath triple-canopy jungle.

American interdiction

The United States waged an enormous bombing campaign against the trail, especially in Laos under operations such as Steel Tiger and Commando Hunt. Sensor networks (Operation Igloo White) attempted to detect truck traffic. Despite tonnages of bombs exceeding those of the Second World War dropped on Laos, traffic on the trail increased year by year; by the early 1970s tens of thousands of tonnes reached the south annually, and the trail supported the conventional offensives of 1972 and 1975.

Legacy

Large numbers of Vietnamese soldiers died on the trail from bombing, disease and exhaustion; the Truong Son Martyrs' Cemetery commemorates them. Today the Ho Chi Minh Highway follows part of the route, and unexploded ordnance in Laos remains a legacy of the bombing.
