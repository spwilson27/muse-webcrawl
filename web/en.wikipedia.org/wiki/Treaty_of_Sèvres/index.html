The Treaty of Sèvres was a treaty signed on 10 August 1920 between the Ottoman Empire and the Allied powers of the First World War, at Sèvres, France. It was one of the series of agreements by which the victors settled with the defeated Central Powers after 1918, and it imposed a drastic partition of the Ottoman Empire. The treaty was never ratified, and its terms were repudiated by the Turkish national movement; after the Turkish War of Independence it was replaced by the far more favourable Treaty of Lausanne in 1923.


The partition of Anatolia envisaged by Sèvres

Terms

The treaty would have stripped the empire of most of its territory. Its Arab provinces had already been assigned to British and French mandates; in Anatolia, Greece was to receive Smyrna (İzmir) and eastern Thrace, Italy and France were granted zones of influence in the south and southeast, and an independent Armenia (with borders to be arbitrated by U.S. President Woodrow Wilson) and a possible Kurdish state were envisaged in the east. The Straits were internationalised, the Ottoman army limited to 50,000 men, and the capitulations restored and extended.

Reaction and supersession

Signed by the Sultan's government in occupied Constantinople, the treaty provoked outrage among Turkish nationalists, who rallied around Mustafa Kemal in Ankara and fought the Greco-Turkish War (1919–1922). Greek defeat, the abolition of the sultanate, and the Armistice of Mudanya forced a renegotiation; the resulting Treaty of Lausanne (1923) recognised a sovereign, undivided Turkish republic in Anatolia and eastern Thrace. The unimplemented Sèvres remains a potent symbol in Turkish politics, sometimes invoked as the "Sèvres syndrome" — a fear of foreign partition plots.
