Roadster (automobile)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A classic two-seat open-top roadster

A roadster (also called a spider or spyder) is an open two-seat car with emphasis on sporting appearance or character. Traditionally, roadsters had no fixed roof and minimal weather protection — often no side windows at all — distinguishing them from convertibles (cabriolets), which have a folding roof and roll-up windows. In modern usage the term is applied more loosely to two-seat open sports cars.

History

The roadster was one of the earliest automobile body styles: in the early 20th century it denoted a light, sporty open car, often with a rumble seat in the rear deck, exemplified by the Ford Model T roadster. In the 1920s and 1930s manufacturers such as MG, Alfa Romeo and Bugatti produced roadsters for sporting motorists. The term "spider" (in Italian, spyder) descends from the light horse-drawn "spider phaeton" carriage.

After the Second World War, British roadsters such as the MG T-series, Triumph TR series, Austin-Healey Sprite and Jaguar E-Type roadster became hugely popular, particularly in the United States. The genre was revived in 1989 by the Mazda MX-5 Miata, which became the best-selling two-seat sports car in history. Other modern roadsters include the BMW Z series, the Honda S2000, the Porsche Boxster (a mid-engined spyder) and the Tesla Roadster, an all-electric sports car produced from 2008.

Characteristics

Roadsters are typically rear-wheel drive (though front- and all-wheel-drive examples exist), lightweight, and designed for handling and driver engagement rather than practicality. Seating is strictly for two in the classic definition, luggage space is limited, and the emphasis is on open-air motoring.
