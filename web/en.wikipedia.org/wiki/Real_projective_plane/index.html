In mathematics, the real projective plane, denoted RP², is a two-dimensional projective space and one of the fundamental examples of a non-orientable surface. It is a compact topological space formed by taking the points of the ordinary plane together with "points at infinity" — one for each direction of parallel lines — so that any two distinct lines meet in exactly one point. Formally, it is the set of lines through the origin in three-dimensional Euclidean space, or equivalently the unit sphere with antipodal points identified.

Models

Several models exhibit the space concretely:

  • Antipodal sphere — the sphere S² with opposite points glued together.
  • Disk with identified boundary — a closed disk whose boundary points are identified with their antipodes; cutting it open displays the projective plane as a Möbius strip capped by a disk.
  • Homogeneous coordinates — points are equivalence classes [x : y : z] of triples, not all zero, with scalar multiples identified.
  • Boy's surface — a self-intersecting immersion in ordinary 3-space, first described by Werner Boy in 1901.

Properties

The real projective plane is non-orientable: it contains Möbius strips, so no consistent notion of "clockwise" exists on it. Its Euler characteristic is 1 and its fundamental group is the cyclic group of order two, Z/2Z. It cannot be embedded in three-dimensional Euclidean space without self-intersection, though it embeds smoothly in four dimensions. Together with the sphere, torus, Klein bottle, and connected sums of these, it appears in the classification theorem for closed surfaces: every closed non-orientable surface is a connected sum of copies of RP².
