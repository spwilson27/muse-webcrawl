George III (George William Frederick; 4 June 1738 – 29 January 1820) was King of Great Britain and Ireland from 1760 until the union of the two kingdoms in 1801, after which he was King of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland, and also Elector (later King) of Hanover. His 59-year reign — the longest of any British monarch at the time — spanned the loss of the American colonies, the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars, and the union with Ireland.

Early reign and America

The first Hanoverian monarch to be born in England and speak English as his first language, George sought a more active role in government than his predecessors. His reign saw the victory in the Seven Years' War that made Britain the dominant colonial power, but growing conflict with the American colonies over taxation led to the American Revolutionary War (1775–1783) and recognition of the independence of the United States. The loss damaged the king's prestige but his popularity recovered in the 1780s under the government of William Pitt the Younger.

War with France and later years

From 1793 Britain was almost continuously at war with revolutionary and Napoleonic France, culminating in the victories of Nelson at Trafalgar (1805) and Wellington at Waterloo (1815). George suffered recurrent bouts of mental illness — long attributed to porphyria, a diagnosis now debated — and after a final relapse in 1810 his eldest son acted as Prince Regent for the rest of the reign. The king, blind and increasingly deaf, died at Windsor Castle in 1820. His marriage to Queen Charlotte produced fifteen children, and his personal frugality and interest in agriculture earned him the nickname "Farmer George".
