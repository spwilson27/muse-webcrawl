Great conjunction

A great conjunction is a conjunction of the planets Jupiter and Saturn, the two largest planets in the Solar System, when they appear closest together in the sky. Great conjunctions occur roughly every 20 years, when Jupiter "overtakes" Saturn in its orbit, but they vary greatly in their minimum separation and visibility.

Mechanics

Jupiter orbits the Sun in about 11.9 years and Saturn in about 29.4 years, so Jupiter laps Saturn at intervals of about 19.9 years. Successive conjunctions occur about 243 degrees further along the zodiac, so the meeting points cycle through the zodiacal triplicities — a pattern much studied by medieval and early modern astrologers, including Johannes Kepler, who investigated the great conjunction of 1603 and the nova of 1604.

The 2020 conjunction

The great conjunction of 21 December 2020 was the closest since 1623: at their nearest, Jupiter and Saturn were separated by only about 0.1 degrees — one fifth of the diameter of the full Moon — and were readily seen as a close pair in the evening twilight. The next comparably close conjunction will occur in 2080.

In history and astrology

Because of their rarity, great conjunctions were long treated as omens of epochal change. One persistent hypothesis links the Star of Bethlehem to the triple conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in 7 BCE. In astrology, the shifting of conjunctions between the zodiacal triplicities was held to mark cycles of about 200 years and broader eras of some 800–960 years.
