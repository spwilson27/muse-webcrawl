Fuel economy in automobiles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A U.S. fuel economy label ("Monroney sticker") showing estimated city and highway mileage.

Fuel economy in automobiles is the relationship between the distance travelled and the quantity of fuel consumed. Consumption can be expressed as the volume of fuel needed to travel a fixed distance — litres per 100 kilometres (L/100 km), used in most of the world — or as the distance travelled per unit volume — miles per gallon (mpg) in the United States and the United Kingdom. Because fuel economy strongly affects running costs and CO2 emissions, it is regulated in most major vehicle markets.

Units and conversion

The two families of units are reciprocals of one another, which makes comparisons awkward: an improvement from 10 to 20 mpg saves far more fuel than an improvement from 30 to 40 mpg over the same distance (the so-called "mpg illusion"). Useful conversions for U.S. gallons:

Measurement

Fuel economy is certified on standardised laboratory driving cycles, which differ by jurisdiction: the WLTP in Europe and many other markets, the EPA city/highway cycles in the United States, and JC08/WLTC variants in Japan. Real-world consumption is typically 15–30% worse than the laboratory figures, a gap that has been studied by the ICCT and consumer organisations.

Factors affecting fuel economy

  • Vehicle design: mass, aerodynamic drag, rolling resistance, and engine thermodynamic efficiency.
  • Technology: turbocharging and downsizing, direct injection, variable valve timing, continuously variable and multi-speed transmissions, start-stop systems, and hybridisation.
  • Driving conditions: speed (aerodynamic losses rise steeply above ~80 km/h), acceleration habits, idling, cold starts, and short trips.
  • Maintenance: tyre pressure, engine condition, and wheel alignment.

Regulation

In the United States, the Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) standards, first enacted in 1975 after the oil crisis, set fleet-average requirements for manufacturers. The European Union regulates CO2 emissions per kilometre (e.g. a 95 g/km fleet target phased in from 2020), which is directly proportional to fuel consumed. China, Japan, Canada and other markets operate comparable schemes. These rules have driven the adoption of hybrid and electric vehicles, whose energy use is expressed in units such as mpg-e or kWh/100 km.

Energy considerations

A litre of petrol contains roughly 34 megajoules of chemical energy; only about 20–30% of it reaches the wheels in a conventional car, the rest being lost as heat in the engine and drivetrain, idling, and accessories. Aerodynamic drag, rolling resistance and braking losses dominate at the wheels.
