Confidentiality

Confidentiality is the obligation and practice of keeping certain information secret, restricting its access to authorized persons only. It involves a set of rules or a promise — arising from law, professional ethics, or contract — that limits the further release of information entrusted to a person or organization. Confidentiality is one of the three core principles of information security, alongside integrity and availability (the "CIA triad").

Legal and professional contexts

Many professions impose enforceable duties of confidentiality on their practitioners. Well-known examples include attorney–client privilege, which protects communications between lawyers and their clients; doctor–patient confidentiality, rooted in medical ethics and statutes such as the United States' HIPAA, which restricts disclosure of health information; the seal of the confessional in some religious traditions; and the confidentiality owed by bankers, accountants, journalists (toward their sources), and public officials handling classified information. Breaching such duties can result in professional discipline, civil liability, or criminal penalties.

Confidentiality in business and technology

In commerce, confidentiality is commonly created by contract through a non-disclosure agreement (NDA), under which parties agree not to reveal specified information such as trade secrets, business plans, or personal data. In computing and communications, confidentiality is protected technically by encryption, access control, and authentication mechanisms, which aim to ensure that data can be read only by intended recipients even if intercepted. Data protection and privacy laws, such as the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation, impose confidentiality and security duties on organizations that process personal data.

Limits

Duties of confidentiality are rarely absolute. They are commonly subject to exceptions where disclosure is required by law or court order, consented to by the person concerned, or necessary to prevent serious harm — for example, a therapist's duty to warn an identifiable person threatened by a patient, or mandatory reporting of suspected child abuse.
