Obergefell v. Hodges

Obergefell v. Hodges, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States which held that the fundamental right to marry is guaranteed to same-sex couples by both the Due Process Clause and the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The 5–4 ruling required all fifty states to perform and recognise same-sex marriages on the same terms as opposite-sex marriages.

Background

The named plaintiff, James Obergefell, had married his terminally ill partner John Arthur in Maryland in 2013 and sued Ohio to be recognised as the surviving spouse on Arthur's death certificate. The case was consolidated with challenges to marriage bans in Michigan, Kentucky and Tennessee after the Sixth Circuit, in an opinion by Judge Jeffrey Sutton, upheld those bans in November 2014, creating a circuit split with the Fourth, Seventh, Ninth and Tenth Circuits. By the time of argument, same-sex marriage was already legal in 37 states, mostly through court decisions following United States v. Windsor (2013), which struck down the federal Defense of Marriage Act's refusal to recognise lawful marriages.

Decision

Justice Anthony Kennedy's majority opinion grounded the right in the Fourteenth Amendment, tracing four principles in the Court's marriage jurisprudence: individual autonomy in choices about marriage; the intimate two-person commitment marriage supports; the safeguards marriage gives children and families; and marriage as a keystone of social order. It held that same-sex couples could not be deprived of that right and that states must also recognise same-sex marriages lawfully performed elsewhere. The opinion closed by observing that the plaintiffs "ask for equal dignity in the eyes of the law. The Constitution grants them that right."

Chief Justice Roberts' principal dissent argued the Constitution does not compel the outcome and that the question should be left to democratic processes; Justice Scalia's dissent attacked the majority's reasoning and rhetoric; Justices Thomas and Alito emphasised religious liberty concerns.

Reactions and aftermath

The decision was announced on the second anniversary of Windsor and the twelfth of Lawrence v. Texas. The White House was illuminated in rainbow colours that evening. A small number of local officials, most prominently Kentucky county clerk Kim Davis, refused to issue licences and were sanctioned. Congress later enacted the Respect for Marriage Act (2022), requiring federal and interstate recognition of lawful marriages, amid concern that the reasoning of Dobbs might endanger substantive-due-process precedents. Earlier justices' approaches to incorporation under the Fourteenth Amendment framed much of the legal debate.
