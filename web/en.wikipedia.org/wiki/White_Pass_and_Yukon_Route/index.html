The White Pass and Yukon Route (WP&YR) is a narrow-gauge railway linking the port of Skagway, Alaska, with Whitehorse, Yukon, in Canada. Built during the Klondike Gold Rush, it climbs from sea level to the 873-metre White Pass summit in a spectacular mountain grade, and today operates primarily as a heritage passenger railway for cruise-ship tourists.

Construction

Construction began in May 1898, financed by British investors and built under contractor Michael J. Heney. Workers — many of them unemployed stampeders — blasted the line through granite in extreme conditions. The railway reached the summit in February 1899, Bennett, British Columbia, that July, and Whitehorse on 29 July 1900, becoming the lifeline of the Yukon trade just as the gold rush faded. The line also operated river steamers on the Yukon River and later pioneered containerised freight in the 1950s using the ship Clifford J. Rogers.

Operations and closure

The railway carried ore, freight, and passengers between Whitehorse and tidewater, connecting with steamships to Vancouver and Seattle. During the Second World War it was leased by the U.S. Army to support construction of the Alaska Highway. Freight traffic ended when the Faro lead-zinc mine closed; the railway suspended operations in 1982, but reopened in 1988 as a seasonal tourist attraction between Skagway and the summit and beyond.

Today

Excursion trains haul hundreds of thousands of passengers each summer, hauled by vintage GE and MLW diesel locomotives and by restored steam locomotives including No. 73, a 1947 Baldwin 2-8-2. The line was designated an International Historic Civil Engineering Landmark in 1994, jointly with Canada's historic sites authorities.
