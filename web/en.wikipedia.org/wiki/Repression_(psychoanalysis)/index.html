Repression (psychoanalysis)

In psychoanalytic theory, repression is the psychological mechanism by which the ego excludes distressing memories, thoughts or feelings from conscious awareness, pushing them into the unconscious. Sigmund Freud regarded repression as the cornerstone of psychoanalysis and the primary defence mechanism, on which the theory of neurosis was built.

Freud's account

Freud introduced repression in the 1890s in his work on hysteria with Josef Breuer and developed it in the 1915 metapsychological paper "Repression". He distinguished primal repression, in which instinctual impulses are denied entry to consciousness in the first place, from repression proper, in which derivatives of the repressed — ideas, memories and wishes — are withdrawn from consciousness because of the anxiety they provoke. Repressed material, Freud held, does not lose its force: it returns in disguised form in dreams, slips of the tongue, jokes and neurotic symptoms (the "return of the repressed"). Psychoanalytic treatment aims to lift repression by making the unconscious conscious, through free association, dream analysis and interpretation of transference.

Later developments

Anna Freud catalogued repression among the ego's defences in The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936). Later traditions reworked the concept: object relations and self psychology treated it alongside splitting and dissociation, while critics from the mid-twentieth century onward questioned its empirical status. Experimental psychology has produced mixed evidence for motivated forgetting, and the "memory wars" of the 1990s — concerning allegedly recovered memories of abuse — damaged the concept's clinical reputation, though research on suppression and retrieval inhibition continues.
