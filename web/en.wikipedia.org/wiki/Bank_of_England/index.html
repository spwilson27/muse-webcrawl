Bank of England

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Bank of England is the central bank of the United Kingdom and the model on which most modern central banks have been based. Established on 27 July 1694 as a private joint-stock bank to act as the English government's banker and debt manager, it was nationalised in 1946 and made operationally independent in 1998 (Bank of England Act 1998), when its Monetary Policy Committee took charge of setting interest rates to meet the government's inflation target (2 per cent CPI).

Functions

Beyond monetary policy, the Bank issues sterling banknotes (a monopoly in England and Wales), manages the Exchange Equalisation Account, acts as lender of last resort (its role theorised by Bagehot in Lombard Street, 1873), and since the post-2008 reforms houses the Prudential Regulation Authority and the Financial Policy Committee — a "twin peaks" architecture of micro- and macro-prudential supervision. Its history threads through British state finance: funding wars from William III's, suspending gold convertibility (1797, restored 1821, abandoned 1931), managing the run-up to Black Wednesday (1992, when sterling left the ERM despite Bank intervention), quantitative easing from 2009, and the 2022 gilt-market intervention during the mini-budget crisis — the modern central bank as crisis manager of both markets and governments.
