Auschwitz trial

The term Auschwitz trial generally refers to the Frankfurt Auschwitz trials (German: Auschwitz-Prozess), a series of trials held in Frankfurt am Main, West Germany, between 20 December 1963 and 19 August 1965, in which 22 former SS members and officials of the Auschwitz concentration and extermination camp were tried for their roles in the Holocaust. It was the largest and longest criminal trial in post-war German history up to that time, and it profoundly shaped public awareness of Nazi crimes in the Federal Republic of Germany.

The term may also refer to the earlier Kraków Auschwitz trial of 1947, held before Poland's Supreme National Tribunal, in which camp commandant Rudolf Höss and 39 other staff members were tried.

The Frankfurt trials

Background

The trials were driven largely by Fritz Bauer, the Attorney General of Hesse, who had gathered evidence on Auschwitz personnel since the late 1950s, partly from documents supplied by a former prisoner. Bauer sought to confront German society with the crimes committed in its name and to establish individual responsibility beyond the defence of "superior orders". Because West German law required prosecution under the ordinary criminal code, defendants were charged with murder and accessory to murder, which required prosecutors to prove individual acts and base motives—a legal constraint that influenced both the proceedings and the sentences.

Proceedings

The trial heard testimony from 360 witnesses, including 211 Auschwitz survivors, and the court travelled to Poland to inspect the camp site in December 1964—an extraordinary step for a German court. The proceedings were extensively reported in the press, and the writer Peter Weiss drew on the trial's testimony for his documentary play The Investigation (1965).

Verdicts

Of the 22 defendants, 17 were convicted: six received life imprisonment for murder, others received prison terms ranging from three years and three months to fourteen years for accessory to murder, and five were acquitted. A second, smaller Frankfurt Auschwitz trial followed in 1965–66, with further proceedings in subsequent years.

Significance

The Frankfurt trials are regarded as a turning point in West Germany's confrontation with the Nazi past (Vergangenheitsbewältigung). They brought survivor testimony before a German court and mass audience on an unprecedented scale, influenced the Bundestag debates on extending the statute of limitations for murder, and remain central to German legal and memory culture. The trial recordings were inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register in 2017.
