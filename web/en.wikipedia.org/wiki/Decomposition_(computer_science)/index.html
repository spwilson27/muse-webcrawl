Decomposition (computer science)

In computer science, decomposition is the process of breaking a complex problem or system into parts that are easier to conceive, understand, program, and maintain. It is a central technique of structured design and of computational thinking more generally.

Overview

Decomposition allows a developer to manage complexity by dividing a large program into modules, functions, classes or services, each responsible for a well-defined part of the whole. Once decomposed, the parts can be developed, tested and reasoned about independently, and can often be reused in other contexts.

The complementary process of combining the parts into a working whole is sometimes called composition. Effective decomposition seeks parts that are strongly cohesive internally and loosely coupled to one another, so that changes in one part have minimal effects on the others.

Types

  • Functional decomposition — breaking a system down by the functions or procedures it performs, typical of structured programming and top-down design.
  • Object-oriented decomposition — organising a system around classes and objects that encapsulate data and behaviour.
  • Data decomposition — dividing the data processed by a program, a common strategy in parallel computing, where the same operation is applied to many data partitions.
  • Task decomposition — splitting a computation into tasks that can execute concurrently.

In parallel computing

In parallel programming, decomposition is the first stage of design methodologies such as Ian Foster's PCAM (partitioning, communication, agglomeration, mapping). The way a problem is decomposed determines the available concurrency and strongly influences the scalability of the resulting program.

Relation to other concepts

Decomposition is related to abstraction, modularity and separation of concerns. Edsger Dijkstra argued that separating concerns is essential to organising effective thought processes about programs. In education, decomposition is widely taught as one of the core practices of computational thinking, alongside pattern recognition, abstraction and algorithm design.
