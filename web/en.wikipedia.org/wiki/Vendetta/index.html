Vendetta

A vendetta is a prolonged, bitter feud between families or clans in which acts of revenge are answered by further acts of revenge, often across generations. In a vendetta, an injury — real or perceived — to one member of a group is treated as an injury to the whole group, and honour is said to demand retaliation in kind. The word comes from Italian, derived from the Latin vindicta, meaning vengeance.

Feuds and honour cultures

Vendettas have been characteristic of societies with weak central authority, where kinship groups must enforce their own rights and deter attack by threatening certain retaliation. Classic examples include the blood feuds of Corsica and Sicily, the Albanian gjakmarrja governed by the Kanun of Lekë Dukagjini, the Pashtun practice of badal under Pashtunwali, and clan feuds in the Scottish Highlands and the Appalachian United States, such as the Hatfield–McCoy feud.

Ending a feud

Feuds could be ended by compensation payments (such as wergild in Germanic law or diya in Islamic law), by marriage alliances, by mediation of elders or religious figures, or by formal reconciliation rituals. The growth of state courts and the monopoly of legitimate violence by governments is generally credited with suppressing vendetta cultures, though the code of honour underlying them can persist long after the killings stop.

In popular culture

The vendetta is a staple of literature and film, from Icelandic sagas and Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet — a feud between the Montagues and Capulets — to mafia films in which loyalty and revenge drive the plot.
