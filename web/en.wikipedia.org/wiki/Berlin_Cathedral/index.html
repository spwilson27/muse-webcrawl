Berlin Cathedral

The Berliner Dom on Museum Island, seen from the River Spree.

Berlin Cathedral (German: Berliner Dom), officially the Evangelical Supreme Parish and Collegiate Church, is the largest Protestant church in Berlin, standing on Museum Island in the Mitte district. Built between 1894 and 1905 in an ornate Renaissance and Baroque Revival style, it served as the court church of the Hohenzollern dynasty and contains the dynasty's most important crypt.

History

A church has stood on the site since the 15th century; successive buildings served first the electors of Brandenburg and then the Prussian kings. The mid-18th-century Baroque cathedral was remodelled by Karl Friedrich Schinkel in the 1820s. Kaiser Wilhelm II, finding it inadequate as a Protestant counterpart to St Peter's in Rome, ordered its demolition and replacement. The new cathedral, designed by Julius Raschdorff, was consecrated in 1905 — a vast domed preaching church richly decorated with mosaics, marble and sculpture, intended to proclaim the unity of throne and altar in the German Empire.

War damage and restoration

The cathedral was severely damaged by bombing in 1944, when its dome collapsed into the nave. In the German Democratic Republic it stood as a ruin for decades before restoration began in 1975, carried out with West German funding; the great church reopened in 1993 with a somewhat simplified dome and interior. The Hohenzollern crypt beneath the church, one of the most significant dynastic burial vaults in Europe, holds nearly a hundred sarcophagi of Brandenburg electors and Prussian kings, including the Great Elector and Frederick I and Queen Sophie Charlotte. The Sauer organ of 1905, with over 7,000 pipes, is among the largest in Germany.

Today

The cathedral is a working parish church, a concert venue, and one of Berlin's most visited monuments; visitors can climb to the dome walkway for views over Museum Island and the city.
