Hetman

Hetman is a political title from Central and Eastern Europe, historically assigned to military commanders. Though the term itself derives from German Hauptmann ("head man" or "captain"), the title was first used in the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, and later spread to the Cossacks of Ukraine, Russia and Moldavia.

History in Poland and Lithuania

In the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth the hetman was the highest-ranking military officer after the monarch himself. The Crown of the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania each had a Grand Hetman (hetman wielki) and a Field Hetman (hetman polny). The office was held for life, and its holder was effectively the commander-in-chief, though formally subordinate to the king. Famous hetmans include Jan Tarnowski, Stanisław Żółkiewski, Jan Karol Chodkiewicz, Jan III Sobieski (who later became king), and Stanisław Koniecpolski. The hetman's symbol of office was the bulawa, a ceremonial mace.

The Cossack Hetmanate

Among the Zaporozhian Cossacks, the hetman was the elected head of the Cossack Host and later of the Cossack Hetmanate, an autonomous state within the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth and later the Tsardom of Russia. Bohdan Khmelnytsky, hetman from 1648, led the great uprising against the Commonwealth and concluded the Treaty of Pereyaslav with Russia in 1654. His successors included Ivan Vyhovsky, Ivan Mazepa — who sided with Charles XII of Sweden at Poltava in 1709 — and Kyrylo Rozumovsky, the last hetman, whose office was abolished by Catherine II in 1764.

Ukraine, 1918

The title was revived briefly in 1918, when Pavlo Skoropadskyi was proclaimed Hetman of the Ukrainian State, a conservative government supported by Germany and Austria-Hungary during the final months of the First World War.
