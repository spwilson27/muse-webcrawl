Hunger (Hamsun novel)

Hunger (Norwegian: Sult) is a novel by the Norwegian author Knut Hamsun, published in 1890. It is regarded as a foundational work of modernist literature, pioneering psychological narrative and the stream-of-consciousness technique that would influence writers such as Franz Kafka and James Joyce.

Plot summary

The novel is narrated in the first person by an unnamed young writer starving in Christiania (present-day Oslo). Driven by hunger, pride, and an increasingly unstable mind, he wanders the city trying to sell articles to newspapers, pawning his possessions one by one, refusing food and money offered out of charity, and oscillating between euphoria, delusion, and despair. He becomes infatuated with a young woman he names "Ylajali". The novel has little conventional plot; its drama is internal, following the narrator's thoughts, impulses, and irrational decisions as physical starvation corrodes his reason. In the final pages he signs on as a crewman aboard a ship and leaves the city.

Background and publication

Parts of the novel appeared anonymously in the Danish journal Ny Jord in 1888; the complete book followed in 1890. Hamsun drew on his own experiences of poverty as an aspiring writer in Christiania and in the United States. In essays of the period he attacked the realistic social novels of his predecessors and called for a literature of the "unconscious life of the mind" — a program Hunger put into practice.

Reception and influence

The novel established Hamsun's reputation; he went on to win the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1920. Isaac Bashevis Singer called Hamsun "the father of the modern school of literature". Hunger has been translated into many languages; the 1996 English translation by Sverre Lyngstad is regarded as definitive. A Danish film adaptation directed by Henning Carlsen was released in 1966.
