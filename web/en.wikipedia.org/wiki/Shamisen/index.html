Shamisen
Other namesSamisen
ClassificationString instrument (plucked lute)
Related instrumentsSanxian, sanshin, biwa

The shamisen (Japanese: 三味線, also spelled samisen) is a three-stringed traditional Japanese musical instrument derived from the Chinese sanxian. It is played with a large plectrum called a bachi. Its long, thin neck and square, skin-covered body give it a distinctive percussive, twanging tone.

History

The instrument reached Japan in the 16th century via the Ryukyu Islands, where its ancestor, the sanshin, was covered with snakeskin. In Japan the body came to be covered with cat or dog skin, and the shamisen quickly became central to narrative singing, kabuki and bunraku theatre, geisha entertainment and folk song.

Construction

The shamisen consists of a hollow body (dō) covered front and back with skin, a fretless neck (sao) divided into three sections for transport, and three strings traditionally made of silk (now often nylon or tetron) tuned by large pegs. A small bridge (koma) rests on the skin. The lowest string sits in a shallow groove at the nut, producing a characteristic buzzing resonance called sawari.

Types

Shamisen are classified by neck thickness: the thin-necked hosozao is used in nagauta (kabuki music), the medium chūzao in folk song and jiuta, and the thick-necked futozao in gidayū narrative for bunraku and in the virtuosic Tsugaru-jamisen style of northern Honshu.

Performance

The player strikes and plucks the strings with the bachi, often hitting the skin of the body for percussive effect. Repertory ranges from the classical narrative genres to min'yō folk songs and contemporary crossover music; the Yoshida Brothers brought Tsugaru-jamisen to international rock and pop audiences in the 2000s.
