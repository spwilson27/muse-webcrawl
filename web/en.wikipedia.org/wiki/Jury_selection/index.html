Jury selection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jury selection is the process of choosing the jurors who will decide a trial, designed to seat a panel that is both representative of the community and impartial in the case. It proceeds in stages: a master list compiled from voter rolls, licence records or census data; random summons of a venire (panel of prospective jurors); hardship and for-cause excusals; and voir dire — questioning by judge and (in US practice) counsel — followed by challenges for cause and peremptory strikes until the jury (typically 12 in serious criminal trials, fewer in civil and minor cases, with alternates) is sworn.

Rights and findings

The Sixth Amendment's fair-cross-section requirement polices the composition of the pool (Durk framework; systematic exclusion of distinctive groups invalidates convictions), while Batson governs discrimination in strikes. Empirical research shapes practice beneath the doctrine: summoned pools under-represent the young, the poor and minorities (source lists, non-response and hardship excusals all skew), voir dire's self-reports of impartiality are unreliable, and an industry of trial consultants applies social science (and stereotypes) to strike strategy — countered by judges who limit attorney voir dire and by reformers who would raise juror pay and broaden source lists. Other systems select very differently (French mixed panels of judges and citizens for serious crimes; Japan's saiban-in lay judges chosen by lot), but all face the same design problem: randomness for legitimacy, screening for impartiality, and the tension between them.
