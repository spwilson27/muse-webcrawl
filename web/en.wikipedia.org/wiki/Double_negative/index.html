Double negative

Double negative
FieldLinguistics, grammar
TypesNegative concord; cancelling (litotes)
Example"I didn't see nothing" (concord)

A double negative is a construction in which two forms of grammatical negation are used in the same clause. Depending on the language or dialect, the two negatives may cancel each other out, yielding a positive meaning, or they may reinforce a single negation — a pattern linguists call negative concord.

Negative concord

In many languages and dialects, multiple negative words within a clause express one negation. Standard Spanish requires it (No vi nada, literally "I didn't see nothing"), and it is normal in Italian, Russian, French (ne … pas historically), and in non-standard and vernacular varieties of English, including African American Vernacular English and Cockney, where "I didn't do nothing" simply means "I didn't do anything". Chaucer's English used multiple negatives freely — his Knight "never yet no villainy ne said" — with as many as four reinforcing a single negation.

Cancelling negatives and litotes

In standard modern English, two negatives in a clause are conventionally read as cancelling, often producing a hedged positive known as litotes: "not uncommon", "not without reason". Prescriptive grammarians of the 18th century, such as Robert Lowth, condemned concord constructions as illogical, and the cancelling interpretation became the rule in formal standard English, even though it does not reflect the grammar of many dialects.

In logic and mathematics

The term is also used by analogy in logic, where double negation (¬¬p) is classically equivalent to p, and in everyday usage for phrases such as "cannot not". Linguists stress that natural language negation is not arithmetic: whether negatives cancel or reinforce is a matter of each variety's grammar, not of logic.
