Sub-Roman Britain is the period of late antiquity in Great Britain between the end of Roman rule in around 410 AD and the arrival of Saint Augustine's mission in 597, when the Romano-British culture gave way to the Anglo-Saxon culture across much of lowland Britain. The term covers the material and documentary record of the fifth and sixth centuries and the shifting polities that emerged as Roman administration collapsed.

The end of Roman rule

In 407 the usurper Constantine III withdrew most of the remaining field army to Gaul. In 410 the Emperor Honorius' rescript told the Britons to look to their own defences. Coinage stopped arriving, towns decayed, and the centralised economy of mass-produced pottery and coin-using markets vanished within a generation. The Britons, according to later sources such as Gildas and Bede, appealed to Rome (the "Groans of the Britons" to Aëtius, c. 446) and invited Germanic mercenaries—the foederati—whose revolt began the Anglo-Saxon settlement.

Society and kingdoms

Political authority fragmented into small kingdoms ruled by "tyrants" (in Gildas's hostile account): Vortigern, Ambrosius Aurelianus—celebrated as the last of the Romans—and the shadowy figure behind the King Arthur legend, associated with the British victory at Mount Badon (c. 490–517). In the west and north, Brittonic kingdoms such as Dumnonia, Powys, Gwynedd, Rheged and Strathclyde endured for centuries. Hillforts such as South Cadbury and Tintagel were refortified, and Tintagel imported Mediterranean pottery and wine, showing that Atlantic trade links survived.

Religion and culture

Christianity persisted and flourished in the British west, producing figures such as Saint Patrick—himself a Romano-Briton—and the monastic culture of Wales and Ireland. Latin literacy contracted to church and aristocratic circles, while Brittonic languages developed into Welsh, Cornish and Breton.

Historiography

Sources are scarce: Gildas's De Excidio Britanniae, Patrick's writings, later Welsh and Anglo-Saxon annals, and archaeology. Historians debate whether the Anglo-Saxon migration was a mass folk movement, an elite takeover, or something between; ancient DNA studies published in 2022 indicated substantial migration from northern continental Europe into eastern England alongside extensive intermarriage.
