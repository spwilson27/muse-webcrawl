Charles Édouard Chamberland

Charles Édouard Chamberland (12 March 1851 – 2 May 1908) was a French microbiologist and a close collaborator of Louis Pasteur. He is known for inventing the Chamberland filter — a porcelain filter fine enough to remove bacteria from liquids — and for his role in developing the autoclave and vaccines against anthrax and chicken cholera.

Life and work

Chamberland trained at the École Normale Supérieure and joined Pasteur's laboratory in 1878. In 1879, during work on chicken cholera, cultures left standing over a holiday proved weakened and protected chickens against fresh infection — the accidental discovery of attenuation that led to the principle of vaccination with weakened microbes; Chamberland shared in this work and in the famous public anthrax vaccine trial at Pouilly-le-Fort in 1881.

In 1884 he developed the filter that bears his name, made of unglazed porcelain, whose pores are smaller than bacteria. The Chamberland filter allowed the sterilisation of liquids that could not be heated, and filtrates that passed through it yet remained infectious later revealed the existence of viruses. He also helped perfect the autoclave, the steam steriliser still standard in laboratories and medicine. Chamberland later served as a deputy in the French National Assembly while continuing his association with the Pasteur Institute.
