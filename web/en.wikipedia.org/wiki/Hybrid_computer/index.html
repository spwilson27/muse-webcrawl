Hybrid computer

A hybrid computer is a computer system that combines the features of analog computers and digital computers. Analog computing elements — integrators, summers, and multipliers built from operational amplifiers — perform fast continuous computations, such as solving differential equations in real time, while a linked digital computer provides programmability, logical control, storage, and precise set-up of the analog hardware. Analog-to-digital and digital-to-analog converters connect the two halves. Hybrid systems flourished from the late 1950s through the 1970s, before rising digital speeds made purely digital simulation fast enough for most purposes.

History

Hybrid installations were produced by companies including Electronic Associates Inc. (EAI), whose HYDAC and EAI 8800/8900 systems were widely used, Applied Dynamics, and Comcor. They were installed in aerospace and defence laboratories for flight simulation, missile and spacecraft trajectory studies, and pilot-in-the-loop trainers, as well as for simulating chemical plants, nuclear reactors, and biomedical systems such as cardiovascular dynamics. Notable users included NASA centres, where hybrid systems simulated rendezvous and docking manoeuvres in real time.

Characteristics

The analog portion of a hybrid computer solves problems at electronic speed, with all variables computed simultaneously, making it ideal for real-time simulation where a human operator or actual hardware interacts with the model. The digital portion sets potentiometers automatically, checks the analog set-up, stores results, performs the slower or more precise parts of a computation, and runs iterative or optimization loops. Limitations included the limited precision of analog components (typically 0.01–0.1 per cent), drift, and the labour of patching and scaling problems.

Decline and legacy

As digital computers grew faster and cheaper in the late 1970s and 1980s, hybrid systems were largely displaced by all-digital simulation. The hybrid concept survives in spirit in real-time digital simulators coupled to physical equipment ("hardware-in-the-loop" testing), in field-programmable analog arrays, and in modern interest in analog and neuromorphic accelerators for specialized computations.
