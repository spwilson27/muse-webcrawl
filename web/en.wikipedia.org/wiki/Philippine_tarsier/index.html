The Philippine tarsier (Carlito syrichta), known locally as mawumag in Cebuano and other Visayan languages, and magô in Waray, is a species of tarsier endemic to the Philippines. It is found in the southeastern part of the archipelago, particularly on the islands of Bohol, Samar, Leyte, and Mindanao. It is a member of the approximately 45-million-year-old family Tarsiidae, whose name is derived from its elongated "tarsus" or ankle bone.

Formerly a member of the genus Tarsius, it is now listed as the only member of the genus Carlito, a new genus named after the conservationist Carlito Pizarras, also known as the "tarsier man".

Description

The Philippine tarsier has a head-and-body length of about 118 to 149 mm (4.6 to 5.9 in) and weighs 113 to 142 grams (4.0 to 5.0 oz). Males are larger than females. The tail is longer than the body. Its most distinctive features are its enormous eyes — among the largest eye-to-body size ratio of all mammals — which are fixed in their sockets, though the animal can rotate its head almost 180 degrees in each direction. The huge eyes provide excellent night vision.

The fur is thick, silky, and colored gray to dark brown. The narrow tail, usually used for balancing, is bald except for a tuft of fur at the end, and is about twice the body length. The elongated ankle bone, from which the group takes its name, allows the tarsier to leap distances of up to 5 meters (16 ft) between trees.

Behaviour and ecology

The Philippine tarsier is primarily insectivorous, feeding on live insects such as grasshoppers and crickets, as well as spiders and small vertebrates such as lizards and birds. It is nocturnal and lives in a solitary lifestyle, though groups of two to four individuals have been observed. During the day it sleeps in dark hollows close to the ground, such as near tree trunks. Communication is mainly by scent marking, and it also uses ultrasonic vocalizations with a dominant frequency around 70 kHz.

Conservation

The species is listed as Near Threatened by the IUCN. Populations have declined due to habitat destruction, hunting for the pet trade, and predation by domestic cats and dogs. The Philippine Tarsier Sanctuary in Corella, Bohol, works to protect the species. Republic Act No. 9147, the Wildlife Resources Conservation and Protection Act, provides for its protection.
