Romanticism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Romanticism was an artistic, literary, musical, and intellectual movement that originated in Europe towards the end of the 18th century and reached its peak in the period from about 1800 to 1850. It was partly a reaction against the Industrial Revolution and against the rationalizing, scientific norms of the Enlightenment, emphasizing emotion, imagination, individualism, and the glorification of nature and the past — particularly the medieval past.

Characteristics

Romantic works prize strong emotion as an authentic source of aesthetic experience, placing new emphasis on horror, terror, awe, and the sublime. They celebrate the individual imagination as a critical authority, elevate folk culture and national traditions, and frequently turn to exotic, remote, or supernatural subject matter. Nature is presented not as a machine to be measured but as a living, spiritually charged presence.

Literature

In Britain, the movement is associated with William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge, whose Lyrical Ballads (1798) is a landmark, and later with Lord Byron, Percy Bysshe Shelley, John Keats, and Mary Shelley, whose Frankenstein (1818) fused Romantic themes with early science fiction. In Germany, Goethe, Schiller, Novalis, and the Brothers Grimm were central; in France, Victor Hugo and George Sand; in the United States, Washington Irving, James Fenimore Cooper, and later the Transcendentalists.

Visual arts and music

Romantic painting is exemplified by Caspar David Friedrich's contemplative landscapes, J. M. W. Turner's turbulent seascapes, and Eugène Delacroix's dramatic history scenes. In music, composers such as Beethoven, Schubert, Chopin, Liszt, and Wagner expanded expressive range, harmony, and form, making music the paradigmatic Romantic art.

Legacy

Romanticism shaped modern ideas of artistic genius, national identity, and the self, and its influence persists in fantasy literature, film, and popular music. Scholars note that the movement was never a single doctrine: national variants differed widely, and the term's meaning remains debated.
