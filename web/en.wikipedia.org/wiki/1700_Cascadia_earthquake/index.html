1700 Cascadia earthquake

Date26 January 1700, about 21:00 local time
EpicentreOffshore Pacific Northwest, Cascadia subduction zone
MagnitudeEstimated 8.7–9.2
EffectsCoastal subsidence, destruction of villages, tsunami reaching Japan

The 1700 Cascadia earthquake was a megathrust earthquake that occurred along the Cascadia subduction zone on 26 January 1700, with an estimated magnitude of 8.7 to 9.2. The earthquake ruptured the roughly 1,000-kilometre (620-mile) fault boundary where the Juan de Fuca Plate subducts beneath the North American Plate, from mid-Vancouver Island in British Columbia south to northern California. Because it predates written records in the Pacific Northwest, its date and size were reconstructed from geological evidence, Indigenous oral traditions, tree rings, and Japanese records of an "orphan tsunami."

Evidence

Along the coast, the earthquake caused the land to drop suddenly by as much as two metres, drowning forests and marshes; the resulting "ghost forests" of dead cedar trees, killed when their roots were submerged in salt water, can still be seen in places such as the Copalis River estuary. Tree-ring studies dated the trees' death to the 1699–1700 growing season. Offshore, layers of sand deposited by a tsunami and turbidites on the seafloor corroborate a great earthquake at that time.

Indigenous peoples of the coast preserved oral traditions of violent shaking and flooding that destroyed villages — for example, the Huu-ay-aht tradition of the destruction of a village at Pachena Bay on Vancouver Island. In Japan, officials documented a tsunami striking on 27–28 January 1700 (local time) along hundreds of kilometres of the Pacific coast, damaging villages and rice stores — but no earthquake had been felt in Japan, making it an "orphan tsunami." By modelling the tsunami's heights in Japan, researchers back-calculated the American earthquake's magnitude at about 9.0 and its origin time to around 9 p.m. Pacific time on 26 January 1700.

Future risk

Geological studies indicate that the Cascadia subduction zone has produced great earthquakes roughly every 300 to 600 years; the 1700 event was the most recent. Seismologists estimate a significant probability — often cited as roughly 10–15 percent over 50 years for a full-margin rupture — of another magnitude-9 event, which would strongly shake the Pacific Northwest and generate a major tsunami. The 1700 earthquake has driven extensive preparedness planning in British Columbia, Washington and Oregon.
