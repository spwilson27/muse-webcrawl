The Naval Special Warfare Development Group (DEVGRU), popularly known as SEAL Team Six, is the United States Navy component of the Joint Special Operations Command (JSOC). It is one of the United States' principal special mission units, tasked with counterterrorism, hostage rescue, special reconnaissance, and direct action missions. Personnel are drawn from the Navy SEAL teams and undergo a further selection and training pipeline.

History

The unit was founded in November 1980 by Commander Richard Marcinko, its first commanding officer, in the aftermath of the failed Operation Eagle Claw hostage rescue attempt in Iran. It was given the name "SEAL Team Six" partly to confuse Soviet intelligence about the number of SEAL teams in existence — at the time there were only two. In 1987 the unit was disestablished and immediately re-formed as the Naval Special Warfare Development Group. DEVGRU is administratively supported by Naval Special Warfare Command but operationally controlled by JSOC.

Organisation and selection

DEVGRU is organised into colour-coded squadrons — historically including Red, Blue, Gold, and Silver assault squadrons, a Black squadron for reconnaissance and surveillance, and a Gray squadron for mobility support. Candidates, usually experienced SEALs, pass a rigorous selection and an approximately eight-month operator training course in close-quarters battle, parachuting, diving, and other skills before assignment to a squadron.

Notable operations

  • Operation Urgent Fury (1983) — Grenada; SEALs from the unit conducted reconnaissance and the rescue of Governor-General Paul Scoon.
  • Maersk Alabama hijacking (2009) — DEVGRU snipers simultaneously killed three Somali pirates holding Captain Richard Phillips.
  • Operation Neptune Spear (2011) — a DEVGRU assault force killed Osama bin Laden at his compound in Abbottabad, Pakistan, the unit's most famous operation.
  • Operation Celestial Balance (2009) and numerous missions in Afghanistan, Iraq, Yemen, and Somalia during the war on terror.

Controversies

Investigative reporting, notably a 2015 New York Times series, has raised allegations about civilian casualties, loose rules of engagement, and a culture of secrecy within the unit, prompting internal reviews. The unit's members have also been the subject of popular books and films, sometimes in tension with the SEAL ethos of quiet professionalism.
