Wampanoag

The Wampanoag (Wôpanâak, "People of the First Light" or "People of the Dawn") are a Native American people of the Northeastern Woodlands, inhabiting what is now southeastern Massachusetts and eastern Rhode Island, including Cape Cod, Martha's Vineyard, and Nantucket. Historically they spoke the Massachusett (Wôpanâak) language, an Eastern Algonquian language.

A reconstructed Wampanoag wetu (dwelling)

History

Before European contact, the Wampanoag comprised a confederation of villages led by sachems under a grand sachem (massasoit). Epidemics between 1616 and 1619 devastated coastal communities, including Patuxet, the village where Plymouth Colony was founded in 1620. The massasoit Ousamequin formed an alliance with the Pilgrims, aided by the English-speaking Tisquantum (Squanto); the harvest feast they shared in 1621 is remembered in the American Thanksgiving tradition. Relations deteriorated, and in 1675–1676 the region was ravaged by King Philip's War, led by Ousamequin's son Metacomet (King Philip), after which many Wampanoag were killed, enslaved, or displaced.

Modern communities

Today several Wampanoag communities persist, including the federally recognised Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe and the Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah) on Martha's Vineyard, recognised in 1987, as well as state-recognised communities such as Assonet, Herring Pond, Pocasset, and Ponkapoag. The Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project, begun in the 1990s, has revived the language from documents such as the 17th-century Eliot Bible, and the first native speakers in generations have been raised.
