Mountain film

A mountain film (German: Bergfilm) is a film genre that centres on mountaineering and the depiction of mountain landscapes, in which the mountains themselves often play the role of a principal character or antagonist. The genre flourished in Germany in the 1920s and 1930s and later influenced adventure and documentary filmmaking worldwide.

History

The genre is associated above all with the German director Arnold Fanck, whose films such as The Holy Mountain (1926), The White Hell of Pitz Palu (1929, co-directed with G. W. Pabst) and Storm over Mont Blanc (1930) combined spectacular location photography in the Alps with melodramatic plots about climbers, rescuers and the struggle against nature. These films made a star of Leni Riefenstahl, who acted in several of them before becoming a director herself, and featured climbers such as Luis Trenker, who went on to direct his own mountain films.

Fanck's pioneering techniques — shooting on location at altitude, using telephoto lenses and filters to dramatise cloud and snow, and filming actual climbing — established much of the visual vocabulary of outdoor cinematography. Ski films formed a closely related subgenre.

Themes and legacy

Mountain films typically celebrate endurance, purity and spiritual transcendence in the high mountains while dwelling on their dangers: storms, avalanches and falls. Scholars have debated the genre's relationship to the ideology of the Nazi era, since its exaltation of struggle, sacrifice and the homeland landscape overlapped with themes later exploited by National Socialist propaganda, although the classic Bergfilme predate the Nazi seizure of power.

The genre's legacy includes later mountaineering dramas and documentaries such as The White Tower (1950), Touching the Void (2003), North Face (2008) and Everest (2015), as well as the many mountain film festivals, of which the Banff Mountain Film Festival in Canada is among the best known.
