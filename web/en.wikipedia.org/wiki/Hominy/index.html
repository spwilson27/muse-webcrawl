Hominy

Hominy is a food produced from dried maize (corn) kernels that have been treated with an alkali, in a process called nixtamalization (from the Nahuatl word nextli, meaning ashes, and tamalli, meaning masa or dough). Lye and food-grade lime are typical alkalis used, though traditional recipes use wood ash. The process softens the kernels, loosens their hulls, and causes them to swell to roughly twice their original size.

History

Nixtamalization originated in Mesoamerica several thousand years ago. Treating maize with alkali has major nutritional benefits: it frees niacin (vitamin B3) that is bound in untreated corn, improves the balance of amino acids, and adds dietary calcium from the lime. Populations that adopted corn as a staple without nixtamalization—such as poor communities in the American South and parts of Europe—suffered epidemics of pellagra, the niacin-deficiency disease.

Uses

Whole hominy kernels are a staple ingredient in the Mexican soup pozole, and in the United States hominy is coarsely ground to make grits, a porridge associated with Southern cuisine. Nixtamalized corn is also ground into masa, the dough used for tortillas, tamales, and pupusas. Canned hominy, sold in white and yellow varieties, is the form most commonly found in North American grocery stores; dried hominy must be soaked and simmered before use.

Preparation

To make hominy, dried corn kernels are soaked and cooked in an alkaline solution—traditionally water mixed with calcium hydroxide (slaked lime) or wood-ash lye—then left to steep for hours. The softened hulls (pericarps) are rubbed off and rinsed away, and the kernels may be dried again for storage or used fresh.
