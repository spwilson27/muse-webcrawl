Analytic signal

In mathematics and signal processing, an analytic signal is a complex-valued function that has no negative frequency components. For a real-valued signal x(t), its analytic signal xa(t) is formed by combining x(t) with its Hilbert transform as the imaginary part: xa(t) = x(t) + j·ĥ(t), where ĥ(t) is the Hilbert transform of x(t). The concept was introduced by Dennis Gabor in 1946.

The magnitude of the analytic signal traces the envelope of the real signal

Construction

In the frequency domain, the analytic signal is obtained by keeping the positive-frequency components of the signal's Fourier transform (doubled, except at DC) and setting the negative-frequency components to zero. Equivalently, the imaginary part is the original signal phase-shifted by −90° at every positive frequency.

Properties and uses

The modulus of the analytic signal gives the instantaneous amplitude (envelope) of the real signal, and its argument gives the instantaneous phase, whose derivative is the instantaneous frequency. These quantities underpin techniques in communications (single-sideband modulation and demodulation), radar, seismology, and biomedical signal analysis. For narrowband signals, the analytic representation behaves intuitively; for wideband signals, instantaneous frequency can take values that require careful interpretation.

Discrete-time version

In digital signal processing, the analytic signal is commonly computed by taking the FFT of a sampled signal, zeroing the negative-frequency bins, doubling the positive bins, and applying the inverse FFT.
