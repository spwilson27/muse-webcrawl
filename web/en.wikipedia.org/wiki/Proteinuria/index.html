Proteinuria

Proteinuria is the presence of excess proteins in the urine. In healthy persons, urine normally contains only trace amounts of protein (less than 150 mg per day), because most proteins are too large to pass through the kidney's glomerular filtration barrier or are reabsorbed in the tubules. Persistent proteinuria is a key marker of kidney damage and an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease.

Causes

Proteinuria is classified by mechanism:

  • Glomerular — damage to the filtering membrane, as in diabetic nephropathy, glomerulonephritis, and amyloidosis; typically the largest quantities of protein, mainly albumin.
  • Tubular — failure of the tubules to reabsorb small proteins normally filtered.
  • Overflow — excess production of small proteins that overwhelm reabsorptive capacity, as in multiple myeloma (Bence Jones protein).
  • Functional/transient — temporary proteinuria during fever, exercise, heart failure or urinary tract infection.
  • Orthostatic — proteinuria only in the upright posture, generally benign in adolescents.

Diagnosis

Proteinuria is most often first detected by urine dipstick, which is sensitive mainly to albumin. Quantification is done by measuring the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) or protein-to-creatinine ratio on a spot sample, or by 24-hour urine collection. Nephrotic-range proteinuria exceeds 3.5 g per day and, together with hypoalbuminaemia, oedema and hyperlipidaemia, defines the nephrotic syndrome. Albuminuria categories (A1–A3) form part of the KDIGO classification of chronic kidney disease.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause. ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers reduce proteinuria and slow progression of kidney disease; blood pressure and blood glucose control are central in diabetic kidney disease. Reducing proteinuria is itself a therapeutic target because the degree of reduction correlates with slower loss of kidney function.
