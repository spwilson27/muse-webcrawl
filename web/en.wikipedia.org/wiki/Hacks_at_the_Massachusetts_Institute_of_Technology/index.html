Hacks at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The celebrated "car on the Dome" hack of 1994.

Hacks at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology are practical jokes and pranks meant to prominently demonstrate technical aptitude and cleverness, or to commemorate popular culture and historical topics. Students, alumni and others execute often elaborate — and usually benign — engineering projects, installations and feats; the pranksters are known as hackers, and their exploits are a celebrated part of MIT student life, documented in books such as Nightwork: A History of Hacks and Pranks at MIT.

The ethos of hacking

MIT hacking operates under an informal code of ethics: hacks should be clever, beautiful, and safe; they should not damage property, must be removable without cost or harm, and should not target individuals. The unwritten rules distinguish hacking from vandalism and encourage designs that leave onlookers amused rather than inconvenienced. The Great Dome (Building 10) and other prominent rooftops are classic venues, and the reaction of the campus authorities has often been indulgent — the MIT Museum maintains a Hall of Hacks.

Notable hacks

  • Campus police car on the Great Dome (1994) — a full-size replica of an MIT police cruiser, complete with flashing lights and a dummy officer, assembled atop the dome; widely considered the archetypal dome hack.
  • Measuring the Harvard Bridge (1958) — pledges of Lambda Chi Alpha measured the bridge using the body of Oliver Smoot, giving rise to the "smoot" as a unit of length; Smoot later became head of ISO and ANSI.
  • Caltech cannon theft (1986) — MIT students spirited away the rival institute's 130-year-old Fleming cannon across the country and installed it outside their dormitory; it was returned twenty years later after a reciprocal prank.
  • Tetris on a building (2012) — the Green Building's windows were converted into a playable Tetris game, long described as hackers' "holy grail".
  • Apollo-era and anniversary hacks — including a weather balloon popping out at a 1982 Harvard–MIT football game ("MIT" printed on it) and numerous tributes to anniversaries of the moon landing and to popular media releases.

Significance

Hacking is frequently cited by MIT itself as expressing the institute's hands-on ethos (mens et manus, "mind and hand"). The tradition helped popularise the word "hack" in its sense of an ingenious, playful technical feat, which spread from MIT's model-railroad and computing clubs into wider computing culture.
