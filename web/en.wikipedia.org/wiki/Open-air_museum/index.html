Open-air museum

Historic buildings reassembled as a village at an open-air museum.

An open-air museum (or open-air exhibition) is a museum that exhibits collections of buildings and artefacts outdoors. Such museums typically present complete historic structures — often dismantled and rebuilt on the site — arranged as farms, villages or town streets, and frequently employ costumed interpreters demonstrating crafts and daily life ("living history").

History

The concept originated in Scandinavia in the late nineteenth century. King Oscar II of Sweden and Norway assembled a collection of relocated buildings at Bygdøy near Oslo from the 1880s. The most influential example is Skansen in Stockholm, founded by Artur Hazelius in 1891, which became the model for hundreds of successors. In North America the form developed through Colonial Williamsburg (from 1928), Old Sturbridge Village and Henry Ford's Greenfield Village.

Types

  • Folk museums — regional rural buildings and peasant culture (e.g. the Zuiderzee Museum, Netherlands; Beamish, England)
  • Living-history farms and towns — interpreters work the site as in a chosen period
  • Ecomuseums — a whole territory and its community treated as the museum
  • Archaeological open-air museums — experimental reconstructions on or near excavation sites
  • Sculpture parks — a related, sometimes distinguished, category

Criticism

Open-air museums are sometimes criticised for presenting an idealised, decontextualised past: buildings are removed from their original settings, and hardships of historical life may be softened for visitors. Defenders reply that relocation often saved structures from demolition, and that immersive presentation reaches audiences conventional museums cannot.
