Cube root

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The graph of y = ∛x is symmetric about the origin.

In mathematics, a cube root of a number x is a number y such that y3 = x. It is written ∛x or x1/3. For example, the cube root of 27 is 3, because 33 = 27; the cube root of 8 is 2; and the cube root of 1 is 1.

Properties

Unlike the square root, the cube root is defined for every real number, including negative numbers, because the cube of a negative number is negative: ∛(−8) = −2. Every real number has exactly one real cube root, and the cube root function is an odd function whose graph is symmetric under a 180-degree rotation about the origin. Cube roots preserve order: if a < b then ∛a < ∛b.

Numbers whose cube roots are integers are called perfect cubes (1, 8, 27, 64, 125, ...). For other numbers the cube root is irrational; for instance, ∛2 ≈ 1.2599... is of historical importance because doubling the cube — constructing a cube with twice the volume of a given cube — requires constructing the length ∛2, one of the classical problems of antiquity that cannot be solved with compass and straightedge alone.

Computation

Cube roots can be computed with Newton's method applied to the equation y3 − x = 0, giving the iteration yn+1 = (2yn + x/yn2)/3, which converges rapidly. General formulas also exist: Cardano's formula gives cube roots as part of the solution of the cubic equation, and indeed the systematic treatment of cube roots developed historically in connection with solving cubics in the work of Scipione del Ferro, Tartaglia, and Gerolamo Cardano in the 16th century.

Complex cube roots

In the complex numbers, every nonzero number has exactly three cube roots. For a positive real x, only one is real; the other two are complex conjugates involving the primitive cube roots of unity, (−1 ± i√3)/2. The cube roots of 1 are 1 and the two complex numbers ω and ω2, which play a role in number theory and in the formula for solving cubic equations.
