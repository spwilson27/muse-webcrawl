Orthopaedic surgery

Orthopaedic surgery or orthopaedics (American spelling: orthopedic surgery / orthopedics) is the branch of surgery concerned with conditions involving the musculoskeletal system. Orthopaedic surgeons use both surgical and nonsurgical means to treat musculoskeletal trauma, spine diseases, sports injuries, degenerative diseases, infections, tumours and congenital disorders.

Etymology

The word orthopaedia was coined by the French physician Nicolas Andry, who published Orthopédie in 1741. It derives from the Greek words ὀρθός (orthos, "straight, correct") and παιδεία (paideia, "child upbringing"). Andry's book was aimed at correcting deformities in children, and the emblem of an irregular tree tied to a straight stake to guide its growth remains a symbol of the specialty.

Scope

Modern orthopaedic surgery addresses a wide range of conditions, including:

  • Fractures and dislocations
  • Arthritis and joint replacement (arthroplasty) of the hip, knee and shoulder
  • Sports injuries, including anterior cruciate ligament tears and meniscal injuries
  • Spinal disorders such as scoliosis, disc herniation and spinal stenosis
  • Hand and microsurgery
  • Musculoskeletal oncology
  • Paediatric orthopaedics, including clubfoot and developmental dysplasia of the hip

Techniques

Common techniques include open reduction and internal fixation of fractures, arthroscopy (minimally invasive joint surgery), joint replacement using metal, ceramic or polyethylene prostheses, spinal fusion, and external fixation. Advances in imaging, biomaterials and computer-assisted navigation have substantially improved outcomes since the mid-20th century, when pioneers such as John Charnley developed modern total hip replacement using a metal femoral component and a polyethylene socket fixed with acrylic bone cement.

Training

In the United States, orthopaedic surgery residency follows medical school and lasts five years, after which many surgeons complete a further fellowship in a subspecialty such as sports medicine, hand surgery, spine surgery or joint replacement. In the United Kingdom, training follows a similar structure under the supervision of the Royal Colleges of Surgeons.
