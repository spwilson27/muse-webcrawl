Princeps senatus

Placeholder image: the Roman Senate in session

The princeps senatus (plural principes senatus; Latin for "first man of the Senate" or "leader of the Senate") was the first member by precedence of the Roman Senate. Although officially out of the cursus honorum and owning no imperium, the princeps senatus was afforded great prestige in the Roman Republic.

Overview

The princeps senatus enjoyed enormous auctoritas and influence within the Senate. He was entitled to speak first on any motion, which allowed him to set the tone for debate, and he could summon and adjourn meetings of the Senate. In the middle Republic the office was usually held by a patrician former censor of great distinction.

Selection

The princeps was chosen by the two censors every five years during the census. The censors were expected to select the senator considered most distinguished in character and public service; by convention the office went to a patrician ex-censor. If the two censors disagreed, lots could be cast.

History

Notable holders include Publius Cornelius Scipio Africanus, elected princeps in 199 BC and re-elected at two further censuses, and Marcus Aemilius Scaurus, who held the position for most of the last decades of the second century BC. The position lapsed as a separate senatorial office after Augustus took the title for himself in 28 BC, declaring himself princeps senatus when the Senate was revised.

Imperial period

Under the Empire, the emperor himself was the princeps senatus, and the title princeps became one of the principal informal designations of the emperor — giving rise to the term Principate for the early imperial regime.
