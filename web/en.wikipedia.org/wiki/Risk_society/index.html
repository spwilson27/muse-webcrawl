Risk society

Risk society (Risikogesellschaft) is a concept developed by the German sociologist Ulrich Beck in his 1986 book Risk Society: Towards a New Modernity, published the year of the Chernobyl disaster. Beck argued that late-modern societies are increasingly organized around the production and distribution of risks — hazards manufactured by industrial and technological development itself — rather than around the distribution of wealth, as in classical industrial society.

Key arguments

Modern risks such as nuclear contamination, chemical pollution, and climate change are global, invisible to the senses, dependent on scientific expertise to be known at all, and tend to escape the control and insurance mechanisms of institutions — a condition Beck called "organized irresponsibility". Whereas class societies are structured by the logic "I am hungry", risk societies are united, at least in principle, by "I am afraid", since such hazards can eventually affect even the wealthy (the "boomerang effect").

Beck's related concepts include reflexive modernization and cosmopolitanism; Anthony Giddens developed a parallel account emphasizing manufactured uncertainty in late modernity. The theory has been influential in environmental sociology, public health, and studies of food and technology controversies.
