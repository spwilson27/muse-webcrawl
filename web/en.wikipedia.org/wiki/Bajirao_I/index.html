Bajirao I (born Bajirao Balaji Vishwanath; 18 August 1700 – 28 April 1740) was the seventh Peshwa (prime minister) of the Maratha Empire in India. Appointed at the age of twenty by Chhatrapati Shahu I, he served from 1720 until his death and is widely credited with transforming the Marathas from a regional Deccan power into an empire spanning much of the Indian subcontinent. In a career of twenty years he is said never to have lost a battle, and military historians often rank him among the greatest cavalry generals of India.

Military campaigns

Bajirao's strategy relied on speed, mobility, and deep cavalry raids, bypassing strong fortresses to strike at the enemy's heartland and supply lines. His masterpiece was the Battle of Palkhed (1728), in which he manoeuvred the much larger army of the Nizam of Hyderabad into a waterless trap and forced it to terms without a major pitched battle. In 1737 he raided to the very outskirts of Delhi, defeating a Mughal army on the way, and in 1738 he overwhelmed the Nizam again at the Battle of Bhopal, gaining Malwa and territory up to the Chambal River in the Treaty of Doraha. Under him Maratha power expanded across Gujarat, Malwa, and Bundelkhand, where he aided the Bundela raja Chhatrasal against the Mughals.

Personal life

Bajirao was the eldest son of Peshwa Balaji Vishwanath. He was married to Kashibai, with whom he had sons including Balaji Baji Rao (Nanasaheb), who succeeded him as Peshwa. His relationship with Mastani, the daughter of Raja Chhatrasal, was controversial at the orthodox Pune court of the time; she bore him a son, Shamsher Bahadur, and the couple's story has become a staple of Marathi literature and Indian cinema, most prominently the 2015 film Bajirao Mastani.

Death and legacy

Bajirao died suddenly of fever on 28 April 1740 while encamped at Raverkhedi on the banks of the Narmada during a campaign against the Nizam. He was cremated there, where a memorial (samadhi) still stands. His legacy includes the expansion of Maratha dominion to the gates of Delhi and the establishment of ruling houses — Scindia, Holkar, Gaekwad, and Bhonsle of Nagpur — whose founders rose as his commanders and sardars.
