The judiciary of France is the system of courts that administers justice under French law. Reflecting the French civil-law tradition and a strict separation between public and private law, it is divided into two parallel orders of courts: the judicial order (ordre judiciaire), handling civil and criminal cases and headed by the Court of Cassation, and the administrative order (ordre administratif), handling disputes involving the state and headed by the Council of State. The Constitutional Council separately reviews the constitutionality of legislation.

The judicial order

Civil justice is administered by judicial tribunals (tribunaux judiciaires, formed in 2020 by merging earlier courts), with specialised courts for commerce (tribunaux de commerce, staffed by elected businesspeople), employment disputes (the conseils de prud'hommes, with equal employer and employee representation) and rural leases. Appeals lie to the courts of appeal (cours d'appel). In criminal matters, police courts try minor offences (contraventions), correctional courts try délits, and the cour d'assises — a mixed panel of professional judges and lay jurors — tries the gravest crimes (crimes). The Court of Cassation, sitting in the Palais de Justice in Paris, is the final court of appeal on points of law for the whole judicial order.

The administrative order

Administrative courts (tribunaux administratifs), administrative courts of appeal and the Council of State adjudicate disputes between citizens and public authorities — taxation, planning, public employment and the legality of administrative acts (recours pour excès de pouvoir). The Council of State also advises the government on bills, a dual role inherited from its Napoleonic origins in 1799. Conflicts of jurisdiction between the two orders are resolved by the Tribunal des conflits.

The magistracy

French judges and prosecutors together form the magistrature; career judges are trained at the National School for the Judiciary (École nationale de la magistrature) in Bordeaux. Judges of the bench (magistrats du siège) enjoy constitutional guarantees of independence and irremovability, while prosecutors (magistrats du parquet) remain hierarchically subordinate to the Minister of Justice — a distinction frequently debated in French public life. The inquisitorial role of the investigating judge (juge d'instruction) in serious criminal cases is a distinctive feature of French criminal procedure.

Constitutional review

The Constitutional Council (Conseil constitutionnel), created by the Constitution of the Fifth Republic in 1958, rules on the constitutionality of statutes before promulgation and, since 2008–2010, on referrals from the courts in pending cases (the question prioritaire de constitutionnalité).
