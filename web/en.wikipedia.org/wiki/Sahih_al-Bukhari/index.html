Sahih al-Bukhari (Arabic: صحيح البخاري, Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī) is a collection of hadith compiled by the Persian Islamic scholar Muhammad ibn Ismail al-Bukhari (810–870). It is regarded by Sunni Muslims as the most authentic (sahih) of all hadith collections, ranking alongside the Quran in esteem within Sunni tradition, and is placed first among the six major canonical hadith collections known as the Kutub al-Sittah.

Compilation

Al-Bukhari travelled widely throughout the Abbasid Caliphate, visiting centres of learning in Iraq, the Hejaz, Syria, Egypt, Khorasan and Transoxiana in search of hadith reports. According to tradition, he examined some 600,000 narrations and selected only those he judged to meet his rigorous criteria of authenticity, ultimately including roughly 7,563 narrations when repetitions are counted, or about 2,600 distinct reports without repetition. The compilation is said to have taken about sixteen years, and al-Bukhari completed it around 846 CE in Bukhara.

Methodology

Al-Bukhari imposed stringent conditions on the chain of transmission (isnad) of each report: every narrator had to be upright and accurate, the chain had to be unbroken, and consecutive narrators had to have actually met one another rather than merely overlapped in time. He also considered the text (matn) of each report for consistency with established knowledge. This methodology set the standard for later hadith criticism.

Arrangement and contents

The collection is organised into books (kutub) covering topics such as the beginning of revelation, faith, knowledge, ritual ablution, prayer, almsgiving, pilgrimage, fasting, trade, and the virtues of the companions of the Prophet, concluding with books on monotheism (tawhid). Each book is divided into chapters whose headings (tarjamat al-bab) often express al-Bukhari's own legal and theological reasoning, making the work a significant source of Islamic jurisprudence as well as of reports.

Reception and commentaries

Sahih al-Bukhari generated an extensive commentary literature. The most celebrated commentary is Fath al-Bari by Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani (d. 1449); other major commentaries include those of al-Kirmani, al-Ayni (Umdat al-Qari), and al-Qastallani (Irshad al-Sari). Abridgements such as al-Zabidi's Tajrid al-Sarih made the collection more accessible. The work was among the earliest Arabic texts printed in Europe and has been translated into many languages, including the widely used English translation by Muhammad Muhsin Khan.
