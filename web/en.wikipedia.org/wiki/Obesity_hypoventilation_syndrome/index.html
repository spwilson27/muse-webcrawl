Obesity hypoventilation syndrome

Other names: Pickwickian syndrome

Specialty: Pulmonology, sleep medicine

Symptoms: Excessive daytime sleepiness, poor sleep quality, breathlessness

Causes: Obesity causing impaired ventilation

Treatment: Weight loss, positive airway pressure

Obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS), historically known as Pickwickian syndrome, is a condition in which severely overweight people fail to breathe rapidly or deeply enough, resulting in low blood oxygen levels (hypoxemia) and high blood carbon dioxide levels (hypercapnia) during both sleep and wakefulness. Many people with the condition also frequently stop breathing for short periods during sleep (obstructive sleep apnea), resulting in many partial awakenings and excessive daytime sleepiness.

Signs and symptoms

Most people with obesity hypoventilation syndrome have obstructive sleep apnea as well. Symptoms include loud snoring, witnessed apneas, unrefreshing sleep, morning headaches, fatigue, and breathlessness on exertion. In advanced cases, elevated carbon dioxide leads to drowsiness, and chronic hypoxemia can cause pulmonary hypertension and right-sided heart failure (cor pulmonale), with swelling of the legs and other signs of fluid retention.

Causes and mechanism

The exact cause is not fully understood. Contributing factors include the mechanical load of excess adipose tissue on the chest wall and abdomen, which reduces lung volumes and increases the work of breathing, and a blunted central ventilatory response to carbon dioxide, partly attributed to leptin resistance. The chronic hypercapnia distinguishes OHS from uncomplicated obstructive sleep apnea.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires a body mass index above 30 kg/m², daytime arterial carbon dioxide above 45 mmHg on arterial blood gas measurement, and exclusion of other causes of hypoventilation such as severe lung or neuromuscular disease. Polysomnography typically shows sleep hypoventilation with or without obstructive events. Elevated serum bicarbonate is a useful screening clue.

Treatment

Treatment centers on weight loss, which can substantially improve or resolve the condition, and on positive airway pressure therapy during sleep — continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) for those with predominant obstructive sleep apnea, or non-invasive ventilation (such as bilevel positive airway pressure) for those with persistent hypoventilation. Bariatric surgery may be considered in appropriate patients.

History

The name "Pickwickian syndrome" derives from Joe, the fat boy in Charles Dickens's 1837 novel The Pickwick Papers, who was described as perpetually sleepy. The association between obesity, somnolence, and hypoventilation was formally described in the medical literature in the 1950s.
