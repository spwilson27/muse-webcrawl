Investment treaty arbitration

AreaInternational law
Also known asInvestor–state dispute settlement (ISDS)
Key bodiesICSID, PCA, UNCITRAL

Investment treaty arbitration is a mechanism of international law by which a foreign investor may bring a claim directly against a host state before an international arbitral tribunal, alleging breach of protections in a bilateral investment treaty (BIT), a multilateral treaty such as the Energy Charter Treaty, or an investment chapter of a free trade agreement.

History

The first BIT was signed between Germany and Pakistan in 1959. The system expanded greatly after the creation of the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID) by the Washington Convention of 1965, and after the proliferation of BITs in the 1990s following the end of the Cold War. Earlier, investors relied on diplomatic protection by their home state or on local courts.

Protections

Typical standards include fair and equitable treatment, protection against expropriation without prompt, adequate and effective compensation, national treatment, most-favoured-nation treatment, full protection and security, and guarantees of free transfer of funds. Tribunals have debated the scope of fair and equitable treatment, especially in relation to legitimate expectations and regulatory change.

Procedure

Cases are commonly administered by ICSID, the Permanent Court of Arbitration, or conducted ad hoc under UNCITRAL rules. Awards are generally final, subject to annulment on narrow grounds under ICSID or set-aside at the seat. Enforcement of non-ICSID awards proceeds under the New York Convention.

Criticism and reform

Critics argue ISDS can chill regulation on health, environment and public services, lacks consistency and transparency, and gives foreign investors rights unavailable to domestic firms. Defenders say it depoliticises disputes and encourages investment. Reform efforts include the EU's proposed Investment Court System, UNCITRAL Working Group III, and newer treaties that narrow definitions, add appellate mechanisms, or omit ISDS, as in the CPTPP side arrangements and USMCA's reduced provisions.
