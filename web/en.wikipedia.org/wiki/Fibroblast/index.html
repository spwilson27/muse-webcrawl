A fibroblast is a type of biological cell that synthesises the extracellular matrix and collagen, produces the structural framework (stroma) for animal tissues, and plays a critical role in wound healing. Fibroblasts are the most common cells of connective tissue in animals.

Structure

Fibroblasts are typically elongated or spindle-shaped cells with an oval, flattened nucleus. Actively synthesising cells ("fibroblasts" in the strict sense) possess abundant rough endoplasmic reticulum and a prominent Golgi apparatus, whereas quiescent cells, often called fibrocytes, are smaller and less active. Unlike epithelial cells, fibroblasts do not form a basement membrane or display strong cell–cell adhesion; they migrate individually through the matrix.

Function

The primary function of fibroblasts is to maintain the structural integrity of connective tissues by continuously secreting the precursors of the extracellular matrix: collagens (chiefly types I and III), fibronectin, elastin, and ground substance rich in glycosaminoglycans. They also secrete growth factors and cytokines that influence neighbouring cells, and they help maintain tissue homeostasis through matrix turnover mediated by matrix metalloproteinases.

Wound healing and fibrosis

After injury, fibroblasts proliferate and migrate into the wound, laying down new collagen that is later remodelled into scar tissue. Under signals such as TGF-β, some fibroblasts differentiate into contractile myofibroblasts expressing α-smooth muscle actin, which help close the wound. Persistent myofibroblast activity drives fibrosis — pathological scarring in organs such as the lung, liver, and heart.

Role in disease

Cancer-associated fibroblasts are major components of tumour stroma and can promote or restrain tumour growth. Fibroblast dysfunction also contributes to chronic wounds, keloid scars, and the stiffened tissues of systemic sclerosis.

Use in research

Fibroblasts are easily cultured from skin biopsies and are workhorses of cell biology. Notably, in 2006 Takahashi and Yamanaka showed that adult mouse fibroblasts could be reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells by four transcription factors — a landmark of regenerative medicine.
