Neusis construction

In geometry, a neusis construction (Greek: νεῦσις, "inclination") is a construction method that uses a marked ruler in addition to the compass and unmarked straightedge of classical Greek geometry. The marked ruler bears two marks a fixed distance apart; in a neusis step, the ruler is slid and rotated ("verged") until one edge passes through a given point, one mark lies on one given curve, and the other mark lies on another given curve. Because this goes beyond the permitted tools of compass-and-straightedge construction, neusis can solve problems that are impossible classically.

What neusis can do

  • Angle trisection — Archimedes gave a neusis construction that trisects an arbitrary angle, impossible with compass and straightedge alone.
  • Doubling the cube — neusis constructions can find two mean proportionals, equivalently a cube root, solving the Delian problem.
  • Regular polygons — with neusis, regular heptagons, nonagons, and other polygons constructible by solving cubic equations become possible.

Mathematical power

Neusis constructions correspond algebraically to solving cubic (and some quartic) equations: the set of constructible numbers grows from the quadratic closure of the rationals (compass and straightedge) to the smallest field closed under square roots and real cube roots, sometimes called the field of neusis numbers. In the 19th century it was proved that angle trisection and cube doubling reduce to irreducible cubic equations, placing them out of reach of compass and straightedge but within reach of neusis.

History

Greek geometers classified problems as "plane" (solvable with line and circle), "solid" (needing conic sections), and "linear" (needing higher curves). Neusis constructions were used by Archimedes (for trisection and in the Book of Lemmas tradition), by Nicomedes, and in Pappus's Collection; Pappus criticised solving plane problems by neusis when simpler methods existed, but acknowledged its necessity for solid problems. The method is closely related to the verging constructions in Apollonius's lost work On Verging Constructions, which Viète attempted to reconstruct in 1600.

Interest in neusis persisted into the modern era: origami (paper folding) axioms are at least as powerful as neusis, and marked-ruler constructions remain a standard topic in the theory of geometric constructions.
