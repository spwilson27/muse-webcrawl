Chronology protection conjecture

A closed timelike curve, whose formation the conjecture forbids

The chronology protection conjecture is a hypothesis first proposed by Stephen Hawking that the laws of physics prevent the formation of closed timelike curves and thus prevent time travel. In a 1992 paper, Hawking argued that the universe will not allow time machines to be created: "It seems that there is a Chronology Protection Agency which prevents the appearance of closed timelike curves and so makes the universe safe for historians."

Background

Solutions of general relativity, such as those involving rotating cylinders, wormholes, and certain cosmological models, permit closed timelike curves — paths through spacetime that return to their starting point in time. Such curves raise paradoxes, including the grandfather paradox, and threaten the consistency of causality.

Evidence and arguments

Hawking's argument rests on semiclassical gravity: as a spacetime approaches the formation of closed timelike curves, vacuum fluctuations of quantum fields circulate and amplify, producing a divergent stress-energy that back-reacts on the geometry and destroys the would-be time machine at or before the chronology horizon. Calculations by Hawking and others showed that the renormalized stress-energy tensor tends to diverge at such horizons in many cases.

Counterarguments note that the divergence may occur too late, or too weakly, to prevent the formation of the curves, and that a full theory of quantum gravity might resolve the question differently. The conjecture remains unproven but influential in discussions of time travel in physics.
