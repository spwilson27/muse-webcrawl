The 1964 Alaska earthquake, also known as the Great Alaskan earthquake and the Good Friday earthquake, occurred at 5:36 p.m. on Friday, March 27, 1964, in south-central Alaska. With a moment magnitude of 9.2, it remains the most powerful earthquake ever recorded in North America and the second most powerful in recorded history, after the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile.

The earthquake

The rupture occurred along roughly 800 km of the megathrust fault where the Pacific Plate subducts beneath the North American Plate. Ground shaking lasted nearly five minutes. Areas around Montague Island were uplifted by up to 11.5 metres, while areas near Kodiak subsided by as much as 2.3 metres. In Anchorage, about 120 km northwest of the epicenter, landslides and soil liquefaction destroyed whole blocks, including the Turnagain Heights neighborhood, where a bluff collapsed into Cook Inlet.

Tsunamis

Most of the 139 deaths were caused by tsunamis, both the tectonic tsunami generated by seafloor displacement and local waves from submarine landslides. The port communities of Valdez, Whittier, and Seward were devastated; Valdez was later relocated to higher ground. Waves damaged coasts as far away as British Columbia, Oregon, and California, where 12 people died at Crescent City.

Legacy

The earthquake provided crucial evidence for the then-controversial theory of plate tectonics, as George Plafker's fieldwork on uplift and subsidence patterns demonstrated megathrust faulting. It also led to the founding of tsunami warning infrastructure on the U.S. West Coast and major revisions to building codes in Alaska.
