Norethynodrel

[Image: Norethynodrel chemical structure]

Norethynodrel, or noretynodrel, is a progestin medication which was previously used in birth control pills and in the treatment of gynaecological disorders but is now no longer marketed. It was the progestin component of Enovid, the first combined oral contraceptive, approved in the United States in 1960.

History

Norethynodrel was synthesized in 1952 by Frank B. Colton at G. D. Searle & Company, building on the related compound norethisterone developed at Syntex. Combined with the estrogen mestranol, it was tested in large contraceptive trials in Puerto Rico in the mid-1950s led by John Rock, Gregory Pincus, and Edris Rice-Wray. Enovid was first approved for menstrual disorders in 1957 and for contraception in 1960, launching the era of oral contraception.

Pharmacology

Norethynodrel is a 19-nortestosterone derivative and the double-bond isomer of norethisterone; it is partially converted into norethisterone in the body. It acts as an agonist of the progesterone receptor, suppressing ovulation and altering cervical mucus and the endometrium. It also has weak estrogenic activity, distinguishing it from many later progestins.

Discontinuation

The high hormone doses of early pills were associated with side effects including thromboembolism, and norethynodrel was superseded by lower-dose formulations using norethisterone, levonorgestrel, and other progestins. Standalone and combined products were withdrawn over the 1970s–1990s.
