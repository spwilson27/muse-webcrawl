Millisecond pulsar

A millisecond pulsar (MSP) is a pulsar with a rotational period of less than about 10 milliseconds. These neutron stars are believed to be "recycled": spun up by accretion of matter from a companion star in a low-mass X-ray binary, which also weakens their magnetic fields. Millisecond pulsars are extraordinarily stable clocks, rivalling atomic clocks over long timescales.

Discovery

The first millisecond pulsar, PSR B1937+21 (period 1.56 ms, still among the fastest known), was discovered in 1982 by Donald Backer and colleagues, upending the belief that pulsars were born spinning fast and only slowed down. The fastest known, PSR J1748−2446ad in Terzan 5, rotates at 716 Hz.

Formation

In the recycling scenario, an old, slow pulsar in a binary accretes gas from its evolving companion for up to billions of years; angular momentum transfer spins it to millisecond periods. Evidence includes the abundance of MSPs in globular clusters and their frequent white-dwarf companions; transitional systems switching between accretion and pulsar states have been observed. Related: pulsar, neutron star.

Applications

Because of their timing precision, MSPs serve as probes of general relativity (binary pulsars), detectors for nanohertz gravitational waves via pulsar timing arrays (NANOGrav, EPTA), and celestial navigation references. Many host planets — the first exoplanets ever found orbit the millisecond pulsar PSR B1257+12.
