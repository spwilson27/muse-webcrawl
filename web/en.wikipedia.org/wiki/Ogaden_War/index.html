The Ogaden War, also known as the Ethio-Somali War, was a conflict fought from July 1977 to March 1978 between Somalia and Ethiopia over the disputed Ogaden region. The Somali government of Siad Barre, emboldened by Ethiopia's internal turmoil following the 1974 revolution and the overthrow of Emperor Haile Selassie, launched a full-scale invasion in support of ethnic Somali insurgents of the Western Somali Liberation Front (WSLF).

Background

The Ogaden, a vast arid plateau inhabited predominantly by ethnic Somalis, had been incorporated into the Ethiopian Empire in the late 19th century. Somalia, independent from 1960, pursued the ideal of "Greater Somalia", uniting all Somali-inhabited territories. A brief border war in 1964 ended inconclusively. After 1974, Ethiopia's new military government, the Derg, was weakened by revolution, the Eritrean insurgency and internal purges, while Somalia had built one of sub-Saharan Africa's largest armies with Soviet assistance.

The invasion

Somali regular forces crossed the border in July 1977 disguised alongside WSLF guerrillas. The invasion initially succeeded spectacularly: by September 1977 Somali forces controlled most of the Ogaden and besieged the cities of Harar and Dire Dawa, capturing Jijiga after heavy fighting. The advance threatened to cut Ethiopia's rail link to Djibouti, its main outlet to the sea.

Soviet and Cuban intervention

The conflict produced a dramatic superpower reversal. The Soviet Union, formerly Somalia's patron, switched sides to Ethiopia, mounting a massive airlift of weaponry worth an estimated one billion dollars. Some 15,000–17,000 Cuban combat troops, commanded by General Arnaldo Ochoa, were deployed to Ethiopia, along with Soviet advisers and South Yemeni units. In February–March 1978 a combined Ethiopian–Cuban counteroffensive broke the siege of Harar and drove Somali forces back across the border. On 9 March 1978 Siad Barre announced the withdrawal of Somali troops, and the war formally ended on 15 March.

Aftermath

Somalia's defeat had far-reaching consequences. Roughly a third of the Somali army was lost, hundreds of thousands of refugees fled into Somalia, and Siad Barre turned toward the United States for support. Resentment over the defeat contributed to failed coups and eventually to the collapse of the Somali state in 1991. The Ogaden remained part of Ethiopia, though low-level insurgency by the Ogaden National Liberation Front continued for decades.
