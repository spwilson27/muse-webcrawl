Thomas Middleton, one of the most successful Jacobean dramatists.

Thomas Middleton (baptised 18 April 1580 – buried 4 July 1627) was an English playwright and poet of the Jacobean era, generally regarded as second only to William Shakespeare among his contemporaries. He wrote prolifically across genres — city comedy, revenge tragedy, tragicomedy, masques, and civic pageantry — and from 1620 served as chronologer (official historian) of the City of London. Alone among early modern English dramatists, he achieved success with plays, pamphlets, Lord Mayor's Shows, and court masques alike.

Life and career

Middleton was born in London, the son of a bricklayer who had risen to the rank of gentleman. He matriculated at Queen's College, Oxford, in 1598 but left without a degree, returning to London to write. He began with verse satires and pamphlets, then found his métier in the theatre, writing for the boy companies and adult troupes — the Children of Paul's, the King's Men, and others. He collaborated with Thomas Dekker, John Webster, and especially William Rowley, and is believed to have adapted or contributed to Shakespeare's Timon of Athens and possibly Macbeth and Measure for Measure; modern editions of Shakespeare have increasingly credited his hand.

Works

Middleton's early city comedies — A Trick to Catch the Old One, Michaelmas Term, and A Mad World, My Masters — dissect London's moneyed world with cynical wit, culminating in A Chaste Maid in Cheapside (c. 1613), often called the finest city comedy of the period. His tragedies turn darker: The Revenger's Tragedy (1606; long attributed to Cyril Tourneur, now generally assigned to Middleton), The Second Maiden's Tragedy, Women Beware Women, and, with Rowley, The Changeling (1622), in which the murder plot of Beatrice-Joanna and De Flores remains one of the most disturbing studies of obsession in English drama.

In 1624 his allegorical chess play A Game at Chess — a transparent satire of Spanish diplomacy and the Spanish Match — ran for an unprecedented nine consecutive performances at the Globe before the authorities suppressed it following the Spanish ambassador's protest; Middleton was briefly imprisoned. It was the greatest box-office success of the early modern English stage, and he wrote no more plays.

Reputation

Neglected for centuries, Middleton was revived by 20th-century scholarship and stage production. T. S. Eliot judged him, after Shakespeare, the greatest of the Jacobean dramatists. The Oxford Thomas Middleton: The Collected Works (2007), edited by Gary Taylor and John Lavagnino, presented his canon — including, provocatively, his adaptations within Shakespeare's texts.
