Projectile point

A projectile point is a sharpened object — historically most often made of stone, but also of bone, antler, ivory, wood or metal — that was hafted to the tip of a projectile weapon such as a spear, dart, arrow or harpoon. Projectile points are among the most common and durable artefacts found at prehistoric archaeological sites, and because their shapes changed in recognisable ways over time and between cultures, they are used by archaeologists as diagnostic tools for dating sites and identifying cultural affiliations.

Terminology

In everyday usage, projectile points are often called "arrowheads", but archaeologists use the broader term because many points tipped darts thrown with a spear-thrower (atlatl) or hand-thrown spears rather than arrows. In North American archaeology, larger, earlier points are commonly associated with spears and darts, whereas smaller points appearing later generally tipped arrows, the bow having been adopted in the region within roughly the last 1,500 years.

Manufacture

Stone projectile points were produced by lithic reduction — striking flakes from a core of fine-grained, brittle stone such as flint, chert, obsidian or quartzite, then shaping the piece by percussion and pressure flaking. Many points have a deliberately shaped base (stemmed, notched, or fluted) to facilitate hafting with sinew or plant fibre and adhesive. Famous production techniques include the fluted points of the Clovis and Folsom cultures of the North American Paleoindian period (roughly 13,000–10,000 years ago), in which a long channel flake was removed from each face of the base.

Classification

Archaeologists classify projectile points by attributes including overall shape, blade edge form, base shape, flaking pattern and size. Hundreds of named types have been defined for North America alone, from large Paleoindian lanceolate points to the small triangular points of the late prehistoric period. Because types are time-sensitive, a point found in a datable context can help establish a chronology — a practice related to seriation.

Function and use

Projectile points were primarily hunting and warfare implements, but studies of wear patterns suggest some were also used as knives or for other cutting tasks. Broken and resharpened points are common finds; the life history of a point — manufacture, use, breakage, resharpening and discard — is a focus of lithic analysis.
