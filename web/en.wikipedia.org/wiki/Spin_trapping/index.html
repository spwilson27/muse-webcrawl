Spin trapping

Spin trapping is an analytical technique used in chemistry and biology to detect and identify short-lived free radicals. Because most radicals are too reactive and short-lived to observe directly by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy, a diamagnetic compound called a spin trap is added; it reacts with the transient radical to form a much more stable radical adduct whose EPR spectrum can be recorded and interpreted.

Common spin traps

The most widely used traps are nitrones and nitroso compounds. 5,5-Dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) is the classic trap for oxygen-centred radicals such as superoxide and the hydroxyl radical; α-phenyl-N-tert-butylnitrone (PBN) and the phosphorylated analogue DEPMPO are also common. The hyperfine splitting pattern of the resulting nitroxide adduct acts as a fingerprint of the radical that was trapped.

Applications and caveats

The technique was developed in the late 1960s (notably by E. G. Janzen) and is used to study oxidative stress, radiation chemistry, polymerisation and enzyme mechanisms. Interpretation requires care: adducts can also form by non-radical pathways ("inverted spin trapping"), so controls are essential before concluding that a free radical was present.
