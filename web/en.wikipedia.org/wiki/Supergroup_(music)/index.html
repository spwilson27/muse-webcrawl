Supergroup (music)

Cream, formed in 1966, is widely regarded as the first supergroup.

A supergroup is a musical group formed by artists who are already successful as solo performers or as members of other successful groups. The term originated in the late 1960s in rock music; Cream — Eric Clapton, Jack Bruce, and Ginger Baker, each previously known from other bands — is usually cited as the first example, and the 1968 album Super Session by Al Kooper, Mike Bloomfield, and Stephen Stills helped popularise the word itself.

Supergroups are often created as one-off projects rather than permanent bands, and many dissolve after a single album or tour because of scheduling conflicts, contractual complications, or clashes between prominent personalities. Others, such as Crosby, Stills, Nash & Young, Asia, and the Traveling Wilburys, achieved major commercial success.

Notable examples

  • Cream (1966–1968) — Clapton, Bruce, Baker
  • Blind Faith (1969) — Clapton, Steve Winwood, Baker, Ric Grech
  • Crosby, Stills, Nash & Young (from 1968) — members of the Byrds, Buffalo Springfield, and the Hollies
  • Emerson, Lake & Palmer (from 1970)
  • Asia (from 1981) — members of Yes, King Crimson, ELP, and the Buggles
  • Traveling Wilburys (1988–1991) — Bob Dylan, George Harrison, Jeff Lynne, Roy Orbison, Tom Petty
  • Temple of the Dog (1990) — members of Pearl Jam and Soundgarden
  • Audioslave (2001–2007) — Chris Cornell with members of Rage Against the Machine
  • Them Crooked Vultures (2009) — Dave Grohl, Josh Homme, John Paul Jones

Criticism

The term is sometimes used derisively: critics have argued that assembling famous musicians does not guarantee chemistry, and that many supergroup records are less than the sum of their parts. The label is also applied retrospectively and loosely, including to ensembles whose members became famous only afterwards — a usage most definitions exclude.
