The Strait of Dover or Dover Strait (French: Pas de Calais), historically known as the Dover Narrows, is the strait at the narrowest part of the English Channel, marking the boundary between the Channel and the North Sea and separating Great Britain from continental Europe. The shortest distance across the strait, about 20.7 miles (33.3 km), is from the South Foreland, northeast of Dover in the English county of Kent, to Cap Gris Nez, a cape near Calais in the French département of Pas-de-Calais.

Shipping traffic

The strait is one of the busiest shipping lanes in the world, used by over 400 commercial vessels daily, including the cross-Channel ferries between Dover and Calais/Dunkirk. A traffic separation scheme, overseen by the Maritime and Coastguard Agency (Channel Navigation Information Service) on the English side and CROSS Gris-Nez on the French side, manages the flow. Most ship traffic between the Atlantic Ocean and the North and Baltic Seas passes through the strait rather than the longer route around the north of Scotland.

Geology

The strait lies over a chalk ridge that once joined Britain to the continent. Most geological historians believe the strait was carved by erosion from two catastrophic megafloods from a proglacial lake in the Doggerland region, events dated to roughly 450,000 and 200,000 years ago, which breached the Weald–Artois anticline.

Crossings

The strait has been crossed by swimmers since Matthew Webb's first successful Channel swim in 1875, and by the Channel Tunnel (opened 1994), which carries Eurostar passenger trains, vehicle shuttles and freight trains between Folkestone and Coquelles.
