Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism

The hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, whose disruption causes the condition.

Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH), also known as secondary or central hypogonadism, is a condition characterised by low levels of the sex hormones (testosterone in men, oestrogen in women) together with low or inappropriately normal levels of the gonadotropins, luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). It results from inadequate secretion of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus or of gonadotropins from the pituitary gland, in contrast to primary hypogonadism, in which the gonads themselves fail and gonadotropin levels are high.

Causes

Congenital

Congenital forms include Kallmann syndrome, in which GnRH deficiency is accompanied by a reduced or absent sense of smell (anosmia), caused by failure of GnRH neurons to migrate into the hypothalamus during development. Mutations in genes such as KAL1, FGFR1, GNRHR and PROKR2 have been identified. Normosmic idiopathic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism presents with normal smell.

Acquired

Acquired causes include pituitary tumours (such as prolactinoma), infiltrative diseases, head injury, haemochromatosis, hyperprolactinaemia, severe chronic illness, malnutrition, extreme exercise, and the use of opioids or anabolic steroids, which suppress the axis.

Presentation

When present from birth or before puberty, HH causes delayed or absent puberty, small testes in males, and underdevelopment of secondary sexual characteristics. In adults it presents with infertility, reduced libido, erectile dysfunction in men, amenorrhoea in women, loss of body hair, and reduced bone density.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on demonstrating low sex steroid levels alongside low or normal LH and FSH, followed by investigation for a cause, typically including prolactin measurement and MRI of the pituitary. Treatment involves sex hormone replacement for sexual development and bone health; when fertility is desired, gonadotropin therapy or pulsatile GnRH can induce spermatogenesis or ovulation in many patients.
