Monodromy

[Image placeholder: monodromy of the logarithm]

In mathematics, monodromy is the study of how objects from mathematical analysis, algebraic topology, algebraic geometry and differential geometry behave as they "run round" a singularity. Roughly, if one follows a locally defined object — such as a branch of a multivalued function — around a closed loop encircling a singular point, one may return with a different value or branch than the one started with. Monodromy measures this failure of global single-valuedness.

Analytic continuation

The classic example is the complex logarithm. Starting near the point 1 and continuing log z analytically along a loop that winds once counter-clockwise around the origin returns a value increased by 2πi. The set of all such changes forms the monodromy group, here the infinite cyclic group generated by adding 2πi. Similarly, analytic continuation of √z around the origin changes its sign.

Covering spaces and fundamental groups

Topologically, monodromy is encoded in the action of the fundamental group of a punctured space on the fibre of a covering space. For a covering map, loops in the base lift to paths that permute the sheets; this permutation representation is the monodromy action. A covering is trivial — the continued object globally single-valued — exactly when the monodromy is trivial.

Differential equations

Solutions of a linear ordinary differential equation on a punctured complex domain acquire, upon analytic continuation around a singular point, a linear transformation described by a monodromy matrix. The Riemann–Hilbert problem asks for equations with prescribed monodromy data. Monodromy also underlies the Picard–Lefschetz theory of degenerating families of algebraic varieties, and appears in the theory of perverse sheaves.
