Haboob

A haboob (Arabic: هَبوب, romanized: habūb, lit. 'blasting/drifting') is a type of intense dust storm carried by the outflow of a thunderstorm. Haboobs occur regularly in dry land area regions throughout the world, including the Sahara, the Arabian Peninsula, and the arid southwestern United States.

Formation

When a thunderstorm collapses, its downdraft spreads outward at the surface as a cold density current. In arid terrain this gust front scoops up loose dust and sand, forming a towering wall of debris that can reach several kilometres in width and height. Visibility can fall to near zero within minutes, and winds at the leading edge may exceed 100 km/h. Haboobs typically last one to three hours.

Regions

The term comes from Sudan, where haboobs precede the summer rains south of the Sahara. They are frequent in Iraq, Kuwait, and Saudi Arabia, and in North America across Arizona, New Mexico, and west Texas, where monsoon thunderstorms generate dramatic dust walls — notably the large haboob that enveloped Phoenix, Arizona, in July 2011. In Australia similar events are sometimes called "haboobs" as well, colloquially "black rollers."

Hazards

Haboobs cause sudden whiteout driving conditions, road accidents, flight delays, respiratory irritation, and damage from wind-driven debris.
