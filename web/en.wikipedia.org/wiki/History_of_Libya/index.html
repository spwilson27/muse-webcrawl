History of Libya

The history of Libya covers the ancient Berber peoples of North Africa, Greek and Roman colonisation, Arab conquest, Ottoman and Italian rule, independence as a kingdom in 1951, the Gaddafi era (1969–2011) and the civil conflicts that followed.

Ancient Libya

Libya's coasts were settled by Phoenicians, who founded Tripoli, and by Greeks, who from the 7th century BCE built the Pentapolis of Cyrenaica, including Cyrene. Rome conquered the region, and Tripolitania and Cyrenaica became prosperous provinces; Leptis Magna was the birthplace of emperor Septimius Severus. Rock art in the Acacus Mountains records a greener prehistoric Sahara.

Arab, Ottoman and Italian rule

Arab armies conquered Libya in the 7th century CE, beginning its Islamisation and Arabisation. From 1551 it was an Ottoman regency; the Karamanli dynasty ruled semi-autonomously in the 18th and early 19th centuries, and Barbary corsairs drew US intervention in the Tripolitan War (1801–1805). Italy invaded in 1911 and, after brutal resistance led in Cyrenaica by Omar Mukhtar, unified Tripolitania and Cyrenaica as Italian Libya in 1934. The North African campaign of the Second World War was fought across its deserts.

Independence and the Kingdom

Libya became independent in 1951 under King Idris I of the Senussi order — the first state created by a UN resolution. Oil discovered in 1959 transformed its economy.

Gaddafi era

On 1 September 1969 Muammar Gaddafi led a coup deposing Idris. He proclaimed the Libyan Arab Republic, expelled foreign bases, nationalised oil, and in 1977 declared the Jamahiriya, a "state of the masses" governed, in theory, by people's congresses. Decades of confrontation with the West included the 1986 US bombing and the Lockerbie bombing of 1988.

2011 revolution and aftermath

Inspired by the Arab Spring, protests in February 2011 escalated into civil war; NATO intervened, and Gaddafi was killed in Sirte in October 2011. Rival governments and militias fought a second civil war (2014–2020), and a UN-backed Government of National Unity was formed in 2021, though political division persists.
