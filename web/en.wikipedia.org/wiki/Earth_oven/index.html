Earth oven

An earth oven, ground oven or cooking pit is one of the simplest and most ancient cooking structures. The earliest known earth ovens date to the Upper Paleolithic, and they remain in use in many cultures around the world. At its most basic, an earth oven is a pit in the ground used to trap heat and bake, smoke, or steam food.

Construction and use

A typical earth oven is made by digging a pit, lining it with stones, and building a fire in it. When the fire has heated the stones and burned down, food — often wrapped in leaves — is placed on the hot stones, sometimes with water added to produce steam, and the pit is covered with earth, vegetation, or wet sacks to seal in the heat. Cooking proceeds slowly for several hours, after which the oven is opened and the food removed.

Regional variations

  • Hāngī — the Māori earth oven of New Zealand, used for communal feasts of meat and root vegetables.
  • Umu — the Samoan and Polynesian above-ground stone oven.
  • Lovo — the Fijian earth oven.
  • Pachamanca — the Peruvian earth oven, named for the Quechua words for "earth pot", in which meats and tubers are cooked on hot stones.
  • Huatia — a related Andean earth oven built of clods of earth.
  • Clambake — the New England tradition of steaming seafood in a pit with hot stones and seaweed.
  • Kalua — Hawaiian cooking in an underground oven called an imu, famously used for whole pig.
  • Barbacoa — the Mexican pit cooking tradition from which the word "barbecue" derives.

History

Earth ovens are among the oldest cooking technologies known archaeologically; hearths and roasting pits appear at sites tens of thousands of years old. Because the method requires no manufactured vessel, it has arisen independently in many societies and remains common for large communal gatherings because an earth oven can cook very large quantities of food at once.
