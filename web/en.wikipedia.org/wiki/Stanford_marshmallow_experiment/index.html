Stanford marshmallow experiment

The Stanford marshmallow experiment was a study on delayed gratification in 1970 and 1972 led by psychologist Walter Mischel, a professor at Stanford University. In this study, a child was offered a choice between one small but immediate reward, or two small rewards if they waited for a period of time. During this time, the researcher left the room for about 15 minutes and then returned. The reward was either a marshmallow or a pretzel stick, depending on the child's preference.

Findings

In follow-up studies, Mischel and colleagues found that children who were able to wait longer for the preferred rewards tended to have better life outcomes, as measured by SAT scores, educational attainment, body mass index, and other measures. The experiment became a classic illustration of the importance of self-control and willpower in later success.

Replication and reinterpretation

A larger, more demographically diverse conceptual replication in 2018 (Watts, Duncan, and Quan) found the correlation between waiting time and later achievement to be roughly half as strong as reported, and largely explained by family background, home environment, and early cognitive ability. Other research showed that children's waiting behavior is also influenced by how reliable they judge the environment and the experimenter to be — children who had experienced a broken promise waited far less long.
