Announced1842
PresidentJohn Tyler
SubjectHawaiian Islands; U.S. opposition to European colonisation
RelationExtension of the Monroe Doctrine to the Pacific

The Tyler doctrine was a statement of United States foreign policy issued in 1842 by President John Tyler concerning the Hawaiian Islands. Building on the principles of the Monroe Doctrine, it declared that the United States would view with disapproval any attempt by a European power to colonise or take control of Hawaii, whose independence and trade the United States wished to preserve. It effectively extended the Monroe Doctrine's non-colonisation principle across the Pacific.

Background

American whalers and missionaries had been prominent in Hawaii since the 1820s, and the islands' position made them strategically important to Pacific commerce. Concerned at British and French designs — fears sharpened by France's imposition of a treaty on Hawaii in 1839 and the brief British seizure of the islands in the Paulet Affair of 1843 — Tyler in his December 1842 message to Congress stated that the United States had a special interest in Hawaii and could not consent to its becoming subject to any European power.

Consequences

The doctrine helped secure international recognition of Hawaiian independence in 1843 and shaped U.S. policy for decades, preceding the reciprocity treaty of 1875, the United States' exclusive rights at Pearl Harbor (1887), the overthrow of the Hawaiian monarchy in 1893, and annexation in 1898. Historians regard it as an early landmark of American expansion into the Pacific.
