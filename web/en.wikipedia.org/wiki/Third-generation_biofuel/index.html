Third-generation biofuel

Third-generation biofuel
FeedstockAlgae (microalgae and macroalgae)
ProductsBiodiesel, bioethanol, biobutanol, biogas, jet fuel, hydrogen
PredecessorsFirst-generation (food crops); second-generation (lignocellulosic biomass)
AdvantagesHigh yield per hectare; no competition for farmland or fresh water; CO₂ uptake

Third-generation biofuels are fuels derived from algae. Microalgae and seaweeds are cultivated — in open ponds, photobioreactors or wastewater — and converted into biodiesel, ethanol, butanol, methane, hydrogen or aviation fuel. The classification by "generation" distinguishes them from first-generation biofuels made from food crops (corn, sugarcane, soy) and second-generation biofuels made from non-food lignocellulosic biomass such as straw and wood.

Advantages

Algae grow far faster than terrestrial crops and can accumulate lipids at 20–60% of dry weight, promising many times the oil yield per hectare of oilseed crops. Cultivation need not occupy arable land or use fresh water — marine and wastewater-grown strains are common — avoiding the food-versus-fuel conflict. Growing algae consume carbon dioxide and can be coupled to flue-gas streams or nutrient removal from effluents, and the residual biomass yields fertiliser or animal feed.

Production and challenges

Fuel production involves strain selection or genetic engineering, cultivation, harvesting and dewatering — an energy-intensive step because cultures are dilute — followed by lipid extraction and transesterification to biodiesel, or fermentation and thermochemical conversion. Despite decades of research and major investment booms in the 2000s and 2010s, costs remain well above those of petroleum fuels, and many ventures pivoted to higher-value products such as nutraceuticals, cosmetics and speciality chemicals. Research continues on cheaper harvesting, co-product valorisation and integrated biorefineries.
