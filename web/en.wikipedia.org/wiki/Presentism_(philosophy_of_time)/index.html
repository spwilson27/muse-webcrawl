Presentism (philosophy of time)

Presentism is a school of philosophy of time holding that only the present exists: neither the future nor the past is real. In some versions, this view holds for time and temporal objects in general; in others, it is restricted to certain classes. Presentism contrasts with eternalism, the view that past, present and future all exist, and with the growing block theory, which holds that the past and present exist but the future does not.

Historical background

Presentist ideas appear in St. Augustine, who held that the past exists only as memory and the future only as expectation, and in later thinkers such as William James. The view was the near-universal assumption of philosophers before the advent of relativity theory in the 20th century.

Arguments and objections

Presentists appeal to ordinary experience — we only ever encounter present things — and to the special status of "now" in language and action. Critics object that relativity's relativity of simultaneity undermines a single, objective present shared by all observers, and that presentism struggles to provide truthmakers for statements about the past ("Caesar crossed the Rubicon") and to explain cross-temporal relations such as causation.

Responses

Defenders have proposed relativised presentism, presentism based on a privileged reference frame, and "ersatz" abstract representations of past times. Presentism remains a live, if minority, position in contemporary metaphysics.
