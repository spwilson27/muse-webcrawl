Functionalism (philosophy of mind)

Functionalism (philosophy of mind)
FieldPhilosophy of mind
Core claimMental states are defined by their causal/functional roles, not their physical composition
Key figuresHilary Putnam (early), Jerry Fodor, David Lewis
[Image: A flowchart of inputs, internal states and outputs representing a mental process]

Functionalism is a theory in the philosophy of mind holding that mental states are constituted by their functional role — their relations to sensory inputs, behavioural outputs and other mental states — rather than by their internal physical makeup. Pain, on this view, is whatever state is typically caused by tissue damage and causes wincing, avoidance and the desire for relief, whether realised in neurons, silicon, or something else: mental kinds are multiply realisable, as software runs on different hardware.

Influence and objections

Developed by Hilary Putnam in the 1960s under the influence of Turing machines and championed by Jerry Fodor, functionalism became the working philosophy of cognitive science and much AI research. Objections centre on qualia: inverted-spectrum and absent-qualia (Block's 'China brain') scenarios allege functional duplicates could lack experience entirely — pressing the same hard problem as Consciousness itself.
