Hedonic treadmill

The hedonic treadmill, also known as hedonic adaptation, is the observed tendency of humans to quickly return to a relatively stable level of happiness despite major positive or negative events or life changes.

According to this theory, as a person makes more money, expectations and desires rise in tandem, which results in no permanent gain in happiness. The metaphor of a treadmill captures the idea that one must keep running merely to stay in the same place.

History

The concept was first articulated by Philip Brickman and Donald T. Campbell in their 1971 essay "Hedonic Relativism and Planning the Good Society". A famous 1978 study by Brickman, Dan Coates and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman compared lottery winners and accident victims with spinal cord injuries, finding that the lottery winners were not significantly happier than controls, while accident victims, though less happy, still rated themselves above the midpoint of the happiness scale.

Research

Later research has refined the model. Ed Diener and colleagues proposed revisions to the hedonic treadmill theory in 2006, noting that people's happiness set points are not hedonically neutral, that individuals differ in their set points, and that adaptation rates vary between people and between types of events. Adaptation appears to be slower or incomplete for some conditions, such as unemployment and the loss of a spouse, than for changes in income or possessions.

Applications

The concept is influential in happiness economics and positive psychology, and is frequently invoked in discussions of consumerism, materialism and the Easterlin paradox, which observes that rising national income does not necessarily raise average reported happiness over long periods.
