Estates General (France)

In France under the Ancien Régime, the Estates General (French: États généraux) was a legislative and consultative assembly of the different classes (or estates) of French subjects. It had a separate assembly for each of the three estates—the clergy (First Estate), the nobility (Second Estate) and the commoners (Third Estate)—which were called and dismissed by the king. It had no true legislative power of its own, unlike the English Parliament, but served as an advisory and consultative body.

History

The Estates General was first summoned by Philip IV in 1302, in his conflict with Pope Boniface VIII. It met intermittently during the Hundred Years' War and the French Wars of Religion, but as royal absolutism developed it fell into disuse: after 1614 it was not summoned again for 175 years.

The Estates General of 1789

Faced with financial crisis, Louis XVI summoned the Estates General to convene at Versailles in May 1789. Dispute over whether voting should be by estate (giving each estate one vote) or by head paralysed the assembly. On 17 June 1789 the Third Estate, joined by some clergy, declared itself the National Assembly; three days later its members swore the Tennis Court Oath not to disperse until France had a constitution. The events marked the beginning of the French Revolution, and the Estates General as an institution ceased to exist.

Composition

For 1789 the First Estate sent some 300 deputies, the Second Estate about 300, and the Third Estate about 600, the latter having been granted double representation but not double voting power—an ambiguity that fuelled the constitutional crisis. Deputies carried with them cahiers de doléances, lists of grievances compiled by their constituents.
