Pre-eclampsia

Pre-eclampsia is a multi-system disorder of pregnancy characterised by new-onset high blood pressure after 20 weeks of gestation, together with protein in the urine or other signs of organ dysfunction. It affects roughly 2–8 percent of pregnancies worldwide and is a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality. If untreated it can progress to eclampsia — seizures — or to HELLP syndrome, placental abruption, and fetal growth restriction. Delivery of the baby and placenta is the only definitive cure.

Signs and symptoms

Many women have no symptoms at first and the condition is detected at routine antenatal checks. Signs include persistent hypertension, proteinuria, swelling of the face and hands, severe headache, visual disturbances, upper abdominal pain, and reduced fetal movement. Severe features include blood pressure ≥ 160/110 mmHg, low platelets, impaired liver or kidney function, and pulmonary oedema.

Causes and pathophysiology

The exact cause is incompletely understood, but the condition is thought to begin with abnormal development of the placenta: incomplete remodelling of the uterine spiral arteries leads to placental underperfusion, and the stressed placenta releases factors — notably the anti-angiogenic protein sFlt-1, which neutralises VEGF and PlGF — that cause widespread maternal endothelial dysfunction. Imbalance between sFlt-1 and PlGF in maternal blood is used clinically to help rule the disease in or out.

Prevention and management

Low-dose aspirin started in the first trimester reduces risk in high-risk women, and calcium supplementation helps where dietary intake is low. Management depends on severity and gestational age: monitoring and antihypertensive drugs (such as labetalol, nifedipine, or methyldopa) for mild disease near term; magnesium sulfate to prevent seizures in severe disease; and planned delivery, generally by 37 weeks, or sooner if mother or fetus deteriorates. Women who have had pre-eclampsia carry increased long-term cardiovascular risk.
