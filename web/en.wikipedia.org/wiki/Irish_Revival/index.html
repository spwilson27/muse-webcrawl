Irish Revival

Irish Literary Revival

Periodc. 1880s – 1920s
LocationIreland, principally Dublin
Key figuresW. B. Yeats, Lady Gregory, J. M. Synge, Douglas Hyde, George Russell (Æ)

The Irish Revival (also called the Irish Literary Revival or Celtic Revival) was a flowering of Irish literary, theatrical and cultural talent in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Allied to the Gaelic revival of the Irish language and to a broader cultural nationalism, it sought to create a distinctively Irish literature in English (and in Irish), drawing on Ireland's mythology, folklore and peasant life.

Origins

The movement had roots in nineteenth-century antiquarian scholarship and translations of early Irish literature, and in the cultural nationalism of Young Ireland. Douglas Hyde's lecture "The Necessity for De-Anglicising Ireland" (1892) and his founding of the Gaelic League (Conradh na Gaeilge) in 1893 promoted the revival of the Irish language, while W. B. Yeats turned to Irish myth and legend in collections such as The Wanderings of Oisin (1889) and The Celtic Twilight (1893).

The Abbey Theatre

Yeats, Lady Augusta Gregory and Edward Martyn founded the Irish Literary Theatre in 1899, which evolved into the Irish National Theatre Society and, in 1904, the Abbey Theatre in Dublin, funded by Annie Horniman. The Abbey staged plays rooted in Irish life and speech, most famously the works of J. M. Synge, whose The Playboy of the Western World (1907) provoked riots, and later the plays of Seán O'Casey. Synge's Hiberno-English dramatic language, fashioned from the speech of Wicklow and the Aran Islands, became a hallmark of the revival.

Other figures and institutions

George William Russell ("Æ") combined poetry, painting and agricultural co-operative organising through the Irish Agricultural Organisation Society. The Dun Emer Press (later the Cuala Press), run by Yeats's sisters, published finely printed revival texts. Writers associated with the revival include James Stephens, Lord Dunsany, Padraic Colum and Oliver St. John Gogarty, while Patrick Pearse and others carried the revival into Irish-language literature and education.

Legacy

The revival transformed Ireland's cultural self-image in the decades before independence and helped define the identity of the new Irish state. It also generated one of the richest bodies of English-language writing of the modernist era: Yeats won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1923, the year after the Irish Free State was established, which he described as recognition of a young nation's literature. James Joyce, though he stood apart from and satirised the revival, emerged from the same Dublin milieu.
