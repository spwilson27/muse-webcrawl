Brake fade

Brake fade
Cause: Overheating of braking components
Types: Pad fade, fluid fade, green fade
Repeated heavy braking can overheat pads and rotors

Brake fade is the reduction in stopping power that can occur after repeated or sustained application of the brakes of a vehicle, especially in high-load or high-speed conditions. It results from heat build-up in the friction surfaces and hydraulic components, and is a particular hazard for heavy vehicles descending long gradients and for racing cars.

Types

In pad fade, the friction material of the brake pads overheats and the resins within it outgas, forming a lubricating layer of gas between pad and disc so that pedal pressure produces little friction. In fluid fade, the brake fluid boils, producing compressible vapour bubbles that make the pedal feel spongy and reduce hydraulic pressure. Green fade occurs early in the life of new pads before they have been properly bedded in. A related phenomenon in drum brakes is the expansion of the hot drum away from the shoes, increasing pedal travel.

Prevention

Fade is mitigated by ventilated and drilled or slotted discs, high-temperature pad compounds and brake fluids with high boiling points, cooling ducts, and driving technique — using engine braking on descents rather than riding the brakes. Runaway truck ramps on mountain roads exist as a last resort for vehicles that have lost braking entirely.
