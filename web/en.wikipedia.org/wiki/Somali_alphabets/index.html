A number of writing systems have been used to transcribe the Somali language. Of these, the Somali Latin alphabet is the most widely used and has been the official script in Somalia since 1972. Before that, Somali was written in an adapted Arabic script (Wadaad writing) and in several indigenous alphabets invented in the early 20th century, principally the Osmanya, Borama, and Kaddare scripts.

Wadaad writing (Arabic script)

For centuries, Somali religious scholars — wadaad — wrote Somali using an adaptation of the Arabic alphabet, alongside Arabic itself, for religious poetry, contracts, and correspondence. Various conventions were employed to represent Somali sounds not found in Arabic, and the lack of standardisation limited its use for general literacy.

Osmanya script

The Osmanya script (Somali: Far Soomaali) was invented between 1920 and 1922 by Osman Yusuf Kenadid, a Somali poet and member of the royal family of the Sultanate of Hobyo. It is a phonetically precise alphabet with distinct vowel letters, designed specifically for Somali. Despite a campaign for its adoption, it competed with Arabic and Latin and lost ground after independence. Osmanya was added to the Unicode Standard in version 4.0 (2003).

Borama and Kaddare scripts

The Borama script, also called the Gadabuursi script, was devised around 1933 by Sheikh Abdurahman Sheikh Nuur for his clan's dialect in the Borama region. The Kaddare script was invented in 1952 by Sheikh Hussein Sheikh Ahmed Kaddare. Both were used locally but never achieved national adoption.

Latin alphabet and standardisation

After independence in 1960, the Somali government commissioned studies on the script question, but political sensitivity — each option had religious and clan associations — delayed a decision for a decade. In January 1972, the Supreme Revolutionary Council under Siad Barre adopted the Latin-based orthography developed by linguist Shire Jama Ahmed, which represents Somali's long vowels by doubling and uses the letters c and x for the pharyngeal fricatives /ʕ/ and /ħ/. The choice was followed by a mass literacy campaign, most famously the rural literacy drive of 1974–75, which dramatically raised reported literacy.

Contemporary use

The Latin alphabet is now universal in Somali government, education, and media in Somalia, Somaliland, and the diaspora. Osmanya retains cultural and ceremonial significance and is occasionally revived in scholarship and signage, particularly in Puntland, the Kenadid family's home region.
