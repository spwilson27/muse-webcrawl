Orbitofrontal cortex

Orbitofrontal cortex

Part of: Frontal lobe, prefrontal cortex

System: Central nervous system

Artery: Anterior cerebral artery

Identifiers: Latin: cortex orbitofrontalis

The orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) is a region of the prefrontal cortex in the frontal lobes of the brain which is involved in the cognitive processing of decision-making. It is so called because of its position immediately above the orbits (eye sockets) in which the eyes are located. In humans the OFC corresponds roughly to Brodmann areas 10, 11 and 47.

The orbitofrontal cortex is among the least understood regions of the human brain, but it is thought to be critically involved in representing the reward value of stimuli, updating those values as contingencies change, and guiding behaviour accordingly. It receives dense projections from sensory association areas, the amygdala, and the mediodorsal nucleus of the thalamus, and sends projections to the striatum and other subcortical structures.

Structure

The OFC lies on the ventral (orbital) surface of the frontal lobe. It is divided into medial and lateral parts, which differ in their connectivity and, to a degree, their function. The medial OFC is more closely connected with the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and structures involved in visceromotor control, while the lateral OFC connects more strongly with the insular cortex and lateral prefrontal areas.

The region receives input from all sensory modalities: gustatory and olfactory information arrives via the primary taste and smell cortices, visual information via the inferior temporal cortex, and somatosensory input via the insula and operculum. This convergence makes the OFC one of the principal sites at which the sensory properties and reward value of stimuli are integrated.

Function

Research in humans, monkeys and rodents has implicated the OFC in:

  • Representing the expected value of rewards and punishments
  • Reversal learning — changing behaviour when reward contingencies reverse
  • Impulse control and the suppression of inappropriate responses
  • Emotion regulation and social behaviour
  • Decision-making under uncertainty

Damage to the orbitofrontal cortex can produce dramatic changes in personality and conduct. Patients may become impulsive, socially inappropriate, and unable to use feedback to guide their choices, classically illustrated by the 19th-century case of Phineas Gage, whose frontal injury following a railway accident transformed his temperament. OFC dysfunction has also been linked to obsessive–compulsive disorder, addiction, and certain features of depression.

Clinical significance

Because of its role in reward processing and behavioural inhibition, the orbitofrontal cortex is a focus of research into substance abuse, pathological gambling, and compulsive behaviour. Deep brain stimulation and neurosurgical interventions for severe psychiatric illness have occasionally targeted circuits passing through or near the OFC.
