Thymol

Thymol
IUPAC name: 2-isopropyl-5-methylphenol
Formula: C10H14O
Molar mass: 150.22 g/mol
Appearance: Colourless crystalline solid
Source: Oil of thyme (Thymus vulgaris)
CAS number: 89-83-8

Thymol (also known as 2-isopropyl-5-methylphenol) is a natural monoterpenoid phenol derivative of p-cymene. It is the principal active component of oil of thyme, extracted from Thymus vulgaris (common thyme) and various other plants. Thymol forms colourless crystals with a strong, pleasant aromatic odour and has powerful antiseptic and antifungal properties.

Thymol is a monoterpene phenol and the main constituent of thyme oil.

History

Thyme preparations were used medicinally in ancient Egypt and Greece, but thymol itself was first isolated by the German chemist Caspar Neumann in 1719, and was purified and characterised in the mid-19th century. Its antiseptic power was recognised in the era of Joseph Lister, and thymol became widely used in medicine before being partly displaced by cheaper synthetic agents.

Uses

Thymol is an active ingredient in many mouthwashes — most famously Listerine, where it is combined with eucalyptol, menthol, and methyl salicylate — as well as in toothpastes, cough remedies, and topical antifungal preparations. It is used against the parasitic Varroa mite in beekeeping, as a preservative and disinfectant, in perfumery and flavouring, and in the laboratory as a preservative for specimens. Thymol blue, a pH indicator, is derived from it.

Biological activity

Thymol disrupts microbial cell membranes, giving it broad antibacterial and antifungal activity. It is only slightly soluble in water but dissolves readily in alcohol and oils. In modest doses it is regarded as safe for use in food and oral products, although concentrated thymol is irritating and toxic.
