Roman amphitheatre

Roman amphitheatres are large, circular or oval open-air venues with raised seating, built by the ancient Romans. They were used for events such as gladiatorial contests, wild beast hunts (venationes), mock sea battles, executions and public spectacles. Around 230 amphitheatres are known across the Roman Empire.

Architecture

The amphitheatre consists of an elliptical arena surrounded by tiers of seating (the cavea) arranged by social rank, supported by vaulted substructures that also housed corridors, stairs and service rooms. Beneath the arena floor of the largest examples lay the hypogeum, a network of tunnels and cages for animals and performers. Awnings called vela could shade the audience.

History

Early amphitheatres were built of wood; the oldest surviving stone example is at Pompeii, dating to about 70 BC. The largest and most famous is the Colosseum (Flavian Amphitheatre) in Rome, completed in AD 80, which could hold an estimated 50,000–80,000 spectators. Others survive at Verona, Arles, Nîmes, Pula and El Jem. Gladiatorial games declined in the 5th century, and many amphitheatres were quarried for stone or converted to other uses.
