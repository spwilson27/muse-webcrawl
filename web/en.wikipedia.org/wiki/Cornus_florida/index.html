Cornus florida, the flowering dogwood, is a species of flowering tree in the family Cornaceae native to eastern North America and northern Mexico. An endemic population once known from Nuevo León in northeastern Mexico has not been found alive since 1994 and is now considered extinct. The tree is widely grown as an ornamental for its showy spring "flowers" — actually clusters of small flowers surrounded by four large, petal-like bracts, usually white or pink.

Description

Flowering dogwood is a small deciduous tree growing to about 10 m (33 ft) tall, often wider than tall when mature. The leaves are opposite, simple and ovate, turning red-purple in autumn. The fruit is a cluster of shiny red drupes, roughly 1 cm long, which ripen in late summer and are eaten by birds but are poisonous to humans. The bark of mature trees breaks into small square plates.

Cultivation and significance

Many cultivars have been selected, including 'Cherokee Chief' (deep pink bracts) and 'Cloud Nine'. The species is the state tree of Virginia and Missouri and the state flower of Virginia and North Carolina. Dogwood anthracnose, a fungal disease caused by Discula destructiva, has seriously affected wild populations since the 1980s, particularly in cool, moist upland sites.

Uses

The very hard, dense wood was historically used for shuttles, tool handles, golf club heads and mallet heads. Native Americans used the bark and roots in traditional remedies, and the bright red roots produce a scarlet dye.
