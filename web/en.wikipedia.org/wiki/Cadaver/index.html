Cadaver

A cadaver or corpse is a dead human body. It is used by medical students, physicians and other scientists to study anatomy, identify disease sites, and determine causes of death. Cadavers are also used in studies to ensure surgical procedures are performed correctly, to test the effects of automobile crashes and other impacts (in which context some cadavers are fitted with sensors), and in forensic science and archaeology. The word derives from the Latin cadere ("to fall").

History

Systematic human dissection for teaching began in the universities of medieval Italy (notably Bologna) and was transformed by Andreas Vesalius's De humani corporis fabrica (1543). Demand for bodies for anatomy schools historically outstripped legal supply, leading to grave-robbing and, in the Burke and Hare murders of 1828, to killing for the anatomy trade — scandals that prompted anatomy acts regulating body donation. Today, most medical-school cadavers come from voluntary whole-body donation programmes.

Decay and preservation

After death the body passes through algor, livor and rigor mortis, then decomposition (putrefaction and autolysis). Embalming with formaldehyde-based fluids slows decay enough for semester-long dissection courses; plastination replaces tissue fluids with polymers for durable teaching specimens. Forensic entomology and "body farm" research (the first established by William Bass at the University of Tennessee in 1981) study decay to estimate time since death.
