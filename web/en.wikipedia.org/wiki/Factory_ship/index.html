A factory ship, also known as a fish processing vessel, is a large ocean-going vessel with extensive on-board facilities for processing and freezing caught fish or whales. Modern factory ships are essentially floating factories, capable of catching, processing, freezing and storing fish for weeks or months at sea without returning to port.

Background

The concept derives from early whaling ships. The first factory ships were converted whale oil-production vessels of the 19th and early 20th centuries, which rendered whale carcasses at sea. The introduction of stern slipways in the 1920s allowed entire whale carcasses to be hauled aboard for processing, greatly expanding pelagic whaling.

Factory trawlers

Modern factory trawlers combine fishing and processing: nets hauled up a stern ramp deliver fish directly to below-deck factories where they are gutted, filleted, skinned, frozen and packaged by machinery and large crews. The first purpose-built stern factory trawler was the British Fairtry of 1954, whose design was widely copied, notably by the Soviet Union, Spain, Japan and Poland, which built large distant-water fleets.

Whaling factory ships

Whale factory ships carried catcher boats and processed whales entirely at sea, producing oil, meat and bone meal. The International Whaling Commission's moratorium on commercial whaling (1986) ended most such operations; Japan's Nisshin Maru remained one of the last whaling factory ships in service into the 21st century.

Environmental concerns

Factory ships are controversial because of their enormous catching capacity, bycatch of non-target species, and contribution to overfishing of stocks such as Atlantic cod and Antarctic krill. Several coastal states have restricted or banned them in their waters. Greenpeace and other groups campaign against "super trawlers".
