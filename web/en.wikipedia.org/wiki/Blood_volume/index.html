Blood volume is the volume of blood — red blood cells and plasma — in the circulatory system of an individual. A typical adult human has a blood volume of roughly five litres. Blood volume is regulated by the kidneys and is a critical variable in cardiovascular physiology and in the management of shock, heart failure and fluid therapy.

Norms

Blood volume correlates with body mass: approximately 70 mL per kilogram in adult men, 65 mL/kg in adult women, about 80 mL/kg in children and 85–90 mL/kg in neonates. Of the total, plasma accounts for about 55% and cellular elements — overwhelmingly red blood cells — for about 45%, a share measured as the haematocrit.

Regulation

The body defends blood volume through thirst, antidiuretic hormone (ADH), the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and natriuretic peptides. Baroreceptors and atrial stretch receptors detect changes in filling pressure, adjusting renal sodium and water excretion. Loss of more than about 15–20% of blood volume produces symptoms of hypovolaemic shock; losses above 40% are life-threatening without transfusion.

Measurement

Blood volume can be measured by indicator dilution: a known quantity of tracer — historically Evans blue dye or radioiodinated albumin — is injected, and its dilution after mixing gives plasma volume, from which total volume is calculated using the haematocrit. Carbon-monoxide rebreathing methods estimate haemoglobin mass and are used in sports science, where endurance training and altitude exposure expand blood volume and plasma volume.
