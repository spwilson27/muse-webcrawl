Berthold Konrad Hermann Albert Speer (19 March 1905 – 1 September 1981) was a German architect who served as Reich Minister of Armaments and War Production in Nazi Germany during most of World War II. A close friend and confidant of Adolf Hitler, he designed many of the regime's monumental buildings and party rally grounds, and from 1942 directed the wartime armaments economy, relying extensively on forced and concentration-camp labor. At the Nuremberg trials he was convicted of war crimes and crimes against humanity and sentenced to twenty years in prison.

Architect to Hitler

Speer joined the Nazi Party in 1931. His designs for the Nuremberg rally grounds — including the Zeppelinfeld with its "cathedral of light" of searchlights — and for the new Reich Chancellery in Berlin won Hitler's favor, and in 1937 he was appointed General Building Inspector for the Reich Capital, charged with the planned transformation of Berlin into "Welthauptstadt Germania". He was responsible for the eviction of Berlin's Jews from housing requisitioned under his rebuilding program.

Minister of Armaments

After Minister Fritz Todt died in a plane crash in February 1942, Hitler appointed Speer his successor. Speer reorganized armaments production, and German output of tanks, aircraft, and ammunition rose sharply despite Allied bombing — the so-called "armaments miracle". The increase depended heavily on millions of foreign forced laborers deported by Fritz Sauckel and on concentration-camp prisoners, for whose conditions Speer shared responsibility. In the war's final weeks he deliberately frustrated Hitler's "Nero Decree" scorched-earth order, an act he later emphasized in his defense.

Trial, imprisonment, and memoirs

At Nuremberg, Speer accepted collective responsibility for the regime's crimes while denying direct knowledge of the Holocaust; he narrowly escaped execution and served his full sentence at Spandau Prison, released in 1966. His memoirs, Inside the Third Reich (1969), and the Spandau Diaries became bestsellers and shaped the image of the "good Nazi" — a portrayal later research substantially undermined, documenting his knowledge of and involvement in persecution and extermination programs. He died in London in 1981.
