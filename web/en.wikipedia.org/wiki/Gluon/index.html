Gluon

[Image: Diagram of a three-jet event in an electron–positron collider, showing a radiated gluon jet]
Three-jet event: evidence for the gluon.

Gluons are elementary particles that act as the exchange particles (or gauge bosons) for the strong force between quarks. They are analogous to the exchange of photons in the electromagnetic force between two charged particles — with the crucial difference that gluons themselves carry colour charge and therefore interact with each other.

There are eight independent types of gluon, corresponding to the eight generators of the SU(3) colour symmetry of Quantum chromodynamics. Because gluons carry colour, the force between quarks does not fall off with distance, leading to colour confinement: gluons and quarks occur only within colour-neutral hadrons, and a stretched 'flux tube' of gluon field between separating quarks eventually snaps by creating new quark–antiquark pairs.

History and evidence

The gluon was implied by the construction of QCD in 1972–73 and discovered experimentally at the PETRA collider at DESY in 1979, where electron–positron annihilations sometimes produced three jets of hadrons — interpreted as a quark, an antiquark and a radiated hard gluon. The strong coupling's running (asymptotic freedom) is measured largely through gluon-initiated processes.

Role in hadron structure

Gluons carry roughly half of a fast-moving proton's momentum, and at high resolution the proton is a dense system of gluons and sea quarks (a regime explored by the future Electron–Ion Collider). Pure-glue bound states — glueballs — are predicted by QCD and lattice calculations but have not been unambiguously identified.
