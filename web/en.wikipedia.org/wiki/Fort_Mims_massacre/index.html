Fort Mims massacre

The Fort Mims massacre took place on August 30, 1813, during the Creek War, when a force of Creek warriors known as the Red Sticks attacked Fort Mims, a hastily built stockade near present-day Tensaw in Baldwin County, Alabama. Several hundred settlers, militia, and their families were killed, making it one of the deadliest attacks on settlers in United States history.

Background

The Creek Nation was divided by a civil war between the Red Stick faction, inspired in part by Tecumseh's pan-Indian movement and supplied through Spanish Florida, and Creeks who accommodated American expansion. After American militia ambushed a Red Stick supply party at the Battle of Burnt Corn in July 1813, frightened settlers crowded into stockades such as Samuel Mims's fortified plantation.

The attack

At midday on August 30, roughly 750–1,000 Red Stick warriors under William Weatherford (Red Eagle) rushed the fort through a gate left open and blocked from closing by sand. The garrison commander, Major Daniel Beasley, was killed early. After hours of fighting, the attackers set the buildings ablaze; most of the roughly 500 people inside were killed, though Weatherford reportedly tried to restrain the killing once the fighting ended. Estimates of the dead range from about 250 to 500.

Aftermath

News of the massacre galvanized the United States' intervention in the Creek War. Tennessee militia under Andrew Jackson invaded Creek country, culminating in the Battle of Horseshoe Bend in March 1814 and the Treaty of Fort Jackson, which stripped the Creek Nation of some 23 million acres.
