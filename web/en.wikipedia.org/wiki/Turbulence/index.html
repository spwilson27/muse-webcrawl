Turbulence

In fluid dynamics, turbulence is chaotic, irregular fluid motion characterised by vortices, eddies and rapid variations of pressure and velocity, in contrast to smooth laminar flow. It occurs at high Reynolds numbers, when inertial forces dominate viscous forces.

Description

Richardson and Kolmogorov described an energy cascade: energy injected at large scales passes through eddies of decreasing size until dissipated as heat at the Kolmogorov microscales, giving the famous −5/3 spectrum. Turbulence greatly enhances mixing, drag and heat transfer, which is both an engineering nuisance (fuel burn, pipeline losses) and a benefit (combustion, chemical reactors).

Theory and modelling

The Navier–Stokes equations govern turbulent flow, but direct numerical simulation of high-Reynolds-number flows is computationally prohibitive; engineers rely on Reynolds-averaged (RANS) models, large-eddy simulation and empirical correlations. Whether smooth solutions to Navier–Stokes always exist is a Millennium Prize Problem. Andrey Kolmogorov's 1941 theory, Landau's critiques, and the discovery of coherent structures remain central. Horace Lamb reportedly quipped that on reaching heaven he hoped for enlightenment on quantum electrodynamics and turbulence — and was optimistic about the first.
