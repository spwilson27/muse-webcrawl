Epic theatre (German: episches Theater) is a theatrical movement arising in the early to mid-20th century, closely identified with the German playwright Bertolt Brecht. It set out to make audiences think critically about social and political conditions rather than lose themselves in emotional identification with characters.

Techniques

Brecht's Verfremdungseffekt ("estrangement" or alienation effect) used devices that remind spectators they are watching a performance: placards and projections announcing scenes, visible lighting equipment, songs that interrupt and comment on the action, actors stepping out of character, and episodic plots with montage-like structure.

Aims

Rooted in Marxist analysis, epic theatre presents events as historical and changeable rather than inevitable, inviting the audience to judge characters' choices and imagine alternatives. Brecht contrasted it with "dramatic" (Aristotelian) theatre built on empathy and catharsis.

Influence

The approach shaped political theatre worldwide — from Augusto Boal's Theatre of the Oppressed to documentary and devised theatre — and influenced film-makers who adopted its anti-illusionist techniques.
