Bounded rationality

Bounded rationality
FieldEconomics, cognitive psychology, decision theory
Proposed byHerbert A. Simon (1950s)
Key conceptSatisficing — choosing an option that is "good enough"
Related fieldsBehavioural economics, ecological rationality
Boundedly rational agents search through options only until they find one that meets an aspiration level, rather than optimising over all possibilities.

Bounded rationality is the idea that rationality of individuals is limited by the information they have, the cognitive limitations of their minds, and the finite amount of time available to make decisions. It was proposed by Herbert A. Simon, a scholar whose work spans the fields of economics, psychology, and computer science, as an alternative basis for the mathematical modelling of decision-making used in economics and related disciplines. It complements the concept of rationality as optimisation, which assumes agents can exhaustively compare all available alternatives and select the best one.

Because their cognitive resources are limited, decision-makers instead use satisficing: they set an aspiration level and choose the first alternative that meets it. Simon argued that models of human behaviour should incorporate these bounds, writing that "bounded rationality" describes rationality that takes into account the cognitive limitations of the decision-maker — limitations of both knowledge and computational capacity.

Origins

Simon introduced the concept in his book Administrative Behavior (1947) and developed it in papers of the 1950s, including "A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice" (1955) and Models of Man (1957). He contrasted the "economic man" of classical theory — an omniscient maximiser — with actual administrators embedded in organisations, who operate with incomplete information and limited computation. He likened rationality to a pair of scissors in which one blade is the structure of the task environment and the other the computational capabilities of the actor. Simon received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1978 in part for this research programme.

Satisficing and heuristics

Satisficing, a portmanteau of "satisfy" and "suffice," is the decision strategy at the core of bounded rationality. Instead of calculating utilities over every possible course of action, an agent searches sequentially and stops at the first satisfactory option; if search is too costly or no option meets the aspiration level, the aspiration level itself is adjusted downward (or upward if satisfactory options are abundant). Simon also argued that decision-makers rely on heuristics — rules of thumb — that exploit the structure of the environment and allow fast, frugal, and frequently accurate judgments.

"The capacity of the human mind for formulating and solving complex problems is very small compared with the size of the problems whose solution is required for objectively rational behavior in the real world." — Herbert A. Simon, Models of Man (1957)

Extensions and related research

Bounded rationality became the founding idea of behavioural economics. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's heuristics-and-biases programme (from the 1970s) documented systematic shortcuts — availability, representativeness, anchoring — and their associated errors. Gerd Gigerenzer and the ABC Research Group developed the complementary framework of "ecological rationality," studying when fast-and-frugal heuristics match or beat optimisation because they are adapted to the structure of real environments. The concept also informs research on organisational decision-making, artificial intelligence (where bounded-optimal agents must trade off computation against decision quality), and public policy "nudging."

Criticism

Critics have noted that bounded rationality is more a framework than a single precise theory, and that its predictions depend on assumptions about search costs and aspiration levels that are hard to test. Defenders of optimisation models argue that market-level outcomes can approximate rational predictions even when individuals deviate, a position associated with Milton Friedman. Others object that cataloguing biases explains errors better than it explains the generally competent performance of human judgment in natural environments — a debate central to contemporary judgment and decision-making research.
