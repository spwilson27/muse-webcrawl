Lactobacillus

Lactobacillus is a genus of Gram-positive, rod-shaped, facultatively anaerobic bacteria that convert sugars to lactic acid. They are a major part of the lactic acid bacteria group and are found in decaying plant material, dairy products, and the gastrointestinal and genital tracts of humans and other animals. In 2020 the sprawling genus was split into 25 genera based on genomic analysis, so many familiar "lactobacilli" now carry new names.

DomainBacteria
PhylumBacillota (Firmicutes)
FamilyLactobacillaceae
TraitsGram-positive rods; lactic acid fermentation

Uses

Lactobacilli are essential starter cultures in the production of yogurt, cheese, sauerkraut, kimchi, pickles, sourdough bread, and fermented sausages, as well as wine and beer (where they can also be spoilage organisms). Species such as Lactobacillus acidophilus and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus (GG) are widely sold as probiotics. Their lactic acid suppresses pathogens by acidifying the environment; in the healthy human vagina, lactobacilli dominate the microbiota and help protect against infection.

Taxonomy

Long a taxonomic "wastebasket," the genus was comprehensively reorganized in 2020 by Zheng and colleagues into Lactobacillus sensu stricto and 24 new or revived genera, including Lacticaseibacillus, Lactiplantibacillus, Limosilactobacillus, and Ligilactobacillus. The reclassification reflected whole-genome phylogeny and ecology, though the old names persist in commerce and medicine.

Health

Lactobacilli are generally regarded as safe, though rare cases of bacteremia occur in severely immunocompromised patients. Some species play roles in dental caries and in the production of biogenic amines in fermented foods.
