José Yves Limantour Marquet (Spanish: [xoˈse ˈiβes limanˈtuɾ]; December 26, 1854 – August 26, 1935) was a Mexican economist and politician who served as Secretary of Finance of Mexico from 1893 until the fall of the regime of Porfirio Díaz in 1911. He was the leading figure of the Científicos, the technocratic group that dominated policy in the late Porfiriato.

Early life

Limantour was born in Mexico City to a French immigrant father, Joseph Yves Limantour, a sea captain and entrepreneur who had made a fortune in Baja California, and a Mexican mother. He studied law at the National School of Jurisprudence and travelled in Europe and the United States studying economics and public administration.

Secretary of Finance

Appointed to the Treasury in 1893, Limantour achieved the first federal budget surplus in independent Mexico's history in 1894. He renegotiated the foreign debt on favourable terms, reformed the banking system with the General Law of Credit Institutions (1897), reorganised the tax system, and in 1905 led Mexico's adoption of the gold standard. Under his administration, foreign investment and railway construction expanded rapidly.

Seen as Díaz's likely successor, Limantour negotiated with the revolutionary leader Francisco I. Madero in 1911, but the talks failed and Díaz resigned. Limantour went into exile in Paris, where he died in 1935.
