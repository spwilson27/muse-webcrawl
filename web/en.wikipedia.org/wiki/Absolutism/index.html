Absolutism

Absolutism or the Age of Absolutism (c. 1610 – c. 1789) is a historiographical term used to describe a form of monarchical power that is unrestrained by any superior authority, such as a parliament, and that rests on the doctrine of the divine right of kings. Absolute monarchs sought to centralise state power, standing armies and bureaucracy in their own hands.

Characteristics

Historians traditionally associate absolutism with rulers such as Louis XIV of France (r. 1643–1715), whose court at Versailles became the model of centralised royal power, and with Peter the Great of Russia and the Habsburg and Prussian monarchies. Absolutist states characteristically developed professional standing armies, royal taxation independent of representative assemblies, and the subordination of the nobility to court and administration.

Historiographical debate

Since the late twentieth century, historians have questioned how "absolute" these monarchs really were. In practice, early modern rulers depended on negotiation with local elites, were constrained by custom, finance and poor communications, and often lacked the means to enforce their will in the provinces. Some scholars therefore prefer to describe the period in terms of state-building and "composite monarchies" rather than true absolutism.

Enlightened absolutism

In the eighteenth century, rulers such as Frederick the Great of Prussia, Catherine the Great of Russia and Joseph II of Austria practised what is called enlightened absolutism, attempting rationalising reforms in law, administration and religion while retaining undivided royal authority.
