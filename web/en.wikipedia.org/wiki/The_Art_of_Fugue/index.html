The Art of Fugue


The subject of The Art of Fugue, BWV 1080

The Art of Fugue (German: Die Kunst der Fuge), BWV 1080, is an incomplete musical work by Johann Sebastian Bach, composed in the last years of his life (the 1740s) and published posthumously in 1751. The work consists of fourteen fugues and four canons, all based on a single musical subject in D minor, arranged in order of increasing complexity — a monumental demonstration of contrapuntal technique.

Structure

Bach calls the movements "Contrapunctus" rather than fugue. The cycle progresses from simple fugues through double and triple fugues to mirror fugues that can be played upside down. The canons explore imitation at various intervals. The final fugue, Contrapunctus XIV, is unfinished: it breaks off shortly after Bach introduces a third subject spelling his own name, B–A–C–H (in German notation, B♭–A–C–B♮). A note by his son Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach states that the composer died while working on this fugue.

Instrumentation debate

Bach wrote the work in open score, with each voice on its own staff, and did not specify instruments. Scholars have long debated whether it was intended for keyboard — organ or harpsichord — or as abstract "eye music" for study. It has since been performed and recorded by string quartets, orchestras, brass ensembles and solo keyboard players alike.

Significance

Along with the Musical Offering, The Art of Fugue is regarded as the culmination of Bach's contrapuntal art and one of the supreme achievements of Western music. Numerous composers and scholars, from Mozart to modern analysts, have studied it; attempts to complete the final fugue continue to the present day.
