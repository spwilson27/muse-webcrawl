The Kingdom of Cochin (Malayalam: കൊച്ചി), also known as Perumpadappu Swarupam, was a Hindu kingdom on the Malabar Coast of southwestern India, centred on the port of Cochin (modern Kochi in Kerala). One of the major powers of the medieval Malabar coast, it became the first Indian kingdom to enter into sustained relations with European powers — Portuguese, Dutch, and finally British — and survived as a princely state of British India until its merger with Travancore in 1949.

History

Origins and the Portuguese alliance

The Cochin royal house traced descent from the later Chera (Kulasekhara) empire of Mahodayapuram, which disintegrated in the twelfth century. The rajas of Cochin ruled a small but strategically valuable territory around the finest natural harbour on the coast; the great flood of 1341, which silted up Cranganore (Kodungallur) and opened the Cochin inlet, raised the port's importance. Cochin's great rival was the Zamorin of Calicut to the north. When Pedro Álvares Cabral reached the coast in 1500 and Vasco da Gama returned in 1502, the Raja of Cochin allied with the Portuguese against Calicut. The Portuguese were granted a factory and, in 1503, built Fort Emmanuel — the first European fort in India — and a Cochin raja was crowned with a crown sent by the King of Portugal.

Dutch and Mysorean periods

The Dutch East India Company captured Cochin from the Portuguese in 1663 and reduced the kingdom to a subordinate ally, rebuilding the Mattancherry Palace (the "Dutch Palace") for the raja. In the eighteenth century the expansion of Travancore under Marthanda Varma squeezed Cochin's territory, and in the 1770s–80s Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan of Mysore invaded and exacted tribute. Cochin's raja Sakthan Thampuran (r. 1790–1805), a celebrated ruler, reorganised the administration and founded the modern city of Thrissur.

Princely state

By treaties of 1791 and 1809 Cochin came under British paramountcy as a princely state, administered day-to-day by the raja with a diwan (prime minister). It was noted for progressive administration, high literacy, and extensive public works; its rulers included Rama Varma XV, who abdicated in 1914 in protest at official interference, and the last maharaja, Kerala Varma VI. In 1947 the maharaja was among the first princely rulers to announce accession to India, and on 1 July 1949 Cochin merged with Travancore to form the state of Travancore-Cochin, a precursor of modern Kerala.
