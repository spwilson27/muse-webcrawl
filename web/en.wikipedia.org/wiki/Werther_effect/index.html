Werther effect


The effect is named for Goethe's novel The Sorrows of Young Werther (1774).

The Werther effect is an increase in suicides following a widely publicised suicide. The term was coined by sociologist David Phillips in 1974, building on research showing that suicide rates tend to rise after front-page suicide stories. It is named after the protagonist of Johann Wolfgang von Goethe's 1774 novel The Sorrows of Young Werther, whose publication was followed by reports of young men imitating Werther's fate and which led to bans of the book in several places.

Evidence and mechanism

Research has associated the effect most strongly with sensational media coverage of celebrity suicides, particularly reporting that describes the method or presents suicide in a glamourised way. The proposed mechanism is social learning or identification: vulnerable readers who identify with the person reported may be more likely to act on suicidal thoughts. The effect is an example of suicide contagion.

Prevention

Public health bodies such as the World Health Organization publish media guidelines recommending that reports avoid explicit descriptions of methods, refrain from sensational language, and include information about support resources. Research has also described a contrasting Papageno effect, in which stories of people overcoming suicidal crises are associated with lower suicide rates.
