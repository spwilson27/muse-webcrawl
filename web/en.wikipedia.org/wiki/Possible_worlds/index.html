Possible worlds

Possible worlds
FieldPhilosophy, logic, metaphysics
Key figuresGottfried Leibniz, Saul Kripke, David Lewis, Robert Stalnaker
Related conceptsModal logic, counterfactuals, many-worlds interpretation

Possible worlds are ways the world could have been, or total ways things could be. The concept plays a central role in philosophy, particularly in the logic of modality, the analysis of counterfactual conditionals, and semantics. A proposition is said to be necessarily true if it is true in all possible worlds, possibly true if it is true in at least one possible world, and contingently true if it is true in some possible worlds and false in others. The actual world is one possible world among many — the one that is realised.

A branching structure of possible worlds departing from the actual world.

History

The idea is often traced to Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, who held that God contemplated infinitely many possible worlds and actualised the best of them. In the twentieth century the notion was given rigorous form by logicians developing modal semantics: Saul Kripke's possible-worlds semantics for modal logic (developed in the late 1950s and 1960s) treated necessity and possibility in terms of quantification over worlds linked by accessibility relations. Clarence Irving Lewis had earlier developed systems of strict implication that anticipated this treatment.

Modal realism

David Lewis defended modal realism, the view that possible worlds are as real and concrete as the actual world, and that "actual" is merely an indexical term, like "here": each world is actual from the standpoint of its own inhabitants. Lewis's On the Plurality of Worlds (1986) is the classic statement. Most philosophers reject this realism in favour of ersatz accounts, on which possible worlds are abstract representations — sets of sentences, properties, states of affairs, or combinations thereof.

Applications

  • Counterfactuals: Robert Stalnaker and David Lewis analyse "if it had been that A, then it would have been that B" in terms of truth at the closest possible worlds where A holds.
  • Semantics: Possible worlds underpin intensional semantics in linguistics and philosophy of language.
  • Epistemology and decision theory: Worlds model information states and alternatives under consideration.
  • Physics: The many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics is sometimes compared, though its "worlds" are decohered branches of a single quantum state rather than Lewisian worlds.

Criticism

Critics ask what possible worlds are made of and how we can have knowledge of them. W. V. O. Quine was famously sceptical of quantified modal logic. Others object that modal realism multiplies entities extravagantly; Lewis replied that the theoretical payoff justifies the ontology.
