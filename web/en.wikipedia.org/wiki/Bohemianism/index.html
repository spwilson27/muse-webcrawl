Bohemianism

Bohemianism is a social and cultural movement associated with an unconventional, artistic lifestyle — valuing creativity, poverty embraced or endured in pursuit of art, communal living, and a disregard for bourgeois conventions and material success. The term originated in 19th-century France, where bohémien had long been a (mistaken) French word for Romani people, believed to have come from Bohemia; it was transferred to impoverished artists and writers of Paris's Latin Quarter and popularised by Henri Murger's Scènes de la vie de bohème (1851), the source for Puccini's opera La bohème.

History

Parisian bohemia of the 1830s–1850s set the template: young writers, painters and musicians living in cheap garrets, frequenting cafés, forming intense friendships and feuds, and staging their poverty as a badge of authenticity. Théophile Gautier, Gérard de Nerval and Baudelaire were associated with the scene; in London, the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood and later Oscar Wilde embodied related ideals; in New York, Greenwich Village became the bohemian capital of the early 20th century, followed by the Beat Generation and the counterculture of the 1960s.

Influence

The bohemian ideal — the artist as outsider, valuing expression over respectability — became a permanent strand of Western culture, periodically commercialised in fashion ("boho-chic") and in the marketing of creative districts. Sociologists note the recurring tension between genuine marginality and its adoption as a lifestyle by the affluent. The term is not related to the geology term Bohemian Massif beyond their shared reference to the region of Bohemia.
