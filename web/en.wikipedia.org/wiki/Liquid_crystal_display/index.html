Liquid-crystal display

Liquid-crystal display (LCD)

Type: Flat-panel display

Working principle: Light modulation by liquid crystals

Invented: Key developments 1888 (Reinitzer), 1960s–70s (Heilmeier; Schadt and Helfrich)

Uses: Televisions, monitors, phones, calculators

A liquid-crystal display (LCD) is a flat-panel display or other electronically modulated optical device that uses the light-modulating properties of liquid crystals combined with polarizers. Liquid crystals do not emit light directly but instead use a backlight or reflector to produce images in color or monochrome. LCDs are available to display arbitrary images (as in a general-purpose computer display) or fixed images with low information content, as in a seven-segment display.

Working principle

Each pixel of an LCD consists of a layer of molecules aligned between two transparent electrodes and two polarizing filters whose axes are perpendicular to each other. Applying an electric field changes the orientation of the liquid crystal molecules — in twisted nematic (TN) cells they untwist — which controls how much light passes through the pixel. Thin-film transistors (TFTs) form an active matrix to address individual pixels in high-resolution displays.

History

Liquid crystals were first described by Friedrich Reinitzer in 1888. Practical display effects were developed in the 1960s and 1970s: George Heilmeier demonstrated the dynamic scattering mode, and Martin Schadt and Wolfgang Helfrich invented the twisted nematic field effect in 1970. LCDs became the dominant flat-panel technology in the 2000s, displacing cathode-ray tubes in monitors and televisions.

Advantages and disadvantages

LCDs are thin, light, and energy-efficient, but historically had limited viewing angles and contrast ratios compared with OLED displays. Technologies such as in-plane switching (IPS) improved viewing angles substantially.
