HLA-B27 (human leukocyte antigen B27) is a class I surface antigen encoded by the B locus of the human major histocompatibility complex (MHC) on chromosome 6. It presents peptide fragments to cytotoxic T cells as part of normal immune function. HLA-B27 is famous in medicine for its strong association with a family of inflammatory diseases called the seronegative spondyloarthropathies, most notably ankylosing spondylitis — one of the strongest associations known between an HLA type and a common disease.

Disease associations

The association was discovered independently in 1973 by groups in London and Los Angeles. Around 85–90% of patients of European descent with ankylosing spondylitis carry HLA-B27, compared with roughly 8% of the general population. The association is also found, at lower strength, in reactive arthritis (formerly Reiter's syndrome), acute anterior uveitis (iritis), the arthritis of psoriasis and inflammatory bowel disease, and undifferentiated spondyloarthritis. Nevertheless, only a small minority of HLA-B27-positive individuals — on the order of 1–5% — ever develop ankylosing spondylitis, so the antigen is a risk factor rather than a cause, and it is neither necessary nor sufficient for the disease.

Subtypes and mechanism

More than 200 subtypes of HLA-B27 (HLA-B*27:01 through B*27:200+) have been described, differing by a few amino acid substitutions. Not all carry equal risk: the common subtypes B*27:05 (ancestral and widespread) and B*27:02 are strongly associated with ankylosing spondylitis, whereas B*27:06 (found in Southeast Asia) and B*27:09 (found in Sardinia) show little or no association — comparisons that have been used to pinpoint the structural features responsible.

How HLA-B27 promotes disease remains unresolved nearly fifty years after the association was found. Leading hypotheses include the "arthritogenic peptide" theory, in which B27 presents a microbial or self peptide that triggers autoreactive T cells; the misfolding theory, in which the B27 heavy chain folds slowly in the endoplasmic reticulum, causing cellular stress responses; and the formation of abnormal heavy-chain homodimers at the cell surface that are recognised by receptors on immune cells. A strong genetic interaction with the enzyme ERAP1, which trims peptides for MHC loading, supports a role for peptide presentation.

Distribution and evolution

HLA-B27 frequency varies dramatically between populations: it is found in up to 25–50% of some Indigenous peoples of the Arctic and sub-Arctic (such as the Haida of Canada and Siberian groups) and in about 8–10% of Scandinavians, but is rare in most sub-Saharan African populations and uncommon (around 0.5–1%) in Japan. The pattern has been attributed to historical epidemics and founder effects. Interestingly, HLA-B27 is also associated with slower progression of HIV infection and spontaneous clearance of hepatitis C virus, suggesting the allele confers an advantage against certain viral infections that may explain its persistence.

Testing

HLA-B27 typing by blood test is used as an adjunct in diagnosing axial spondyloarthritis in patients with suggestive symptoms, but because most carriers are healthy, it is a poor screening test and is not recommended in the absence of clinical suspicion.
