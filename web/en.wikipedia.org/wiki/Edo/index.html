Edo (Japanese: 江戸, "bay entrance"), formerly spelled Yedo, was the seat of power of the Tokugawa shogunate from 1603 to 1868 and grew to become one of the largest cities in the world. In 1868, with the Meiji Restoration, it was renamed Tokyo ("Eastern Capital") and became the capital of Japan, the emperor moving there from Kyoto.

History

Edo was a modest castle town when Tokugawa Ieyasu made it his headquarters in 1590 and then, after winning the Battle of Sekigahara, became shogun in 1603. Under the Tokugawa, the town expanded rapidly: land was reclaimed from Edo Bay, canals were dug, and Edo Castle became the largest fortress in Japan.

The system of sankin-kōtai, requiring the daimyō (feudal lords) to maintain residences in Edo and attend the shogun's court in alternate years, filled the city with samurai households and their retainers, while merchants and artisans — the chōnin — supplied their needs. By the early 18th century Edo's population was around one million, probably making it the world's largest city at the time.

Society and culture

Formally the city was divided between the samurai estates around the castle (Yamanote) and the dense merchant quarters of the low town (Shitamachi). Despite merchants' low formal status in the Confucian class order, Edo developed a rich urban culture: kabuki theatre, sumo, the pleasure district of Yoshiwara, publishing, and the ukiyo-e woodblock prints that are now iconic images of the period. Fires were so frequent they were called "the flowers of Edo"; the Great Fire of Meireki in 1657 killed perhaps 100,000 people and reshaped the city's planning.

End of the shogunate

Commodore Perry's arrival in Edo Bay in 1853–54 opened Japan to foreign trade and began the shogunate's final crisis. In 1868 the last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, surrendered Edo Castle without a major battle; the city was renamed Tokyo and the Meiji emperor took up residence in the former castle grounds.
