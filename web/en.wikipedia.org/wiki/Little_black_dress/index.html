Little black dress

The little black dress (LBD) is a black evening or cocktail dress, cut simply and often quite short. Fashion historians credit its origins to the 1920s designs of Coco Chanel and Jean Patou, intended to be a long-lasting, versatile, affordable garment accessible to the widest market possible and in a neutral colour. It is often simply referred to as the "little black dress".

History

Black had earlier been associated with mourning, but in the 1920s Chanel transformed it into a chic uniform for the modern woman. In 1926 American Vogue published a picture of a short black Chanel dress with long sleeves, accessorised with a string of pearls, and called it "Chanel's Ford" — like the Model T automobile, it was simple, democratic and destined to become a staple in every woman's wardrobe. Vogue predicted it would become "the frock that all the world will wear".

The little black dress remained popular through the economic austerity of the Great Depression and the Second World War, because it was elegant yet economical. In the postwar era, Christian Dior's New Look of 1947 revived it in a more opulent form, and Hollywood cemented its glamour. The most famous LBD of all is the Hubert de Givenchy dress worn by Audrey Hepburn in the opening scene of Breakfast at Tiffany's (1961); the dress sold at Christie's in 2006 for about £467,000.

Cultural significance

The little black dress is considered essential to a complete wardrobe and is often described as a "rule of fashion" that every woman should own a simple, elegant black dress that can be dressed up or down depending on the occasion and accessories. Other famous examples include the "revenge dress" worn by Diana, Princess of Wales, in 1994, and the safety-pin dress worn by Elizabeth Hurley in 1994.
