Herod Agrippa (c. 11 BC – 44 AD), also known as Agrippa I, was the last king of Judea of the Herodian dynasty, reigning over a kingdom briefly reunited to the extent it had enjoyed under his grandfather, Herod the Great. Raised at the imperial court in Rome, he used his friendships with Caligula and Claudius to restore the Herodian monarchy, and is remembered in Jewish tradition as a pious king and in the New Testament as a persecutor of the early church.

Early life

Agrippa was the son of Aristobulus IV, executed by Herod the Great, and was sent to Rome at the age of three, growing up with the future emperor Claudius and with Drusus, son of Tiberius. Extravagant living bankrupted him; after Drusus's death he fled his creditors to Idumea and Alexandria before returning to Rome, where he attached himself to Caligula. Imprisoned by Tiberius after a careless remark wishing the emperor's death, he was freed and made king of the northern tetrarchies by Caligula on his accession in 37 AD.

Kingdom

Agrippa played a key role in Claudius's accession after Caligula's assassination in 41 AD, and was rewarded with Judea and Samaria, ruling the whole of his grandfather's realm. He courted Jewish support assiduously — keeping the Law publicly, offering the daily sacrifices, and beginning a new northern wall for Jerusalem (halted by Roman order) — while presenting himself to his Greek subjects as a Hellenistic benefactor, building theatres and baths at Berytus and Caesarea. The Acts of the Apostles records that he executed the apostle James, son of Zebedee, and imprisoned Peter.

Death

In 44 AD, celebrating games at Caesarea in a robe of woven silver that glittered in the sun, Agrippa was acclaimed by flatterers as a god. Josephus and Acts agree that he was immediately stricken — Acts says eaten by worms — and died in agony after five days, aged about 54. His son Agrippa II was judged too young to succeed, and Judea reverted to direct Roman rule under procurators — a settlement whose deterioration led within a generation to the First Jewish–Roman War.
