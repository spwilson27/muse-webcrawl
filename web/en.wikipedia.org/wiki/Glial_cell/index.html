Glial cell

Glial cells (neuroglia) are the non-neuronal cells of the nervous system. Once dismissed as mere ‘glue’ (Greek glia), they maintain homeostasis, form myelin, support and protect neurons, and modulate synaptic transmission.

Types

In the CNS: astrocytes (metabolic support, blood–brain barrier, ion balance), oligodendrocytes (myelin sheaths on many axons), microglia (resident immune cells) and ependymal cells (line the ventricles). In the peripheral nervous system: Schwann cells (myelin, one axon each) and satellite cells.

Glia roughly equal neurons in number in the human brain — older claims of a 10:1 glia-to-neuron ratio are a myth.

Roles in disease

Glia are implicated in Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease, chronic pain and psychiatric illness; most brain tumours (gliomas) arise from glial lineages. Their active role in signalling is a major theme of modern neuroscience.
