Artemisia absinthium

Artemisia absinthium, commonly known as absinthium, absinthe wormwood, or simply wormwood, is a species of Artemisia native to temperate regions of Eurasia and North Africa and widely naturalised in North America and elsewhere. It is a herbaceous perennial plant with silvery-grey, bitter foliage, grown as an ornamental and historically used as a medicinal herb and as the principal flavouring of the spirit absinthe and an ingredient in vermouth and other bitters.

Description

The plant grows to 0.8–1.2 m tall with spirally arranged, silky silvery-white leaves and pale yellow tubular flowers appearing in summer. All parts are intensely bitter due to sesquiterpene lactones such as absinthin; the essential oil contains thujone, a compound once blamed (largely exaggeratedly) for the supposed hallucinogenic effects of absinthe.

Uses

Wormwood has a long history in herbal medicine — as a digestive bitter and anthelmintic (expelling intestinal worms, hence the name) — and is mentioned in ancient Egyptian and Greek texts and in the Bible as a symbol of bitterness. In brewing, it was used before hops became standard. Modern clinical evidence for medicinal uses is limited, and concentrated preparations can be toxic in overdose. As the flavouring of absinthe, the plant gave the drink both its name and its notoriety during the drink's bans in the early twentieth century, since lifted in most countries with limits on thujone content.

Cultivation

Wormwood is hardy and drought tolerant, prefers poor, well-drained soils in full sun, and can inhibit the growth of neighbouring plants through compounds leached from its leaves and roots.
