Codrus

Codrus, the legendary last king of Athens.

Codrus (Greek: Κόδρος, Kódros) was the legendary last king of Athens, traditionally dated to the eleventh century BCE during the Dorian invasion of Attica. According to the legend, Codrus sacrificed his own life to save the city, and after his death the Athenians abolished the kingship, judging no one worthy to succeed him.

Legend

The story, told by authors including Lycurgus and Justin, relates that the Dorians, invading Attica, consulted the oracle at Delphi and were told they would take Athens provided they did not kill its king. Codrus, learning of the oracle, disguised himself as a woodcutter, went out to the enemy camp, deliberately provoked a quarrel, and was slain by Dorian soldiers. When the Dorians discovered that the dead man was the king of Athens, they abandoned the invasion rather than defy the oracle. His sons Medon and Neleus disputed the succession; the Athenians settled the matter by replacing the monarchy with the office of archon, held first by Medon. Neleus was said to have led the Ionian migration to Asia Minor, where his descendants, the Codridae, founded cities including Miletus and Ephesus.

Legacy

Codrus became a stock exemplar of patriotic self-sacrifice in Greek and Roman rhetoric, frequently paired with figures who died for their country. Although modern historians treat the king-list of early Athens as largely legendary, the story reflects the historical memory of a transition from monarchy to aristocratic magistracy in early Athens.
