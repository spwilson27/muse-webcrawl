Thunder

Thunder is the sound caused by lightning. Depending on the distance from and nature of the lightning, it can range from a sharp, loud crack to a long, low rumble.

Cause

A lightning discharge heats the air along its channel to temperatures of roughly 30,000 kelvin — several times hotter than the surface of the Sun — in microseconds. The superheated air expands explosively, faster than the speed of sound, creating a shock wave that decays into the acoustic wave heard as thunder. The flash and the sound are produced simultaneously, but light travels far faster than sound (about 343 metres per second in air), so thunder is heard after the flash is seen; counting the seconds between them gives an estimate of the distance to the strike (roughly one kilometre per three seconds).

Characteristics

The character of thunder depends on the geometry of the lightning channel, atmospheric conditions, and terrain. A close strike produces a sharp crack or tearing sound from the nearest part of the channel; the extended, branching channel and reflections from clouds and terrain produce prolonged rumbling as sound arrives from different segments of the bolt at different times. Temperature inversions can refract thunder so that it is inaudible beyond about 25 kilometres, giving rise to "heat lightning" — distant flashes with no audible thunder.

Culture

Thunder has been attributed in mythology to gods such as Zeus, Jupiter, and Thor, and storms and thunder feature in folklore, measurement traditions, and safety guidance worldwide: when thunder roars, people are advised to go indoors, since audible thunder means lightning is close enough to pose a risk.
