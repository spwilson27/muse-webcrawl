Basal-cell carcinoma


Nodular basal-cell carcinoma

Basal-cell carcinoma (BCC), also called basal-cell cancer, is the most common type of skin cancer. It arises from the basal cells of the epidermis and typically appears as a painless, slowly growing, pearly or translucent bump with fine visible blood vessels (telangiectasia), sometimes developing a central ulcer — the classic "rodent ulcer". Risk factors include ultraviolet light exposure, fair skin, older age, arsenic exposure, immunosuppression and the genetic disorder Gorlin syndrome, which involves mutations in the PTCH1 gene of the hedgehog signalling pathway.

Types and behaviour

Main subtypes are nodular (the most frequent), superficial, morpheaform (sclerosing) and infiltrative. BCC grows slowly and very rarely metastasises, but it can be locally invasive and destructive if neglected, particularly around the eyes, nose and ears.

Treatment

Diagnosis is by skin examination and biopsy. Most cases are cured by surgical excision; Mohs micrographic surgery offers the highest cure rates for facial and recurrent tumours. Alternatives include curettage and electrodesiccation, cryotherapy, topical imiquimod or 5-fluorouracil for superficial lesions, radiotherapy, and, for rare advanced cases, hedgehog-pathway inhibitors such as vismodegib. Prevention focuses on sun protection; millions of cases occur each year worldwide, most often in sunny climates such as Australia's.
