Hematophagy

Hematophagy (sometimes spelled haematophagy or hematophagia) is the practice by certain animals of feeding on blood (from the Greek words αἷμα haima "blood" and φαγεῖν phagein "to eat"). Since blood is a fluid tissue rich in nutritious proteins and lipids that can be taken without great effort, hematophagy is a preferred form of feeding for many small animals, such as worms and arthropods.

Obligate and facultative feeders

Some intestinal parasites, such as hookworms, feed from blood extracted from the capillaries of the gut, and about 75 percent of all species of leeches are hematophagous. Some species, such as lampreys and candiru fish, feed on the blood of larger animals. Obligate hematophages include the vampire bats of Central and South America, bed bugs, ticks, fleas, many mosquitoes (in which only females take blood meals), tsetse flies, triatomine "kissing bugs", and the parasitic copepods and isopods of fish. Facultative hematophages supplement other food sources, and some species take blood only for egg production.

Adaptations

Blood feeders possess specialised mouthparts for piercing skin and sucking blood, saliva containing anticoagulants and anaesthetic compounds (such as the anticoagulant hirudin in leeches and draculin in vampire bats), and digestive systems adapted to a diet rich in protein and iron but poor in other nutrients; gut symbionts often supply B-vitamins. Because blood-feeding exposes hosts and feeders to pathogens, hematophagous arthropods are important vectors of disease, transmitting malaria, dengue, Chagas disease, Lyme disease and many other infections.
