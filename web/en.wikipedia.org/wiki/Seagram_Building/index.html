The Seagram Building is a skyscraper at 375 Park Avenue, between 52nd and 53rd streets, in Midtown Manhattan, New York City. It was designed by Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, in collaboration with Philip Johnson, Ely Jacques Kahn and Robert Allan Jacobs, and completed in 1958 for the Canadian distiller Joseph E. Seagram & Sons. The 38-storey, 516-foot (157 m) tower is regarded as a masterpiece of the International Style and of corporate modernism.

Rather than filling its site, Mies set the tower back 100 feet (30 m) from Park Avenue behind a pink-granite plaza with fountains and pools — a radical gesture for Manhattan office development that influenced the 1961 Zoning Resolution's incentive zoning for public plazas. The tower's bronze-and-glass façade uses extruded bronze I-beam mullions and amber-tinted glass to express its structure. The building's interiors included the celebrated Four Seasons and Brasserie restaurants in its podium.

Design and construction

Phyllis Lambert, daughter of Seagram president Samuel Bronfman, was placed in charge of the project and selected Mies as architect in 1954. Because New York fire codes required structural steel to be fireproofed, the expressed bronze beams are non-structural, applied to the façade to articulate the frame behind the enclosure. The building cost around $41 million, making it the most expensive skyscraper of its time, with lavish materials throughout its public spaces, including travertine, marble and bronze.

Influence and landmark status

The Seagram Building transformed American corporate architecture: its plaza, curtain wall and restrained proportions were widely imitated in office towers of the 1960s and 1970s. The exterior and several interiors were designated New York City landmarks in 1989, and the building was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 2006. RFR Holding acquired the building in 2000; its former Four Seasons space reopened as The Grill and The Pool under new operators in 2017.
