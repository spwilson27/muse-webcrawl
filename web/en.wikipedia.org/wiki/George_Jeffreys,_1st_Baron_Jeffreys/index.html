George Jeffreys, 1st Baron Jeffreys

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

George Jeffreys, 1st Baron Jeffreys
Born: 15 May 1645, Acton, Wrexham, Wales
Died: 18 April 1689 (aged 43), Tower of London
Office: Lord Chancellor (1685–1688); Lord Chief Justice (1683–1685)
Known for: Presiding over the "Bloody Assizes" of 1685

George Jeffreys, 1st Baron Jeffreys, PC (15 May 1645 – 18 April 1689), known as "the hanging judge", was a Welsh judge who became notorious during the reign of James II for his severity, most infamously as the presiding judge of the Bloody Assizes that followed the Monmouth Rebellion in 1685, in which hundreds of rebels were hanged or transported.

Rise

Jeffreys was born at Acton Hall near Wrexham, educated at Shrewsbury and Westminster schools and briefly at Trinity College, Cambridge, and was called to the bar in 1668. He advanced rapidly through assiduous court connections: recorder of London, knighted in 1677, chief justice of Chester, and in 1683 Lord Chief Justice of the King's Bench, where he conducted the Rye House Plot trials with conspicuous zeal for the Crown. In May 1685 James II raised him to the peerage as Baron Jeffreys of Wem.

The Bloody Assizes

After the Duke of Monmouth's defeat at Sedgemoor in July 1685, Jeffreys led the Western Circuit commission sent to try the captured rebels. At Winchester, Taunton, Dorchester, Exeter, and Wells, roughly 1,400 prisoners came before the courts; several hundred were executed — some estimates say around 250–300 — and many more were flogged, fined, or transported to the West Indies. Jeffreys' browbeating of defendants, including the elderly Lady Alice Lisle, condemned for harbouring a rebel, fixed his reputation for cruelty. Rewarded with the Lord Chancellorship in September 1685, he remained James's loyal servant to the end of the reign.

Fall and death

When William of Orange invaded in 1688 and James fled, Jeffreys attempted to escape to Hamburg disguised as a sailor but was recognised — legend says by a former litigant who knew his fierce eyebrows — at a Wapping tavern and seized by a mob. Placed in the Tower of London for his own safety, he died there in April 1689, probably of kidney disease aggravated by heavy drinking. Modern historians note that while Jeffreys' brutality was real, he was also executing the settled policy of the king and council, and his legal ability was acknowledged even by enemies.
