Drug synergy

Drug synergy is an interaction between two or more drugs that causes the total effect of the combination to be greater than the sum of the individual effects of each drug taken alone. It is a form of drug interaction exploited deliberately in many therapies, and stands in contrast to antagonism, in which a combination produces a smaller effect than expected.

Assessment

Synergy is quantified using reference models such as Loewe additivity, Bliss independence and the Chou–Talalay combination index, as well as isobologram analysis. Because conclusions depend on the model chosen, reported synergy must be interpreted together with the assumptions of the method used.

Clinical examples

Classic examples include the antibacterial combination trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, which blocks two sequential steps of folate synthesis; multi-drug regimens for HIV and tuberculosis that attack the pathogen at several points while suppressing resistance; and combination chemotherapy in oncology. Synergy can also be harmful, as when two central nervous system depressants combine to produce dangerous respiratory depression.
