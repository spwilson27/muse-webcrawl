Intersubjectivity

Intersubjectivity, in philosophy, is the psychological relationship between people: the sharing of subjective states by two or more individuals. It is used in philosophy of mind, phenomenology, psychology, and social science to describe how a common world of meaning is possible between separate subjects.

Phenomenology

Edmund Husserl developed the concept most fully, notably in his Cartesian Meditations, where he addressed the problem of how the ego can experience another subject as a subject — through empathy and "appresentation" — thereby constituting an objective world shared by a community of egos. Alfred Schutz carried intersubjectivity into social theory, analysing the taken-for-granted "lifeworld" that people assume they share.

Psychology and development

Developmental psychologists use intersubjectivity for the reciprocal sharing of attention, intention, and emotion between infants and caregivers. Colwyn Trevarthen distinguished primary intersubjectivity (face-to-face attunement in early infancy) from secondary intersubjectivity (joint attention to objects, emerging around nine months). Impairments in these capacities are studied in relation to autism.

Social science

In social research, intersubjectivity underpins the possibility of shared knowledge: agreement between observers is a practical criterion of objectivity, and ethnographic methods depend on building intersubjective understanding between researcher and participants.
