Sex linkage

Sex linkage is the phenotypic expression of a trait governed by a gene located on a sex chromosome. Because males and females carry different complements of sex chromosomes, sex-linked traits show characteristic inheritance patterns that differ from those of autosomal traits. In humans and most mammals, sex-linked genes are overwhelmingly found on the much larger X chromosome; traits they control are called X-linked.

Inheritance patterns

In X-linked recessive inheritance, a male who inherits one copy of the mutant allele expresses the trait, because he has no second X to mask it, whereas a female usually must inherit two copies to be affected and is otherwise a carrier. Consequently conditions such as haemophilia and red–green colour blindness are far commoner in males. In X-linked dominant inheritance, one copy suffices in either sex, and affected males cannot pass the trait to their sons. Y-linked (holandric) traits are rare and pass only from father to son.

History

Sex linkage was demonstrated in 1910 by Thomas Hunt Morgan, whose work on the white-eyed mutant of the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster provided the first proof that genes reside on chromosomes and helped found modern genetics. The unequal expression of X-linked genes between the sexes is compensated in female mammals by X-inactivation, described by Mary Lyon in 1961, which explains phenomena such as the patchwork coats of tortoiseshell cats.

In other organisms

Birds and some other animals use the ZW sex-determination system, in which females are the heterogametic sex, so Z-linked traits show the reverse pattern of mammalian X-linkage. Sex linkage is exploited commercially in poultry breeding, where sex-linked plumage genes allow day-old chicks to be sexed by appearance.
