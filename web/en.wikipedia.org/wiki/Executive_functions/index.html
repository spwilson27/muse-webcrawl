Executive functions (collectively referred to as executive function and cognitive control) are a set of cognitive processes that are necessary for the cognitive control of behaviour: selecting and successfully monitoring behaviours that facilitate the attainment of chosen goals. They include basic cognitive processes such as attentional control, inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility, as well as higher-level functions such as planning, reasoning, and problem-solving.

The unity/diversity model

In the influential model of Miyake and colleagues (2000), executive functions comprise three core, partially separable components: inhibition (suppressing prepotent responses), shifting (switching between tasks or mental sets), and updating (monitoring and revising working-memory contents). Later work added a common executive factor underlying all three. Development of these functions continues through childhood and adolescence as the prefrontal cortex matures, and they decline in older age.

Neuroanatomy and disorders

Executive functions are mediated primarily by the prefrontal cortex and its networks with parietal cortex, basal ganglia, and thalamus. The classic case of Phineas Gage illustrated how frontal injury can transform personality and self-regulation. Deficits of executive function are prominent in ADHD, schizophrenia, autism, depression, and dementias, and are assessed clinically with tests such as the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, the Stroop test, and the Trail Making Test. The term dysexecutive syndrome describes the pattern of disorganisation, distractibility, and poor planning following frontal lobe damage.
