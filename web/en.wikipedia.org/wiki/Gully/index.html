Gully

A gully is a landform created by running water, mass movement or commonly a combination of both eroding sharply into soil or other relatively erodible material, typically on a hillside or in river floodplains or terraces. Gullies resemble large ditches or small valleys, but are metres to tens of metres in depth and width, are characterised by a distinct headscarp and headward erosion, and are formed by intermittent water flow, rainfall and snowmelt runoff.

Gully erosion on a hillside after heavy rainfall

Formation and consequences

Gully erosion occurs when surface water concentrates in narrow pathways and removes soil at a rate far greater than sheet erosion. The process often begins when vegetation cover is removed by overgrazing, deforestation, construction or tillage, leaving the soil exposed. Once a gully head forms, it migrates upslope through headward erosion, capturing an ever-larger drainage area.

Gullies are a major source of sediment in agricultural catchments and can remove large volumes of productive topsoil, damage roads and buildings, lower water tables, and fragment farmland into unusable parcels.

Control

Control measures include check dams, drop structures, re-vegetation of the gully walls and floor, diversion of runoff away from the gully head, and land-management practices such as contour farming and reduced tillage. Small gullies can sometimes be filled and regraded, but large active gullies usually require engineered structures combined with long-term catchment management.
