Factorial experiment

A factorial experiment is an experiment whose design consists of two or more factors, each with discrete possible values or "levels", and whose experimental units take on all possible combinations of these levels across all such factors. A factorial design may also be called a fully crossed design. Such an experiment allows the investigator to study the effect of each factor on the response variable, as well as the effects of interactions between factors on the response variable.

History

Factorial designs were used in the 19th century by John Bennet Lawes and Joseph Henry Gilbert of the Rothamsted Experimental Station. Ronald Fisher argued in 1926 that "complex" designs, such as factorial designs, were more efficient than studying one factor at a time. Frank Yates made significant contributions, particularly in the analysis of designs, by the Yates method.

Advantages and disadvantages

For the vast majority of factorial experiments, each factor has only two levels, for example temperature and pressure, yielding a 2k factorial experiment. Factorial designs are efficient: instead of conducting a series of experiments each studying only one factor, a factorial experiment studies all factors simultaneously and can reveal interactions, where the effect of one factor depends on the level of another. The main disadvantage is combinatorial growth — the number of runs grows exponentially with the number of factors, which motivates fractional factorial designs that run only a subset of the full combinations.

Notation and analysis

A 2×3 factorial design has two factors, the first with two levels and the second with three, giving six treatment combinations. Effects are typically estimated and tested using analysis of variance (ANOVA), with main effects and interaction terms. In two-level designs, Yates's algorithm provides a computationally simple method for estimating all effects.
