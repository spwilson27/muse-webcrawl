The regulatory T cells (Tregs), formerly known as suppressor T cells, are a subpopulation of T cells that modulate the immune system, maintain tolerance to self-antigens, and prevent autoimmune disease. Tregs are immunosuppressive and generally suppress or downregulate induction and proliferation of effector T cells. They typically express the biomarkers CD4, FOXP3, and CD25 and are thought to be derived from the same lineage as naïve CD4+ cells.

Because effector T cells also express CD4 and CD25, Tregs are difficult to discriminate from effector cells; the transcription factor FOXP3 is the defining marker. Mutations in the gene encoding FOXP3 cause IPEX syndrome in humans, a severe systemic autoimmune disease, demonstrating how essential Tregs are to self-tolerance.

Populations

Thymic and peripheral Tregs

Regulatory T cells develop either in the thymus (thymus-derived, or tTregs) or are induced in the periphery from naïve CD4+ T cells (peripherally induced, or pTregs), often in response to antigens encountered in the gut and at other mucosal surfaces. A third category, in-vitro-induced Tregs (iTregs), is generated experimentally. All share FOXP3 expression and suppressive function, though they differ in stability and in epigenetic marks at the FOXP3 locus.

Mechanisms of suppression

Tregs suppress through several mechanisms: secretion of inhibitory cytokines such as interleukin-10 (IL-10), TGF-β and IL-35; direct killing of target cells via granzyme and perforin; metabolic disruption, including high CD25 expression acting as an IL-2 "sink" and production of adenosine; and modulation of dendritic cell function through molecules such as CTLA-4.

Role in disease

Deficient or defective Tregs contribute to autoimmunity and allergy, whereas excessive Treg activity can impair immune responses to infections and cancer. Tumours frequently recruit Tregs into their microenvironment, shielding themselves from immune attack; depleting or disabling Tregs is therefore an active strategy in cancer immunotherapy. Conversely, expanding Tregs or administering them as cell therapy is being explored to treat autoimmune diseases and to prevent rejection of transplanted organs.

History

The existence of suppressor T cells was proposed in the 1970s but fell into disrepute when the supposed molecular basis could not be confirmed. The field revived in 1995, when Shimon Sakaguchi and colleagues showed that CD4+CD25+ T cells prevent autoimmunity in mice, and with the identification of FOXP3 in 2001 as the lineage-defining factor, work for which Sakaguchi shared recognition in the broader story of immune tolerance research.
