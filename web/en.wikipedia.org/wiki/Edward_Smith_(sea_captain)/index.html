Edward John Smith (27 January 1850 – 15 April 1912) was a British sea captain and naval officer. He served as commanding officer of numerous White Star Line vessels and was the captain of RMS Titanic, which struck an iceberg and sank during her maiden voyage on 15 April 1912; Smith went down with the ship.

Early life and career

Smith was born in Hanley, Staffordshire. He left school at thirteen and went to sea in 1867, serving an apprenticeship on the Liverpool-owned sailing ship Senator Weber. He joined the White Star Line in 1880 and rose rapidly, receiving his first command in 1887. Smith became the line's most trusted senior captain, favoured by wealthy passengers — some reportedly would sail only with him — and commanded the maiden voyages of several of the company's largest liners, including Baltic, Adriatic, and Olympic. He held a commission in the Royal Naval Reserve.

Titanic

For the maiden voyage of Titanic in April 1912, Smith was transferred from Olympic; the command was widely reported to be his last before retirement. On the night of 14 April, the ship received several ice warnings. At 23:40 the liner struck an iceberg in the North Atlantic. During the evacuation Smith directed the loading of lifeboats and, by consistent survivor testimony, behaved with composure and concern for his passengers; his last reported actions were to order the crew to look after themselves. His body was never recovered. Memorials to Smith stand in Hanley and Lichfield.

Legacy

Smith has been portrayed in every major film of the disaster, notably by Laurence Naismith in A Night to Remember (1958) and Bernard Hill in Titanic (1997). Debates continue among historians over his speed through the ice field and the details of his final moments, but the "captain going down with his ship" image fixed his place in the public memory of the sinking.
