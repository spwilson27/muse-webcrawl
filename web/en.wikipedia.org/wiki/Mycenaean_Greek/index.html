Mycenaean Greek is the earliest attested form of the Greek language, spoken on mainland Greece and Crete during the Mycenaean period (approximately 1600–1100 BC). It is known exclusively from administrative records written in the Linear B syllabic script on clay tablets preserved by the fires that destroyed the Mycenaean palaces. The decipherment of Linear B by Michael Ventris, working with John Chadwick, in 1952–1953 demonstrated that the language of the tablets was an archaic Greek dialect, pushing the documented history of Greek back by some five centuries.

Corpus and decipherment

Tablets were first excavated by Arthur Evans at Knossos on Crete from 1900; further archives were found at Pylos (excavated by Carl Blegen from 1939), Mycenae, Tiryns, Thebes and Chania. The texts are palace inventories and ration lists — records of land, livestock, textiles, bronze, oil, wine and personnel — written by professional scribes in a script adapted from the earlier, still undeciphered Linear A of Minoan Crete. Building on Alice Kober's analysis of sign patterns, the architect Michael Ventris constructed syllabic grids and in 1952 announced that the language was Greek; Chadwick helped establish the decipherment in their 1953 article and the book Documents in Mycenaean Greek (1956).

Phonology and script

Linear B is a syllabary of roughly 87 signs, mostly of consonant-plus-vowel shape, supplemented by ideograms for commodities. The script is ill-suited to Greek: it does not distinguish voiced, voiceless and aspirated stops (except in the dental series), writes /r/ and /l/ with one series, omits most consonant clusters and final consonants, and does not mark vowel length. Scribes wrote, for example, ko-no-so for Knōsos (Κνωσός) and wa-na-ka for wanax ("king", later ἄναξ). Mycenaean preserves several archaic features lost in later Greek, notably the labiovelar consonants (written with the q- series, e.g. qo-u-ko-ro = gʷoukolos, "cowherd", later boukólos) and initial /w/ (the digamma), still heard in words such as wa-na-ka.

Grammar and vocabulary

Mycenaean nouns inflect for three genders and at least five cases, including a distinct instrumental plural. The verb system shows augmentless past tenses in the tablets' terse style and preserves early forms of the infinitive. Much of the vocabulary is directly ancestral to later Greek: te-o = theos (god), do-e-ro = doulos (slave/servant), ku-ru-so = khrusos (gold), and divine names already familiar from Homer, including Zeus (di-we), Hera, Poseidon (po-se-da-o), Athena (a-ta-na) and Dionysus (di-wo-nu-so). Homeric words such as wanax and lawagetas ("leader of the people") reflect genuine Mycenaean titles.

Dialectal position

Mycenaean is usually grouped with the later Arcadocypriot dialects (and sometimes Pamphylian) in an "Achaean" branch of Greek, on the basis of shared innovations. Its apparent uniformity across sites from Crete to Thebes suggests a standardised palace administrative language, possibly masking greater spoken diversity.
