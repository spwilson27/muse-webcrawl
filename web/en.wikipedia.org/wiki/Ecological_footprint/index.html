The ecological footprint is a measure of human demand on the Earth's ecosystems, developed in the early 1990s by Mathis Wackernagel and William Rees at the University of British Columbia. It quantifies the amount of biologically productive land and sea area needed to supply the resources a population consumes and to absorb its wastes — especially carbon dioxide emissions — expressed in global hectares (hectares of world-average productivity).

Method and findings

Footprint accounting covers six land-use categories: cropland, grazing land, fishing grounds, forest land, built-up land, and the forest area hypothetically needed to sequester carbon emissions. The footprint is compared with biocapacity — the productive area actually available. According to the Global Footprint Network, humanity's footprint began to exceed Earth's total biocapacity in the 1970s, a condition called ecological overshoot; recent estimates put demand at roughly 1.7 Earths, meaning Earth Overshoot Day falls earlier each year, in late July or early August in recent years. Per-capita footprints differ widely, from over 8 gha in some wealthy states to under 1 gha in many low-income countries, against a global average biocapacity of about 1.6 gha per person.

Criticism

The methodology has been criticised for aggregating unlike impacts into a single area metric, for treating carbon sequestration by forests as the sole offset for emissions, and for results sensitive to assumptions about yields and equivalence factors. Supporters reply that it is a communication and accounting tool rather than a complete measure of sustainability, and it is widely used by governments, cities, and organisations to track resource demand.
