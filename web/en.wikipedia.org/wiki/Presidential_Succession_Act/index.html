Presidential Succession Act

The Presidential Succession Act (codified at 3 U.S.C. § 19) is a United States federal law establishing the line of succession to the presidency if neither the president nor the vice president can serve. The current version, enacted in 1947 and amended since, places the Speaker of the House and the President pro tempore of the Senate first, followed by the heads of the executive departments (Cabinet secretaries) in the order in which their departments were created.

History

The Constitution (Article II, and later the 20th and 25th Amendments) allows Congress to declare "what Officer shall then act as President" if both offices fall vacant. The first Succession Act of 1792 placed the President pro tempore and then the Speaker after the vice president. The Act of 1886 removed the legislators and substituted the Cabinet, beginning with the Secretary of State. President Truman, arguing that elected officials should precede appointed ones, persuaded Congress to restore the Speaker and President pro tempore at the head of the line in 1947 — with the Speaker placed first. Later amendments added new departments (most recently Homeland Security in 2006).

Operation

An officer in the line acts as president only if constitutionally eligible (natural-born citizen, at least 35, and 14 years resident). Cabinet officers must have been confirmed by the Senate; acting secretaries are passed over. A legislative officer who succeeds must resign from Congress. The statute also contains a "bumping" provision allowing a higher-ranked official to displace a lower one who has begun acting as president. The Act has never been invoked beyond the vice presidency, but it underpins the practice of the "designated survivor" kept away from events where the whole line gathers.

Criticism

Scholars have questioned the constitutionality of legislators as "Officers" under the Succession Clause, the risk that succession could flip the presidency to the opposing party (as nearly occurred in 1868 and 1912 configurations), and the competence of some officials in the line. Reform proposals surface after crises but have not been enacted.
