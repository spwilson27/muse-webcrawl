The Farm Security Administration (FSA) was a New Deal agency created in 1937 to combat rural poverty during the Great Depression in the United States. It succeeded the Resettlement Administration (1935) and is best remembered for its small but highly influential photography programme, which produced some of the most iconic images of the era.

Programmes

The FSA made loans to tenant farmers and sharecroppers to buy land and equipment, ran camps for migrant farm workers — notably in California, where they housed Dust Bowl refugees — supported rural rehabilitation and debt adjustment, and experimented with cooperative and resettlement communities. Under Rexford Tugwell the predecessor agency had pursued ambitious resettlement and "greenbelt town" projects that drew criticism from Congress, and the FSA's work was repeatedly attacked by conservative opponents and farm lobbies before its functions were folded into the Farmers Home Administration in 1946.

Photography programme

The FSA's Historical Section, headed by Roy Stryker, employed photographers including Dorothea Lange, Walker Evans, Arthur Rothstein, Gordon Parks, Jack Delano and Russell Lee. Between 1935 and 1944 they produced some 175,000 negatives documenting American life; Lange's Migrant Mother (1936) became the defining image of the Depression. The file is now held by the Library of Congress and remains a foundational archive of documentary photography.
