Overview

The prime meridian of the world is the line of 0° longitude passing through the former Royal Observatory in Greenwich, London. It divides Earth into the Eastern and Western hemispheres and is the line from which Greenwich Mean Time — and today the world's time zones — are counted.

The meridian is defined by the Airy Transit Circle, the great telescope installed by Astronomer Royal George Biddell Airy in 1851 for timing star transits. In 1884 the International Meridian Conference in Washington, D.C., voted to adopt the Greenwich line as the common zero of longitude for the world, a choice that ratified what most charts and almanacs already used.

The meridian today

Modern satellite geodesy places the zero meridian of the IERS reference system about 102 metres east of the brass strip in the Observatory courtyard — the small discrepancy arises because Airy's instrument aligned itself to the local direction of gravity, which the tilt of the geoid deflects slightly. Tourists nevertheless straddle the historic line, one foot in each hemisphere.
