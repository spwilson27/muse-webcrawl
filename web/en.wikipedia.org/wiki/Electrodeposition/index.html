Electrodeposition

Electrodeposition is the process of depositing a layer of material onto a surface using an electric current in an electrolytic cell. When the deposited material is a metal and the object is coated by immersion in a bath, the process is generally known as electroplating; electrodeposition more broadly includes the production of bulk metal shapes (electroforming), the winning and refining of metals (electrowinning and electrorefining), and the deposition of semiconductors, oxides and conductive polymers.

Process

The workpiece forms the cathode of the cell and is immersed in a solution containing ions of the material to be deposited. Applying a direct current reduces the ions at the cathode surface, building up a coherent layer. The anode may be soluble, replenishing the ions (as in copper refining), or inert. The amount deposited follows Faraday's laws of electrolysis, being proportional to the charge passed. Bath chemistry — including complexing agents, brighteners and levellers — together with current density, temperature and agitation determine the deposit's thickness, adhesion, grain structure and finish.

Applications

Electrodeposited coatings of chromium, nickel, zinc, tin, gold and silver provide corrosion protection, wear resistance, solderability and decoration. Copper electrodeposition forms the interconnects of printed circuit boards and integrated circuits (the damascene process), while electroforming produces moulds, foils and microcomponents. Electrophoretic deposition, a related technique, deposits charged paint or ceramic particles and is used to prime car bodies.
