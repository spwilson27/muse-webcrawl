Energy in the United Kingdom describes the production, consumption, import and export of energy and electricity in the UK. The country was a pioneer of coal-fired industrialisation and, from the 1970s, a major producer of North Sea oil and gas. Since the 1990s the system has been reshaped by market liberalisation, the decline of coal, growth of natural gas and, in the 21st century, rapid expansion of renewable generation — particularly offshore wind, in which the UK is a world leader.

Electricity supply

Coal, which supplied the majority of electricity in 1990, fell to zero when the last coal-fired power station, Ratcliffe-on-Soar, closed in September 2024. In recent years natural gas has typically supplied about a third of electricity, wind roughly a quarter to a third (occasionally the largest single source), nuclear around 13–15 per cent from a shrinking fleet of advanced gas-cooled reactors plus Sizewell B, and solar and biomass most of the remainder, alongside imports through interconnectors to France, the Netherlands, Belgium, Norway, Denmark and Ireland. Hinkley Point C, under construction in Somerset, is the first new nuclear station in a generation.

Oil and gas

North Sea production peaked around 1999–2000 and has declined since; the UK is now a net importer of both oil and gas, although domestic fields still supply a substantial share of demand. Liquefied natural gas imports and pipeline connections to Norway and continental Europe supplement domestic output. Heating remains dominated by natural gas boilers in homes, making heat decarbonisation a major policy challenge.

Policy and climate targets

The Climate Change Act 2008 committed the UK to cutting greenhouse gas emissions, and in 2019 the target was strengthened to net zero by 2050 — the first such statutory commitment by a major economy. Electricity market reform, contracts for difference for low-carbon generation, a carbon price floor, and the phase-out of unabated coal and petrol and diesel cars form the main policy instruments. Energy prices and security of supply became politically salient after wholesale gas prices spiked in 2021–22 following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, prompting temporary government price support for households.
