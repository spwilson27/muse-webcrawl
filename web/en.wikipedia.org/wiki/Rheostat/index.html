Rheostat

A rheostat is an adjustable resistor used to control current in an electrical circuit. It consists of a resistive element — commonly a coil of resistance wire wound on an insulating former, or a carbon composition track — with a movable contact (wiper) whose position determines how much of the element is included in the circuit. Unlike a potentiometer, which is normally connected as a three-terminal voltage divider, a rheostat is used as a two-terminal device in series with the load.

History and naming

The device descends from experiments by Charles Wheatstone in the 1840s, and the name, from Greek rheos (stream) and statos (standing), was coined by analogy with other instruments of the period. Early rheostats controlled telegraph and arc-lamp circuits and the speed of electric motors.

Applications

Large wire-wound rheostats were long used for dimming theatre lights, controlling the speed of DC and universal motors, and in laboratory work for varying current. Because a rheostat dissipates the removed power as heat, it is inefficient; most of its traditional roles have been taken over by electronic controls such as triac dimmers and pulse-width modulation. Rheostats remain in use in school laboratories, welding equipment and high-power applications where simplicity and robustness matter, and as current-limiting devices in battery charging.

Types

  • Rotary: resistive track arranged in an arc, adjusted by a knob.
  • Linear (slide): straight track with a sliding contact, common in teaching laboratories.
  • Liquid rheostat: electrodes immersed in an electrolyte, historically used for very large motor loads.
