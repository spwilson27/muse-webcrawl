Bandeirantes

The bandeirantes (singular bandeirante) were slavers, explorers and fortune hunters in early colonial Brazil, active mainly in the 17th and early 18th centuries. They led expeditions called bandeiras ("flags") westward from São Paulo into the interior of South America, enslaving indigenous peoples, destroying Jesuit missions, and prospecting for gold and precious stones. Their expeditions greatly extended Brazil's effective territory beyond the limits set by the Treaty of Tordesillas.

Origins

São Paulo, poor in sugar land and remote from the coast's plantation economy, developed a population of Portuguese settlers and mameluco (mixed Portuguese-indigenous) frontiersmen who organised expeditions into the sertão (backlands). The bandeiras were privately financed ventures that could number thousands of men, most of them indigenous auxiliaries.

Slave raids and the missions

In the 1620s and 1630s bandeirantes raided the Jesuit reductions of Guayrá and the Paraguay region, capturing tens of thousands of Guaraní to sell as slaves in São Paulo and driving the missions south. They also destroyed the quilombo (maroon settlement) of Palmares in 1694 under Domingos Jorge Velho.

Gold and territorial expansion

From the 1690s bandeirantes such as Fernão Dias and Bartolomeu Bueno da Silva (the "Anhanguera") discovered gold in Minas Gerais and later Goiás and Mato Grosso, triggering Brazil's gold rush. Their routes and settlements pushed Portuguese control deep into the continent, a reality later recognised in the Treaty of Madrid (1750).

Legacy

In São Paulo the bandeirantes are commemorated as founders and heroes — notably in the Monument to the Flags in Ibirapuera Park — while historians also emphasise their role in enslavement and violence against indigenous peoples.
