Muzzleloader

A flintlock muzzleloader with its loading equipment.

A muzzleloader is any firearm into which the projectile and the propellant charge are loaded from the muzzle (the forward, open end of the barrel), as opposed to a breech-loading firearm, in which a pre-assembled cartridge is inserted at the rear. Muzzleloading weapons dominated warfare and hunting from the earliest hand cannons of the 14th century until the mid-19th century, when reliable breech-loaders and metallic cartridges replaced them. Today muzzleloaders survive in historical re-enactment, target shooting and specialised hunting seasons.

Loading and firing

To load a typical muzzle-loading rifle, the shooter pours a measured charge of black powder (or a substitute) down the barrel, adds wadding or a cloth patch, seats the ball or bullet on the charge with a ramrod, and then primes the ignition system. Ignition methods developed over the centuries: the matchlock used a smouldering cord, the wheellock a spinning steel wheel against pyrite, the flintlock a flint striking steel to shower sparks into a priming pan, and finally the percussion cap, a small copper cap containing a shock-sensitive explosive placed on a nipple. Rifled barrels with patched round balls or elongated Minié balls gave muzzleloaders considerable accuracy, but loading remained slow — two to four shots per minute for a trained soldier.

Modern use

Modern muzzleloading includes traditional flintlock and percussion arms and modern "in-line" rifles using shotgun primers and saboted bullets, designed for the special muzzleloader hunting seasons held in many US states, during which hunters may take game before the general firearms season. Organisations such as the National Muzzle Loading Rifle Association promote the sport. Because antique and replica muzzleloaders are treated differently in some firearms laws, regulations vary by country. See also flintlock and black powder.
