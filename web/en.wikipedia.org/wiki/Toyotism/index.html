Toyotism

Toyotism is a term used by sociologists and economists for the system of work organisation embodied in the Toyota Production System, considered the successor regime to Fordism in the development of industrial capitalism. Where Fordism relied on mass production, large inventories and rigid assembly lines, Toyotism emphasises lean production, just-in-time supply, continuous improvement (kaizen) and flexible, team-based work.

Characteristics

The core elements of Toyotism include production pulled by customer demand rather than pushed by output targets; minimal stockpiles, with components delivered just in time; the andon system allowing any worker to stop the line when a defect is found; multi-skilled teams responsible for quality; and close, long-term integration of suppliers into the production process. Waste (muda) of every kind — overproduction, waiting, transport, defects — is systematically hunted down and eliminated.

Comparison with Fordism

Regulation-school theorists contrast Toyotism with Fordism: small-batch, diversified production replaces standardised mass output; flexible machinery and labour replace dedicated lines; and subcontracting networks replace vertical integration. Critics argue the system intensifies work — "management by stress" — and exports insecurity to suppliers and temporary workers.

Influence

From the 1980s the Toyota system, popularised in the West as "lean production" after the MIT study published in The Machine That Changed the World (1990), was adopted across the automobile industry and later in manufacturing, healthcare, software development and services generally.
