Velocity of money

The velocity of money (or velocity of circulation) is a measurement of the rate at which money is exchanged in an economy: the number of times that money is spent to buy goods and services per unit of time. Because nominal GDP equals the total value of spending, velocity can be computed as the ratio of nominal GDP to the average money stock, V = PQ / M, where PQ is nominal GDP and M is the chosen monetary aggregate (for example M1 or M2).

The equation of exchange

Velocity appears in Irving Fisher's equation of exchange, MV = PQ, where P is the price level and Q real output. In the quantity theory of money, if velocity and real output are treated as determined by non-monetary factors, the price level varies in proportion to the money supply. Monetarists such as Milton Friedman argued velocity is stable or at least predictable in the long run, while Keynesian economists emphasise that velocity moves with interest rates and liquidity preference, falling when people and firms wish to hold money.

Determinants and measurement

Velocity depends on the opportunity cost of holding money (nominal interest rates), payment technology and habits, the structure of the financial system, and expectations. It is not directly observed but calculated residually from GDP and money stock statistics, so its measured value depends on the aggregate chosen: velocity of the narrow, transactional M1 is higher than that of broad M2. In the United States, M2 velocity trended upward through the post-war decades, peaked around the late 1990s at about 2.2, and then declined markedly after the 2008 financial crisis, dropping to record lows around 2020 as money balances grew faster than spending.

Interpretation

A high velocity is read as indicating a vigorous economy with rapid turnover of money; a falling velocity suggests hoarding, weak demand, or an increased desire for liquid balances. Because of its residual character and sensitivity to financial innovation, many central banks now place less weight on velocity and monetary aggregates than they did during the monetarist era.
