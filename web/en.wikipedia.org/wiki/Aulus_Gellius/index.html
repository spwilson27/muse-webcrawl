Aulus Gellius (c. 125 – after 180 AD) was a Roman author and grammarian, educated in Athens, who is best known for his Attic Nights (Latin: Noctes Atticae), a miscellany of extracts, anecdotes, and discussions on grammar, etymology, history, philosophy, law, and antiquarian lore. The work is a valuable source for lost texts of earlier Latin authors whom Gellius quotes extensively.

Life

Little is known of Gellius's life beyond what he recounts himself. He studied rhetoric and philosophy in Rome and Athens, where his teachers included the sophist Herodes Atticus, the philosopher Taurus of Beirut, and the lexicographer Sulpicius Apollinaris. He travelled in Greece, was present at the Pythian Games, and served as a judge (iudex) in civil cases at Rome. The playful intellectual circle depicted in the Attic Nights reflects the culture of the Second Sophistic.

Attic Nights

The Attic Nights takes its name from the winter evenings Gellius spent in Attica compiling notes from his reading. It consists of twenty books (book 8 is lost except for its table of contents) comprising some 275 short chapters in no fixed order—deliberately, as Gellius explains in his preface, following the miscellaneous order of his notebooks. The chapters take the form of dialogues, dinner-table conversations, quotations with commentary, and essays on disputed questions of usage.

Gellius quotes or paraphrases a vast range of earlier writers, including Plautus, Ennius, Cato the Elder, Varro, Cicero, and the archaic legal and annalistic sources. Because many of these works are otherwise lost, the Attic Nights preserves fragments of great importance to classical scholarship. His discussions of Latin usage—such as the proper meanings of words and the distinction between archaic and classical style—influenced later grammarians.

Transmission and influence

The work was popular in late antiquity and the Middle Ages; authors such as Macrobius and John of Salisbury drew on it. The text survives in a number of manuscripts, and the first printed edition appeared in Rome in 1469. Gellius's anecdotal, curious style made the Attic Nights a model for the early modern commonplace book and miscellany.
