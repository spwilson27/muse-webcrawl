Hydraulics

Hydraulics is a technology and applied science using engineering, chemistry, and other sciences involving the mechanical properties and use of liquids. At a very basic level, hydraulics is the liquid counterpart of pneumatics, which concerns gases. Fluid mechanics provides the theoretical foundation, focusing on the applied engineering use of fluid properties.

Principles

Hydraulic power systems exploit the near-incompressibility of liquids: pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted equally in all directions (Pascal's law), so a small force on a small piston can generate a large force on a larger piston, multiplying force. Hydraulic circuits use pumps, valves, actuators (cylinders and motors), reservoirs and accumulators.

History

The word derives from the Greek hydraulikos, from hydor (water) and aulos (pipe). Ancient civilisations applied hydraulics in irrigation, aqueducts and water clocks; Ctesibius and Hero of Alexandria built water devices. Blaise Pascal formulated the hydrostatic principle in the 17th century, and Joseph Bramah patented the hydraulic press in 1795. William Armstrong developed hydraulic power networks in 19th-century Britain, supplying cranes, dock gates and lifts.

Applications

Hydraulics powers excavators, bulldozers and aircraft control surfaces; operates brakes, steering and lifts; drives presses and machine tools; and controls flow in dams, canals and water supply. Open-channel hydraulics, by contrast, studies flow with a free surface in rivers and canals, and is a major branch of civil engineering.
