The ohm (symbol: Ω, the uppercase Greek letter omega) is the unit of electrical resistance in the International System of Units (SI). It is named after German physicist Georg Ohm. Various empirically derived standard units for electrical resistance were developed in connection with early telegraphy practice, and the British Association for the Advancement of Science proposed a unit derived from existing units of mass, length and time, which proved convenient for telegraph work; the ohm as defined has been standardised internationally since the late 19th century.

Definition

One ohm is the resistance between two points of a conductor when a constant potential difference of one volt, applied to these points, produces in the conductor a current of one ampere, the conductor not being the seat of any electromotive force. Equivalently, Ω = V/A. This relationship is expressed by Ohm's law: V = IR, where V is voltage, I is current and R is resistance in ohms. The reciprocal of resistance is conductance, measured in siemens.

Realisation and history

The ohm was adopted as an international unit at the 1881 International Electrical Congress in Paris. Since the 2019 redefinition of the SI, the ohm is realised in practice using the quantum Hall effect, which links resistance to the Planck constant and the elementary charge via the von Klitzing constant. Common multiples include the kilohm (kΩ) and megohm (MΩ); the symbol may be written "ohm", and the informal name "meg" is sometimes used for a megohm in electronics.
