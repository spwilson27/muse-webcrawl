Cecil (c. 2002 – 1 July 2015) was a male African lion who lived in Hwange National Park in Zimbabwe. He was a major attraction at the park, recognisable by his black-fringed mane, and was being studied by researchers from the Wildlife Conservation Research Unit (WildCRU) of the University of Oxford as part of a long-term lion research project. Cecil wore a GPS collar fitted by the research team.

Death

On the night of 1 July 2015, Cecil was lured out of the protected area of the park onto adjacent private land, where he was shot and wounded with an arrow by Walter Palmer, an American dentist and recreational trophy hunter, working with local professional hunter Theo Bronkhorst. Cecil was tracked the following day and killed with a second shot. He was then skinned and his head removed as a trophy. Zimbabwean authorities later charged Bronkhorst and the landowner with poaching offences, alleging the hunt had no valid quota; the charges were eventually dropped. Palmer was not charged in Zimbabwe, and officials stated he would not be prosecuted.

Reaction

Cecil's killing provoked an international outcry. News of the incident spread rapidly on social media, and Palmer's dental practice in Minnesota received protests and was temporarily closed. Animal welfare organisations condemned the hunt, and several airlines announced bans on transporting big-game hunting trophies. The United States Fish and Wildlife Service placed the African lion on the threatened species list under the Endangered Species Act in December 2015, a process under way before the killing but widely associated with it in public debate.

WildCRU reported a surge in donations, which it used to expand its conservation work in Zimbabwe. The case became a prominent example in debates about trophy hunting, conservation funding, and the ethics of hunting iconic individual animals.

Legacy

Cecil's brother Jericho assumed leadership of the pride after his death and was himself mourned when he died of natural causes in 2019. Cecil's cubs were protected by the pride structure, defying expectations that a new male would kill them. The lion's name continues to be invoked in campaigns against trophy hunting and in proposed legislation, such as the CECIL Act introduced in the United States Congress.
