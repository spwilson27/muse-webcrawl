ʻAʻā

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

ʻAʻā (Hawaiian; pronounced approximately "ah-ah") is one of three basic types of lava, characterised by a rough, jagged surface of broken blocks called clinker. Together with pāhoehoe — the smooth, ropy type — and pillow lava formed underwater, it accounts for most of the lava seen in basaltic eruptions, especially those of Hawaii.

Formation

ʻAʻā forms when relatively viscous lava, partly cooled and losing dissolved gas, is torn apart as it moves. A stiff crust of cooled rock breaks into angular fragments that tumble along the front and top of the flow, producing the flow's characteristic rubble-strewn, spinose surface that is extremely difficult to walk across. ʻAʻā lavas generally move faster and are thicker than pāhoehoe flows. A single flow can change from pāhoehoe to ʻaʻā downslope as it cools and loses gas, though the reverse transformation does not occur.

Etymology and names

The Hawaiian term was introduced into the geological literature by Clarence Dutton and was adopted worldwide. Legend has it — apocryphally — that the word imitates the cries of people walking barefoot across it; more soberly, it may derive from Hawaiian ʻaʻā, "to burn, blaze", or the word for stony lava generally.
