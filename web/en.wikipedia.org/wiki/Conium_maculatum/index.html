Conium maculatum, known as poison hemlock, is a highly poisonous biennial herbaceous flowering plant in the carrot family Apiaceae, native to Europe and North Africa and now widely naturalised in North America, Australia and elsewhere. It is recognisable by its smooth, hollow, purple-spotted stems, fern-like divided leaves, and small white flowers in umbels. All parts of the plant are toxic to humans and livestock.

Toxicity

The plant contains piperidine alkaloids, principally coniine and γ-coniceine, which disrupt the neuromuscular junction and cause ascending muscular paralysis; death results from respiratory failure while the victim remains conscious. Poison hemlock is famous as the poison used in ancient Greece for executions — the philosopher Socrates was put to death by drinking a hemlock preparation in 399 BC. The plant can be mistaken for edible relatives such as wild carrot or parsley, a confusion that has caused accidental poisonings; its musty, "mousy" odour when crushed is a useful warning sign.

Ecology

Hemlock grows in damp waste ground, roadsides, ditches and riverbanks, reaching 1.5–2.5 m in its second year. It is considered an invasive weed in many regions and a hazard to grazing animals.
