Sanctions (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sanctions, in international law and policy, are coercive measures applied by one or more states or international organisations against a target state, group or individual to induce a change in behaviour, constrain capabilities or signal condemnation. They include economic sanctions (trade and financial restrictions), arms embargoes, travel bans and asset freezes, and diplomatic measures. The UN Security Council may impose binding sanctions under Chapter VII of the UN Charter; individual states and the EU maintain extensive autonomous regimes.

History and effectiveness

Modern sanctions practice grew from League of Nations measures against Italy (1935) to the comprehensive embargoes of the Cold War and 1990s (Iraq, Yugoslavia, Haiti) — whose humanitarian costs prompted a shift towards "smart" or targeted sanctions aimed at elites and specific sectors. Landmark cases include the anti-apartheid campaign against South Africa, Iran sanctions culminating in the 2015 nuclear deal, North Korea's comprehensive UN regime, and the unprecedented financial and energy measures against Russia after 2022. Empirical research finds sanctions succeed in a minority-to-substantial share of cases depending on definitions and goals; they reliably impose costs and stigmatise, less reliably compel. Evasion networks, third-country intermediaries and humanitarian spillovers are standing policy problems, as is the extraterritorial reach of US "secondary sanctions".
