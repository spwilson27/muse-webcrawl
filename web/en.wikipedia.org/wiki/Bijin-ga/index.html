Bijin-ga

Bijin-ga (美人画, "pictures of beautiful women") is a generic term for pictures of beautiful women in Japanese art, used especially of woodblock prints of the ukiyo-e genre. Along with scenes of kabuki actors, bijin-ga formed one of the principal subjects of ukiyo-e from the Edo period through the Meiji period and beyond.

Bijin-ga idealised the fashions and manners of contemporary women.

History

The genre's foundations were laid by Hishikawa Moronobu in the late seventeenth century. Kaigetsudō school artists produced stately, full-length beauties in the early 1700s; Suzuki Harunobu brought delicate colour printing to the subject in the 1760s; and Kitagawa Utamaro, the genre's most famous master, popularised the ōkubi-e close-up "large-head picture" in the 1790s, capturing expressions and coiffures with unmatched refinement. Torii Kiyonaga favoured tall, graceful figures in outdoor settings.

Themes and later development

Bijin-ga rarely portrayed individuals so much as ideals of beauty, fashion and deportment — courtesans of the Yoshiwara, teahouse waitresses, and townswomen shown at their toilette, reading, or enjoying the seasons. In the Meiji era and the shin-hanga ("new prints") movement of the early twentieth century, artists such as Tsukioka Yoshitoshi, Toyohara Kunichika and Hashiguchi Goyō revived and modernised the genre.
