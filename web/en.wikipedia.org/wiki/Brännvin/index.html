Brännvin is Sweden's traditional distilled spirit.

Brännvin (Swedish for "burnt wine") is a general Swedish term for distilled alcoholic spirits made from grain or potatoes. The name derives from the early method of production, from the German Branntwein, and historically covered everything from rough farmhouse moonshine to refined products. Flavoured varieties, and the unflavoured spirit drunk as snaps, are central to Swedish festive drinking culture.

History

Distillation reached Sweden in the late 15th century, initially for gunpowder and medicine; monks and apothecaries produced "burnt wine" long before it became a popular drink. By the 17th and 18th centuries household distillation was widespread, and the Swedish crown alternately encouraged, taxed and banned it — Gustav III established a crown monopoly on production in 1775, provoking widespread illegal distilling. In the 19th century improved stills (including the column still) industrialised production, while the temperance movement and, from 1919 to 1955, a ration-book system (motbok) restricted consumption. Since 1955 retail sales have been channelled through the state monopoly Systembolaget.

Styles

Plain brännvin is a clear spirit similar to vodka, distilled from grain or potato mash and sometimes charcoal-filtered. It is flavoured with spices and herbs to make kryddat brännvin (spiced brännvin) — caraway, dill, wormwood, St John's wort and elderflower are traditional — and the strongly spiced Scandinavian form is known across the Nordic countries as akvavit. Famous Swedish brands include O.P. Anderson (an akvavit first made in 1891), Skåne Akvavit and the wormwood-flavoured Bäska Droppar.

Cultural role

Brännvin is drunk chilled as snaps at the Swedish festive table — at Midsummer, crayfish parties in August, and Christmas — accompanied by drinking songs (snapsvisor), the best known being "Helan går". A "helan" is the first full glass; the "halvan" is the second.
