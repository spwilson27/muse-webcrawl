Lotto

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lotto is a form of lottery in which players choose a set of numbers from a defined pool and win prizes according to how many of their numbers match those drawn at random. The term derives from the Italian lotto, and lotto-style games are the most common format of national and multi-jurisdictional lotteries worldwide, including games such as Powerball and Mega Millions in the United States and the EuroMillions in Europe.

Format

In a typical lotto game, players select six numbers from 1 to 49, or use a two-matrix format (for example five numbers from a main pool plus one or two bonus numbers from a separate pool). Draws are held one or more times a week. The jackpot is awarded for matching all main numbers and can "roll over" to subsequent draws when unwon, sometimes growing to hundreds of millions or billions of dollars before being capped or won. Lower prize tiers reward partial matches, often as fixed amounts or shares of a prize pool funded by a proportion of ticket sales.

History

The modern lotto traces its origins to Genoa in the sixteenth century, where betting on the drawing of council members evolved into a numbers game. State lotteries spread across Europe and later North America; in the United States, state-run lotto games proliferated after New Hampshire introduced a modern state lottery in 1964. Multi-state compacts, formed in the 1980s and 1990s, enabled the giant jackpots that characterise the modern game.

Odds and criticism

The odds of winning a typical national lotto jackpot are extremely low — on the order of 1 in 300 million for large two-matrix games — and lotteries are frequently criticised as a regressive form of revenue, although proponents note that participation is voluntary and proceeds commonly fund education and other public services.
