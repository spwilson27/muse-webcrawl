French Geodesic Mission to the Equator

The French Geodesic Mission to the Equator was an 18th-century scientific expedition to what is now Ecuador, conducted between 1735 and 1744 under the auspices of the French Academy of Sciences. Its purpose was to measure the length of a degree of latitude at the equator, in order to settle the debate over the shape of the Earth: whether it was flattened at the poles, as Newtonian theory predicted, or elongated, as Cartesian measurements in France suggested.

The expedition

The expedition included the astronomers and mathematicians Pierre Bouguer, Charles Marie de La Condamine and Louis Godin, along with the botanist Joseph de Jussieu and other specialists. Working in the Viceroyalty of Peru near Quito, they spent years triangulating a chain of measurements across the Andes and making astronomical observations to determine the length of three degrees of latitude along the meridian. A companion mission to Lapland, led by Pierre Louis Maupertuis, measured a degree nearer the pole.

Results and legacy

The equatorial degree proved shorter than the Lapland degree, confirming that the Earth is an oblate spheroid flattened at the poles. The expedition also produced landmark work in geodesy, astronomy, cartography and natural history, and its hardships — disease, accidents, hostile terrain and internal quarrels — became famous. The measurements formed a basis for the definition of the metre. The modern country of Ecuador takes its name from the equator whose arc the mission measured, and the "Middle of the World" monument near Quito commemorates the work.
