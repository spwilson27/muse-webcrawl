Antarctic oscillation

The Antarctic oscillation (AAO), also known as the Southern Annular Mode (SAM), is the dominant pattern of climate variability in the extratropical Southern Hemisphere. It describes the north–south movement of the belt of westerly winds that circles Antarctica, and is defined by pressure anomalies of opposite sign over Antarctica and the mid-latitudes at roughly 40–50°S.

The AAO is the southern counterpart of the Northern Annular Mode (NAM), associated with the Arctic oscillation. It varies on timescales from weeks to decades and strongly influences weather in Australia, New Zealand, southern South America and southern Africa.

Phases

In its positive phase, the westerly wind belt contracts toward Antarctica: pressure is lower than normal over the pole and higher than normal over the mid-latitudes. The stronger, poleward-shifted westerlies tend to bring drier conditions to parts of southern Australia, South Africa and Patagonia, cooler conditions over Antarctica's interior, and warmer conditions over the Antarctic Peninsula. In its negative phase, the wind belt expands equatorward, bringing the storm track — and its rainfall — more often over the mid-latitude continents.

Measurement

The AAO index is commonly computed from the difference in zonal-mean sea-level pressure between 40°S and 65°S, or as the leading principal component of Southern Hemisphere geopotential height anomalies. A station-based index derived from pressure observations at Antarctic and mid-latitude stations extends the record back through the twentieth century.

Trends and causes

Observations show a trend toward the positive phase of the SAM during the late twentieth century, particularly in austral summer. Research attributes much of this trend to stratospheric ozone depletion over Antarctica, with a contribution from increasing greenhouse gas concentrations. The trend has been linked to observed rainfall declines in south-western and south-eastern Australia, warming of the Antarctic Peninsula, and cooling of the Antarctic interior. Climate models project that ozone recovery may partly offset the greenhouse-gas-driven trend during the twenty-first century.
