Stolon

In biology, stolons (from Latin stolō, "branch, shoot, scion") are horizontal connections between organisms or parts of an organism. In plants, a stolon is a horizontal stem that grows along the surface of the ground — commonly called a runner — rooting at its nodes and giving rise to new, genetically identical plants (clones). In fungi and in colonial animals, analogous structures perform similar functions of spread and asexual propagation.

In plants

The classic example is the cultivated strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa), which produces long above-ground runners that root where their nodes touch moist soil, forming daughter plants that are then severed in propagation. Many grasses (including Bermuda grass and creeping bentgrass), spider plants, and aquatic plants such as Vallisneria spread in the same way. Stolons differ from rhizomes in growing above or on the surface and having long internodes; potatoes, by contrast, form at the tips of underground stolons. Stolons let a plant colonise open ground quickly and are a common cause of the invasiveness of garden and turf species.

In fungi and animals

In the bread mould Rhizopus stolonifer, stolons are horizontal hyphae that arch between groups of rhizoids and sporangiophores, spreading the colony across its substrate. Colonial animals such as certain hydrozoans, bryozoans, and tunicates also grow by stolons: hollow tubes through which tissue and circulation extend, budding new zooids at intervals. In both kingdoms the stolon is a clonal growth device rather than a sexual structure.

Ecology and horticulture

Stoloniferous growth trades mobility and rapid coverage against the vulnerability of the connecting stem. Horticulturists remove strawberry runners to direct energy into fruiting, or peg them down to produce planting stock. Ecologists regard stolon production as part of the "guerrilla" growth strategy, in which ramets forage widely through a habitat, contrasted with the compact "phalanx" strategy of tussock-forming plants.
