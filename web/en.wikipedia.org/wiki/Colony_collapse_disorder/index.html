Colony collapse disorder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


CCD is marked by the sudden disappearance of a hive's worker bees.

Colony collapse disorder (CCD) is an abnormal phenomenon that occurs when the majority of worker bees in a honey bee colony disappear, leaving behind a queen, plenty of food, and a few nurse bees to care for the remaining immature bees. Such disappearances have occurred sporadically throughout the history of apiculture, but the term was coined after a drastic rise in disappearances of western honey bee colonies in North America in late 2006.

Symptoms

In collapsed colonies, adult bees are simply absent — few or no dead bees are found in or near the hive. The queen, brood, and food stores remain, and the abandoned stores are not immediately robbed by other bees or hive pests, as would normally happen. Beekeepers have reported losses of 30–90 percent of colonies in affected operations.

Causes

No single cause has been established. Research implicates a combination of stressors: the parasitic mite Varroa destructor and the viruses it transmits (notably deformed wing virus), other pathogens such as Nosema fungi, pesticide exposure (with neonicotinoid insecticides attracting particular scrutiny and partial restrictions in the European Union), poor nutrition from monoculture forage, and the stresses of migratory beekeeping. Israeli acute paralysis virus was identified in many collapsed colonies in early studies but is no longer considered the sole or primary cause.

Impact

Because honey bees pollinate a large share of the world's food crops — including almonds, apples, and berries — CCD raised widespread alarm about food security and prompted increased research funding and monitoring of pollinator health. Annual colony losses in the United States have remained elevated since CCD appeared, though the specific pattern of sudden disappearance has become less frequent than the broader problem of high overwinter mortality.
