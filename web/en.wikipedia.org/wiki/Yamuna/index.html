The Yamuna (Hindi: यमुना, pronounced [jəmʊnaː]) is the second-largest tributary of the Ganges by discharge and the longest tributary in India. Originating from the Yamunotri Glacier at a height of about 4,421 m (14,505 ft) on the southwestern slopes of Bandarpunch peaks in the Lower Himalaya in Uttarakhand, it travels a total length of 1,376 kilometres (855 mi) and has a drainage system of 366,223 square kilometres (141,399 sq mi), about 40 percent of the entire Ganges Basin.

The Yamuna crosses several states—Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, Haryana, Delhi, and Uttar Pradesh—and merges with the Ganges at the Triveni Sangam in Prayagraj, a site of great religious significance in Hinduism. Along its banks stand some of India's most famous cities and monuments, including Delhi, the national capital, and Agra, where the Taj Mahal was built on its right bank.

Course

The river rises at the Yamunotri Glacier in the Garhwal Himalayas. It descends rapidly through mountain valleys, passing the temple town of Yamunotri, one of the four sites of the Char Dham pilgrimage. Near Dakpathar in Uttarakhand the river is impounded by a barrage, and the Tons River—its largest tributary in the mountains—joins it near Kalsi.

The Yamuna enters the Indo-Gangetic Plain near Paonta Sahib in Himachal Pradesh and flows south along the border of Haryana and Uttar Pradesh. It passes through Delhi, where much of its water is diverted into canals, and continues past Mathura and Vrindavan, cities closely associated with the Hindu god Krishna. It then flows by Agra and Etawah, receives its largest southern tributary, the Chambal, and later the Sindh, Betwa, and Ken rivers, before finally merging with the Ganges at Prayagraj.

Tributaries

  • Tons – the largest tributary by volume in the upper reaches
  • Chambal – the largest tributary overall, joining near Etawah
  • Hindon – flows through the industrial belt west of Delhi
  • Sindh, Betwa, and Ken – southern tributaries from the Vindhyan and Bundelkhand regions
  • Giri and Asan – smaller Himalayan tributaries

Religious significance

In Hindu tradition the Yamuna is personified as the goddess Yamuna, sister of Yama, the god of death, and daughter of the sun god Surya. Bathing in the river is believed to cleanse sins and free the devotee from the fear of death. The river is intimately connected with the legends of Krishna, who is said to have spent his childhood on its banks at Mathura and Vrindavan, subduing the serpent Kaliya in its waters. The confluence with the Ganges and the mythical Saraswati at Prayagraj, the Triveni Sangam, is the site of the Kumbh Mela, the largest religious gathering in the world.

History

The Yamuna valley has been inhabited since ancient times; the region around the river formed part of the early Vedic heartland, and the city of Hastinapura, capital of the Kuru kingdom in the Mahabharata tradition, stood near its banks. Delhi's successive cities grew up along the river, whose water sustained the Mughal capital at Agra and later Shahjahanabad. During the colonial period the British constructed major canal systems, including the Eastern and Western Yamuna Canals and the Agra Canal, to irrigate the doab between the Yamuna and the Ganges.

Pollution

The Yamuna is one of the most polluted rivers in the world, especially around New Delhi, which contributes the great majority of the pollution load while accounting for only a small fraction of the river's length. Untreated sewage, industrial effluents, and solid waste have reduced dissolved oxygen to near zero in the Delhi stretch, rendering the water unfit even for bathing. Successive government programmes, including the Yamuna Action Plan supported by Japanese aid since the 1990s, have attempted to restore the river, but water quality remains severely degraded, and the river's environmental flow is a continuing subject of dispute among the basin states.
