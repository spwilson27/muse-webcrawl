Sympathetic magic, also called imitative magic, is a type of magic based on imitation or correspondence, founded on the belief that a person, object, or event can be magically affected through an image, a simulacrum, or something once connected with it. The term is principally associated with the anthropologist Sir James George Frazer, who systematised it in The Golden Bough (1890).

Frazer's two laws

Frazer proposed that sympathetic magic rests on two principles of thought:

  • The law of similarity — "like produces like": an effect resembles its cause, so a magician can produce any desired effect by imitating it. Frazer called magic based on this law homeopathic or imitative magic. Examples include injuring an effigy of an enemy in order to injure the enemy, and rain dances that imitate thunder and falling water.
  • The law of contagion — things that have once been in contact remain in contact at a distance: whatever is done to an object once associated with a person (hair, nail clippings, clothing) will affect that person. Frazer called this contagious magic.

Frazer regarded magic as a "false science" — a mistaken application of the association of ideas — that preceded religion in cultural evolution, a sequence later anthropologists have largely rejected even while retaining his descriptive categories.

Examples across cultures

Practices explained by sympathetic magic are found worldwide: the use of relics of saints (contagion), healing charms shaped like the afflicted body part, hunting rituals in which the hunt is mimed beforehand, and the treatment of jaundice with yellow objects in European folk medicine. Votive offerings in the shape of limbs at healing shrines are a classic case of the law of similarity. Émile Durkheim, Marcel Mauss, and later anthropologists debated and refined the concept, and psychologists have shown that intuitions resembling the two laws persist even in modern, educated populations.
