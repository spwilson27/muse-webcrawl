IPv4 address exhaustion

IPv4 address exhaustion is the depletion of the pool of unallocated IPv4 addresses. Because the original Internet architecture had about 4.3 billion addresses (232), and because IP addresses are needed by every connected device, the pool of free addresses ran out as the Internet grew. IANA's primary address pool was exhausted on 3 February 2011, and the five regional Internet registries (RIRs) exhausted their free pools between 2011 and 2020.

Causes

Address depletion was accelerated by the proliferation of Internet-connected devices, always-on broadband connections, and mobile devices, and by historically inefficient allocation under the early classful addressing system, which granted very large blocks to single organizations.

Mitigations

Exhaustion was anticipated from the 1990s and slowed by classless inter-domain routing (CIDR), dynamic address allocation, and especially network address translation (NAT), which lets many devices share a single public address. After RIR exhaustion, organizations increasingly bought addresses on a transfer market, where prices per address rose from a few dollars to tens of dollars.

Long-term solution

The long-term solution is migration to IPv6, which provides 2128 addresses. Deployment has proceeded gradually since the 2010s, with IPv4 and IPv6 operating in parallel (dual stack) on most networks.
