Finite element method

The finite element method (FEM) is a popular method for numerically solving differential equations arising in engineering and mathematical modeling. Typical problem areas include structural analysis, heat transfer, fluid flow, mass transport, and electromagnetic potential. FEM subdivides a large system into smaller, simpler parts called finite elements, connected at points called nodes; the governing equations over each element are approximated — usually by polynomials — and assembled into a large system of algebraic equations whose solution approximates the solution of the original problem.

Method

The domain is discretised into a mesh of elements (triangles and quadrilaterals in two dimensions; tetrahedra and hexahedra in three). Within each element, the unknown field is interpolated from nodal values using shape functions. The weak (variational) form of the governing equations — obtained by multiplying by test functions and integrating, as in Galerkin's method — is applied element by element, and the elemental stiffness matrices and load vectors are assembled into a global system, typically sparse. Boundary conditions are imposed and the system is solved for the nodal unknowns; stresses, fluxes, and other derived quantities follow by differentiation.

Accuracy generally improves with mesh refinement and higher-order elements, at the cost of more computation. Error estimation and adaptive refinement concentrate elements where gradients are steep. Variants and extensions include mixed formulations, discontinuous Galerkin methods, and the extended finite element method (XFEM) for cracks.

History

The method's roots lie in the work of Alexander Hrennikoff (1941) and Richard Courant (1943), and in structural engineering practice of the 1950s, notably the 1956 paper by Turner, Clough, Martin, and Topp on stiffness analysis of aircraft structures. Ray Clough coined the term "finite element" in 1960. Olgierd Zienkiewicz and colleagues at Swansea established the method's mathematical generality. As computing power grew, commercial codes — NASTRAN (developed for NASA in the 1960s), ANSYS, Abaqus, and others — made FEM a standard design tool, and finite element analysis is now routine across aerospace, automotive, civil, and biomedical engineering.

Applications and limitations

FEM is used to predict stress, vibration, fatigue, heat flow, fluid–structure interaction, crash behaviour, and electromagnetic fields before physical prototypes are built. Its results are approximations whose reliability depends on the mesh, material models, and boundary conditions; verification and validation against experiments remain essential engineering practice.
