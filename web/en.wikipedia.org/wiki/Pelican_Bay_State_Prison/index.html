Pelican Bay State Prison (PBSP) is a maximum-security prison in Del Norte County, California, near Crescent City, operated by the California Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation. Opened in 1989, it was designed — and became nationally known — as one of the first purpose-built "supermax" facilities in the United States, centred on a Security Housing Unit (SHU) in which prisoners were held in prolonged solitary confinement.

History

Pelican Bay opened in December 1989 on roughly 275 acres of land a few miles from the Oregon border. It was built during California's prison construction boom to house inmates the state classified as its most dangerous, including members of prison gangs, and at its opening it received prisoners transferred from other institutions across the state.

The prison consists of two parts: a general-population maximum-security facility and the SHU, a windowless, X-shaped pod complex where inmates were confined to concrete cells for about 22½ hours a day, exercising alone in small concrete yards. The SHU's conditions — near-total isolation, sensory monotony, and indeterminate terms based on gang "validation" — drew repeated legal challenges and national scrutiny from the 1990s onward.

Litigation and reforms

In Madrid v. Gomez (1995), a federal court found that conditions at Pelican Bay inflicted cruel and unusual punishment on mentally ill prisoners, though it declined to shut the SHU. Two decades later, the class-action lawsuit Ashker v. Governor of California, brought by SHU prisoners who had organised hunger strikes in 2011 and 2013, was settled in 2015. The settlement ended indeterminate SHU confinement based solely on gang affiliation, dramatically reduced the SHU population, and moved most long-term SHU prisoners into restricted-custody general population settings.

Hunger strikes

In July and September 2011, and again in July 2013, prisoners in the Pelican Bay SHU launched hunger strikes protesting long-term solitary confinement. The 2013 strike spread across California prisons, with participation peaking at roughly 30,000 prisoners, and it helped drive legislative hearings and the eventual Ashker settlement.
