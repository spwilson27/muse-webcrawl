Structuralism

Structuralism
Key figuresFerdinand de Saussure, Claude Lévi-Strauss, Roland Barthes, Roman Jakobson
FieldsLinguistics, anthropology, literary theory, philosophy
Succeeded byPost-structuralism

Structuralism is an intellectual movement and methodology which holds that elements of human culture must be understood in terms of their relationship to a broader, overarching system or structure. It works to uncover the structures that underlie all the things that humans do, think, perceive, and feel.

Origins

Structuralism grew out of the linguistic theory of Ferdinand de Saussure, whose Course in General Linguistics (1916) argued that a language is a system of signs whose meanings arise from differences among them rather than from any natural link between word and thing. Roman Jakobson and the Prague School developed these ideas, which flourished in France in the 1950s and 1960s.

Applications

Claude Lévi-Strauss applied structural analysis to kinship and myth in anthropology, arguing that myths worldwide share common underlying structures built from binary oppositions. Roland Barthes extended the approach to literature and popular culture. Structuralism's emphasis on systems over individual subjects was later criticised and transformed by post-structuralist thinkers such as Jacques Derrida and Michel Foucault.
