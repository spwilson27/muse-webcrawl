Short sale (real estate)

A short sale in real estate is a sale of property for less than the amount owed on the mortgage, in which the lender agrees to accept the sale proceeds in settlement of the debt. Short sales typically occur when a homeowner, facing financial hardship and often negative equity, wishes to avoid foreclosure; the lender may prefer a short sale because it can recover more than through foreclosure proceedings.

Process

The seller lists the property and accepts an offer, but the sale is contingent on the lender's approval. The homeowner submits a hardship letter, financial statements, and the purchase offer; the lender orders an appraisal or broker price opinion and reviews the package, a process that historically took months. The lender may approve, reject, or counter, and may require the seller to sign a promissory note for part of the deficiency or may forgive it — forgiven debt sometimes having tax consequences.

Effects

For the seller, a completed short sale generally damages credit less than a foreclosure and may allow a sooner return to homeownership. For buyers, short sales can offer below-market prices but involve long, uncertain timelines. Short sales surged in the United States after the 2007–2008 housing crisis, prompting government programmes such as HAFA to standardise the process. Junior lien holders (second mortgages, home-equity lines) must also agree, which frequently complicates or blocks deals.
