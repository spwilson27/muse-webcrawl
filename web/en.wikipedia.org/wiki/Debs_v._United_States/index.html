Debs v. United States, 249 U.S. 211 (1919), was a United States Supreme Court decision upholding the conviction of Eugene V. Debs, the Socialist Party leader, under the Espionage Act of 1917 for a speech praising draft resisters and opposing American participation in the First World War.

Background

On June 16, 1918, Debs delivered an anti-war speech at Canton, Ohio, in which he praised comrades imprisoned for obstructing the draft and denounced the war as a capitalist enterprise. He was arrested, tried, and sentenced to ten years in federal prison. Debs argued that the Espionage Act violated the First Amendment's guarantee of freedom of speech.

Decision

Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., writing for a unanimous Court, upheld the conviction. Applying the reasoning of Schenck v. United States, decided a week earlier and the source of the "clear and present danger" test, the Court held that the natural and intended effect of Debs's speech was to obstruct recruiting, and that the First Amendment did not protect speech used as part of such an effort.

Aftermath

Debs ran for President in 1920 from his cell at the Atlanta Federal Penitentiary, receiving over 900,000 votes. President Warren G. Harding commuted his sentence in December 1921. The case is studied as part of the era of wartime speech prosecutions; the Court's doctrine was substantially liberalised fifty years later in Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), which protects advocacy short of incitement to imminent lawless action.
