Dysthymia, also known as persistent depressive disorder in the DSM-5, is a mood disorder consisting of a chronic, low-grade depressed mood lasting for at least two years in adults. Its symptoms are similar to those of major depression but are generally milder and longer-lasting; because of its chronicity it can seriously impair functioning and quality of life.

Signs and symptoms

Alongside depressed mood present most of the day, more days than not, sufferers commonly experience poor appetite or overeating, insomnia or hypersomnia, low energy or fatigue, low self-esteem, poor concentration and feelings of hopelessness. People with dysthymia may have episodes of major depression superimposed on the chronic low mood, a pattern called "double depression". Onset is often insidious and early, in childhood, adolescence or early adulthood, and sufferers and those around them may come to regard the low mood as simply their personality.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires the chronic course criterion — symptoms for at least two years without a break of more than two months — and exclusion of other causes such as hypothyroidism or substance use. The DSM-5 merged dysthymia with chronic major depression under the single heading of persistent depressive disorder, with specifiers distinguishing pure dysthymic syndrome from persistent major depressive episodes.

Treatment

Dysthymia responds to the same treatments as major depression. Antidepressant medications, particularly SSRIs, are effective, and psychotherapies such as cognitive behavioural therapy — including the Cognitive Behavioral Analysis System of Psychotherapy (CBASP), developed specifically for chronic depression — are recommended. Combined medication and psychotherapy often performs better than either alone for chronic forms of depression.
