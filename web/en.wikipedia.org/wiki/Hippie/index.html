Hippie

A hippie is a member of the counterculture movement that emerged in the United States in the mid-1960s and spread worldwide. Hippies created their own communities, listened to psychedelic rock, embraced communal living and non-violence, experimented with cannabis and psychedelic drugs, and protested the Vietnam War. The movement's centers included San Francisco's Haight-Ashbury, whose "Summer of Love" in 1967 drew perhaps 100,000 young people.

Origins and beliefs

The word derives from "hip" (aware), via the Beat Generation's "hipsters." Hippie philosophy drew on Eastern religions and spirituality, anarchist and pacifist thought, environmentalism, and the psychedelic advocacy of figures such as Timothy Leary. Cultural historian Theodore Roszak analyzed the movement in The Making of a Counter Culture (1969). Long hair, colorful and second-hand clothing, and slogans like "make love, not war" and "flower power" became its visual language.

Decline and legacy

By the early 1970s the movement had faded amid commercialization, drug casualties, and disillusionment sealed by events such as the Altamont Free Concert in December 1969. Its legacy persists in environmentalism, organic food, festival culture, New Age and modern pagan movements such as modern Druidry and Heathenry's wider milieu, and in the ethos of the early personal-computing industry in California.
