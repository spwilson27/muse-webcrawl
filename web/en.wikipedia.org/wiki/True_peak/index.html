True peak

Inter-sample peaks can exceed the highest sample value

In digital audio, a true peak is the maximum absolute level of a signal's waveform in the continuous (analogue-equivalent) domain, as opposed to the sample peak, which is merely the largest individual sample value. Because the waveform reconstructed between samples can rise above any actual sample, true peaks — also called inter-sample peaks — can exceed 0 dBFS even when no sample does.

Measurement and standards

True peak levels are estimated by oversampling the signal, typically by at least a factor of four, as specified in the ITU-R BS.1770 standard for loudness measurement. The unit used is dBTP (decibels true peak). Broadcast delivery specifications such as EBU R 128 and ATSC A/85 require audio to be normalised by loudness while keeping true peaks at or below a ceiling, commonly −1 dBTP, to avoid clipping in digital-to-analogue converters and in lossy codecs, which can shift peak levels further.

Why it matters

A master that peaks exactly at 0 dBFS in sample terms may clip audibly when converted to analogue or encoded to formats such as MP3 or AAC. True-peak limiting in mastering therefore applies a ceiling below full scale and accounts for the reconstructed waveform, and streaming services use true peak measurements in their loudness normalisation.
