The abolition of monarchy is the ending of monarchical rule in a state, whether by revolution, military coup, referendum, legislation or forced abdication, and its replacement by a republic or another form of government. Once the nearly universal form of government, monarchy has been abolished in most of the world over the past three centuries, most dramatically in the upheavals following the two world wars; about forty sovereign monarchies survive today.

Methods

Monarchies have been ended by several routes. Revolution destroyed the monarchies of France (1792), Russia (1917) and Iran (1979), often violently. Defeat in war brought down the German, Austrian, Ottoman and many smaller thrones in 1918. Referendums abolished the monarchies of Italy (1946), Greece (1974) and — unsuccessfully for republicans — have been held in Australia (1999). Constitutional or parliamentary action ended the monarchy in Nepal (abolished by the Constituent Assembly in 2008) and in Barbados (which became a republic in 2021 by legislation, retaining the same head of government). In other cases rulers abdicated under pressure, as in Spain in 1931 and Egypt in 1952.

History

Early precedents include the brief English republic of 1649–1660 and the American Revolution. The French Revolution and the nineteenth-century national movements undermined dynastic legitimacy; the First World War eliminated the Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires, and the Second World War and decolonisation ended most remaining European and colonial monarchies, including those of Italy, Romania, Bulgaria, Yugoslavia, Egypt, Iraq, Yemen, Libya, Laos and Ethiopia (1974). Nepal's abolition in 2008 is the most recent case of a long-established monarchy being ended.

Current debates

Republican movements remain active in several surviving constitutional monarchies, including the United Kingdom, Spain, the Netherlands, Sweden, Australia and other Commonwealth realms, while monarchist sentiment has produced occasional restorations — notably Spain in 1975 — and continuing pretenders elsewhere.
