Law of supply and demand

The law of supply and demand is a fundamental principle of microeconomics describing how the price of a good is determined by the relationship between its availability (supply) and the desire of buyers for it (demand). Other things equal, when supply exceeds demand the price tends to fall, and when demand exceeds supply the price tends to rise, until the market reaches an equilibrium where quantity supplied equals quantity demanded.

The supply and demand curves intersect at the equilibrium price.

The model

The law of demand states that, all else equal, the quantity demanded of a good falls as its price rises; the law of supply states that the quantity supplied rises as price rises. Plotted together, the downward-sloping demand curve and upward-sloping supply curve cross at the market-clearing equilibrium. Shifts in either curve — caused by changes in income, input costs, technology, taxes, or expectations — move the equilibrium price and quantity.

History

Elements of the theory appear in early writers, but its modern diagrammatic form is associated with Alfred Marshall's Principles of Economics (1890), which popularised the crossed-curve diagram and the metaphor of the two blades of a pair of scissors.
