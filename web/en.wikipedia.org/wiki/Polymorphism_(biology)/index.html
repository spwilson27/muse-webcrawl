Polymorphism (biology)

In biology, polymorphism is the occurrence of two or more clearly different morphs or forms, also referred to as alternative phenotypes, in the population of a species. To be classified as such, morphs must occupy the same habitat at the same time and belong to a panmictic population (one with random mating). Polymorphism is maintained by a balance of selective forces and is common in nature, where it relates to biodiversity, genetic variation, and adaptation.

Terminology

Polymorphism was originally used to describe hidden genetic differences that manifest in different forms, whether or not the differences were visible. In modern usage, visible differences are usually included because they reflect underlying genetic variation. When only two forms occur, the situation is called dimorphism; sexual dimorphism, in which males and females differ, is a special and very common case. The term contrasts with monomorphism, in which only one form exists, and with continuous variation, in which traits grade insensibly from one extreme to another.

A distinction is often drawn between genetic polymorphism, in which the morphs are determined by alleles at one or a few loci, and polyphenism, in which the same genotype produces different phenotypes in response to environmental cues, such as the seasonal forms of some butterflies or the caste system of social insects determined by diet.

Mechanisms

Polymorphisms are maintained by several mechanisms, including:

  • Balancing selection, in which heterozygotes have higher fitness than either homozygote (heterozygote advantage), as with sickle-cell trait and malaria resistance in humans.
  • Frequency-dependent selection, where a morph's fitness declines as it becomes more common — for example, predators forming search images for common prey types.
  • Disruptive selection in heterogeneous environments, favouring different morphs in different microhabitats.
  • Neutral polymorphism, transient variation not subject to selection.

Examples

The classic example is industrial melanism in the peppered moth (Biston betularia), whose dark morph rose in frequency in soot-polluted British woodlands during the nineteenth century and declined again after clean-air legislation. Other well-known cases include the shell colour and banding patterns of the grove snail Cepaea nemoralis, the multiple female morphs of the common blue butterfly, blood group systems in humans, and the alternative male mating strategies (territorial, satellite, and female-mimic) of the ruff.

Genetic polymorphism

In genetics, polymorphism refers to the presence of two or more alleles at a locus, each at an appreciable frequency (conventionally above 1%), in a population. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are the most common form of genetic variation in genomes and are widely used as markers in genome-wide association studies.
