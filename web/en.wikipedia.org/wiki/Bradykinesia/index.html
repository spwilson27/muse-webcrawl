Bradykinesia

SpecialtyNeurology
SymptomsSlowness of movement, with progressive reduction in speed and amplitude of repetitive actions
Associated conditionsParkinson's disease and other parkinsonian syndromes
EtymologyGreek bradys "slow" + kinesis "motion"

Finger-tapping tests reveal the decrement characteristic of bradykinesia.

Bradykinesia means slowness of movement and is one of the cardinal motor features of Parkinson's disease and other parkinsonian disorders. In its full clinical sense it is not mere slowness: it combines delayed initiation of movement, reduced speed and amplitude, and a characteristic progressive decrement — movements become smaller and slower as they are repeated, sometimes halting altogether. Bradykinesia must be distinguished from akinesia (absence or poverty of movement) and hypokinesia (reduced amplitude), though the terms overlap in everyday use.

Clinical features

Bradykinesia shows itself in everyday tasks: slow, shuffling gait with short steps; difficulty buttoning clothes or using cutlery; handwriting that trails off into tiny script (micrographia); a masked, expressionless face (hypomimia); soft, monotonous speech; and reduced blinking and arm swing. Examiners test for it by asking the patient to tap fingers or thumb, open and close the hand, or tap the foot as fast and as fully as possible, watching for the decrement in speed and amplitude that separates true bradykinesia from the uniform slowness of weakness, depression or hypothyroidism.

Causes and mechanism

The classic cause is loss of dopamine-producing neurones in the substantia nigra, which disrupts the basal ganglia circuits that energise and scale voluntary movement. Bradykinesia occurs in idiopathic Parkinson's disease, in drug-induced parkinsonism from dopamine-blocking drugs, in vascular parkinsonism, and in atypical parkinsonian syndromes such as multiple system atrophy and progressive supranuclear palsy. Under modern diagnostic criteria, bradykinesia (with the decrement feature) is the obligatory motor sign for a diagnosis of parkinsonism.

Treatment

Bradykinesia usually responds to dopaminergic therapy, principally levodopa, often with dopamine agonists or MAO-B inhibitors; deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus can also improve it. Exercise and physiotherapy help maintain mobility, though the sign tends to progress over time and correlates strongly with overall disability and quality of life.
