Centurion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

This article is about the Roman officer. For other uses, see Centurion (disambiguation).

A centurion (Latin: centurio; Greek: ἑκατόνταρχος, hekatontarchos) was a professional officer of the Roman army after the Marian reforms of 107 BC. Centurions commanded a centuria, the smallest unit of a Roman legion, nominally about 80–100 men. They formed the backbone of the legion, providing discipline, leadership and continuity of experience, and could rise to senior ranks themselves.

Role and organisation

A legion contained 59 or 60 centurions, one for each century in its ten cohorts. Centurions were ranked by the seniority of their cohort and century; the senior centurion of the first cohort, the primus pilus ("first spear"), was the most senior centurion in the legion, attended the general's councils, and on completing his year in post could enter the equestrian order. Junior officers under the centurion included the optio (his deputy), the signifer (standard-bearer) and the tesserarius (officer of the watch).

Selection and career

Centurions were usually promoted from the ranks after long service, though some were directly commissioned from the equestrian class or transferred from the Praetorian Guard. Literacy, experience and a reputation for steadiness were valued: the Roman author Vegetius describes the ideal centurion as sober, vigilant and more ready to obey and enforce orders than to talk. Centurions enjoyed much higher pay than ordinary legionaries and a share of booty, and could accumulate substantial wealth and status.

Insignia

Centurions are distinguished in art by a transverse helmet crest (crista transversa), greaves, a cuirass often decorated with military awards, and the vitis, a vine staff that was the symbol of their authority and an instrument of punishment. Corporal punishment by centurions was notorious; the historian Tacitus records a centurion nicknamed Cedo alteram — "Fetch me another" — because he broke his vine staffs beating soldiers.
