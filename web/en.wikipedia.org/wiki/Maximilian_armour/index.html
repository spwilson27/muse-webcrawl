Maximilian armour

Maximilian armour is a modern term for a style of early 16th-century German plate armour, characterised by extensive fluting — narrow parallel grooves worked into the steel plates. Named after Emperor Maximilian I (1459–1519), who promoted the style at his court, it was fashionable roughly from 1515 to 1540 and was produced mainly in the armoury centres of Nuremberg, Augsburg and Innsbruck.

Characteristics

The fluting served several purposes at once: it stiffened the plates, allowing thinner (and therefore lighter) steel without loss of strength; it helped glance and deflect weapon points; and it created striking patterns of light and shadow that imitated the pleated and slashed fashions of contemporary civilian dress. A complete Maximilian field armour typically comprised a close helmet or sallet with a bellows-faced visor, fluted breastplate and backplate, large asymmetrical pauldrons, mitten gauntlets, and sabatons that were characteristically broad and square-toed ("bear's paw" form), replacing the pointed Gothic toe of the previous century.

Development and decline

Maximilian armour succeeded the smoother, rounded "Gothic" style of the late 15th century (though fluting already appears on some earlier German work). As the 16th century progressed, fashions moved toward plain, smooth surfaces in the Italian taste, and toward etched and gilt decoration; by about 1540 the heavily fluted style had largely passed out of use, though it remains one of the most recognisable silhouettes in armour history. Fine examples are preserved in the Wallace Collection, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and the Hofjagd- und Rüstkammer in Vienna.
