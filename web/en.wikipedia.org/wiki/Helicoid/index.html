Helicoid

The helicoid, also known as the spiral plane, is a surface in three-dimensional space. It is the ruled surface traced by a line segment that rotates about a fixed axis while moving along it at a constant rate, so that the segment sweeps out a shape resembling a spiral ramp or the thread of a screw. It was discovered by Jean Baptiste Meusnier in 1776.

Geometry

The helicoid can be described parametrically by x = ρ cos θ, y = ρ sin θ, z = cθ, where ρ ranges over the real numbers and θ over all angles. It is a minimal surface: along with the plane and the catenoid, it is one of the classical minimal surfaces, and for many years the plane, helicoid and catenoid were the only known complete embedded minimal surfaces. The helicoid is also the only minimal ruled surface other than the plane.

The helicoid and the catenoid are locally isometric, and each can be continuously deformed into the other while remaining minimal at every stage, a deformation known as the associated family of these surfaces. A generalised form, in which the generating line is tilted, is called the generalised helicoid; helical ramps, screw threads and the surfaces of DNA-like structures in architecture frequently use the ordinary helicoid.

Applications

Helicoidal shapes appear in screw conveyors, drill bits, spiral staircases and ramps, and in the modelling of physical phenomena such as the shape of a soap film stretched between two rings joined by a rod.
