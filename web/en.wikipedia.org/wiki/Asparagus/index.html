Asparagus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Bundles of green asparagus spears tied for market]

Asparagus (Asparagus officinalis) is a perennial vegetable eaten for its young shoots, harvested in a brief spring season that gives it a festival aura: green asparagus in the English-speaking world, purple varieties, and the white asparagus of Germany and France — the same plant blanched by mounding earth over the spears to exclude light, celebrated through Spargelzeit with dedicated menus, routes and roadside stands. Asparagus was eaten in ancient Egypt, offered in temples, and prized by Rome — Augustus is credited with the phrase "faster than cooking asparagus" for anything done in a flash.

Cultivation

A bed of asparagus, grown from crowns, takes two to three years to establish and then crops for fifteen years or more; every spear is cut by hand, daily in season, which explains its price. Peru, Mexico and China dominate export production, flying green asparagus to northern winters. The spears' afterlife in the body is famous: asparagusic acid metabolises into sulphurous compounds that scent the urine of most people — though whether a given person can smell it is itself genetic, a twin quirk of production and perception that studies keep returning to.

In the kitchen

Asparagus rewards restraint: steamed or grilled until just tender, with hollandaise, brown butter, a fried egg, or shaved Parmesan; white asparagus takes the classic route with ham and hollandaise in Germany's spring restaurants. Louis XIV kept a gardener charged with forcing it year-round at Versailles. The season's brevity is the point — asparagus is spring's first green argument.
