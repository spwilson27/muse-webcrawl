Andrew Hill (June 30, 1931 – April 20, 2007) was an American jazz pianist and composer. He is regarded as one of the most distinctive and original pianists of the post-bop era, known for harmonically adventurous compositions characterised by angular melodies, shifting metres and an idiosyncratic rhythmic sense. His series of albums for Blue Note Records in the 1960s, above all Point of Departure (1964), are considered classics of modern jazz.

Early life

Hill was born in Chicago, Illinois. He began playing piano at the age of thirteen and was encouraged by the pianist Earl Hines. As a teenager he performed with local rhythm and blues bands and bebop musicians visiting Chicago, including Charlie Parker. He studied briefly with the composer and arranger William Russo. In the mid-1950s he worked as accompanist to the singer Dinah Washington and toured with the Johnny Griffin quartet.

Career

Hill moved to New York City in 1961. His breakthrough came when Blue Note founder Alfred Lion signed him in 1963. Between 1963 and 1970 he recorded a remarkable run of albums for the label, including Black Fire (1963), Smokestack (1963), Judgment! (1964), Point of Departure (1964) — featuring Eric Dolphy, Joe Henderson, Kenny Dorham, Richard Davis and Tony Williams — and Compulsion (1965). His music from this period blended hard bop with avant-garde elements without fully belonging to either camp.

During the 1970s Hill's recording opportunities diminished, and he taught and performed mainly on the West Coast, holding a position at Portland State University in Oregon. A revival of interest in the 1990s and 2000s brought renewed recording, including the big band album A Beautiful Day (2002) and Time Lines (2006) for Blue Note. In 2003 he received the Jazzpar Prize, and in 2007 he was named an NEA Jazz Master.

Death and legacy

Hill died of lung cancer at his home in Jersey City, New Jersey, on April 20, 2007. Critics have praised the singularity of his musical language: compositions such as "New Monastery", "Refuge" and "Dedication" remain in the repertoire, and pianists including Jason Moran have cited him as a major influence.
