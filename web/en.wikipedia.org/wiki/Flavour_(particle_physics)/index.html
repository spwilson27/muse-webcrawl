Flavour (particle physics)

In particle physics, flavour or flavor refers to the species of an elementary particle. The Standard Model counts six flavours of quarks and six flavours of leptons, each with associated flavour quantum numbers. Quarks occur in three generations: up and down; charm and strange; top and bottom. Leptons likewise: the electron and electron neutrino; the muon and muon neutrino; the tau and tau neutrino.

Flavour quantum numbers

Quarks carry flavour quantum numbers including strangeness (S), charm (C), bottomness (B′), and topness (T), together with isospin (I3) distinguishing up from down. Electric charge, baryon number and these flavour quantum numbers are related by the Gell-Mann–Nishijima formula. Leptons carry separate lepton flavour numbers: electron number, muon number, and tau number, which are conserved in most interactions, although neutrino oscillation shows that the individual lepton flavour numbers are not exactly conserved — only total lepton number is.

Flavour-changing interactions

The strong and electromagnetic interactions conserve flavour, but the weak interaction does not: quarks change flavour via the exchange of W bosons, with transition probabilities described by the Cabibbo–Kobayashi–Maskawa (CKM) matrix. Flavour-changing neutral currents are strongly suppressed in the Standard Model (the GIM mechanism). The pattern of quark masses and mixing — why there are three generations and why their parameters take the values they do — is known as the flavour problem, an unexplained feature of the Standard Model motivating theories of flavour physics and experiments such as those at LHCb and Belle II.
