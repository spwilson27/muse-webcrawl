Hummus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A bowl of hummus with olive oil, paprika and warm pita]

Hummus is a Middle Eastern dip or spread made from cooked, mashed chickpeas blended with tahini (sesame paste), lemon juice and garlic, finished with Olive oil and often paprika or whole chickpeas. Its full Arabic name, ḥummuṣ bi ṭ-ṭaḥīna, means "chickpeas with tahini".

Hummus is eaten with pita as a meze or breakfast dish across the Levant, Egypt, Turkey and Greece, and has become a supermarket staple worldwide. The earliest known recipes appear in medieval Arabic cookbooks from Cairo and Damascus; which nation owns it is cheerfully contested, including record-breaking "hummus wars" between Lebanon and Israel.

Contents
1. Variations

Variations

Regional versions add fava beans, yoghurt, butter and pine nuts (hummus kawarma with spiced lamb), or beetroot and herbs in modern fusion cooking. Smoothness — achieved by peeling chickpeas and long blending — is a point of craft pride.
