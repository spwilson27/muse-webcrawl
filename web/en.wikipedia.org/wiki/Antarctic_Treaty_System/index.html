Antarctic Treaty System

The Antarctic Treaty System is the complex of agreements that regulates international relations with respect to Antarctica, Earth's only continent without a native human population. Its centrepiece, the Antarctic Treaty, was signed in Washington on 1 December 1959 by the twelve countries whose scientists had been active in and around Antarctica during the International Geophysical Year of 1957–58 — Argentina, Australia, Belgium, Chile, France, Japan, New Zealand, Norway, South Africa, the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom and the United States — and entered into force on 23 June 1961. The treaty set Antarctica aside as a scientific preserve devoted to peace and science: it prohibits military activity, weapons testing and nuclear explosions and waste disposal on the continent, guarantees freedom of scientific investigation and the free exchange of data and personnel, and — its most famous diplomatic device — in Article IV freezes all territorial claims, neither recognising, disputing nor establishing any claim while the treaty is in force. Around it have grown the later instruments of the "system": conventions on seals (1972) and marine living resources (1980), and the Madrid Protocol of 1991, which designates Antarctica a "natural reserve, devoted to peace and science" and bans mineral extraction. By the twenty-first century the system had over fifty parties, including all the major Antarctic research nations, and annual consultative meetings administer a continent held in a unique legal suspension.

Background

Between 1908 and 1943 seven states had staked territorial claims to slices of Antarctica — Britain, New Zealand, France, Australia, Norway, Chile and Argentina — several of them overlapping in the Antarctic Peninsula, where Britain, Chile and Argentina maintained rival bases in a low-grade confrontation that included shots fired over the Argentine flag at Hope Bay in 1952. The United States and the Soviet Union, the two powers with the largest Antarctic programmes during the International Geophysical Year (1957–58), recognised no claims and reserved their own rights. The IGY's spectacularly successful scientific cooperation — some seventy stations operated by twelve nations, including the American station at the South Pole itself — supplied both the model and the moment: President Eisenhower invited the eleven other IGY Antarctic nations to Washington, and after eighteen months of secret negotiation the treaty was signed.

The system in operation

The treaty's fourteen articles made Antarctica the world's first nuclear-free zone and first demilitarised continent, with an inspection regime — any consultative party may inspect any station — that anticipated later arms-control verification. Subsequent decades added the Agreed Measures for flora and fauna (1964), the Convention for the Conservation of Antarctic Seals (1972), the Convention on the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CCAMLR, 1980) — which manages the krill and toothfish fisheries of the Southern Ocean — and the Protocol on Environmental Protection signed at Madrid in 1991, which followed the collapse of a minerals convention (CRAMRA) under environmentalist pressure led by Australia and France, and which bans mining for at least fifty years and subjects all activity to environmental assessment. The system has weathered the Falklands War of 1982 (fought partly within the treaty area between two parties), the growth of tourism to tens of thousands of visitors a year, and the entry of new research powers such as China, India and South Korea; its consensus rule and claims freeze remain intact, making it one of the most durable pieces of Cold-War-era diplomacy — a diplomatic settlement in the direct line of the conference settlements this encyclopedia traces from Utrecht to Potsdam, and nearly alone among them in never having been followed by a war.
