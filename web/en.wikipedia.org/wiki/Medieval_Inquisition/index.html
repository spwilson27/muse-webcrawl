Medieval Inquisition

The Medieval Inquisition was a series of inquisitions — Church bodies charged with suppressing heresy — operating within the Roman Catholic Church from roughly the 12th to the 15th century. It should be distinguished from the later, more famous Spanish Inquisition (founded 1478) and Roman Inquisition (1542).

Origins

Growing movements such as the Cathars in southern France and the Waldensians in the Alps challenged Church authority in the 12th century. The Church responded first with preaching missions and the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229), then with legal machinery: episcopal inquisitions, in which local bishops investigated heresy, were supplemented from the 1230s by papal inquisitors, usually Dominicans or Franciscans, appointed directly by the pope. Pope Gregory IX is often credited with institutionalizing the papal Inquisition around 1231–1233.

Procedure

Inquisitors arrived in a town, proclaimed a "period of grace" during which heretics could confess voluntarily and receive light penance, and then conducted investigations. The inquisitorial procedure differed from older accusatorial practice: the judge actively gathered evidence, witnesses' names might be withheld from the accused, and defense counsel was limited. Torture was authorized in restricted circumstances by Pope Innocent IV's bull Ad extirpanda (1252), though its frequency is debated by historians. Penalties ranged from prayers, pilgrimages, fines, and wearing crosses of shame, to imprisonment; unrepentant or relapsed heretics were "relaxed to the secular arm," which could impose execution, typically by burning.

Scope and historiography

The medieval Inquisition operated chiefly in France, Italy, and the Crown of Aragon. Modern scholarship has both documented real abuses and corrected exaggerated popular images — the institution kept meticulous records, applied formal rules, and executed far fewer people than later legend suggests, though for its victims the experience was often devastating. Its records are a major source for the study of medieval popular religion.
