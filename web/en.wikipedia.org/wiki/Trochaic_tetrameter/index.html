Trochaic tetrameter

Trochaic tetrameter is a metre in poetry composed of four trochaic feet per line; a trochee is a stressed syllable followed by an unstressed syllable (DUM-da). A line therefore typically has eight syllables: DUM-da DUM-da DUM-da DUM-da. Lines frequently end with the final unstressed syllable dropped (catalexis), giving seven syllables and a strongly stressed ending.

Examples

The best-known English example is Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's The Song of Hiawatha (1855), modelled on the Finnish epic Kalevala:

"By the shores of Gitche Gumee, / By the shining Big-Sea-Water"

William Blake's "The Tyger" is written in trochaic tetrameter catalectic ("Tyger Tyger, burning bright"). The witches' lines in Shakespeare's Macbeth ("Double, double toil and trouble") use the same catalectic form, distinguishing their speech from the play's iambic pentameter. Edgar Allan Poe's "The Raven" uses trochaic octameter.

Character

Because English naturally falls into iambic patterns, sustained trochaic verse creates a distinctive, chanting, forward-driving rhythm, which has made the metre popular for incantations, nursery rhymes, and narrative poems.
