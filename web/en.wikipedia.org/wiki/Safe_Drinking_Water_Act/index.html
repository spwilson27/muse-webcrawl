Safe Drinking Water Act

The Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA) is the principal federal law in the United States intended to ensure safe drinking water for the public. Pursuant to the act, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) is required to set standards for drinking water quality and oversee all states, localities, and water suppliers that implement the standards.

Overview

The SDWA applies to every public water system in the United States. There are currently more than 148,000 public water systems providing water to almost all Americans at some time during their lives. The act does not regulate private wells serving fewer than 25 individuals. SDWA authorizes the EPA to establish minimum standards to protect tap water and requires all owners or operators of public water systems to comply with these primary (health-related) standards. The 1996 amendments greatly enhanced the existing law by recognizing source water protection, operator training, funding for water system improvements, and public information as important components of safe drinking water.

National Primary Drinking Water Regulations

Under the SDWA, EPA sets legally enforceable maximum contaminant levels (MCLs) or treatment techniques for over 90 contaminants, including microorganisms, disinfectants and disinfection byproducts, inorganic and organic chemicals, and radionuclides. For each contaminant, EPA also sets a non-enforceable maximum contaminant level goal (MCLG) at a level at which no known or anticipated adverse health effects occur. In setting MCLs, EPA considers the best available technology and the costs and benefits of regulation.

Amendments

The 1986 amendments required EPA to set standards for 83 named contaminants within three years and to regulate 25 additional contaminants every three years thereafter; they also banned the use of lead pipes, solder, and flux in public water systems. The 1996 amendments established the Drinking Water State Revolving Fund to provide federal grants to states for loans to water systems, emphasized source water assessment and protection programs, and required consumer confidence reports informing the public about the quality of their drinking water.

Underground injection control and sole-source aquifers

Beyond tap water standards, the act regulates underground injection wells through the Underground Injection Control (UIC) program, protecting underground sources of drinking water from contamination, and authorizes EPA to designate sole-source aquifers whose contamination would create a significant hazard to public health.
