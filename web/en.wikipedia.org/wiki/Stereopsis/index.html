Stereopsis

Stereopsis (from the Greek stereos, "solid", and opsis, "sight") is the perception of depth and three-dimensional structure obtained on the basis of visual information arising from two eyes. Because the eyes are horizontally separated by several centimetres, each receives a slightly different image of the world; the visual system uses these differences—binocular disparities—to compute relative depth with remarkable precision. Stereopsis is one of several depth cues available to humans, alongside monocular cues such as occlusion, perspective, and motion parallax.

History

That two eyes contribute to depth perception was noted in antiquity, but the mechanism was not understood until Charles Wheatstone invented the stereoscope in 1838 and demonstrated that horizontal disparity between the images in the two eyes produces a vivid impression of solidity. Brewster's lenticular stereoscope and the Victorian vogue for stereo photographs followed. In the 1960s, Bela Julesz's random-dot stereograms proved that disparity alone, without any monocular form, suffices for depth perception, refuting the idea that the brain first solves each eye's image and then compares them. Around the same period, Hubel and Wiesel found neurons in cat visual cortex tuned to specific disparities.

Mechanism

Points in space project to "corresponding" locations in the two retinas depending on their distance relative to the fixation point, which defines the horopter. Points nearer or farther than fixation fall on non-corresponding locations, producing crossed or uncrossed disparities respectively. The fusion of the two images into a single percept occurs within a limited range of disparities called Panum's fusional area; beyond it, diplopia (double vision) occurs, though depth can still be perceived. Disparity-selective neurons, first concentrated in primary visual cortex, encode absolute and relative disparity and support stereoacuity thresholds as fine as a few seconds of arc—so-called hyperacuity.

Development and disorders

Stereopsis emerges abruptly in human infants at about three to four months of age, coincident with cortical maturation. Childhood conditions that disrupt binocular alignment or equal image quality—especially strabismus and anisometropia—can prevent its development, leaving an adult stereoblind despite normal vision in each eye; roughly 5–10% of the population has reduced or absent stereopsis. The neuroscientist Oliver Sacks popularised cases of adults who gained or regained stereo vision, suggesting some plasticity persists.

Applications

Stereopsis underlies stereoscopic displays, 3D cinema, virtual and augmented reality headsets, and binocular microscopy. In robotics and computer vision, stereo camera pairs replicate the principle computationally to recover scene depth. Clinical tests such as the Randot and Titmus stereotests quantify stereoacuity in arcseconds for screening children and certain professions.
