Blood plasma
Composition~92% water, ~7% proteins, ~1% other solutes
Share of blood volume~55%
Key proteinsAlbumin, globulins, fibrinogen

Blood plasma is the liquid component of blood in which blood cells are suspended. It makes up about 55% of the body's total blood volume and is the intravascular part of extracellular fluid. Plasma is mostly water (about 92% by volume) and contains dissolved proteins (6–8%), glucose, clotting factors, electrolytes, hormones, carbon dioxide and oxygen. It plays a critical role in maintaining blood pressure and volume, transporting nutrients and waste products, and mediating immunity and coagulation.

Composition

The principal plasma proteins are albumin, which maintains oncotic pressure and transports fatty acids and drugs; globulins, including antibodies (immunoglobulins); and fibrinogen, essential for clotting. Plasma from which clotting factors have been removed is called serum. Plasma differs from serum in that it retains fibrinogen and other coagulation factors.

Clinical use

Donated plasma is transfused as fresh frozen plasma to patients with coagulation disorders, massive haemorrhage or liver disease. Large volumes are also collected by plasmapheresis for fractionation into medicinal products: albumin, intravenous immunoglobulin, and concentrated clotting factors used to treat haemophilia. During the Second World War, dried plasma became a standard battlefield treatment for shock, and Charles Drew's work on plasma banking helped establish modern transfusion services.
