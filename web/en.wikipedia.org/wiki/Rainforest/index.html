Rainforest

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rainforests are forests characterised by high and continuous rainfall — typically over 1,750–2,000 mm a year — and by the highest biodiversity of any terrestrial biome. Tropical rainforests cluster near the equator (the Amazon, Congo Basin and Southeast Asia), while temperate rainforests grow on some cool coastal margins. A rainforest's structure is layered: emergent giants, a closed canopy, understorey and a dark forest floor where most nutrients cycle rapidly through decomposers rather than soils.

Though they cover only about 6% of land, rainforests harbour well over half of the world's plant and animal species by most estimates, store vast carbon in biomass, and drive regional rainfall — the Amazon generates much of its own rain through transpiration ('flying rivers'). Indigenous peoples have managed and inhabited them for millennia.

Threats

Deforestation for cattle ranching, soy, palm oil, logging and mining destroyed on the order of 10% of remaining tropical rainforest in recent decades (rates vary by region and year). Scientists warn of tipping points — Amazon dieback into savanna beyond roughly 20–25% loss — that would release enormous carbon and collapse regional climates.

Life in the canopy

Most rainforest life never touches the ground: epiphytes, lianas, canopy insects, frogs, birds and mammals form a second ecosystem in the treetops, which science only began to access with climbing, cranes and canopy rafts in the 1980s.
