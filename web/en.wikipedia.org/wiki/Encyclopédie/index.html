The Encyclopédie was a general encyclopedia published in France between 1751 and 1772, edited by Denis Diderot and, until 1759, by Jean le Rond d'Alembert. Its full title, Encyclopédie, ou dictionnaire raisonné des sciences, des arts et des métiers ("Encyclopedia, or a Systematic Dictionary of the Sciences, Arts, and Crafts"), announced its ambitions: to gather all human knowledge and to give unprecedented attention to the mechanical arts and trades.

History

The project began as a translation of Ephraim Chambers' Cyclopaedia but grew under Diderot into an original work written by a circle of Enlightenment thinkers — the philosophes — including Voltaire, Rousseau, Montesquieu and the Chevalier de Jaucourt, who wrote some 17,000 articles. Its sceptical treatment of religion and authority provoked suppression: the royal privilege was revoked in 1759 and the work was placed on the Catholic Index, yet publication continued clandestinely, and later volumes were issued under a false Neuchâtel imprint.

Significance

The Encyclopédie is regarded as the supreme intellectual monument of the Enlightenment. Its famous preliminary discourse by d'Alembert mapped the branches of knowledge, its cross-references quietly subverted orthodox doctrine, and its plates documented the tools and workshops of craftsmen with dignity. Sold across Europe in numerous editions and reprints, it spread Enlightenment ideas far beyond France and shaped the modern encyclopedia.
