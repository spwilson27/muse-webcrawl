Winter squash

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Winter squash is an annual fruit representing several squash species within the genus Cucurbita. Unlike summer squash, it is harvested and eaten in the mature stage, when the seeds have fully matured and the skin has hardened into a tough rind — which, provided the fruit is kept dry, allows storage for one to six months into the winter, hence the name.

Common winter squash include butternut and hubbard squash (Cucurbita maxima and C. moschata), acorn and spaghetti squash (C. pepo), buttercup and kabocha, and pumpkins. The flesh is generally sweeter, denser and more orange — rich in carotenoids — than summer squash, and is usually baked, roasted, or made into soups and pies.

Description

Winter squash grow on trailing or bushy vines with large lobed leaves and yellow-orange trumpet flowers. Fruits vary from the small, ribbed acorn squash to giant Hubbards and pumpkins exceeding 50 kg. A correctly harvested fruit has a hard rind that resists thumbnail pressure and a dried, corky stem.

History

Squashes were domesticated in the Americas at least 8,000–10,000 years ago. Together with maize and beans they formed the "Three Sisters" companion planting of Indigenous agriculture in North America. After the Columbian exchange, winter squash spread worldwide, becoming staples from Japanese kabocha to Italian zucca.

Varieties

  • Butternut — tan, bell-shaped, smooth-fleshed; the most popular in many markets.
  • Acorn — dark green, deeply ribbed, usually baked in halves.
  • Hubbard — large, warty blue-grey or orange types with sweet dry flesh.
  • Spaghetti squash — flesh separates into pasta-like strands when cooked.
  • Kabocha — Japanese type with very sweet, chestnut-like flesh.
  • Delicata — thin, edible skin; does not store as long.

Culinary use

Winter squash is roasted, puréed into soups and risottos, baked into pies (most "canned pumpkin" is in fact Dickinson or other moschata squash), and its seeds are toasted as a snack. The flesh is a good source of vitamin A, vitamin C, potassium and fibre.

Cultivation and storage

Plants need a long warm season (75–120 days). Fruits are cured in warm conditions for about ten days to harden the rind and convert starches, then stored cool (around 10–13 °C) and dry.
