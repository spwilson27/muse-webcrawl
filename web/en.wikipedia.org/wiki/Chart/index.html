Chart

Chart
Common typesBar chart, line chart, pie chart, scatter plot, histogram, box plot
PurposeData visualization: summarization, comparison, trend and distribution analysis
Key theoristsWilliam Playfair, Edward Tufte, Jacques Bertin

A chart (sometimes known as a graph) is a graphical representation for data visualization in which "the data is represented by symbols, such as bars in a bar chart, lines in a line chart, or slices in a pie chart". A chart can represent tabular numeric data, functions, or some kinds of qualitative structure, and it provides different kinds of information depending on its form.

History

The modern chart is largely the invention of the Scottish engineer and political economist William Playfair, whose Commercial and Political Atlas (1786) introduced the bar chart and line chart, and whose Statistical Breviary (1801) introduced the pie chart. Florence Nightingale popularized the polar area ("coxcomb") diagram in her campaign for sanitary reform after the Crimean War. In the 20th century, Jacques Bertin's Semiology of Graphics (1967) and Edward Tufte's The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (1983) established systematic principles for effective chart design, including Tufte's concepts of the data-ink ratio and the "lie factor".

Types

Common chart types include the bar chart (comparing categories), the line chart (change over time), the pie and doughnut charts (part-to-whole composition), the scatter plot (relationships between two variables), the histogram (distributions), the box plot (statistical summaries), and the heat map (values in a matrix). Specialized forms include nautical and aeronautical charts (maps for navigation), medical charts (patient records), and music charts (rankings of recordings by popularity) — the word "chart" also denotes these documents by extension.

Design principles

Effective charts use position and length encodings, which readers judge most accurately, and avoid misleading axes, gratuitous three-dimensional effects, and "chartjunk". Titles, labeled axes, units, and legends should make the data's source and meaning clear without clutter.
