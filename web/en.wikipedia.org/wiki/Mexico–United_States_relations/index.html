Mexico–United States relations

Placeholder image: Mexican and American flags at a shared border crossing

The relations between Mexico and the United States are among the most consequential bilateral relationships in the world. The two countries share a land border of approximately 3,145 kilometres (1,954 miles), one of the most frequently crossed international borders on earth, and are bound together by extensive trade, migration, security cooperation, and cultural exchange. Mexico is consistently one of the United States' top trading partners, and some 37 million Mexican Americans live in the United States.

History

Diplomatic relations began in 1822, a year after Mexican independence. Relations were soon strained by American expansionism: the annexation of Texas in 1845 led to the Mexican–American War of 1846–1848, after which Mexico lost roughly half its territory under the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo; the Gadsden Purchase of 1853 completed the modern border. During the Mexican Revolution, tensions peaked with the U.S. occupation of Veracruz in 1914, the Pershing Punitive Expedition of 1916–17, and the Zimmermann Telegram incident. Relations improved markedly under Franklin D. Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy, and Mexico cooperated with the Allies in World War II, contributing the 201st Fighter Squadron and labour through the Bracero Program.

Trade and economic ties

The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA), in force from 1994, integrated the two economies along with Canada's; it was replaced on July 1, 2020, by the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA). Total goods and services trade runs to hundreds of billions of dollars annually, with deeply integrated supply chains in automobiles, electronics, agriculture, and energy; in 2023 Mexico became the United States' largest single trading partner in goods.

Migration and security

Migration — legal and unauthorised — and border management are perennial issues, as are drug trafficking and organised crime. Security cooperation has included the Mérida Initiative (from 2008), which provided U.S. equipment and training to combat cartels, and later frameworks addressing fentanyl and arms smuggling. The two countries also cooperate on management of the Rio Grande and Colorado River waters under the International Boundary and Water Commission, founded in 1889.

Cultural ties

Millions of citizens cross the border legally every year for work, family, tourism, and study. American culture is influential in Mexico and Mexican culture — cuisine, music, language, and festivals such as Cinco de Mayo — is deeply woven into life in the United States, particularly in the Southwest.
