Salted duck egg

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

OriginChina
Main ingredientDuck egg, brine or salted charcoal paste
Chinese鹹鴨蛋 / 咸鸭蛋 (xián yā dàn)

A salted duck egg is a Chinese preserved food product made by soaking duck eggs in brine, or packing each egg in damp, salted charcoal paste. It is widely eaten across China and Southeast Asia, including in the Philippines (where it is known as itlog na maalat), Vietnam, Malaysia and Indonesia.

Production

In the brine method, fresh duck eggs are steeped in a saturated salt solution for several weeks. In the paste method, eggs are coated individually in a mixture of salt, ash or charcoal and sometimes clay, then stored for a similar period. During curing, salt migrates through the shell, firming the white and transforming the yolk into a rich, orange-red, oily and slightly granular sphere. A successful salted egg is judged by its yolk, which should exude oil when cooked.

Culinary use

Salted duck eggs are normally boiled or steamed before eating and are a classic accompaniment to congee (rice porridge). The yolks are prized as an ingredient in their own right: they are baked into mooncakes and other pastries such as zongzi, used to make salted egg yolk sauces and custards, and in modern Southeast Asian cooking tossed with fried snacks. In the Philippines, salted eggs are commonly eaten with tomatoes and rice. The whites are very salty and are used more sparingly, crumbled into salads or stir-fries.

Characteristics

The cooked egg has a firm, sharply salty white and a yolk that is deeply flavoured, fatty and crumbly. Salted eggs keep for weeks thanks to the preserving effect of the salt.
