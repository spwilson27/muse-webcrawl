Subject (grammar)

The subject in a sentence or clause is one of the two main constituents of a clause, the other being the predicate. The subject is a noun phrase or pronoun that typically identifies who or what the sentence is about, and with which the verb agrees. In English, the subject usually appears before the verb in declarative clauses: in "The cat slept", the cat is the subject.

Defining the subject

Grammarians characterize the subject by a bundle of properties rather than a single criterion. Typical diagnostics include:

  • Agreement: the finite verb agrees with the subject in person and number ("she runs" vs. "they run").
  • Case: in languages with morphological case, the subject commonly bears the nominative case.
  • Position: subjects occupy a characteristic position, e.g. preverbal in English declarative clauses, and invert with auxiliaries in questions ("Is she running?").
  • Tag questions: the pronoun in a tag question refers back to the subject ("She is running, isn't she?").
  • Control and raising: the subject is the argument understood as the subject of an embedded infinitive.

Subjects across languages

In nominative–accusative languages the subject groups together the sole argument of an intransitive verb and the agent-like argument of a transitive verb. In ergative–absolutive languages, the morphosyntactic grouping differs, leading some linguists to question whether "subject" is a universally uniform category. Some languages allow dummy subjects (expletives), such as English it in "It is raining," which fill the subject position without semantic content. Other languages, like Spanish or Italian, are null-subject languages, permitting the subject pronoun to be omitted.

Syntactic theories

In generative grammar, the subject is typically analyzed as the noun phrase occupying the specifier of the tense phrase (Spec-TP), having moved there from within the verb phrase. In dependency grammar, the subject is a dependent of the finite verb. Functional and typological approaches treat the subject as a grammatical relation defined by a cluster of coding and behavioral properties.
