Cauchy stress tensor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In continuum mechanics, the Cauchy stress tensor σ (also called the true stress tensor) is a second-order tensor that completely defines the state of stress at a point inside a material. Named after Augustin-Louis Cauchy, who developed the theory in the 1820s, it relates the unit normal vector n of an internal surface through the point to the traction vector t acting across that surface:

t = σ · n   (ti = σij nj)

Components

The tensor has nine components in three dimensions. The diagonal components σxx, σyy, σzz are normal stresses (tensile positive by the usual sign convention); the off-diagonal components are shear stresses. Balance of angular momentum implies that, in the absence of body couples, the tensor is symmetric, σij = σji, reducing it to six independent components. Because it is symmetric, at every point there exist three mutually perpendicular principal directions along which the traction is purely normal; the corresponding principal stresses are the tensor's eigenvalues.

Governing equations

Combined with the balance of linear momentum, the tensor gives Cauchy's equation of motion, ρa = ∇·σ + ρb, which governs deformation in solids and, with an appropriate constitutive law, fluid flow. Invariants of the tensor — including hydrostatic pressure (one third of its trace) and the von Mises equivalent stress derived from its deviatoric part — are central to theories of yielding and failure in engineering.

Related measures

Alternative stress measures used in finite-deformation analysis include the first and second Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensors, which refer forces to the undeformed configuration, whereas the Cauchy tensor is defined per unit area of the deformed configuration.
