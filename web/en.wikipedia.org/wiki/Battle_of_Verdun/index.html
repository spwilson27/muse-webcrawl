Battle of Verdun

The Battle of Verdun (21 February – 18 December 1916) was the longest battle of the First World War, fought between the German and French armies around the fortified city of Verdun in north-eastern France. Some 700,000 men were killed or wounded in a battle of attrition that became a symbol of French national resolve — summed up in the phrase Ils ne passeront pas ("They shall not pass").

Background

German Chief of Staff Falkenhayn planned to attack a position France would defend at all costs, to "bleed France white" with artillery. The offensive opened, after a delay, on 21 February 1916 with one of the heaviest bombardments of the war.

Battle

Fort Douaumont, the keystone of the defences, fell almost undefended on 25 February. Pétain organised the defence, keeping the garrison supplied over the single road — the Voie Sacrée — and rotating divisions through the noria system, so that most of the French army passed through Verdun. Fort Vaux fell in June after a heroic defence; German attacks reached Fort Souville in July but went no further, as the Somme offensive and Russia's Brusilov offensive drained German resources. In October–December Nivelle's counteroffensives retook Douaumont and Vaux.

Legacy

Millions of shells churned the battlefield into a moonscape still scarred today, with villages destroyed and never rebuilt. The Douaumont Ossuary holds the remains of some 130,000 unidentified soldiers. Verdun remains central to French and Franco-German memory, symbolised by the 1984 hand-in-hand commemoration of François Mitterrand and Helmut Kohl.
