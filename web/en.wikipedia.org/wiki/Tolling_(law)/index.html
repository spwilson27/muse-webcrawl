Tolling (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tolling is a legal doctrine which allows for the pausing or delaying of the running of the period of time set forth by a statute of limitations, such that a lawsuit may potentially be filed even after the statute of limitations has nominally run out. Although grounds for tolling vary by jurisdiction, the clock generally stops while a condition prevents, or makes it unfair to expect, the claimant to sue.

Grounds

Classic statutory grounds include the claimant's minority or mental incapacity, the defendant's absence from the jurisdiction or concealment of the claim, and periods during which another mandatory proceeding (such as bankruptcy's automatic stay or required administrative exhaustion) is pending. Equitable tolling, developed by courts — notably in US federal law in Irwin v. Department of Veterans Affairs (1990) — applies where a claimant diligently pursued their rights but some extraordinary circumstance prevented timely filing; it is to be applied sparingly. Equitable estoppel, sometimes grouped with tolling, bars a defendant who misled the claimant from relying on the limitation defence at all.

Effect

Tolling suspends rather than resets the clock: when the tolling condition ends, the remaining time resumes running. If a claim accrues while the claimant is a minor, for instance, the period commonly begins to run on reaching majority, giving the claimant the full statutory period thereafter. During the COVID-19 pandemic, several jurisdictions, including New York State by executive order, tolled limitation periods wholesale for a matter of months.
