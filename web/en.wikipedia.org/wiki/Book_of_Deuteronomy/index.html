The Book of Deuteronomy (from Greek Δευτερονόμιον, Deuteronómion, "second law"; Hebrew: דְּבָרִים, Dəḇārīm, "words") is the fifth book of the Torah (in Judaism), where it is called Devarim, and the fifth book of the Christian Old Testament. The book consists of three sermons or speeches delivered to the Israelites by Moses on the plains of Moab, shortly before they enter the Promised Land.

Contents

The first sermon recounts the forty years of wilderness wandering and exhorts obedience. The second — the core of the book — restates the Ten Commandments and sets out the Deuteronomic Code (chapters 12–26): laws centralizing worship in "the place that the LORD your God will choose," regulating festivals, judges, kingship, prophecy, warfare, and social and humanitarian obligations. The third sermon pronounces blessings for obedience and curses for disobedience, and the book closes with the song and blessing of Moses and the account of his death on Mount Nebo, succeeded by Joshua.

Among its most famous passages is the Shema (6:4): "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one," central to Jewish prayer and theology.

Composition

Most scholars hold that Deuteronomy's core originated in the northern kingdom of Israel or in Judah in the 8th–7th centuries BCE, and associate its "discovery" with the book of the law found in the Temple during Josiah's reform (2 Kings 22–23, c. 622 BCE). It was expanded during and after the Babylonian exile and serves as the theological introduction to the Deuteronomistic History (Joshua through Kings). Traditional Jewish and Christian belief attributes it to Moses, with the final chapter describing his death.
