Infective endocarditis

Infective endocarditis
SpecialtyCardiology, infectious disease
SymptomsFever, new heart murmur, fatigue, embolic phenomena
CausesBacterial (e.g. Staphylococcus aureus, viridans streptococci) or fungal infection of the endocardium
Risk factorsValvular disease, prosthetic valves, intravenous drug use, indwelling catheters

Infective endocarditis (IE) is an infection of the inner surface of the heart (the endocardium), usually the heart valves. It may involve the heart valves, the mural endocardium, or a septal defect, and can lead to severe valvular insufficiency and heart failure. A characteristic lesion, the vegetation, is a mass of platelets, fibrin, microorganisms, and inflammatory cells.

Signs and presentation

Presentation ranges from an acute, rapidly progressive illness with high fever to a subacute course with low-grade fever, weight loss, and fatigue. Classic peripheral stigmata — reflecting the older subacute disease — include splinter haemorrhages, Janeway lesions, Osler's nodes, Roth's spots, and splenomegaly. Fragments of vegetation may embolise to the brain, lungs, spleen, or limbs, causing stroke or abscesses. Most cases are bacterial; Staphylococcus aureus is now the most common cause in developed countries, followed by streptococci and enterococci. Risk is elevated in people with prosthetic valves, congenital heart disease, prior endocarditis, or intravenous drug use (which typically affects the tricuspid valve).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on blood cultures taken before antibiotics and on echocardiography (transoesophageal imaging when needed), organised clinically by the modified Duke criteria. Treatment requires prolonged courses of intravenous antibiotics — typically four to six weeks — and valve surgery in a substantial minority of cases, particularly with heart failure, uncontrolled infection, or large vegetations at risk of embolism. Even with modern therapy, in-hospital mortality is roughly 15–30 percent. Antibiotic prophylaxis before dental procedures is now recommended only for the highest-risk patients.
