Late Roman army

The Late Roman army is the term used for the armed forces of the Roman Empire from the accession of Diocletian in AD 284 until the division of the empire in 395 and, in the West, its fall in the later fifth century. Reorganised by Diocletian and Constantine I after the Crisis of the Third Century, it differed from the Principate's army in its division between static frontier troops (limitanei) and mobile field armies (comitatenses), its much greater reliance on cavalry, and its extensive recruitment of barbarian soldiers and allies (foederati). Evidence for its structure comes above all from the Notitia Dignitatum, a register of commands from around AD 400.

Organisation

Diocletian multiplied the number of provinces and legions and strengthened frontier fortifications; Constantine is credited with completing the division between the frontier army and a central mobile reserve. The field armies (comitatus) contained the empire's best troops: legions of perhaps 1,000–1,200 men (far smaller than the old 5,000-man legions), elite infantry auxilia palatina, and cavalry vexillationes, commanded by magistri militum (masters of the soldiers). Frontier districts were held by limitanei under duces, garrisoning forts and fortified towns. The emperor's guard was provided by the scholae palatinae. The old Praetorian Guard was disbanded by Constantine after the Battle of the Milvian Bridge (312).

Equipment and warfare

Late Roman soldiers typically carried an oval or round shield, a long sword (spatha), spears and heavy javelins (spiculum), and in some units lead-weighted darts (plumbatae) — Vegetius and Ammianus Marcellinus are key literary sources. Helmets of the "ridge" type and mail or scale armour were standard. Cavalry, including cataphracts and horse archers, gained steadily in importance. Strategy combined frontier defence-in-depth debate (scholars dispute how deliberate this was) with rapid response by field armies. Recruitment drew increasingly on Germanic and other barbarian volunteers and treaty contingents; by the late fourth century some of the empire's highest commanders — Stilicho, and later the era of Aëtius — were themselves of barbarian origin.

Decline in the West

The army suffered a major blow at Adrianople (378), where Emperor Valens and much of the eastern field army were destroyed by the Goths. In the fifth-century West, shrinking tax revenue, civil wars, and the loss of Africa to the Vandals (439) starved the army of funds; western defence devolved onto foederati under their own kings, and the last western field armies effectively dissolved by the 470s. The eastern army survived, evolving into the Byzantine military system.
