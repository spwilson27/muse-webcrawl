Deontological ethics

Deontological ethics or deontology (from Greek δέον, déon, "obligation, duty") is the normative ethical theory that the morality of an action should be based on whether the action itself is right or wrong under a series of rules and principles, rather than on the consequences of the action. On this view some acts are obligatory or forbidden regardless of their outcomes — for example, duties not to lie, steal or kill. Deontology is contrasted with consequentialism and virtue ethics.

Kantian deontology

The theory is most closely associated with Immanuel Kant, whose categorical imperative requires that one act only according to maxims that one could consistently will to become universal laws, and that humanity be treated always as an end in itself, never merely as a means. Other deontological approaches are based on divine command, natural law, social contract, or lists of prima facie duties, as in the work of W. D. Ross.

Features and criticism

Deontologists distinguish between duties owed to specific people and agent-relative permissions, and typically hold that there are constraints on action (things one must not do even to prevent greater harm) as well as options. Critics argue that rigid rule-following can produce morally paradoxical results when duties conflict, while defenders reply that deontology alone captures the inviolability of persons and the separateness of individuals.
