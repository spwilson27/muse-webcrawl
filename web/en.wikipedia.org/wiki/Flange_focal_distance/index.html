Flange focal distance

Flange focal distance

DefinitionDistance from the lens-mount flange to the film or sensor plane
Also calledFlange focal depth, flange back distance, register
ExamplesCanon EF: 44.0 mm; Nikon F: 46.5 mm; Sony E: 18 mm; Micro Four Thirds: 19.25 mm

Flange focal distance (FFD), also called flange focal depth, flange back distance, or the register, is the distance from the mounting flange (the metal ring on the camera and the corresponding part of the lens) to the film plane or image sensor in a camera body. It is a defining specification of every interchangeable-lens system, because a lens focuses correctly only when it is held at exactly the designed distance from the image plane.

Typical values

Single-lens reflex mounts require room for the reflex mirror, so their registers are comparatively large: the Nikon F mount is 46.5 mm, Canon EF 44.0 mm, Pentax K 45.5 mm, and Leica R 47.0 mm. Rangefinder mounts are shorter — the Leica M mount is 27.8 mm — and mirrorless digital systems, freed from the mirror box, use much shorter distances: Sony E-mount 18 mm, Nikon Z 16 mm, Canon RF 20 mm, the L-mount 20 mm, and Micro Four Thirds 19.25 mm. The C-mount used for cine and machine-vision cameras has a flange focal distance of 17.526 mm.

Adaptation between mounts

The flange focal distance determines whether lenses of one system can be adapted to cameras of another. A lens designed for a longer register can be fitted to a camera with a shorter register using a simple spacer adapter while retaining infinity focus — one reason short-register mirrorless cameras can use vast ranges of legacy SLR and rangefinder lenses. The reverse (a short-register lens on a longer-register body) requires corrective optics in the adapter, which act as a weak teleconverter, or else infinity focus is lost and the combination is usable only for close-up work.

Adjustment

In camera manufacture and servicing the register is set precisely with shims and measured with specialised gauges or collimators; errors cause systematic front- or back-focusing that cannot be corrected by the lens's focus mechanism alone. In cinema cameras, the corresponding adjustment of the lens mount is a standard part of camera preparation, since cine lenses are exchanged constantly and must all focus to their witness marks.
