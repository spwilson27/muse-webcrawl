The Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast are composed of many nations and tribal affiliations, each with distinctive cultural and political identities, but they share certain beliefs, traditions, and practices, such as the centrality of salmon as a resource and spiritual symbol. The term Northwest Coast or North Pacific Coast is used in anthropology to refer to the groups of Indigenous people residing along the coast of what is now British Columbia, the Alaska Panhandle, Washington, and northern Oregon.

Culture

The extraordinary abundance of salmon, eulachon, shellfish, and marine mammals, together with great forests of western red cedar, allowed Northwest Coast societies to develop dense, settled village life, ranked social orders, and elaborate ceremonialism without agriculture. Ranked societies were organised into nobles, commoners, and slaves; hereditary crests and privileges were displayed and validated at the potlatch, a ceremonial feast at which chiefs distributed wealth.

The region is famous for its monumental art: carved and painted house fronts, memorial and mortuary totem poles, bentwood boxes, masks, and the highly formalised two-dimensional style known as formline art. Large cedar plank houses sheltered extended families, and ocean-going canoes connected villages in trade networks that carried dentalium shells, copper, eulachon oil, and slaves over long distances.

History

European contact from the late 18th century brought the maritime fur trade, new wealth, and devastating epidemics of smallpox and other diseases that reduced populations by as much as 80–90 percent in some communities. Colonial governments in Canada and the United States suppressed the potlatch (banned in Canada from 1885 to 1951) and removed children to residential and boarding schools. Since the mid-20th century, Northwest Coast nations have led movements for land claims, treaty rights, fishing rights, and cultural revival; the art tradition, carried forward by artists such as Bill Reid, is internationally celebrated.
