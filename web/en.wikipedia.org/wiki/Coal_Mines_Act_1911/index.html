Coal Mines Act 1911

A British colliery in the period when the 1911 Act was in force.

The Coal Mines Act 1911 (1 & 2 Geo. 5. c. 50) was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that comprehensively regulated safety and working conditions in coal mines. It was the principal statute governing mine safety in Britain for more than forty years and consolidated and strengthened the earlier Coal Mines Regulation Acts.

Background

The Act followed a series of catastrophic colliery explosions, including the Pretoria Pit disaster of December 1910, in which 344 miners died, and it built on the work of a Royal Commission on Mines that had reported over several years. The liberal government of H. H. Asquith introduced the bill as a single code replacing piecemeal legislation.

Provisions

The Act tightened requirements for mine management and inspection, mandating certificated managers and under-managers, systematic inspection of workings, and standards for ventilation to dilute flammable firedamp. It regulated the use of safety lamps and explosives, required precautions against coal dust — including stone-dusting and watering — restricted the hours and conditions of employment underground, and prohibited the employment of women and young children below ground. It also required mines to maintain rescue apparatus and to have trained rescue teams available, a response to disasters in which would-be rescuers had themselves been killed by afterdamp.

Legacy

The Act remained the governing safety code until it was largely superseded by the Mines and Quarries Act 1954. Its framework of certificated management, statutory inspection, and prescribed safety duties shaped British mining practice for the remainder of the industry's history of large-scale coal mining.
