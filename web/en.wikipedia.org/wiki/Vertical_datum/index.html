Vertical datum

A vertical datum (or height datum) is a reference surface from which elevations are measured in surveying, geodesy, cartography and engineering. Because "sea level" varies from place to place and over time, precise work requires a defined zero surface and a network of benchmarks with published heights.

Types

Traditional vertical datums were based on mean sea level observed at one or more tide gauges over a full tidal epoch (about 19 years), then extended inland by precise levelling. Examples include the North American Vertical Datum of 1988 (NAVD 88), Britain's Ordnance Datum Newlyn, and Australia's AHD. Modern datums increasingly use the geoid — the equipotential surface of Earth's gravity field — derived from satellite gravity missions, so that heights from GNSS (orthometric heights) can be computed by subtracting geoid height from ellipsoid height.

Importance

Consistent heights matter for flood mapping, drainage and canal engineering, construction and aviation. Datums can differ by a metre or more between countries and between successive national datums, so maps must always state which datum their heights use.
