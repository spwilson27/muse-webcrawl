Carrying capacity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The carrying capacity of an environment (often written K) is the maximum population size of a species that the environment can sustain indefinitely, given its food, water, habitat and other resources. It is a central concept of population Ecology: populations growing beyond carrying capacity overshoot and decline, while those below it tend to grow.

Dynamics and limits

In the logistic growth model, growth is fastest at half of carrying capacity and slows to zero as the population approaches K, producing an S-shaped curve. Carrying capacity is not fixed: it changes with seasons, technology, disease and the population's own effects on habitat — grazing herds can degrade the land that feeds them. Overshoot followed by crash is documented in introduced populations such as reindeer on St Matthew Island.

Human carrying capacity

Applying the concept to humanity is contentious: estimates of how many people Earth can support vary enormously depending on assumed living standards, diet and technology. The debate runs from Malthus through The Limits to Growth to modern discussions of ecological footprints and planetary boundaries.
