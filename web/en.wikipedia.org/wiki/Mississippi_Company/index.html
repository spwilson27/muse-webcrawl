The Mississippi Company (French: Compagnie du Mississippi) was a corporation holding a business monopoly in French colonies in North America and the West Indies. Founded in 1717 by the Scottish economist John Law, it became central to one of the earliest great speculative bubbles, the Mississippi Bubble, whose collapse in 1720 shook the French financial system.

Background

After the death of Louis XIV in 1715, France was burdened with enormous debts from decades of war. The Regent, Philippe II, Duke of Orléans, turned to John Law, who established the Banque Générale and proposed refinancing the debt through a company that would exploit the supposed riches of French Louisiana. In 1717 Law's Compagnie d'Occident received a 25-year monopoly on trade with Louisiana and Canada; it soon became known as the Mississippi Company.

The bubble

In 1719 the company absorbed the French East India and China companies to become the Compagnie des Indes, and it took over collection of French taxes and the mint. Shares were issued to the public and could be bought with government debt certificates. Frenzied demand in the Rue Quincampoix in Paris drove the share price from 500 livres to around 10,000 livres, making sudden fortunes — the word "millionaire" entered common use at this time.

Collapse

In 1720, as shareholders attempted to convert shares into coin, confidence broke and the share price collapsed. Law's bank suspended payments, riots erupted, and Law fled France at the end of the year. The episode ruined many investors and discredited paper money and joint-stock companies in France for decades, while the colony of Louisiana, including the new settlement of New Orleans, failed to deliver the promised wealth.
