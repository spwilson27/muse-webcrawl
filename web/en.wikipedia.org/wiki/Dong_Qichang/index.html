Dong Qichang (Chinese: 董其昌; pinyin: Dǒng Qíchāng; 1555–1636), courtesy name Xuanzai, was a Chinese painter, calligrapher and art theorist of the late Ming dynasty. A high official who rose to Minister of Rites in Nanjing, he was the most influential arbiter of taste in Chinese painting for the following three centuries.

Theory

Dong is chiefly remembered for his doctrine of the Northern and Southern Schools of painting, borrowed by analogy from Chan (Zen) Buddhism. He exalted the "Southern School" of literati (wenren) painters — expressive, calligraphic amateurs descending from Wang Wei through Dong Yuan, Juran, Mi Fu and the Four Masters of Yuan — over the "Northern School" of professional and academic painters. His theory, promoted through his writings and his rulership of the Songjiang school, shaped collecting, attribution and practice well into the Qing dynasty, though modern scholars criticise its distortions.

Art

His own landscapes reinterpret old masters in radically abstracted, rhythmically structured compositions of dry brush and pale ink. As a calligrapher he modelled himself on Mi Fu and Zhao Mengfu. Dong's personal conduct as an official was controversial — his estate was attacked by a mob in 1616 over his family's alleged abuses — but his artistic authority was unmatched.
