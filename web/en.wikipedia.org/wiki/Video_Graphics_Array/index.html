Video Graphics Array (VGA) is a video display controller and accompanying de facto graphics standard, first introduced by IBM in 1987 with its PS/2 line of computers. VGA became the baseline graphics capability of the IBM PC compatible world, and the term is also used for the 15-pin D-subminiature VGA connector and the 640 × 480 resolution it popularised.

Technical details

VGA supports all graphics modes of its predecessors (CGA and EGA) and adds new ones, most notably 640 × 480 in 16 colours and 320 × 200 in 256 colours (Mode 13h), the latter becoming a staple of PC games of the early 1990s. It uses an analogue signal rather than the digital signals of earlier standards, allowing a palette of 262,144 colours (18-bit RGB), from which 256 could be displayed simultaneously in 256-colour modes. Standard VGA hardware had 256 KB of video memory.

Legacy

Although superseded by the Super VGA standards (VESA BIOS Extensions, XGA, and later hardware), later display standards retained VGA compatibility at the register level for start-up and low-level modes. The VGA connector remained common on monitors and projectors well into the era of digital interfaces such as DVI and HDMI, and "VGA resolution" persists as a shorthand for 640 × 480.
