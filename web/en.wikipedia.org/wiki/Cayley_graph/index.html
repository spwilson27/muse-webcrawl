Cayley graph

In mathematics, a Cayley graph, also known as a Cayley colour graph, Cayley diagram, group diagram, or colour group, is a graph that encodes the abstract structure of a group. Its definition is suggested by Cayley's theorem (named after Arthur Cayley) and uses a specified, usually finite, set of generators for the group.

Definition

Suppose that G is a group and S is a generating set. The Cayley graph Γ = Γ(G, S) is a coloured directed graph constructed as follows: each element g of G is assigned a vertex; for each generator s in S and each g in G, there is a directed edge of colour s from g to gs. If S is closed under inverses, the graph is commonly treated as undirected.

Examples

The Cayley graph of the infinite cyclic group with generator 1 is an infinite path. For the free group on two generators it is a 4-regular infinite tree, illustrating that the group has no relations. Finite examples include the cycle graph for a cyclic group and cube-like graphs for elementary abelian 2-groups. The Cayley graph of the symmetric group generated by adjacent transpositions is a permutohedron.

Properties and applications

Cayley graphs are vertex-transitive and regular. They connect group theory with graph theory and are used in geometric group theory, where word metrics on groups are defined by path lengths in the graph, and in computer science, where Cayley graphs provide topologies for interconnection networks; expander families of Cayley graphs underpin constructions in complexity theory and coding.
