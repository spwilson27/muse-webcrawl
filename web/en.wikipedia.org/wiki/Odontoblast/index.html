Odontoblast

Odontoblasts form a layer at the periphery of the dental pulp.

In vertebrates, an odontoblast is a cell of neural crest origin that is part of the outer surface of the dental pulp, and whose biological function is dentinogenesis, which is the formation of dentin, the substance beneath the tooth enamel on the crown and the cementum on the root.

Structure

Odontoblasts are tall columnar cells arranged in a single layer along the inner wall of the pulp chamber, adjacent to the predentin. The cell body contains a large nucleus, abundant rough endoplasmic reticulum, and a prominent Golgi apparatus, reflecting its secretory function. Each odontoblast extends a slender cytoplasmic projection, the odontoblast process (also called Tomes's fibre), into a dentinal tubule within the dentin it has secreted.

Development

Odontoblasts differentiate from cells of the dental papilla under the influence of signals from the inner enamel epithelium during tooth development. They first appear at the site of tooth development around 17–18 weeks in utero, and are present until death unless killed by bacterial or chemical attacks, or indirectly through other means such as suction from a dental aspirator. As they mature, odontoblasts secrete predentin, which then mineralizes into dentin, beginning at the cusp tips and proceeding toward the root.

Function

The primary function of odontoblasts is the secretion of dentin. During initial tooth formation they produce primary dentin; after the tooth erupts and the root completes formation, they continue to lay down secondary dentin at a much slower rate throughout life, gradually reducing the size of the pulp chamber. In response to injury, such as carious attack or restorative procedures, surviving odontoblasts can be stimulated to produce reactionary (tertiary) dentin, while severe injury that destroys the cells may trigger progenitor cells in the pulp to form reparative dentin.

Beyond matrix secretion, odontoblasts are increasingly recognized as sensory cells: their processes extend into the dentinal tubules and are thought to participate in the transmission of stimuli that underlie dentin sensitivity, and the cells express components involved in innate immune defense of the pulp.
