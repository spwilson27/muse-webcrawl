An aerosol spray is a type of dispensing system which creates an aerosol mist of liquid particles. The can contains the product (paint, deodorant, insecticide, etc.), a propellant under pressure, and a valve mechanism. When the actuator is pressed, the valve opens and the pressure forces the product out through a nozzle as a fine spray.

History

Pressure-dispensing concepts date to the 1790s, but the first practical aerosol can was developed in 1941 by USDA chemists Lyle Goodhue and William Sullivan for dispensing insecticide to protect soldiers from malaria — known as the "bug bomb". Swiss engineer Robert Abplanalp's invention of the crimped-on valve in 1949 made the modern spray can cheap and reliable, leading to explosive growth in household and industrial products through the 1950s.

Components and operation

A typical can contains the product concentrate, a pressurized gas propellant, a dip tube drawing product from the bottom, and the valve. Most aerosols use liquefied propellants (butane/propane mixtures or hydrofluoroolefins) that maintain constant pressure until the can is nearly empty. Alternative systems include compressed gases, pump sprays, and bag-on-valve designs that keep propellant and product separate.

Environmental concerns

In the 1970s, chemists F. Sherwood Rowland and Mario Molina showed that chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), then the standard propellants, destroyed stratospheric ozone. Most countries phased out CFCs in consumer aerosols following the 1987 Montreal Protocol, with hydrocarbon and HFC propellants taking their place. Today's concerns focus on volatile organic compounds (VOCs) contributing to ground-level ozone and on the climate impact of some propellant gases.

Uses

Aerosol sprays are used for paints and coatings, personal care products, cleaning agents, pesticides, food products such as whipped cream, and medical inhalers. The global market for aerosol products exceeds tens of billions of units per year.
