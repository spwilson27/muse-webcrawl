Flamenco

Flamenco is an art form based on the various folkloric music traditions of southern Spain, developed within the gitano (Romani) subculture of the region of Andalusia, and also having historical presence in Extremadura and Murcia. In the broadest sense, it is a complex musical and cultural tradition; in its strictest sense it is an art form incorporating song (cante), dance (baile), and guitar playing (toque), along with hand-clapping (palmas), finger-snapping (pitos) and rhythmic stamping. In 2010 UNESCO declared flamenco a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity.

History

Flamenco crystallised in Andalusia in the late 18th and 19th centuries, drawing on Andalusian, Romani, Moorish, Jewish and sub-Saharan African influences. It emerged from private family gatherings to the cafés cantantes of the later 19th century, where professional singers such as Silverio Franconetti performed. The 20th century saw both popularisation — including the theatrical ópera flamenca — and a purist revival championed by figures such as Manuel de Falla and Federico García Lorca, who organised the Concurso de Cante Jondo in Granada in 1922.

Elements and forms

Flamenco comprises dozens of song forms called palos, distinguished by rhythm (compás, often in twelve-beat cycles), mode, and mood: solemn forms such as soleá, siguiriyas and tonás contrast with light forms such as alegrías, bulerías and tangos. Great interpreters include singers La Niña de los Peines, Antonio Mairena, Camarón de la Isla; guitarists Ramón Montoya, Sabicas, Paco de Lucía and Tomatito; and dancers Carmen Amaya and Antonio Gades. From the 1970s, nuevo flamenco fused the tradition with jazz, rock and Latin music.
