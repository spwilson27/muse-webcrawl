Tonadilla

The tonadilla was a Spanish musical stage genre of the 18th and early 19th centuries: a short comic musical interlude, typically lasting about twenty minutes, performed between the acts of plays, operas and other entertainments. Sung in Spanish and filled with popular dance rhythms and topical humour, tonadillas satirised contemporary manners — fops, soldiers, majos and majas, bullfighters, and affected devotees of Italian opera.

History

The genre developed from the short tonadas and entremeses of earlier Spanish theatre and flourished from about 1750 to 1820, particularly in Madrid's public theatres, the Príncipe and the Cruz. Early examples were essentially sung duets or trios; later tonadillas a tres and a cuatro added characters and ensemble numbers. Leading composers included Luis Misón, generally credited with establishing the genre, Pablo Esteve, Blas de Laserna, and Jacinto Valledor. Star singers — tonadilleras such as La Caramba (María Antonia Fernández) — became popular celebrities.

Characteristics and legacy

Tonadillas used Spanish song and dance forms — seguidillas, tiranas, polos, fandangos — rather than Italian operatic conventions, and their vernacular wit made them a genuinely popular counterweight to opera seria. The genre declined in the early 19th century as tastes changed, but it fed directly into the 19th-century zarzuela and the later género chico, and it remains an important source for the social history of Enlightenment Spain.
