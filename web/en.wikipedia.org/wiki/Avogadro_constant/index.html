Avogadro constant

Avogadro constant
SymbolNA or L
Value6.02214076 × 1023 mol−1 (exact)
Named afterAmedeo Avogadro
StatusDefining constant of the SI since 2019

The Avogadro constant, commonly denoted NA, is the number of constituent particles (usually atoms or molecules) contained in one mole of a substance. Since the 2019 revision of the SI, its value is defined exactly as 6.02214076 × 1023 reciprocal moles, making the Avogadro constant the defining constant of the mole itself.

History

The constant is named after the Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro (1776–1856), who proposed in 1811 that equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules — though he never estimated the number itself. Johann Josef Loschmidt made the first rough estimate in 1865 (German-language literature often uses "Loschmidt constant"). Jean Perrin's experiments on Brownian motion and sedimentation equilibrium (1908–1909) produced accurate values and provided decisive evidence for the reality of atoms; he proposed naming the number in Avogadro's honour and won the 1926 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Measurement and redefinition

Twentieth-century determinations used electrochemistry (via the Faraday constant), X-ray crystallography of silicon, and other methods. The international Avogadro project counted atoms in near-perfect silicon-28 spheres, achieving the precision needed for the 2019 SI redefinition, in which the mole ceased to be defined by the mass of carbon-12 and was instead fixed by assigning the constant its exact value.

Role in science

The Avogadro constant links the microscopic and macroscopic worlds: it connects the atomic mass unit to the gram-scale molar mass, appears in the definitions of the gas constant (R = NAkB) and the Faraday constant (F = NAe), and underlies stoichiometric calculation throughout chemistry.
