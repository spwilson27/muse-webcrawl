Trabeculae carneae

The trabeculae carneae (columnae carneae, or meaty ridges) are rounded or irregular muscular columns which project from the inner surface of the right and left ventricles of the heart. They should not be confused with the pectinate muscles, which are found in the atria and are morphologically distinct.

Interior of a ventricle showing the trabeculae carneae.

Types

There are three kinds of trabeculae carneae:

  • Ridges, which are attached along their entire length on one side and merely form prominent ridges on the ventricular wall.
  • Bridges, which are attached at both ends but free in the middle, spanning across the ventricular cavity.
  • Pillars, which are attached by their bases to the ventricular wall while their apices give origin to the chordae tendineae; these are the papillary muscles.

Function

The trabeculae carneae help to prevent suction that would occur with a smooth-surfaced membrane and thus impair the heart's ability to pump efficiently. They also assist the papillary muscles, via the chordae tendineae, in preventing inversion of the atrioventricular valves during ventricular contraction. The moderator band (septomarginal trabecula) of the right ventricle is a specialised trabecula that carries part of the right bundle branch of the cardiac conduction system to the anterior papillary muscle, coordinating contraction.

Clinical significance

Excessive or abnormal trabeculation of the left ventricle is associated with left ventricular non-compaction cardiomyopathy, a condition in which prominent trabeculae and deep recesses between them can impair cardiac function and promote thrombus formation. Prominent trabeculae are also a normal finding in some individuals and in athletes, so imaging criteria must be applied carefully.
