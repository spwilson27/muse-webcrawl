Forging

TypeMetalworking process
MethodShaping metal by compressive force (hammering or pressing)
VariantsOpen-die, closed-die (impression), roll, upset forging
TemperaturesHot, warm and cold forging
Typical productsCrankshafts, connecting rods, gears, hand tools, bolts

Forging is a manufacturing process involving the shaping of metal using localised compressive forces, delivered by hammers, presses or dies. It is one of the oldest known metalworking techniques, practised by smiths for thousands of years, and remains essential for parts that demand high strength and fatigue resistance. Forging refines the grain structure of the metal and aligns it with the shape of the part, producing components that are generally stronger than equivalent castings or machined parts.

Processes

In open-die forging, the workpiece is shaped between flat or simply contoured dies that do not enclose it, as in a blacksmith's work at the anvil. Closed-die (impression-die) forging forces hot metal into a die cavity of the desired shape; excess metal squeezed out forms a thin "flash" that is later trimmed. Roll forging shapes stock between rotating rolls, while upset forging increases a bar's diameter by compressing its length, as in making bolt heads.

Hot, warm and cold forging

Most forging is done hot, with the workpiece heated above its recrystallisation temperature so it deforms easily and can be reworked without hardening. Cold forging, performed at or near room temperature, gives excellent surface finish and dimensional accuracy and work-hardens the part, but requires much greater forces. Warm forging occupies an intermediate range, balancing formability against precision.

Equipment

Traditional forging used the smith's hammer and anvil; industrial practice employs mechanical and hydraulic presses, power hammers and drop hammers, which strike the workpiece with a falling weight. Modern forging lines are highly automated and supply the automotive, aerospace, rail, energy and defence industries.

History

Forged artefacts in copper and bronze date to prehistory; iron forging spread in the Iron Age, and water-powered trip hammers mechanised the craft in medieval Europe. The steam hammer of the nineteenth century allowed very large forgings for ships and artillery, and the process was a foundation of the Industrial Revolution.
