RS-232

RS-232, in telecommunications, is a standard originally introduced in 1960 for serial communication transmission of data. It formally defines signals connecting between a DTE (data terminal equipment) such as a computer terminal or PC, and a DCE (data circuit-terminating equipment) such as a modem. The standard defines electrical characteristics, signalling rates, and connector pinouts.

Electrical characteristics

RS-232 signals are single-ended and represented by voltages: a voltage between +3 V and +15 V represents binary 0 ("space"), while −3 V to −15 V represents binary 1 ("mark"), with the range between −3 V and +3 V undefined. Typical implementations use ±5 V, ±10 V, or ±12 V. Data is transmitted asynchronously as a stream of bits framed by start and stop bits, usually at rates from 300 to 115,200 baud in common PC use. Hardware flow control may use the RTS/CTS lines; many signals defined in the standard were rarely implemented on personal computers, which popularized the 9-pin DE-9 connector in place of the original 25-pin DB-25.

History and legacy

Published by the Electronic Industries Association (EIA), the standard went through revisions (RS-232-C in 1969, later TIA-232-F) and defined the serial ports that were ubiquitous on computers until the early 2000s. It has largely been replaced by USB for consumer peripherals, but survives in industrial equipment, networking console ports, scientific instruments, and embedded systems, often through USB-to-serial adapters.
