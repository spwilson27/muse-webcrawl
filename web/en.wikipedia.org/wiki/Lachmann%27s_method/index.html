Lachmann's method

Lachmann's method is a technique of textual criticism associated with the German philologist Karl Lachmann (1793–1851). It seeks to reconstruct the earliest recoverable form of a text by systematically comparing all surviving manuscript witnesses.

Procedure

The method involves three stages: recensio, the collection and comparison of witnesses, in which shared errors are used to build a family tree (stemma codicum) and eliminate manuscripts copied from other surviving copies; examinatio, judging which readings of the archetype are authentic; and emendatio, correcting the archetype where all witnesses are corrupt.

Influence and criticism

Lachmann's approach, applied in his editions of Lucretius and the New Testament, laid the foundations of modern stemmatics. Critics such as Joseph Bédier argued that the method imposes artificial two-branched stemmata and excessive confidence in mechanical reconstruction; later theorists, including Sebastiano Timpanaro, examined its origins and limits in detail.
