Self-propelled artillery

Self-propelled artillery (also called locomobile artillery) is artillery equipped with its own propulsion system to move towards its target. Within the term are covered self-propelled guns (or howitzers), self-propelled mortars and rocket artillery; such vehicles combine the firepower of towed artillery with far greater battlefield mobility, allowing them to deploy, fire and relocate quickly — "shoot and scoot" — to avoid counter-battery fire.

History

The first self-propelled guns appeared in the First World War, such as the British Gun Carrier Mark I. Between the wars, mechanisation accelerated development, and in the Second World War self-propelled artillery became ubiquitous: German Sturmgeschütz assault guns, Soviet SU-series vehicles, and American M7 Priest and M109 predecessors all saw extensive service. Rocket artillery such as the Soviet Katyusha also used self-propelled launchers.

Modern systems

Contemporary self-propelled howitzers include the American M109 Paladin, German Panzerhaubitze 2000, South Korean K9 Thunder, French CAESAR (a wheeled system), and Russian 2S19 Msta-S. Modern systems typically fire 155 mm shells to ranges exceeding 30 km, and over 40 km with assisted projectiles, with automated laying and loading enabling rates of fire of six to ten rounds per minute.

Comparison with towed artillery

Self-propelled systems are heavier and more expensive than towed guns but protect their crews, deploy faster, and keep pace with mechanised forces; towed artillery remains cheaper, air-transportable and easier to conceal.
