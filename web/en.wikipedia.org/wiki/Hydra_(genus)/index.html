Hydra (genus)

Hydra is a genus of small freshwater hydrozoans of the phylum Cnidaria. Hydras are found in temperate and tropical fresh water worldwide and are of particular interest to biologists for their extraordinary regenerative abilities and apparent biological immortality — they do not appear to senesce under laboratory conditions.

Anatomy

The body is a hollow tube, typically 1 to 20 mm long, with a basal disc ("foot") that secretes a sticky substance for attachment, and a mouth surrounded by one to twelve tentacles armed with stinging cells called cnidocytes, each containing a nematocyst used to capture prey such as small crustaceans. The body wall consists of only two cell layers with a nerve net but no brain or organs.

Reproduction and regeneration

Hydra reproduce asexually by budding: a protrusion of the body wall grows into a complete new individual that detaches when mature. Under stress they can also reproduce sexually. If cut into pieces, each piece can regenerate a complete organism, a property first studied in the 1740s by Abraham Trembley. This regeneration is driven by three stem-cell lineages, and the animals show no measurable increase in mortality with age, making them a model for research on aging.

Ecology

Some species, such as the green hydra Hydra viridissima, host symbiotic algae that photosynthesize within their cells. Hydras are preyed upon by flatworms and other small invertebrates.
