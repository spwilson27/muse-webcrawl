In mathematics and signal processing, the Z-transform converts a discrete-time signal, which is a sequence of real or complex numbers, into a complex frequency-domain representation. It can be considered a discrete-time equivalent of the Laplace transform. This similarity is explored in the theory of time-scale calculus.

The z-plane, with a region of convergence shown as an annulus.

Definition

The Z-transform of a discrete-time signal x[n] is the function X(z) defined as

X(z) = Σn=−∞∞ x[n] z−n

where n is an integer and z is a complex number. This is the bilateral (two-sided) Z-transform; the unilateral transform sums from n = 0. The set of values of z for which the series converges is called the region of convergence (ROC).

History

The basic idea dates to Laplace and was reintroduced in 1947 by Witold Hurewicz. The name "Z-transform" was coined by researchers in the sampled-data control group at Columbia University in 1952, including John Ragazzini and Lotfi Zadeh.

Properties and applications

The transform is linear, and convolution of sequences in the time domain corresponds to multiplication in the z-domain, which makes it the principal tool for analysing linear time-invariant discrete systems, digital filters, and sampled-data control systems. The poles and zeros of X(z) determine system stability and frequency response; a causal system is stable when all poles lie inside the unit circle. The inverse Z-transform is computed by a contour integral or by partial-fraction expansion and table lookup.
