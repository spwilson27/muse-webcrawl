Catacombs of Paris

The Catacombs of Paris (French: Catacombes de Paris) are underground ossuaries beneath Paris, France, which hold the remains of more than six million people in a small part of the ancient Mines of Paris tunnel network. The ossuary was created at the end of the 18th century to solve the problem of the city's overflowing cemeteries, whose decomposition had become a serious public health hazard to nearby residents. The official museum occupies a 1.5-kilometre circuit in the 14th arrondissement, while the full network of quarries extends for some 280 kilometres beneath the city.

History

Most Parisian churches had their own burial grounds, but by the late 1700s the Saints-Innocents cemetery in the centre of the city had accumulated two million bodies over ten centuries. In 1786, work began to transfer remains from city cemeteries to the disused limestone quarries at Tombe-Issoire; nightly processions of covered carts carried bones to be dumped into the quarry shafts. The transfers continued through the 19th century, including remains displaced by Haussmann's renovation of Paris. The crypt took on its romantic, organised character — bones arranged in walls with decorative patterns and commemorative plaques — under Inspector Louis-Étienne Héricart de Thury after 1810.

The mines of Paris

Beyond the official ossuary, the abandoned quarries attract urban explorers known as cataphiles, despite entry being illegal since 1955; a dedicated police unit patrols the tunnels. During the Second World War, members of the French Resistance used the tunnels, while German forces built a bunker beneath the Lycée Montaigne.
