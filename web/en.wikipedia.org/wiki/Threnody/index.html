Threnody

A threnody is a lament or song of mourning composed or performed as a memorial to a dead person. The term derives from the Greek thrēnōidia, from thrēnos ("wailing, lament") and ōidē ("ode, song"). Threnodies were a recognised genre of ancient Greek poetry, associated with poets such as Simonides and Pindar, and the form has been revived throughout Western literature and music for elegiac purposes.

In literature

In classical Greece the thrēnos was the dirge sung by professional mourners and the family over the body of the deceased, contrasting with the communal hymn of praise. The genre survives in passages of Homer and in tragedy. Later literature adopted "threnody" as a title for poems of lament: Ralph Waldo Emerson's "Threnody" (1847) mourns his young son Waldo, and the term is applied to works in the elegiac tradition more broadly.

In music

Composers have used the title for memorial works, most famously Krzysztof Penderecki's Threnody to the Victims of Hiroshima (1960), for 52 string instruments, a landmark of avant-garde sonorism whose searing textures were associated by the composer with the victims of the atomic bombing after its composition. Other notable threnodies include works written in memory of public tragedies and of individual artists.

Related forms

  • Elegy — a reflective poem of lament, often for an individual.
  • Dirge — a short hymn or song of mourning, often liturgical.
  • Requiem — the Mass for the dead and its musical settings.
  • Monody — in its original sense, a lament sung by a single voice.
