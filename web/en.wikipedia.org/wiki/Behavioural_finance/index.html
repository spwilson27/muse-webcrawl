Behavioural finance

Behavioural finance is the study of how psychological influences and cognitive biases affect the financial behaviour of investors and practitioners, and consequently the operation of financial markets. It challenges the assumptions of fully rational agents underlying the efficient-market hypothesis, and seeks to explain market anomalies — bubbles, crashes, excess volatility and momentum — through systematic errors in human judgement combined with limits to arbitrage that prevent rational traders from immediately correcting mispricing.

Foundations

The field draws on the heuristics-and-biases research of Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in the 1970s, especially their prospect theory (1979), which describes how people evaluate gains and losses relative to a reference point, overweight losses relative to equivalent gains (loss aversion), and distort probabilities. Richard Thaler brought these ideas into economics, documenting anomalies such as the endowment effect and mental accounting. In 2013 Robert Shiller shared the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for work including behavioural explanations of asset-price volatility; Thaler received the prize in 2017, and Kahneman in 2002.

Key biases and findings

  • Overconfidence — investors trade excessively and underestimate risk (Barber and Odean showed heavy traders underperform).
  • Disposition effect — the tendency to sell winning investments too early and hold losers too long.
  • Herding and bubbles — investors imitate others, inflating speculative episodes (Shiller's Irrational Exuberance).
  • Anchoring, representativeness and availability — judgement biased by salient reference points and recent events.
  • Equity premium puzzle and excess volatility — stock returns and price swings larger than standard rational models predict (Mehra and Prescott; Shiller).
  • Momentum and long-run reversal — medium-term continuation and long-term overreaction in returns (De Bondt and Thaler).

Limits to arbitrage

Behavioural finance explains why mispricing can persist: arbitrage is risky (fundamental and noise-trader risk), costly, and constrained by short-sale limits and agency problems faced by professional fund managers. Andrei Shleifer and Robert Vishny's work formalised these limits, so prices can deviate from fundamental value for extended periods.

Criticism

Defenders of efficient markets, notably Eugene Fama, argue that the anomalies are inconsistent, often offsetting (overreaction and underreaction), sensitive to methodology, and difficult to exploit profitably after costs. Behavioural researchers reply that the patterns are systematic, predictable from psychological principles, and important for corporate finance, policy and market design — including "nudge"-style interventions such as automatic enrolment in pension saving.
