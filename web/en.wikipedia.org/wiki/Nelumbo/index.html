Nelumbo

Nelumbo is a genus of aquatic plants with large, showy flowers, commonly known as lotus. It is the only living genus in the family Nelumbonaceae. The genus has two living species: Nelumbo nucifera, the sacred lotus native to Asia, and Nelumbo lutea, the American lotus native to North America. Despite the common name, the genus is not closely related to the water lilies (Nymphaeaceae), to which it was once assigned; it belongs instead to the order Proteales.

Description

Nelumbo plants are rooted in the mud of ponds and slow rivers, with leaves and flowers raised above the water on long stalks. The round, peltate leaves are famously water-repellent — the "lotus effect" — because of microscopic wax structures on their surface. The flowers, pink or white in N. nucifera and pale yellow in N. lutea, are thermogenic and mature into a distinctive conical seed receptacle. Lotus seeds are exceptionally long-lived; seeds more than a thousand years old have been germinated.

Uses and symbolism

Nearly all parts of the sacred lotus are eaten in Asian cuisines: the rhizomes ("lotus root"), seeds, young leaves, and stamens are used in cooking and traditional medicine. The flower is a profound religious symbol in Hinduism and Buddhism, representing purity, enlightenment, and rebirth, and is the national flower of India and Vietnam.

Fossil record

Fossils show that Nelumbo and its relatives were once far more widespread, with numerous extinct species known from the Cretaceous and Cenozoic of North America, Europe, and Asia.
