14-bis

The Santos-Dumont 14-bis was an early powered aircraft built by the Brazilian aviation pioneer Alberto Santos-Dumont in France in 1906. On 23 October 1906, at Bagatelle in Paris, it made the first powered, heavier-than-air flight in Europe — and the first anywhere to be officially observed and certified by a recognised aviation authority, the Aéro-Club de France. On 12 November 1906 it flew 220 metres, winning the Aéro-Club's prize for the first flight over 100 metres.

Design

The 14-bis was a canard biplane of box-kite construction, with the tail surfaces at the front and a pusher propeller at the rear, powered by a 50 hp Antoinette water-cooled V8 engine. The pilot stood in a wicker basket. Its name arose because it was first tested suspended beneath Santos-Dumont's No. 14 airship. Initially flown with dihedral wings, it later received primitive ailerons between the wings — among the first effective lateral controls.

Flights and significance

The October hop of about 60 metres at 3 metres altitude won the Archdeacon Prize for the first flight over 25 metres; the November flight set the first world record recognised by the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale. Because the Wright brothers' 1903–05 flights had been unpublicised and unwitnessed by officials, Europeans long credited the 14-bis flights as the first true aeroplane flights; the 14-bis remains a point of pride in Brazil, where Santos-Dumont is honoured as the "father of aviation." The aircraft itself flew only a few more times before being retired in 1907.
