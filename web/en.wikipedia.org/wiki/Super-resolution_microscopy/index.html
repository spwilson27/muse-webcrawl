Super-resolution microscopy

Super-resolution methods reveal structures such as cytoskeletal filaments at tens-of-nanometres resolution.

Super-resolution microscopy is a series of techniques in optical microscopy that allow images to be taken with a resolution higher than the diffraction limit of light, roughly 200–250 nanometres for conventional fluorescence microscopy. Since their development in the 1990s and 2000s, super-resolution methods have allowed cell biologists to image structures and molecular assemblies inside cells at resolutions down to a few tens of nanometres, and in some cases near-molecular resolution.

The 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to Eric Betzig, Stefan W. Hell, and William E. Moerner "for the development of super-resolved fluorescence microscopy."

Techniques

Stimulated emission depletion (STED)

STED, developed by Stefan Hell and Jan Wichmann in 1994, uses a second, doughnut-shaped laser beam to deplete fluorescence everywhere except a tiny central region, shrinking the effective point spread function. Scanning this sub-diffraction spot builds up a high-resolution image. STED was the first far-field super-resolution method demonstrated experimentally (1999).

Single-molecule localisation microscopy (PALM/STORM)

Photoactivated localisation microscopy (PALM) and stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM), reported in 2006, switch on only a sparse subset of fluorescent molecules at a time. Each isolated molecule's position is localised precisely by fitting its point spread function, and thousands of frames are combined into a reconstructed image. Resolution of about 20 nanometres is routine.

Structured illumination microscopy (SIM)

SIM projects a patterned illumination onto the sample; the moiré interference between pattern and sample structure shifts otherwise unresolvable high-frequency information into the observable range, doubling resolution to about 100 nanometres, with faster imaging and lower light doses than most competing methods.

MINFLUX and later developments

MINFLUX, introduced in 2017, combines the concepts of STED and single-molecule localisation to reach resolutions of 1–3 nanometres with far fewer photons. DNA-PAINT and expansion microscopy offer complementary routes to nanoscale imaging.
