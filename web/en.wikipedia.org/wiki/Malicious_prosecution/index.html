Malicious prosecution

Malicious prosecution is a common law intentional tort available in many jurisdictions, allowing a person who has been subjected to groundless legal proceedings to recover damages. A claimant must generally show that the defendant instituted (or continued) criminal — and in some jurisdictions civil — proceedings against them, that the proceedings terminated in the claimant's favour, that there was no reasonable and probable cause, and that the defendant acted with malice, meaning an improper purpose other than bringing an offender to justice.

Elements

The classic statement of the tort comes from cases such as Martin v Watson [1996] in English law, which clarified when a complainant who lays information before the police counts as the effective prosecutor. The "favourable termination" requirement means an acquittal, a discontinuance or a conviction overturned on appeal — not merely a procedural dismissal that leaves guilt open. Lack of probable cause and malice are distinct but often inferred from one another: a prosecution launched without honest belief in guilt, or to extort money or silence a critic, supplies both.

Scope and related claims

In England and Wales the tort traditionally covers malicious criminal prosecutions and certain civil proceedings (extended by the Supreme Court in Willers v Joyce [2016] to civil claims generally). In the United States the tort is recognised in most states, and a federal constitutional analogue exists under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 following Thompson v. Clark (2022), which held that a plaintiff need only show the prosecution ended without a conviction. The tort differs from abuse of process, which concerns using a properly issued process for an ulterior purpose, and from false imprisonment, which concerns unlawful detention.

Rationale

Courts balance two policies: compensating victims of baseless prosecutions and avoiding a chilling effect on citizens and officials who report crime in good faith. The demanding elements — particularly proof of malice — reflect that balance, and successful claims remain comparatively rare.
