Investigative journalism

[Image: Boxes of documents and highlighted records in an investigations office]

Investigative journalism is a form of reporting in which journalists deeply investigate a topic — typically wrongdoing, corruption or hidden systems of power — over months or years, using documents, data, sources and sometimes undercover methods, with the aim of exposure and reform.

Landmarks

The tradition runs from the muckrakers of the Progressive Era and Nellie Bly's asylum exposé (1887), through the Pentagon Papers and Watergate (1972–74), to cross-border collaborations such as the Panama Papers (2016) coordinated by the ICIJ, and non-profit newsrooms like ProPublica.

Methods and risks

Investigative work relies on public-records requests, leaked documents, forensic accounting and data analysis; reporters face legal threats, surveillance and, in many countries, violence.
