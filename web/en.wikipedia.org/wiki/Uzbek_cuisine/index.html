Uzbek cuisine

Uzbek cuisine is the traditional cooking of Uzbekistan in Central Asia, shaped by the region's grain farming, nomadic herding heritage, and its position on the Silk Road, which brought influences from Persian, Turkic, Mongol, Russian, and other cuisines. Mutton is the most common meat, and meals are traditionally served communally, accompanied by green tea.

Signature dishes

  • Plov (also called osh) — the national dish: rice cooked in a large cast-iron cauldron (qozon) over an open flame with lamb, onions, yellow carrots, and spices, often garnished with chickpeas, raisins, or quail eggs. Regional variants exist for Samarkand, Bukhara, Ferghana, and Tashkent.
  • Samsa — baked triangular or round pastries filled with minced lamb and onions, traditionally baked in a tandoor (tandir) oven.
  • Manti — large steamed dumplings filled with spiced meat.
  • Shashlik — skewers of marinated meat grilled over charcoal.
  • Lagman — hand-pulled noodles served in a spiced broth with vegetables and meat, reflecting Uyghur and Chinese influence.
  • Norin — finely sliced noodles with boiled horse meat.

Bread and accompaniments

Bread (non or obi non) is sacred in Uzbek culture and is baked in tandoor ovens as round, stamped loaves; it is never placed face-down or wasted. Meals typically begin with tea, sweets, and fruit, and may include salads such as achichuk (tomato and onion). Cottonseed oil and lamb tail fat (kurdyuk) are traditional cooking fats, and spices include cumin, coriander, barberries, and black caraway.
