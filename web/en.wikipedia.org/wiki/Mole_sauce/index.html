Mole sauce

Mole (from Nahuatl mōlli, "sauce") is a generic name for a number of sauces originally used in Mexican cuisine, characterised by a complex, layered flavour produced by blending many ingredients — most characteristically dried chiles, spices, nuts or seeds, and often a small amount of chocolate — into a thick sauce. In contemporary Mexico the best-known varieties are mole poblano, from the state of Puebla, and the seven moles of Oaxaca.

History

Sauces of ground chiles and seeds predate the Spanish conquest of Mexico, and the Nahua peoples prepared such sauces for ceremonial and everyday dishes. After the conquest, Old World ingredients — including almonds, raisins, cinnamon, and chocolate's transformation through added sugar — were incorporated, and legend credits the nuns of the Convent of Santa Rosa in Puebla with creating mole poblano, combining New and Old World ingredients, to serve to a visiting dignitary.

Varieties

  • Mole poblano – a dark reddish-brown sauce containing chocolate, typically served over turkey or chicken.
  • Mole negro – the darkest and most complex of the Oaxacan moles, made with chilhuacle chiles and chocolate.
  • Mole amarillo, coloradito, verde, chichilo, rojo, and manchamanteles – the other traditional Oaxacan moles, ranging from herb-based green sauces to fruity "tablecloth stainers".
  • Mole almendrado, mole de cacahuate – nut-based variants thickened with almonds or peanuts.

Preparation

Preparing a traditional mole is labour-intensive: dried chiles are toasted and soaked, spices are ground, nuts and seeds are fried, and the ingredients are blended and then slowly simmered, traditionally in a clay cazuela, until the sauce thickens and its flavours meld. Ready-made mole pastes are now widely sold in Mexican markets.
