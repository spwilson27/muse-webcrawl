An oil refinery or petroleum refinery is an industrial process plant where petroleum (crude oil) is transformed and refined into useful products such as gasoline (petrol), diesel fuel, asphalt base, fuel oils, heating oil, kerosene, liquefied petroleum gas and petroleum naphtha. Petrochemical feedstock like ethylene and propylene can also be produced directly by cracking crude oil without the need of using refined products of crude oil such as naphtha.

How a refinery works

The first stage is distillation: crude oil is heated and fed into a fractionating column, where hydrocarbons separate by boiling point — light gases and gasoline-range naphtha near the top, kerosene and diesel in the middle, and heavy gas oil and residue at the bottom. Because modern demand favours light products, refineries then convert heavy fractions into lighter ones by catalytic cracking and hydrocracking, breaking large molecules apart with heat, pressure and catalysts.

Other units rearrange molecules (catalytic reforming and isomerisation raise gasoline octane), treat products to remove sulfur and other impurities (hydrotreating), and blend finished fuels to specification. Delayed coking turns the heaviest residue into petroleum coke, and sulfur recovery units convert hydrogen sulfide into elemental sulfur.

Economics and scale

Refineries are capital-intensive and run continuously, typically processing from tens of thousands to over a million barrels of crude per day. Profitability is measured by the "crack spread" — the difference between crude cost and the value of the product slate. Complexity indices such as the Nelson index rate a refinery's ability to upgrade cheap heavy crude into valuable light products.

Environmental and safety issues

Refineries emit air pollutants, greenhouse gases and wastewater contaminants, and are major point sources near the often low-income communities that surround them, raising environmental justice concerns. Process safety is a persistent challenge: accidents such as the 2005 Texas City refinery explosion, which killed 15 workers, led to major reforms in process safety management.
