High-frequency trading

High-frequency trading
TypeAlgorithmic trading strategy
CharacteristicsHigh speed, high turnover, co-location, short holding periods
IntroducedLate 1990s – early 2000s

High-frequency trading (HFT) is a type of algorithmic trading in finance characterised by high speeds, high turnover rates, and high order-to-trade ratios. High-frequency traders use sophisticated technological tools and computer algorithms to rapidly trade securities, often holding positions for only fractions of a second and ending each trading day with no open positions.

Co-located servers minimise latency between trading firms and exchange matching engines.

Characteristics

HFT firms invest heavily in low-latency infrastructure, including co-location of servers at exchanges, microwave and millimetre-wave transmission links, and custom hardware such as FPGAs. Common strategies include electronic market making, statistical arbitrage, event arbitrage, and latency arbitrage across related instruments and venues.

History

High-frequency trading grew rapidly after the decimalisation of US stock prices in 2001 and regulatory changes such as Regulation NMS (2005), which fragmented trading across many venues. By around 2009–2010, estimates suggested HFT accounted for a majority of US equity trading volume. The Flash Crash of 6 May 2010, in which major indices plunged and recovered within minutes, intensified scrutiny of the practice, as did Michael Lewis's 2014 book Flash Boys.

Debate and regulation

Supporters argue that HFT narrows bid–ask spreads, adds liquidity, and improves price discovery. Critics contend that some strategies amount to front-running, that apparent liquidity vanishes in stressed markets, and that the speed race imposes social costs with little benefit. Regulators in the United States and Europe have introduced rules on market access, circuit breakers, and minimum resting times, and the topic remains contested in market microstructure research.
