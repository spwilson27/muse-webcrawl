John Maurice of Nassau-Siegen (Dutch: Johan Maurits van Nassau-Siegen; June 17, 1604 – December 20, 1679), called "the Brazilian," was a Dutch prince and soldier who served as governor-general of Dutch Brazil from 1636 to 1644. A grandson of John VI, Count of Nassau-Dillenburg, and thus a relative of the House of Orange, he pursued a military career in the service of the Dutch Republic.

Dutch Brazil

Appointed by the Dutch West India Company, Johan Maurits governed the colony in northeastern Brazil from Recife, which was renamed Mauritsstad in his honor. His administration combined military expansion with relative religious toleration and an exceptional program of cultural and scientific patronage: he brought artists such as Frans Post and Albert Eckhout and the naturalists Georg Marcgraf and Willem Piso to Brazil, producing an unrivaled visual and scientific record of the colony, including the Historia Naturalis Brasiliae (1648). His policies made him popular with many Portuguese planters, but disagreements with the Company over spending led to his recall in 1644; the Portuguese revolt against Dutch rule followed soon after his departure, and Dutch Brazil was lost by 1654.

Later life

Back in Europe, Johan Maurits served as stadtholder of Cleves for the Elector of Brandenburg and commissioned the Mauritshuis in The Hague (now a museum) and the gardens at Kleve. His Brazilian collections of art and ethnographic objects were dispersed to several European courts. Modern assessments of his legacy weigh his cultural patronage against his role in a slaveholding colonial regime; statues and institutional names connected to him have been subjects of public debate in the Netherlands.
