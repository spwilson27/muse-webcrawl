Draupner wave


The Draupner wave as recorded by laser sensors, 1 January 1995.

The Draupner wave was a large rogue wave measured on 1 January 1995 at the Draupner E oil platform in the North Sea, off the coast of Norway. It was the first rogue wave to be captured by scientific measuring instruments, providing firm evidence for a phenomenon that mariners had reported for centuries but which oceanographers had often dismissed as exaggeration.

Measurement

The Draupner platform, operated by Statoil, stood in about 70 metres of water roughly 160 kilometres southwest of Norway. During a storm with a significant wave height of approximately 11.8 metres, laser wave recorders on the platform registered a single wave with a maximum height of 25.6 metres — more than twice the significant wave height — with a steep crest some 18.5 metres above the still-water level. Minor damage was sustained by equipment on the platform, corroborating the recorded height.

Significance

Before 1995, rogue (or "freak") waves existed mainly in anecdotal accounts, and standard linear wave theory held that such extreme waves should be vanishingly rare. The Draupner measurement, presented by Sverre Haver and colleagues, demonstrated that waves far exceeding design expectations occur more frequently than Gaussian statistics predict. The event spurred intensive research into nonlinear wave dynamics, including modulational instability and the effects of crossing seas, and led to revisions in the design standards for offshore structures and ships.
