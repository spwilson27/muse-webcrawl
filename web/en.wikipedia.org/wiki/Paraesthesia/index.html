Paraesthesia

Transient paraesthesia is commonly felt in a limb that has "fallen asleep".

Paraesthesia is an abnormal sensation of the skin (such as tingling, pricking, chilling, burning or numbness) with no apparent physical cause. It is commonly known as a sensation of pins and needles. Paraesthesia may be transient or chronic, and may have many possible underlying causes.

Transient paraesthesia

Transient paraesthesia, such as that felt when a limb "falls asleep", is usually harmless and results from sustained pressure on a nerve or brief periods of reduced blood circulation. When the pressure is released, the sensation gradually subsides. Other transient causes include hyperventilation, dehydration, panic attacks, and certain medications.

Chronic paraesthesia

Chronic paraesthesia can be a symptom of a wide variety of disorders, including peripheral neuropathy (often associated with diabetes), vitamin B12 deficiency, multiple sclerosis, carpal tunnel syndrome, stroke, and damage to nerves from injury or infection. Persistent paraesthesia generally indicates a problem with the functioning of neurons in the sensory pathways.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis typically involves a medical history, neurological examination, blood tests, and sometimes nerve conduction studies or imaging. Treatment is directed at the underlying cause; symptomatic relief may be provided by medications used for neuropathic pain.
