Overdraft

An overdraft occurs when money is withdrawn from a bank account in excess of the available balance, so that the account balance becomes negative. In effect, the bank extends credit to the customer, who incurs a debt repayable on demand. Overdrafts may be authorised (arranged in advance with the bank, up to an agreed limit, sometimes called an overdraft facility or overdraft protection) or unauthorised (where the customer spends beyond any agreed limit, or with no facility at all), the latter typically attracting higher interest rates and penalty fees.

How overdrafts work

An arranged overdraft is a revolving credit facility attached to a current (checking) account. The customer may draw funds down to an agreed negative limit and pays interest — often expressed as an annual percentage rate (APR) or an "EAR" (effective annual rate) in the United Kingdom — only on the amount borrowed and only for the days it is used. Because the credit is unsecured and repayable on demand, interest rates are typically much higher than for term loans or credit cards used for planned borrowing, commonly around 35–40% EAR at British high-street banks since regulatory reforms in 2020. Some accounts offer an interest-free buffer of a small amount.

History

The overdraft is one of the oldest forms of bank lending. It is commonly credited to William Hogg, a Scottish merchant who in 1728 obtained from the Royal Bank of Scotland the first known overdraft facility, allowing him to draw £1,000 more than he held in his account. The flexibility of the arrangement — borrow and repay at will, paying interest only on the daily balance — made overdrafts a staple of commercial banking, especially for businesses managing irregular cash flow.

Charges and controversy

Unauthorised overdrafts and returned-item ("bounced payment") fees have been controversial in many jurisdictions. Critics have argued that complex fee structures made the true cost of borrowing opaque and that charges fell disproportionately on financially vulnerable customers. In the United States, a 2010 amendment to Regulation E required banks to obtain customers' opt-in consent before charging fees for covering debit-card overdrafts. In the United Kingdom, the Financial Conduct Authority introduced reforms effective April 2020 that banned fixed daily or monthly overdraft fees and required a single interest rate, after finding that firms earned over £2.4 billion from overdrafts in 2017, with more than half of unauthorised-overdraft fees coming from just 1.5% of customers.

Business overdrafts

For businesses, an overdraft facility is a common form of working-capital finance, used to bridge the gap between paying suppliers and receiving payment from customers. Banks normally review the facility periodically, may require security or personal guarantees, and can reduce or withdraw the limit — technically the debt is repayable on demand at any time.
