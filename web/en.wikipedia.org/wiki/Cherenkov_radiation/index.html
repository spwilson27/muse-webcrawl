Overview

Cherenkov radiation is electromagnetic radiation emitted when a charged particle, such as an electron, passes through a dielectric medium at a speed greater than the phase velocity of light in that medium. It is characterised by a distinctive blue glow, famously seen in the water surrounding nuclear reactor cores.

The effect is analogous to a sonic boom: just as an object travelling faster than sound produces a shock wave, a particle travelling faster than light in a medium produces a cone of coherent light. The radiation is named after the Soviet physicist Pavel Cherenkov, who first detected it in 1934 and shared the 1958 Nobel Prize in Physics with Ilya Frank and Igor Tamm, who developed the theory of the effect.

Physical origin

In a medium with refractive index n, light travels at c/n rather than c. A charged particle moving faster than c/n polarises the molecules of the medium along its path; as those molecules relax back to their ground state they emit photons. When the particle outruns the light it generates, the wavefronts constructively interfere along a cone whose opening angle depends on the particle's speed, allowing the velocity to be measured from the geometry of the cone.

Applications

Cherenkov detectors are widely used in particle physics to identify particles and measure their velocities. Large water Cherenkov detectors such as Super-Kamiokande in Japan use vast tanks of ultra-pure water lined with photomultiplier tubes to detect neutrinos. The blue glow of spent fuel pools and reactor cores is a direct, visible manifestation of the effect, and Cherenkov imaging is also explored in medical physics for radiotherapy dose verification.
