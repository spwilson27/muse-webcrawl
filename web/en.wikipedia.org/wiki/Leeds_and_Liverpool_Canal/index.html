Leeds and Liverpool Canal

The Leeds and Liverpool Canal is a canal in Northern England, linking the cities of Leeds and Liverpool. At 127 miles (204 km) long, it is the longest canal in northern England, crossing the Pennines and incorporating 91 locks on its main line. Construction began in 1770 and was completed in 1816, making it one of the major achievements of the canal age.

History

The canal was promoted by merchants in Yorkshire and Lancashire who wanted a trans-Pennine route linking the woollen districts of the West Riding with the port of Liverpool and the coalfields of south Lancashire. Because it was built for broad barges, its locks are 62 feet (19 m) long and 14 feet 3 inches (4.3 m) wide. The route follows the Aire valley west from Leeds, climbs through the spectacular Bingley Five Rise and Three Rise staircase locks, crosses the Pennine watershed at Foulridge Tunnel, and descends through Burnley, Blackburn, Chorley and Wigan to Liverpool.

Features

Notable structures include the Bingley Five Rise Locks, the longest staircase in Britain when built; the 1,640-yard (1,500 m) Foulridge Tunnel; the Burnley Embankment, an embankment carrying the canal nearly 60 feet (18 m) above the town, sometimes counted among the "Seven Wonders of the Waterways"; and the Stanley Dock complex in Liverpool. The canal's Liverpool terminus was extended to the Pier Head by the Canal Link opened in 2009.

Commerce and decline

The canal carried coal, limestone, grain and manufactured goods throughout the nineteenth century. Commercial traffic declined sharply after the Second World War, with the last regular long-distance cargoes ending in the 1960s. Branches included the Bradford Canal (closed 1922) and the Leigh Branch, which connects with the Bridgewater Canal.

Today

The canal is now a popular leisure cruising route and is managed by the Canal & River Trust. Its towpath is used by walkers and cyclists, and the main line forms part of the trans-Pennine "Desmond Family Canoe Trail" route.
