Treadmill of production

The treadmill of production is a theory in environmental sociology describing how modern capitalist economies are structurally driven toward ever-expanding production and consumption, with escalating environmental harm. The concept was introduced by sociologist Allan Schnaiberg in his 1980 book The Environment: From Surplus to Scarcity.

Theory

In the model, producers invest profits in labor-saving, resource-intensive technologies to stay competitive; displaced workers and the demand for jobs push the state and labor unions to support further growth; and consumption must rise to absorb output. The result is a self-reinforcing cycle of "withdrawals" (resource extraction) and "additions" (pollution and waste) that cannot slow without threatening employment and profits. Environmental reforms are constrained because firms, workers, and governments all depend on continued expansion.

Development and criticism

Schnaiberg and later collaborators such as Kenneth Gould and Adam Weinberg extended the framework to globalization, arguing the treadmill has become transnational. The theory is a cornerstone of environmental sociology and has been applied to topics from logging conflicts to climate change. Critics note it can understate the potential of green technology, regulation, and shifts in consumer values to decouple growth from environmental damage, themes taken up by ecological modernization theory as a rival framework.
