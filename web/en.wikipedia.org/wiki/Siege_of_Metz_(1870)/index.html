Siege of Metz (1870)

The siege of Metz was a battle fought during the Franco-Prussian War from 19 August to 27 October 1870 and ended in a decisive Prussian victory. The French Army of the Rhine, commanded by Marshal François Achille Bazaine, retreated into the fortifications of Metz after the Battle of Gravelotte and was encircled there by the Prussian Second Army under Prince Friedrich Karl. The capitulation of the fortress, with more than 140,000 French soldiers, was one of the great disasters of the war for France.

Background

After defeats on the frontier, Bazaine's Army of the Rhine — the main French field army in the east — attempted to retreat on Verdun and Châlons but was brought to battle at Mars-la-Tour and Gravelotte and driven back into Metz, where it was cut off from the rest of France.

The siege

Bazaine made only half-hearted attempts to break out, notably at Noisseville (31 August–1 September) and Bellevue (7 October). Inside the city, food and forage ran short, the troops ate the cavalry horses, and disease spread. Politically paralysed after the fall of the Second Empire at Sedan, Bazaine also engaged in secret negotiations with the Prussians. On 27 October 1870 the army capitulated — some 142,000 men marched into captivity, the largest surrender of the war. The fall of Metz freed the Prussian Second Army to campaign against the French armies of the Loire.

Aftermath

Bazaine was tried for treason after the war and sentenced to death, the sentence being commuted to imprisonment, from which he escaped. Metz was annexed to the German Empire by the Treaty of Frankfurt (1871) and remained German until 1918.
