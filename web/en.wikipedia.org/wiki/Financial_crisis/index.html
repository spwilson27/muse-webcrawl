Financial crisis

A financial crisis is any of a variety of situations in which some financial assets suddenly lose a large part of their nominal value. Forms include bank runs and panics, stock market crashes, currency crises, sovereign defaults and bursting asset bubbles; many crises combine several forms and spill into the real economy as recessions.

TypesBanking, currency, speculative bubble, sovereign debt
Notable examples1929 crash, 1997 Asian crisis, 2007–08 global crisis

Causes

Scholars cite leverage and maturity mismatch in banks, asset-liability mismatches, herding and irrational exuberance, moral hazard, regulatory failure, fraud, and contagion through interlinked institutions and trade. Charles Kindleberger and Hyman Minsky described a recurring pattern: displacement, boom, euphoria, distress and panic.

Consequences

Crises cause bank failures, credit crunches, unemployment, fiscal costs from bailouts, and long-lasting output losses. Deflation and liquidity traps can follow, as after 1929 when the Great Depression spread worldwide.

Policy responses

Central banks act as lenders of last resort, governments recapitalise or guarantee banks, deposit insurance prevents runs, and macroprudential regulation, capital requirements (Basel accords) and resolution regimes aim to reduce systemic risk.
