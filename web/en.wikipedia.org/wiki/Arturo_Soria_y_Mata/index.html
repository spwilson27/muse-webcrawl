Arturo Soria y Mata (15 December 1844 – 6 November 1920) was a Spanish urban planner and engineer best known for developing the concept of the Linear City (Ciudad Lineal), one of the most original proposals in the history of urban planning.

The Linear City

Soria first published his idea in 1882 in the Madrid newspaper El Progreso. He proposed that cities should grow along a single spine — a wide avenue carrying a railway or tramway — rather than spreading out concentrically. Housing, industry and green space would be arranged in bands parallel to the transport axis, so that every resident lived close both to rapid transit and to the countryside. Water, electricity and other services would run along the same corridor. His motto captured the idea: "Ruralise urban life; urbanise rural life."

The Madrid project

In 1894 Soria founded the Compañía Madrileña de Urbanización to build a linear city around Madrid, planned as a ring roughly 55 kilometres long linking existing satellite towns. Only a section about five kilometres long was constructed in the north-east of the capital; it survives as the Ciudad Lineal district, and its main street still bears his name. The scheme attracted international attention and influenced later linear-city proposals, including Soviet planners' designs of the 1920s and 1930s, and remains a reference point in debates about transit-oriented development and urban sprawl.

Other work

Soria was also a prolific journalist and political figure, an advocate of the telephone network in Madrid — he proposed one of the city's first telephone concessions — and a deputy in the Spanish parliament. He wrote extensively on the social problems of the growing industrial city, arguing that transport infrastructure, rather than monumental architecture, should shape urban growth.
