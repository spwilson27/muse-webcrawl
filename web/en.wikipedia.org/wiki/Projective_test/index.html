Projective test

In psychology, a projective test is a personality test designed to let a person respond to ambiguous stimuli, presumably revealing hidden emotions, motivations, and internal conflicts. The underlying assumption, the projective hypothesis, is that individuals project their own unconscious feelings and attitudes onto the ambiguous material, and that these projections can be interpreted by a trained examiner.

Common tests

The best-known projective tests are the Rorschach inkblot test, developed by Hermann Rorschach in 1921, in which respondents describe what they see in a series of inkblots; and the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT), developed by Christiana Morgan and Henry Murray in the 1930s, in which respondents invent stories about ambiguous pictures. Others include sentence-completion tests, word association, and drawing tests such as Draw-a-Person.

Scoring and criticism

Some systems, notably John Exner's Comprehensive System for the Rorschach, attempt to standardize administration and scoring. Nevertheless, projective tests have been widely criticized for low reliability and validity, susceptibility to examiner bias, and weak empirical support compared with objective inventories such as the MMPI. Despite this, they remain in use in some clinical and forensic settings, usually as one component of a broader assessment.
