Archaic human

Fossil skull of an archaic human (Homo heidelbergensis).

Archaic humans is a broad category denoting any variety of Homo other than anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens) in the time period beginning 500,000 years ago. The category is frequently contrasted with the terms "anatomically modern humans", "early modern humans", and "behaviorally modern humans". The term is not used in formal taxonomic classification but remains common in paleoanthropological writing as a convenient grouping for Middle Pleistocene hominins.

Archaic humans are generally considered to have evolved from Homo erectus and to have given rise to modern humans in Africa and to the Neanderthals and Denisovans in Eurasia. They include such groups as Homo heidelbergensis, Homo rhodesiensis, Homo naledi, and the Neanderthals and Denisovans themselves, depending on the classification used.

Characteristics

Archaic humans typically possessed larger brains than Homo erectus, with cranial capacities often between 1,100 and 1,400 cubic centimetres, overlapping the modern human range. They retained some primitive features, including a prominent brow ridge, a receding forehead, and a robust skull without a strongly defined chin. Their stone tool industries, such as the Acheulean and Mousterian, show increasing sophistication, and there is evidence for controlled use of fire, cooperative hunting of large game, and in some populations deliberate burial of the dead.

Interbreeding

Genetic evidence published since 2010 has shown that archaic humans interbred with modern humans. Present-day non-African populations carry roughly 1–4% Neanderthal ancestry, while some Oceanian and Asian populations carry additional Denisovan ancestry. These findings have blurred the boundary between "archaic" and "modern" humans and suggest that the groups were regional populations of an interconnected species complex.
