Hard Times (novel)

Hard Times: For These Times (commonly known as Hard Times) is the tenth novel by Charles Dickens, first published in 1854. It was serialised in Dickens's weekly periodical Household Words between April and August 1854. The novel is a critique of utilitarianism and of the social and industrial conditions of Victorian England, set in the fictional northern mill town of Coketown, modelled on Preston.

Plot summary

The novel is divided into three books titled "Sowing", "Reaping", and "Garnering". Thomas Gradgrind, a wealthy, fact-obsessed retired merchant and school superintendent, raises his children Louisa and Tom on a rigid diet of facts, suppressing imagination and emotion. Louisa, emotionally starved, agrees to marry Josiah Bounderby, a bombastic, self-made mill owner and banker thirty years her senior, partly to secure her brother Tom's position at Bounderby's bank.

Tom, embittered and dissolute, robs the bank and frames Stephen Blackpool, an honest mill worker ("hand") who has refused to join a trade union and whose marriage to an alcoholic wife traps him. Sissy Jupe, a circus girl taken into the Gradgrind household after her father disappears, grows up with warmth and sympathy intact and becomes the family's moral centre. When the robbery is uncovered, Louisa confronts her father with the wreckage of her emotional life, and Gradgrind comes to recognise the failure of his philosophy. Stephen Blackpool, falsely accused, falls into a disused mine shaft on his way back to clear his name and dies; Tom escapes abroad with the help of the circus folk.

Themes

Dickens attacks the doctrine of utilitarianism, associated with thinkers such as Jeremy Bentham and James Mill, arguing that a society governed purely by fact, statistics, and self-interest crushes fancy, compassion, and individuality. The novel also addresses industrial labour relations, the divorce laws, and the condition of women. The famous opening chapter, in which Gradgrind demands "Facts" in the schoolroom, is among the most anthologised passages in Dickens.

Reception

Contemporary reviewers were divided; John Ruskin admired the novel's social purpose, while others found its characterisation schematic. F. R. Leavis championed it in The Great Tradition (1948), praising its "completely serious work of art" and moral intensity, which secured its modern critical reputation.
