Natron is a naturally occurring mixture of sodium carbonate decahydrate (Na2CO3·10H2O, a kind of soda ash) and around 17% sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3, baking soda), along with small quantities of sodium chloride and sodium sulfate. Natron is white to colourless in its pure form, varying to grey or yellow with impurities. Natron deposits are sometimes found in saline lake beds that arose in arid environments.

Etymology

The word natron derives, through Latin natrium and Greek nitron, from the ancient Egyptian nṯrj, meaning "divine (salt)". The chemical symbol for sodium, Na, comes from the related Latin natrium. The Egyptian valley of Wadi Natrun, west of the Nile Delta, was the classic ancient source and gives the material its name.

Occurrence

Natron crystallises from the brines of alkaline lakes rich in sodium carbonate when evaporation concentrates the water, typically in hot, arid climates. Important deposits occur in Egypt's Wadi Natrun, at Lake Natron in Tanzania, in the lakes of Chad, and in dry lake beds in the western United States and elsewhere. Natron is deliquescent in moist air and effloresces — losing its water of crystallisation — in dry air, converting toward thermonatrite.

Historical uses

For thousands of years, natron was used as a cleansing agent for the body and home, as a component in early glassmaking (as a flux lowering the melting point of silica), in ceramics and faience, for bleaching cloth, as a mouthwash and antiseptic, and in food preparation. Mixed with oil, it formed an early form of soap. Because it absorbs water, dry natron was also used as a desiccant and preservative for fish and meat.

Mummification

Natron's best-known use was in ancient Egyptian mummification. Embalmers packed the body and its cavities with dry natron for a period of about 40 days within the customary 70-day process, desiccating the tissues and inhibiting bacterial decay, before washing, anointing with oils and resins, and wrapping the body in linen. Herodotus described the process in his Histories.
