Mallet locomotive

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A Mallet compound articulated steam locomotive

A Mallet locomotive is a type of articulated steam railway locomotive, divided into two powered units. The front unit swivels on a pivot attached to the main frame; the back unit is rigidly attached to the main frame. Often there is a supporting truck at each end as well. Large Mallets are usually classified as having two driving groups of wheels.

The Mallet is characterised by being a compound locomotive, in which the steam is used twice, first in a set of high-pressure cylinders, and then in a set of low-pressure cylinders. This gives the Mallet a distinctive appearance, as the low-pressure cylinders, on the front engine unit, are much larger than the high-pressure cylinders on the rear unit.

History

The type was invented by Swiss engineer Anatole Mallet (1837–1919), who designed the first successful compound locomotives and patented the articulated compound system in 1884. The first Mallet locomotives were built in 1888 for narrow-gauge lines, where their ability to negotiate sharp curves while delivering high power made them attractive.

Mallets were widely used in Europe for heavy freight work before the type found its greatest success in North America, where railroads adopted them for slow, heavy drag freight service in mountainous territory. Notable American users included the Baltimore and Ohio, the Norfolk and Western, and the Union Pacific, whose huge simple-articulated "Big Boy" and "Challenger" types were developments of the Mallet principle, though they used single-expansion (simple) rather than compound working.

Design

In a true Mallet, the rear group of drivers is powered by high-pressure cylinders, and the exhaust steam from these is fed through a receiver pipe to the larger low-pressure cylinders driving the front, swinging engine unit. A flexible steam pipe connection crosses the hinge between the two units. Compounding improves fuel efficiency, although at the cost of more complex starting arrangements, and many later large articulateds reverted to simple expansion.

The articulation allows a very long locomotive to traverse curves that a rigid frame of the same length could not, making Mallets well suited to mountain railroading and heavy mineral traffic.
