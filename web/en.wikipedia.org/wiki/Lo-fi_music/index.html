Lo-fi music

Lo-fi (from low fidelity) is a music or production quality in which elements usually regarded as imperfections of a recording or performance are audible, sometimes as a deliberate aesthetic choice. The standards of sound quality and music production have evolved throughout the decades, meaning that some older examples of lo-fi may not originally have been recognised as such. Lo-fi began to be recognised as a style of popular music in the 1990s, when it became alternately known as DIY music.

Harmonic distortion, tape hiss, and phonographic imperfections such as wow and flutter are characteristic features, and the genre is closely associated with inexpensive recording equipment, bedroom production, and independent artists.

History

The roots of lo-fi stretch back to early home recordings and artists such as the Shaggs and R. Stevie Moore, who recorded at home decades before the practice was fashionable. In the 1980s and 1990s, the availability of inexpensive cassette four-track recorders such as the TASCAM Portastudio enabled a wave of indie rock artists — including Guided by Voices, Sebadoh, Pavement, and Daniel Johnston, who distributed hand-dubbed cassettes — to embrace imperfect recordings as a badge of authenticity in opposition to slick studio production. Beck's 1994 single "Loser", recorded in a kitchen, brought lo-fi aesthetics into the mainstream.

Lo-fi hip hop

In the 2010s, "lo-fi" came to denote a popular style of instrumental hip hop — often called lo-fi hip hop or "chillhop" — characterised by downtempo beats, jazz and soul samples, vinyl crackle, and a relaxed, nostalgic mood. Pioneered by producers such as J Dilla and Nujabes, the style spread widely through YouTube and streaming playlists, most famously the 24/7 "lofi hip hop radio" livestream, whose studying anime-girl mascot became an internet icon. The genre is widely used as background music for studying and relaxation.
