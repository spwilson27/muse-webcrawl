Ascomycota is a phylum (division) of the kingdom Fungi that, together with the Basidiomycota, forms the subkingdom Dikarya. Its members are commonly known as the sac fungi or ascomycetes. It is the largest phylum of fungi, with over 64,000 described species. The defining feature of the group is the ascus (from Greek askos, "sac" or "wineskin"), a microscopic sexual structure in which nonmotile spores, called ascospores, are formed.

Characteristics

Ascomycetes produce their sexual spores inside a sac-like cell, the ascus, which typically contains eight ascospores formed by meiosis followed by a mitotic division. Many species also reproduce asexually by means of conidia. The group includes single-celled yeasts as well as complex multicellular forms with large fruiting bodies (ascocarps), which may take the form of cups (apothecia), flasks (perithecia), or closed spheres (cleistothecia).

Ecology and importance

Ascomycetes are found in virtually every terrestrial and aquatic ecosystem, where they act as decomposers of dead organic matter, as symbionts in lichens and mycorrhizae, and as pathogens of plants and animals. Economically important members include the yeasts used in baking and brewing (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), the source of penicillin (Penicillium chrysogenum), edible morels and truffles, and serious crop pathogens such as the agent of Dutch elm disease.

Classification

The phylum is divided into three major subphyla: the Saccharomycotina (the true yeasts), the Pezizomycotina (the filamentous fungi, by far the largest group), and the Taphrinomycotina (a basal group including fission yeasts and plant pathogens such as Taphrina).
