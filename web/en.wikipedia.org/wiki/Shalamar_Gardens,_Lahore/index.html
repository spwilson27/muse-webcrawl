Shalamar Gardens, Lahore

The Shalamar Gardens (Punjabi/Urdu: شالیمار باغ), sometimes written Shalimar Gardens, are a Mughal garden complex in Lahore, Pakistan. Built by the emperor Shah Jahan in 1641–42, they are among the finest surviving examples of the Mughal charbagh paradise garden, and were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1981 together with the Lahore Fort.

Layout

The gardens form an oblong parallelogram enclosed by a high brick wall, covering about 16 hectares (40 acres). They descend in three terraces — Farah Baksh ("Bestower of Pleasure"), Faiz Baksh ("Bestower of Goodness") and Hayat Baksh ("Bestower of Life") — watered by a canal drawn from the Ravi River. The layout follows the Persian charbagh pattern, divided by water channels into planted squares, with some 410 fountains playing in a sequence of marble pools.

Buildings

The terraces contain a series of pavilions and chambers, including the Sawan Bhadun pavilions, the marble Daulat Khana, hammams (baths) and the Aiwan audience hall. Water engineering was supervised by the emperor's engineers and the court noble Khalilullah Khan, and the speed of construction — completed in about a year and a half — was celebrated by court chroniclers.

Later history

The gardens suffered during the decline of the Mughal Empire, when marble and ornament were stripped under Sikh rule in the 19th century, but the basic plan, hydraulic system and terraces survive. The name "Shalamar" — popularised in the West through the Cliffs of Dooneen song tradition and the novel-based film culture — has come to suggest an earthly paradise in its own right.
