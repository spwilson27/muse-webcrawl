A drum machine is an electronic musical instrument that creates percussion sounds, drum beats and patterns. Drum machines may imitate drum kits and other percussion instruments, or produce unique electronic sounds. Most modern drum machines are programmable, letting musicians compose rhythms using a step sequencer in which sounds are placed on a grid of (usually) sixteen steps per measure.

History

The first programmable rhythm devices date to the 1930s, and commercial units such as the Wurlitzer Side Man (1959) provided preset rhythms for organists. Through the 1970s, preset "rhythm boxes" from Ace Tone and Roland accompanied home organs and performers. The arrival of programmable machines changed music: the Roland CR-78 (1978) allowed user patterns, and the Linn LM-1 (1980) introduced sampled drum sounds.

The Roland TR-808 Rhythm Composer (1980), initially a commercial disappointment because its synthesised sounds were unrealistic, was adopted by early hip hop and electro producers — notably on Afrika Bambaataa's "Planet Rock" (1982) — and became the defining beat box of hip hop, house and techno; its successor the TR-909 (1983) added MIDI and a punchy analogue kick central to house and techno. Sampling drum machines such as the E-mu SP-1200 and the Akai MPC series became production workstations in their own right.

Operation

Users program patterns step by step, assigning kick, snare, hi-hat, clap and other voices to steps, then chain patterns into songs. Parameters such as tempo, swing, accent and tuning shape the groove. Since the 1990s, software drum machines and virtual instruments in digital audio workstations have largely taken over the role, though hardware units remain popular for live performance.
