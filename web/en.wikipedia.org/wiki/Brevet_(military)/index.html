Brevet (military)

In military terminology, a brevet (from French, "patent" or "certificate") is a warrant giving a commissioned officer a higher rank title as a reward for gallantry or meritorious conduct, without necessarily granting the corresponding authority, precedence, or pay. The practice was used in the British Army and, most famously, in the United States Army during the American Civil War.

United States

Brevet promotions were granted from the Revolutionary War era onward. During the Civil War they were awarded on a large scale for gallant and meritorious service; by 1865 there were well over a thousand brevet generals in the Union Army. A celebrated mass award dated 13 March 1865 breveted many officers simultaneously. Officers were often styled by their brevet rank — for example, George Armstrong Custer, a substantive captain and brevet major general of volunteers, was widely called "General Custer," though after the war he reverted to a Regular Army rank well below general. The U.S. Army largely ceased granting brevets after the early 20th century; the modern equivalent is an honorary or "tombstone" promotion in limited contexts.

United Kingdom

In the British Army, brevet rank allowed an officer to hold a higher rank in the army at large than in his regiment, facilitating staff and command appointments. A "brevet majority" or "brevet lieutenant-colonelcy" was a common reward for distinguished service, particularly colonial campaigning, before the system's gradual decline in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.
