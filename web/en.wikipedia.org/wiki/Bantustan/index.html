A Bantustan (also known as a Black homeland) was a territory set aside for Black South Africans under the apartheid policy of "separate development". Between the 1950s and 1994, the South African government assigned every Black citizen to one of ten ethnically defined homelands—amounting to only about 13 per cent of the country's land—and stripped them of South African citizenship, in a bid to make South Africa a white state.

Origins

The homelands built on the earlier "reserves" of the 1913 Natives Land Act. The Promotion of Bantu Self-Government Act (1959) created the homeland framework, and the Bantu Homelands Citizenship Act (1970) assigned all Black South Africans citizenship of a homeland whether or not they lived there. Millions were forcibly removed from "white" areas, cities, and "black spots" into these often fragmented, arid territories.

The ten homelands

The ten South African bantustans were Transkei, Ciskei, Bophuthatswana, Venda, Lebowa, Gazankulu, KaNgwane, KwaNdebele, KwaZulu, and QwaQwa. Four—Transkei (1976), Bophuthatswana (1977), Venda (1979), and Ciskei (1981)—accepted nominal "independence", recognised by no other country and not the United Nations. Similar homelands were imposed in South West Africa (Namibia). The bantustans were politically repressive, economically dependent on Pretoria, and served as reservoirs of migrant labour.

Abolition

With the first democratic elections in 1994, the bantustans were formally reincorporated into South Africa and reorganised into the nine modern provinces, although their boundaries and underdevelopment still shape provincial geography and politics.
