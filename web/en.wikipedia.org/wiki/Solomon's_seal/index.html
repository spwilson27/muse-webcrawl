Solomon's seal

Solomon's seal is the common name for plants of the genus Polygonatum, a group of flowering plants in the asparagus family, Asparagaceae. The genus comprises roughly 70 species distributed across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere, especially in East Asia. The name is traditionally linked to the scars on the rhizome left by previous years' stems, which resemble the impression of a seal, said to be that of King Solomon; other legends hold that Solomon himself set his seal upon the root in recognition of its medicinal virtues.

Description

Polygonatum species are herbaceous perennials with thick creeping rhizomes. Arching stems bear alternate (or sometimes whorled) leaves, from whose axils hang small, tubular, greenish-white bell-shaped flowers, followed by blue-black or red berries. Familiar garden species include Polygonatum odoratum (angular Solomon's seal), P. multiflorum (common Solomon's seal), and the variegated P. × hybridum.

Uses

Solomon's seal has a long history in herbal medicine in Europe and Asia, used for treating inflammation, wounds, and respiratory complaints; in China, the rhizome (yùzhú) is a traditional medicinal and food plant. Young shoots have been eaten in some regions, though the berries are considered toxic. The plants are also valued ornamentals for shady gardens.

Related plants

The "false Solomon's seal" is Maianthemum racemosum, a related genus formerly placed in Smilacina. The Star of David-like hexagram is also sometimes called the Seal of Solomon, as is the magical ring of legend — distinct from the plant.
