The Dresden Codex is the oldest surviving book written in the Americas and one of only four Maya codices to survive the Spanish conquest. A screenfold manuscript of fig-bark paper (amatl) coated with gesso and painted on both sides, it dates to the 11th or 12th century, probably from the vicinity of Chichén Itzá in Yucatán, and contains 78 pages of ritual and divinatory almanacs, astronomical tables and calendrical calculations in Maya hieroglyphic script.

Contents

The codex is famed for its astronomy: it includes a highly accurate Venus table tracking the planet's synodic cycle over centuries, eclipse prediction tables, and a table associated with Mars. Other sections give almanacs for rituals keyed to the 260-day tzolk'in calendar, New Year ceremonies, and a "flood page" depicting water pouring from the sky and from a celestial dragon, long interpreted in popular culture as a catastrophe prophecy.

History

The manuscript reached Europe in the colonial period and was purchased in Vienna in 1739 by Johann Christian Götze, director of the Royal Library at Dresden, from which it takes its name. Ernst Förstemann, the Dresden librarian who studied it in the late 19th century, first deciphered its calendrical and astronomical systems, including the workings of the Long Count. The codex was damaged by water in the cellar where it was stored during the bombing of Dresden in February 1945, though high-quality facsimiles published beforehand preserve its content. Today it is held by the Saxon State and University Library in Dresden, and a full digital facsimile is freely available.
