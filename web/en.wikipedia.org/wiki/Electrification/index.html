Electrification

Electrification is the process of powering by electricity and, in many contexts, the introduction of such power into an area or technology that previously used other energy sources. Historically it meant building generating stations and distribution networks to bring light and motive power to cities, farms and factories. In the twenty-first century the term also describes converting end uses — vehicles, heating, industrial processes — from fossil fuels to electricity.

History

Electrification began in the 1880s with urban lighting and tramways, and spread with national grid programmes: the Soviet GOELRO plan of 1920, the United States Rural Electrification Act of 1936, and post-war expansion in Europe and Asia. Global access to electricity rose from about 71 percent of the world population in 1990 to over 90 percent by the early 2020s, though hundreds of millions, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa, still lack access.

Benefits and challenges

Electricity is clean at the point of use, efficient (electric motors and heat pumps far outperform combustion), and controllable. Extending grids is capital-intensive, however, and in remote areas off-grid solar and mini-grids may be cheaper. Electrification is central to decarbonisation strategies: as grids become cleaner, switching transport and heating to electricity cuts emissions.

Railway electrification

A major specialised sense is the electrification of railways, replacing steam or diesel traction with overhead lines or third rails, common across Europe and Asia.
