Fehling's solution

Fehling's solution is a chemical reagent used to differentiate between water-soluble carbohydrate and ketone functional groups, and as a test for reducing sugars. It was developed by the German chemist Hermann von Fehling in 1849. The reagent consists of two solutions that are mixed immediately before use: Fehling's A, an aqueous solution of copper(II) sulfate, and Fehling's B, an alkaline solution of sodium potassium tartrate (Rochelle salt).

Test procedure

Equal volumes of solutions A and B are mixed to give a deep blue solution containing a copper(II)–tartrate complex. When heated with a reducing sugar such as glucose, the copper(II) is reduced to copper(I), which precipitates as brick-red copper(I) oxide (Cu2O). The test is positive for aldehydes and alpha-hydroxy ketones but negative for ordinary ketones and aromatic aldehydes.

Uses

Fehling's test was historically used to detect glucose in urine as a screening test for diabetes mellitus. It was largely superseded by the similar Benedict's reagent, which is more stable as a single solution, and by modern enzymatic methods. The reagent remains a staple of school and undergraduate chemistry teaching.
