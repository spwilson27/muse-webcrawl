Sir James Whyte Black (14 June 1924 – 22 March 2010) was a Scottish pharmacologist who revolutionised medicine by developing propranolol, the first successful beta blocker, and cimetidine, the first H2 receptor antagonist for treating stomach ulcers. His strategy of designing drugs rationally to block specific receptors, rather than relying on serendipity, transformed pharmaceutical research. He shared the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Gertrude B. Elion and George H. Hitchings.

Early life and education

Black was born in Uddingston, Lanarkshire, and educated at Beath High School in Cowdenbeath, Fife. He studied medicine at the University of St Andrews, graduating in 1946, and was influenced by the pharmacologist Robert Campbell Garry. After a period at the University of Malaya he returned to Scotland, then joined the University of Glasgow veterinary school, where his interest in adrenaline and the heart developed.

Beta blockers

At ICI Pharmaceuticals in the late 1950s, Black set out to find a drug that would block the effects of adrenaline on the heart, reducing its oxygen demand and so relieving angina. Guided by Raymond Ahlquist's concept of alpha and beta receptors, his team developed pronethalol and then propranolol (1964), which became one of the most widely prescribed drugs in the world and a landmark of 20th-century medicine.

Cimetidine and receptor theory

Moving to Smith, Kline & French, Black applied the same logic to histamine: if histamine stimulated acid secretion in the stomach through a distinct receptor, blocking that receptor should treat ulcers. His team's work defined the H2 receptor and produced cimetidine (launched as Tagamet in 1976), the first of the H2 blockers, which transformed ulcer treatment and became the world's best-selling prescription drug of its time.

Later career and honours

Black held chairs at University College London and King's College London, founded his own research foundation at King's, and served as chancellor of the University of Dundee from 1992 to 2006. He was knighted in 1981 and appointed to the Order of Merit in 2000. He died in London in 2010.
