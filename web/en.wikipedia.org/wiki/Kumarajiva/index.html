Kumārajīva (Sanskrit: कुमारजीव; Chinese: 鳩摩羅什, Jiūmóluóshí; 344–413 CE) was a Buddhist monk, scholar, missionary, and translator from the kingdom of Kucha in the Tarim Basin. He is regarded as one of the greatest translators of Mahayana Buddhist scriptures from Sanskrit into Chinese, and his elegant, accurate renderings remain the standard versions used throughout East Asia.

Life

Kumārajīva was born to an Indian father, Kumārāyana, and a Kuchean princess, Jīvā. He became a novice monk at seven and studied in Kashmir and Kashgar, mastering Sarvastivada and then Mahayana philosophy, especially Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka. His fame led the Former Qin ruler Fu Jian to send an army to capture him in 384; the general Lü Guang instead kept him in Liangzhou for some 17 years. When the Later Qin emperor Yao Xing took Chang'an, Kumārajīva was finally brought to the capital in 401 and honoured as National Preceptor.

Translations

At Chang'an, Kumārajīva directed a large, state-sponsored translation bureau with hundreds of assistants, revising earlier, clumsier translations. Works attributed to him include the Lotus Sutra, the Diamond Sutra, the Heart Sutra (attribution debated), the Vimalakīrti Sutra, the Amitābha Sutra, and key Madhyamaka treatises of Nāgārjuna. His translations — around 35 works in 294 scrolls by conservative counts — are celebrated for their clarity and literary grace and fixed much of the vocabulary of Chinese Buddhism.

Legacy

Kumārajīva's Madhyamaka teaching formed the basis of the Chinese Sanlun ("Three Treatise") school. A legend holds that, having sworn his tongue would not burn if his translations were without error, his tongue remained intact after cremation; a stupa said to enshrine it stands at Huayan Monastery in Shaanxi.
