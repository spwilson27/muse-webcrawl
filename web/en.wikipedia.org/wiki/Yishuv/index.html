The Yishuv (Hebrew: ישוב, literally "settlement"), or Ha-Yishuv Ha-Ivri ("the Hebrew Yishuv"), is the term for the body of Jewish residents in the Land of Israel — in Ottoman Syria and later Mandatory Palestine — prior to the establishment of the State of Israel in 1948. Historians distinguish the religious Old Yishuv, the long-established Jewish communities of Jerusalem, Safed, Tiberias and Hebron, from the New Yishuv built by Zionist immigration from the 1880s onward.

The Old Yishuv

The Old Yishuv consisted mainly of Orthodox communities in the Four Holy Cities, sustained by charitable funds (halukka) from the diaspora and devoted to prayer and study. A Jewish presence had been continuous in the region, and the community grew during the 19th century with immigration from North Africa and Europe.

The New Yishuv

Beginning with the First Aliyah (1882–1903), waves of Zionist immigration founded agricultural settlements (moshavot), then the collective kibbutzim and cooperative moshavim, and the city of Tel Aviv (1909). Under the British Mandate, the Yishuv built a proto-state infrastructure: the Jewish Agency, the Va'ad Leumi (National Council), an elected assembly, the Histadrut labour federation, the Hebrew University (1925), the Haganah defence organisation and its Palmach strike force, and a Hebrew school system and press. By 1947 the Yishuv numbered roughly 630,000 people, about a third of Palestine's population.

1948

Following the UN Partition Plan of November 1947 and the ensuing civil war, David Ben-Gurion proclaimed the State of Israel on 14 May 1948, and the Yishuv's institutions became the basis of the new state.
