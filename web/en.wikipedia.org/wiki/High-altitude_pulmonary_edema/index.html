High-altitude pulmonary edema

High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE)
SpecialtyPulmonology, wilderness medicine
SymptomsBreathlessness at rest, cough (often with pink frothy sputum), weakness, cyanosis
Usual onset2–4 days after ascent above ~2,500–3,000 m
CausesExaggerated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction
TreatmentImmediate descent, supplemental oxygen, nifedipine

High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is a life-threatening form of non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema (fluid accumulation in the lungs) that occurs in otherwise healthy people at altitudes typically above 2,500 metres (8,200 ft). It is one of the three main forms of altitude sickness, alongside acute mountain sickness and high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE).

Mechanism

In HAPE, exposure to low oxygen pressure causes uneven constriction of the pulmonary arteries. Blood is diverted into unconstricted vessels, where greatly elevated pressure damages the capillary walls ("stress failure"), allowing protein- and red-cell-rich fluid to leak into the alveoli. Susceptible individuals have an exaggerated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstrictor response and reduced nitric oxide availability in the lungs.

Signs and symptoms

Early signs include breathlessness disproportionate to exertion, reduced exercise performance, and a dry cough. As the condition progresses, dyspnea occurs at rest, the cough produces pink frothy sputum, and cyanosis (bluish lips and nails), low-grade fever and crackles on auscultation may appear. Without treatment it can progress rapidly to respiratory failure and death.

Treatment and prevention

The definitive treatment is immediate descent to lower altitude and supplemental oxygen; a portable hyperbaric chamber can simulate descent. Nifedipine (and in some guidelines phosphodiesterase inhibitors such as tadalafil or sildenafil) can reduce pulmonary artery pressure. Prevention relies on gradual ascent, acclimatisation, and — for those with a history of HAPE — prophylactic nifedipine.
