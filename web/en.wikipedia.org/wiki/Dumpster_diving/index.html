Dumpster diving

Dumpster diving (also called skipping in the United Kingdom, or bin raiding) is the practice of salvaging usable items — such as food, furniture, electronics, and clothing — from large commercial, residential, or industrial waste containers, particularly dumpsters. Practitioners range from people motivated by poverty or necessity to freegans motivated by opposition to consumerism and food waste, and to collectors and resellers.

Motivations and practice

A significant share of what is discarded by supermarkets, bakeries, and restaurants is edible but unsellable because of damaged packaging or passed sell-by dates, and food recovery is the most common focus of dumpster diving. Divers also recover discarded electronics, office supplies, and household goods, some of which are repaired and resold. In information security, "dumpster diving" also refers to searching discarded documents or media for passwords, personal data, or trade secrets, a technique used in social engineering and identity theft.

Legality and safety

The legality of dumpster diving varies by jurisdiction. In the United States, the Supreme Court's decision in California v. Greenwood (1988) held that there is no reasonable expectation of privacy in trash left at the curb, but local trespassing and anti-scavenging ordinances may still apply, and locked or fenced enclosures are generally off-limits. Divers face risks including contaminated food, sharp objects, and compacting equipment, and commonly follow informal norms such as leaving a site tidier than they found it.
