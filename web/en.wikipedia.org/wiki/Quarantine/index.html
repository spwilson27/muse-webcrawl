Quarantine

A quarantine is a restriction on the movement of people, animals and goods intended to prevent the spread of disease or pests. It is distinct from medical isolation, which separates people known to be ill: quarantine separates and restricts people who were exposed to a contagious disease to see whether they become sick.

History

The word derives from the Venetian quarantena, "forty days": during the era of the Black Death, ships arriving in Venice from infected ports were required to anchor for forty days before landing, extending an earlier thirty-day isolation (the trentino) first imposed by Ragusa (Dubrovnik) in 1377. Quarantine stations and lazarettos were established at ports worldwide, and quarantine flags (yellow jack) signalled possible disease aboard.

Modern practice

Modern quarantines are governed by public-health law and the WHO International Health Regulations, and were widely applied during the SARS outbreak of 2003, the Ebola epidemic in West Africa, and the COVID-19 pandemic. Quarantine is also used in agriculture, animal control, spaceflight (for returning astronauts and samples), and computing, where suspect files are isolated.
