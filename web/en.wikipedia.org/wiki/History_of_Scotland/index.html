History of Scotland

The history of Scotland begins with prehistoric settlement, passes through Roman incursions and the formation of the medieval kingdom, the Wars of Independence, union with England in 1707, and the modern devolved Scotland within the United Kingdom.

Early Scotland

Mesolithic settlers arrived after the last Ice Age; Neolithic monuments such as Skara Brae and the Ring of Brodgar in Orkney survive from c. 3000 BCE. Rome invaded from 79 CE, built the Antonine Wall, but never conquered Caledonia. In the early medieval period the Picts, the Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata, Britons and Angles shared the north of Britain. Kenneth MacAlpin is traditionally credited with uniting Picts and Scots as Alba around 843.

The medieval kingdom

David I (1124–1153) introduced feudalism, burghs and reformed monastic orders. The death of Alexander III (1286) and the Maid of Norway (1290) opened a succession crisis; Edward I of England claimed overlordship, provoking the Wars of Scottish Independence. William Wallace won at Stirling Bridge (1297) but was executed in 1305; Robert the Bruce secured victory at Bannockburn in 1314, and the Declaration of Arbroath (1320) asserted Scottish sovereignty.

Reformation and union of crowns

The Stewart dynasty ruled through a turbulent era. The Reformation Parliament of 1560 made Scotland Protestant under John Knox's influence; Mary, Queen of Scots was executed in England in 1587. Her son James VI inherited the English throne in 1603 (Union of the Crowns). In 1707 the Acts of Union joined Scotland and England in Great Britain, driven by English security concerns and Scotland's financial ruin after the Darien scheme.

Jacobitism, Enlightenment and industry

Jacobite risings in 1715 and 1745 ended at Culloden (1746), followed by repression and the Highland Clearances. The Scottish Enlightenment — Hume, Adam Smith, Hutton — made Edinburgh a European intellectual capital, while Glasgow and the Clyde drove industrialisation in cotton, coal, iron and shipbuilding within the British Empire.

Modern Scotland

The 20th century brought heavy industry's decline, North Sea oil, and a nationalist revival. A 1997 referendum restored a Scottish Parliament, which convened in 1999. In the 2014 independence referendum voters chose to remain in the UK by 55% to 45%.
