Inner ear

The inner ear (internal ear, auris interna) is the innermost part of the vertebrate ear. In vertebrates, the inner ear is mainly responsible for sound detection and balance. It consists of the bony labyrinth, a hollow cavity in the temporal bone lined with periosteum and containing a fluid called perilymph, within which is suspended the membranous labyrinth, containing a second fluid, endolymph. The inner ear converts mechanical vibrations into nerve impulses for hearing, and senses the position and movement of the head for the vestibular system.

Structure

The bony labyrinth has three parts: the vestibule, the semicircular canals, and the cochlea. Within them, the membranous labyrinth contains the utricle and saccule (within the vestibule), the semicircular ducts, and the cochlear duct.

Cochlea

The cochlea is a spiral-shaped, snail-like structure that transduces sound. Vibrations arriving from the ossicles of the middle ear at the oval window move the perilymph, causing the basilar membrane to vibrate. The organ of Corti, resting on this membrane, contains hair cells whose stereocilia bend in response, generating electrical signals that travel along the cochlear nerve to the brain. Different positions along the membrane respond to different frequencies, high frequencies at the base and low frequencies at the apex.

Vestibular system

The three semicircular canals, oriented in roughly perpendicular planes, detect rotational acceleration of the head through movement of endolymph against the cupula in each ampulla. The utricle and saccule contain otolith organs, in which calcium carbonate crystals (otoconia) shift with gravity and linear acceleration, informing the brain of head position and movement.

Disorders

Disorders of the inner ear include sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, Ménière's disease (characterized by vertigo, fluctuating hearing loss, and tinnitus), benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), and labyrinthitis. Sound exposure, aging, ototoxic drugs, and infections can all damage the delicate hair cells, which in mammals do not regenerate.
