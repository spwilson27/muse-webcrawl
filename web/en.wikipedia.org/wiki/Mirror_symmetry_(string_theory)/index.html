Mirror symmetry (string theory)

In algebraic geometry and theoretical physics, mirror symmetry refers to the relationship between pairs of Calabi–Yau manifolds. Roughly, two geometrically very different Calabi–Yau manifolds can give rise to identical physics when used as the compactification spaces of string theory; such manifolds are called mirror pairs.

Overview

When string theory is formulated on a Calabi–Yau manifold, geometric features of the manifold — such as its numbers of holes of different dimensions, encoded in its Hodge numbers — determine physical quantities. For a mirror pair, these Hodge numbers are interchanged: what counts holes of one kind in one manifold counts holes of another kind in its mirror. Discovered through work in the late 1980s by physicists including Brian Greene and Ronen Plesser, and given striking applications by Candelas and collaborators, mirror symmetry allowed physicists to compute the numbers of rational curves on Calabi–Yau manifolds — problems that had resisted algebraic geometers.

Mathematics

Mirror symmetry has generated deep conjectures and new fields in mathematics, including homological mirror symmetry proposed by Maxim Kontsevich, relating the symplectic geometry of one manifold to the complex geometry of its mirror, and the SYZ conjecture of Strominger, Yau and Zaslow.
