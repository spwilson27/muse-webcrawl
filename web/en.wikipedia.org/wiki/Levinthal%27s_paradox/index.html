Levinthal's paradox

Levinthal's paradox is a thought experiment in the theory of protein folding. In 1969, Cyrus Levinthal noted that, because of the very large number of degrees of freedom in an unfolded polypeptide chain, the molecule has an astronomical number of possible conformations.

The argument

In his famous estimate, Levinthal supposed a protein of about 100 residues, each with a handful of possible conformations, giving on the order of 3100 or more possible structures. If the chain were to sample conformations randomly — even at the fastest physically plausible rate, around a trillion per second — it would take vastly longer than the age of the universe to stumble upon the single native fold. Yet real proteins fold spontaneously in milliseconds to seconds. Hence the apparent paradox: proteins cannot fold by random search.

Resolution

The paradox is resolved by recognizing that folding is not a random search. Proteins fold along guided pathways on a funnel-shaped energy landscape, biased toward the native state by local interactions, secondary-structure formation, and the hydrophobic effect, so that partial structure forms early and constrains the remaining search. Partially folded intermediates and nucleated growth mean the chain never explores most of conformation space.

Levinthal himself proposed that proteins fold via specific pathways. The paradox remains influential in computational biology, framing the goal of structure prediction — a problem addressed in recent years by machine-learning systems such as AlphaFold.
