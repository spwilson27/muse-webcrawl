Hassan-i Sabbah (Persian: حسن صباح, romanized: Ḥasan-i Ṣabbāḥ; c. 1050 – 12 June 1124), also known as Hassan I of Alamut, was a religious and political leader, founder of the Nizari Isma'ili da'wa and state in Persia, and the first ruler of the mountain fortress of Alamut. His followers became known in the West as the Assassins, and Hassan himself as the "Old Man of the Mountain" (Shaykh al-Jabal).

Life

Hassan was born in Qom to a Twelver Shia family of Arab origin and was educated in Rayy, where he converted to Isma'ili Islam around the age of seventeen. He travelled to Fatimid Cairo in 1078 to deepen his training, then returned to Persia as a missionary (da'i), methodically building a network of converts in Daylam and the Alborz mountains.

Alamut and the Nizari state

In 1090 Hassan seized the nearly impregnable castle of Alamut, reputedly by converting its garrison from within, founding a territorial state that endured for 166 years. To defend a small state surrounded by the powerful Seljuk Empire, he developed a strategy of targeted assassination of military and political leaders — most famously the vizier Nizam al-Mulk in 1092 — carried out by devoted agents, the fidā'īs. After the Fatimid succession dispute of 1094, Hassan broke with Cairo and led the Nizari branch independently, ruling Alamut with strict ascetic discipline for 34 years, reportedly leaving his fortress only twice. He died in 1124 and was succeeded by his lieutenant Buzurg-Umid.

Legends

Marco Polo and other medieval sources embellished Hassan's story with tales of drugged recruits shown a false paradise — the probable origin (via the Arabic ḥashīshiyya) of the word "assassin" — which modern historians treat as hostile legend rather than fact.
