Chlorophyll

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chlorophyll is a green pigment found in cyanobacteria and in the chloroplasts of algae and plants, which absorbs light energy to power photosynthesis. It absorbs most strongly in the blue and red parts of the spectrum and reflects green, which is why most leaves look green.

The molecule has a ring structure, related to the haem group of blood, with a magnesium atom at its centre and a long hydrocarbon tail anchoring it in the thylakoid membrane. Several forms exist (a, b, c, d and f); chlorophyll a is the principal photosynthetic pigment in land plants.

Role in photosynthesis

Chlorophyll molecules are organised into antenna complexes that funnel light energy to reaction centres, where it drives electron transfer — splitting water, releasing oxygen, and ultimately producing ATP and NADPH for carbon fixation. In autumn, deciduous trees break down chlorophyll, unmasking the yellow and orange carotenoid pigments beneath.

Measurement and uses

Chlorophyll concentration in the oceans, measured by satellite, is a standard proxy for phytoplankton abundance and marine primary production. Chlorophyll content in leaves is used in agriculture as an indicator of plant nitrogen status.
