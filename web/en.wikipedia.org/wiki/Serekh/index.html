Serekh

The serekh of Narmer, surmounted by the Horus falcon.

In ancient Egyptian art and writing, a serekh is an ornamental rectangular vignette representing the niched or panelled façade of a palace or tomb, within which a king's Horus name was written. The serekh is one of the oldest royal insignia of Egypt, appearing from the late Predynastic Period, and it is surmounted by the falcon of the god Horus, with whom the king was identified. The word serekh derives from the Egyptian srḫ, "façade."

Form and meaning

The rectangle's lower portion is filled with a pattern of recessed niches imitating the mudbrick palace façade, while the upper portion forms a field for the hieroglyphs of the king's Horus name. The motif proclaimed that the king named inside was the earthly embodiment of Horus residing in his palace. Because the Horus name was the oldest of the five names of the Egyptian royal titulary, serekhs are among the principal sources for identifying the earliest kings of Egypt.

History

Serekhs first appear on pottery, labels, and sealings of the Naqada III period (late fourth millennium BCE), including some of the earliest known Egyptian writing, such as the labels from tomb U-j at Abydos. Well-known examples include the serekhs of kings Iry-Hor, Ka, and Narmer; the Narmer Palette (c. 3100 BCE) bears the king's serekh at the top of both faces. During the Early Dynastic Period and Old Kingdom the serekh was the primary frame for the royal name.

One king of the Second Dynasty, Peribsen, exceptionally replaced the Horus falcon atop his serekh with the animal of the god Seth — a striking deviation that has fueled debate about religious or political conflict in his reign — while his successor Khasekhemwy combined both Horus and Seth above his serekh. From the late Old Kingdom onward, the Horus name in its serekh, though retained in the formal titulary for the rest of pharaonic history, was increasingly overshadowed by the king's throne name and birth name enclosed in cartouches.
