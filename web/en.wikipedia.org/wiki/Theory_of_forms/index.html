The theory of forms (or theory of ideas) is a philosophical doctrine attributed to the ancient Greek philosopher Plato. It holds that the physical world is not the real world; instead, ultimate reality consists of timeless, unchanging, abstract Forms (Greek: eidos or idea) — perfect exemplars of which sensible things are imperfect copies and in which they "participate."

Overview

For every class of things — beautiful things, equal things, good things, horses, tables — Plato posits a single Form: Beauty itself, Equality itself, the Good, Horseness. Sensible objects are beautiful only by participating in Beauty, and they fall short of it; the Forms alone are fully real and are the proper objects of knowledge (episteme), whereas the senses yield only opinion (doxa). In the Republic, Plato ranks the Form of the Good above all others, as the source of intelligibility and being, illustrating the doctrine through the similes of the Sun, the divided line, and the allegory of the cave.

Criticism

Plato himself scrutinized the theory in the Parmenides, where the young Socrates faces objections, including the "Third Man Argument": if men are men by resembling the Form of Man, a further Form is needed to explain the resemblance between men and that Form, generating an infinite regress. Aristotle, Plato's student, rejected separately existing Forms in favour of forms immanent in things, and the debate seeded the medieval problem of universals.
