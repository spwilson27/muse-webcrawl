Symmetry in quantum mechanics

Symmetries in quantum mechanics describe features of the space-time and particle system that remain unchanged under some transformation. In quantum mechanics, symmetries are represented by unitary or antiunitary operators on the Hilbert space of states, as guaranteed by Wigner's theorem, and they play a foundational role: by Noether's theorem each continuous symmetry corresponds to a conserved quantity, while the irreducible representations of symmetry groups classify particles and give rise to selection rules governing transitions.

Symmetry transformations act on quantum states by unitary (or antiunitary) operators.

Space-time symmetries

Translations in space and time correspond to conservation of momentum and energy; rotations correspond to conservation of angular momentum and lead to the quantum theory of spin. The full symmetry group of relativistic quantum mechanics is the Poincaré group, whose irreducible representations were classified by Eugene Wigner in 1939 in terms of mass and spin (or helicity), providing the modern definition of an elementary particle. Discrete symmetries — parity (P), charge conjugation (C) and time reversal (T) — are each violated by the weak interaction, though their combination CPT is conserved in any local Lorentz-invariant quantum field theory (the CPT theorem).

Internal symmetries

Internal symmetries act on particle labels rather than space-time. Examples include isospin SU(2), the flavour SU(3) of the Eightfold Way that led to the quark model, colour SU(3) of quantum chromodynamics, and the electroweak SU(2)×U(1) gauge symmetry. Gauge symmetries, which are local internal symmetries, dictate the form of interactions and the existence of force-carrying bosons. Spontaneous symmetry breaking, as in the Higgs mechanism, explains how symmetric laws can produce asymmetric states.

Consequences

Symmetry principles yield degeneracy of energy levels, conservation laws, superselection rules, and selection rules for matrix elements; they also underlie the spin–statistics theorem and the structure of the Standard Model of particle physics.
