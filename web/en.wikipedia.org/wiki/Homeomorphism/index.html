Homeomorphism

Homeomorphism
Field: Topology
Type: Continuous bijection with continuous inverse
Related: Diffeomorphism, Isomorphism, Deformation

In the mathematical field of topology, a homeomorphism is a continuous function between topological spaces that has a continuous inverse function. Homeomorphisms are the isomorphisms in the category of topological spaces — that is, they preserve all the topological properties of a given space. Two spaces with a homeomorphism between them are called homeomorphic, and from a topological viewpoint they are the same.

The word homeomorphism comes from the Greek words homoios (similar) and morphe (shape, form), introduced by Henri Poincaré in 1895.

Definition

A function f: X → Y between two topological spaces is a homeomorphism if it is bijective, continuous, and its inverse is continuous. If such a function exists, X and Y are homeomorphic. A homeomorphism from a space onto itself is called a self-homeomorphism.

Examples

An open interval (−1,1) is homeomorphic to the real line via f(x)=tan(πx/2). A circle is homeomorphic to an ellipse and to a square, but not to a solid torus. The classic joke that a topologist cannot distinguish a coffee mug from a doughnut reflects that both are a genus-one surface — a torus — and are homeomorphic.

Properties

Homeomorphic spaces share topological invariants such as compactness, connectedness, and homology groups. Homeomorphism is coarser than diffeomorphism: a trefoil knot is not ambient-isotopic to an unknot in three dimensions, though they are homeomorphic as abstract spaces.
