African-American music is an umbrella term covering a diverse range of music and musical genres largely developed by African Americans in the United States. Its roots lie in the musical traditions brought from Africa and reshaped under slavery — work songs, field hollers, spirituals and ring shouts — blended with European instruments, harmony and forms. African-American music has been the single most influential stream in American popular music, giving rise to ragtime, blues, jazz, gospel, rhythm and blues, rock and roll, soul, funk, disco and hip hop.

Origins

Enslaved Africans preserved rhythmic complexity, call-and-response patterns, blue notes and improvisation from West and Central African traditions. After emancipation, black musicians adapted European brass and string instruments, producing minstrel-era songs, the cakewalk and, by the 1890s, ragtime, popularised by Scott Joplin.

20th century

The blues, emerging in the Deep South around the turn of the century, supplied the harmonic and lyrical foundation for much later popular music. Jazz developed in New Orleans and spread north during the Great Migration, evolving through swing, bebop and beyond with figures such as Louis Armstrong, Duke Ellington and Charlie Parker. Gospel music flourished in churches through composers like Thomas A. Dorsey. In the 1940s and 1950s, rhythm and blues fed directly into rock and roll, while soul and Motown brought black music to the centre of the pop charts in the 1960s, followed by funk and disco in the 1970s.

Hip hop and contemporary music

Hip hop, created by black and Latino youth in the Bronx in the 1970s, became the dominant popular genre worldwide by the early 21st century, alongside contemporary R&B. African-American musical innovations — from the backbeat to sampling — remain foundational to global popular culture.
