Spent nuclear fuel

Spent nuclear fuel, occasionally called used nuclear fuel, is nuclear fuel that has been irradiated in a nuclear reactor (usually at a nuclear power plant). It is no longer useful in sustaining a nuclear reaction in an ordinary thermal reactor and, depending on its point along the nuclear fuel cycle, may have slightly different isotopic constituents. The term "fuel" is slightly misplaced, as spent fuel remains thermally hot and highly radioactive for extended periods, and must be cooled in spent fuel pools and later in dry casks before disposal or reprocessing.

Composition and radioactivity

Fresh fuel for a light-water reactor is typically 3–5% fissile uranium-235 in uranium dioxide. After years in the core, the fuel has accumulated fission products (such as caesium-137 and strontium-90), plutonium bred from uranium-238, and minor actinides like americium and curium. On discharge it generates roughly 6% of its operating thermal power as decay heat, falling to a small fraction over the first years, but its radiotoxicity remains significant for millennia — dominated at long times by plutonium-239 (half-life 24,100 years) and other actinides.

Storage and disposal

Spent fuel is first stored on site in water-filled pools, then transferred to steel-and-concrete dry casks. Permanent disposal in deep geological repositories is the internationally favoured end point: Finland's Onkalo repository is the world's first such facility for spent fuel, while the U.S. Yucca Mountain project was defunded and WIPP in New Mexico accepts only defence transuranic waste. France, Russia, Japan and others reprocess spent fuel to recover uranium and plutonium for MOX fuel, though the United States abandoned commercial reprocessing over proliferation concerns.

Risks and debates

Debate centres on the security of pool storage (highlighted by the Fukushima Daiichi accident), theft and proliferation risks of separated plutonium, transport safety, and the ethics of imposing waste on future generations. Modern assessments note that spent fuel is a solid, compact waste form — the entire U.S. commercial inventory would cover a football field roughly ten metres deep.
