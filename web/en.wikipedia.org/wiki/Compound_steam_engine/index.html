Compound steam engine

A compound steam engine is a type of steam engine in which steam is expanded in two or more stages: first in a high-pressure cylinder, then, having given up some of its energy, in one or more larger low-pressure cylinders, improving efficiency over a simple-expansion engine.

Operation

Exhaust steam from the high-pressure cylinder passes to a low-pressure cylinder of larger volume, where further expansion extracts more work. Engines with three expansion stages are called triple-expansion engines; four stages, quadruple-expansion.

History

Compounding was patented in various forms in the early nineteenth century (including Jonathan Hornblower's 1781 patent) and became standard in marine practice from the 1860s, especially after higher boiler pressures became practical. Triple-expansion engines dominated ocean shipping until supplanted by turbines and diesels.

Applications

Compound engines were used in ships, locomotives, mill engines, and pumping stations. Notable locomotive arrangements include the de Glehn and Vauclain compounds.
