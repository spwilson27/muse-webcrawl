Perpetual Edict (1667)

The Perpetual Edict (Dutch: Eeuwig Edict) was a law enacted on 5 August 1667 by the States of Holland within the Dutch Republic. Drafted under the leadership of Grand Pensionary Johan de Witt, it abolished the office of stadtholder in Holland and transferred command of the army's officer appointments largely to the States General, in an effort to permanently exclude the Prince of Orange, William III, from power.

Provisions

The edict declared that the stadtholderate of Holland was abolished "for eternity". It also stipulated that the captain-generalcy of the Union could not be combined with the stadtholderate of any province, thereby blocking William from supreme military command even if another province appointed him stadtholder. The other provinces were pressed to adopt similar measures, and the edict formed part of the so-called "Harmony" by which the States Party regime sought to settle the constitutional order.

Consequences

The Perpetual Edict lasted only five years. In the Rampjaar (disaster year) of 1672, with the Republic invaded by France and England, popular Orangist pressure forced its repeal; William III was appointed Captain-General and then Stadtholder of Holland, and De Witt resigned shortly before being lynched by a mob in The Hague along with his brother Cornelis.
