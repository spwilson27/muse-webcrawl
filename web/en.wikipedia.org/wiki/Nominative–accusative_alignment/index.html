Nominative–accusative alignment

In linguistic typology, nominative–accusative alignment is a type of morphosyntactic alignment in which subjects of intransitive verbs are treated in the same way as subjects (agents) of transitive verbs, and differently from transitive objects. This is the alignment found in most European languages, including English in its pronoun system (he serves for both "he sleeps" and "he sees her", while the object takes a different form, him).

Terminology and notation

Linguists commonly use three primitive roles: S, the single argument of an intransitive verb; A, the agent-like argument of a transitive verb; and P (or O), the patient-like argument of a transitive verb. In nominative–accusative alignment, S and A are grouped together (the nominative) while P is marked distinctly (the accusative). This contrasts with ergative–absolutive alignment, in which S and P are grouped together.

Marking strategies

The grouping can be expressed through case marking on nouns, agreement on the verb, or both. Latin, German, Russian, and Japanese (with the particles ga for subjects and o for objects) are classic examples with overt case. Many languages show "split" systems in which alignment differs by tense, aspect, or the animacy of the arguments.

Distribution

Nominative–accusative alignment is the most common alignment type worldwide, found in the Indo-European, Uralic, Turkic, Mongolic, and many other language families, and in most languages of Africa and the Americas as well. It is the default assumed in traditional grammar, whose terms "nominative" and "accusative" cases come from this system.
