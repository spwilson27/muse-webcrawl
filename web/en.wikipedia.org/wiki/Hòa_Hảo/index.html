Hòa Hảo

Hòa Hảo is a Vietnamese new religious movement founded in 1939 by Huỳnh Phú Sổ (1920–1947), a young mystic from the Mekong Delta village of Hòa Hảo in An Giang Province, from which the faith takes its name. Rooted in the earlier Bửu Sơn Kỳ Hương tradition, it teaches a simplified, lay-oriented form of Buddhism centred on prayer at home, moral conduct and patriotism, without an ordained monastic clergy.

Beliefs and practice

Huỳnh Phú Sổ — revered by followers as the "Virtuous Master" (Đức Thầy) — preached that devotees could practise the faith at home before a simple altar with a plain brown cloth symbolizing harmony, offering fresh water, flowers and incense, and praying morning and evening. He emphasized the Four Debts of Gratitude (to ancestors and parents, to the nation, to the Three Jewels, and to humanity), discouraged superstition, elaborate funerals and weddings, alcohol and opium, and urged charitable work and national resistance to foreign domination.

History

The movement grew rapidly among Mekong Delta peasants, and during the 1940s it fielded its own armed forces. Huỳnh Phú Sổ was seized and killed in 1947 during the conflict with the Việt Minh, an event followers commemorate as his "absence" rather than death. Hòa Hảo forces later fought alongside and against successive governments in South Vietnam; the sect's political and military wings were suppressed after 1975, but the church was officially recognised by the Vietnamese state in 1999, and its anniversary celebrations at the ancestral temple in An Giang draw hundreds of thousands of pilgrims, notable for providing free food, lodging and medical care.
