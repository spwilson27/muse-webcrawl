Chip log

A chip log, also called a common log, ship log, or just log, is a navigation tool mariners use to estimate the speed of a vessel through water. Early chip logs were used from the late 16th or early 17th century, and the device is the origin of the nautical unit the knot.

Construction and use

The device consisted of a wooden board (the chip), usually quadrant-shaped and weighted with lead on its curved edge so that it floated upright and remained stationary in the water. The chip was attached by a bridle to a long line wound on a reel. The line had knots tied at regular intervals.

The chip was thrown over the stern, and as the ship moved away from it, the line ran out. A sailor counted how many knots passed in a fixed time measured by a sandglass (commonly 28 seconds), giving the ship's speed in "knots" — that is, nautical miles per hour.

Knot spacing

For a 28-second glass, knots were spaced about 47 feet 3 inches apart, since one nautical mile per hour corresponds to that distance in 28 seconds. The count was entered in the ship's logbook — hence the term "log" for a record of a vessel's progress.

History

The chip log is mentioned by William Bourne in 1574. It remained the standard method of speed measurement until mechanical taffrail logs and, later, pitot and Doppler logs superseded it in the 19th and 20th centuries.
