Hip replacement

Hip replacement is a surgical procedure in which the hip joint is replaced by a prosthetic implant, that is, a hip prosthesis. Hip replacement surgery can be performed as a total replacement or a hemi (half) replacement. Such joint replacement orthopaedic surgery is generally conducted to relieve arthritis pain or fix severe physical joint damage as part of a hip fracture treatment.

Indications

A total hip replacement (total hip arthroplasty) is the most common type of hip replacement surgery. It is performed primarily for osteoarthritis of the hip, but also for rheumatoid arthritis, avascular necrosis, traumatic arthritis and certain hip fractures. The procedure is generally recommended when pain and loss of function are severe and conservative treatments — medication, physical therapy and activity modification — have failed.

Procedure

In a total hip replacement, the damaged ball (femoral head) and socket (acetabulum) are removed and replaced with prosthetic components. The implant typically consists of a metal stem inserted into the femur, a ball component (metal or ceramic) replacing the femoral head, and a metal cup lined with plastic (polyethylene), ceramic or metal fitted into the socket. Components may be fixed with bone cement (polymethylmethacrylate) or designed for cementless fixation, in which bone grows into a porous surface. Surgery may be performed through posterior, lateral or anterior approaches.

History

Early attempts at hip replacement in the late 19th and early 20th centuries used materials such as ivory and glass. The modern era began with Sir John Charnley in the 1960s, whose low-friction arthroplasty combined a metal femoral stem, a small metal ball and a polyethylene cup fixed with acrylic bone cement. Charnley's design principles underpin most contemporary implants.

Recovery and outcomes

Most patients walk with assistance on the day of surgery or the following day and are discharged within a few days. Full recovery takes several weeks to months. Modern implants commonly last 20 years or more, though younger and more active patients may eventually require revision surgery.

Risks and complications

Complications can include infection, dislocation of the prosthetic ball, deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, leg-length inequality, fracture, loosening or wear of the implant, and — with metal-on-metal bearings — metallosis. Overall, hip replacement is regarded as one of the most successful operations in modern medicine.
