Jajmani system

The jajmani system is a traditional system of reciprocal exchange of goods and services between families of different castes in Indian villages. Under the system, landowning patron families (the jajmans) receive hereditary services from artisan and labouring families (the kamins), who are compensated with shares of the harvest, food, clothing, or small payments rather than cash wages.

Features

Relationships under the jajmani system are hereditary and enduring: a barber, carpenter, blacksmith, potter, washerman, priest, or agricultural labourer serves the same patron families generation after generation, and the right to serve is treated as a form of property that can be divided among heirs. In return, the patrons provide the serving families with a customary share of grain at harvest, meals at festivals, and assistance in times of need, giving the kamins a measure of economic security.

Interpretations

The system was described in detail by anthropologist William H. Wiser in his 1936 study of the Hindu jajmani system in a village in Uttar Pradesh. Later scholars debated its character: some emphasised its reciprocity and village solidarity, while others, notably Joan Mencher and Louis Dumont's critics, stressed the exploitation of lower-caste labourers by dominant landowning castes and described the system as a mechanism of caste hierarchy and coercion.

Decline

Since the mid-20th century the system has weakened under the influence of monetisation, mechanisation, migration to towns, land reform, and the commercial availability of manufactured goods and hired services, though elements of it persist in rural areas.
