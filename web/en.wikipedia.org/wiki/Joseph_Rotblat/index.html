Joseph Rotblat

Sir Joseph Rotblat (4 November 1908 – 31 August 2005) was a Polish-born British physicist who worked on the Manhattan Project and was the only scientist to leave it on moral grounds when it became clear Germany would not build an atomic bomb. He devoted the rest of his life to nuclear disarmament, co-founding the Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs, with which he shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 1995.

Early life and wartime work

Rotblat was born in Warsaw and studied physics at the Free University of Poland and the University of Warsaw, working on radioactivity. In 1939 he moved to Liverpool to work with James Chadwick; his wife, trapped in Poland by the outbreak of war, was murdered in the Holocaust. In Liverpool Rotblat showed the feasibility of a fission chain reaction, and in 1944 he joined the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos. When intelligence established that the German bomb programme had failed, he resigned and returned to Britain in late 1944 — the project's only such departure on grounds of conscience, an episode that led to his being briefly and baselessly suspected of espionage.

Peace work

After the war Rotblat redirected his research to medical physics at St Bartholomew's Hospital in London, becoming a leading authority on the biological effects of radiation, including fallout from atmospheric tests. In 1955 he was the youngest signatory of the Russell–Einstein Manifesto, and in 1957 he helped organise the first Pugwash Conference in Nova Scotia, serving as the movement's secretary-general and later president. He campaigned for a nuclear test ban, for the Hippocratic-style ethical obligations of scientists, and for the abolition of nuclear weapons. In 1995, fifty years after Hiroshima, he and the Pugwash Conferences received the Nobel Peace Prize. He died in London in 2005, aged 96.
