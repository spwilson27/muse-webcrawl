Hydrogen economy

The hydrogen economy is an umbrella term for proposed systems of delivering energy using hydrogen as an energy carrier. The term was popularized in the early 1970s, notably by John Bockris, and has been applied to proposals in which hydrogen produced from low-carbon sources replaces fossil fuels in sectors such as transport, industry, heating, and electricity generation. Hydrogen produces only water when burned or used in a fuel cell, which makes it attractive as a means of reducing greenhouse gas emissions, though it is not a primary energy source and must be manufactured from other resources.

Production

Almost all hydrogen produced today comes from fossil fuels, principally steam reforming of natural gas ("grey hydrogen"); reforming coupled with carbon capture and storage is termed "blue hydrogen". Electrolysis of water powered by renewable or nuclear electricity produces "green hydrogen" with near-zero direct emissions, but at higher cost. Global hydrogen production is around 95 million tonnes per year, used mainly in oil refining and ammonia and methanol production.

Proposed uses

  • Transport: fuel cell electric vehicles, trains, ships, and potentially aircraft.
  • Industry: direct reduced iron for steelmaking, high-temperature heat, and chemical feedstocks.
  • Energy storage: long-duration and seasonal storage of renewable electricity.
  • Heating: blending into natural gas networks or dedicated hydrogen boilers.

Criticism

Critics note the poor round-trip efficiency of hydrogen pathways compared with direct electrification: producing, compressing, transporting, and reconverting hydrogen can lose 60–75 percent of the original energy, making battery electric vehicles and heat pumps substantially more efficient for most uses. Other concerns include hydrogen leakage (hydrogen is an indirect greenhouse gas), the cost of new infrastructure, embrittlement of pipelines, and the small energy density of hydrogen by volume, requiring compression to 350–700 bar or liquefaction at −253 °C.
