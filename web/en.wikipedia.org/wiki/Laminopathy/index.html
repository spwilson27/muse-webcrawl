Laminopathy

The nuclear lamina underlies the inner nuclear membrane

Genes involvedLMNA, LMNB1, LMNB2, EMD and others
Structures affectedNuclear lamina, nuclear envelope
ExamplesEmery–Dreifuss muscular dystrophy, Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome, familial partial lipodystrophy

Laminopathies are a group of rare genetic disorders caused by mutations in genes encoding proteins of the nuclear lamina or proteins associated with it. The nuclear lamina is a meshwork of intermediate filaments — the lamins — lining the inner nuclear membrane, which gives the nucleus mechanical stability and regulates chromatin organisation and gene expression.

More than a dozen distinct clinical syndromes are recognised, ranging from muscular dystrophies and peripheral neuropathies to premature ageing (progeroid) syndromes and metabolic disorders. Despite the near-universal expression of lamins, most laminopathies affect only particular tissues, a puzzle that has driven much research into lamina function.

Classification

Laminopathies are commonly divided into those affecting striated muscle (such as Emery–Dreifuss muscular dystrophy and LMNA-related congenital muscular dystrophy), those affecting adipose tissue (familial partial lipodystrophies of the Dunnigan type), peripheral nerve (Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 2B1), and systemic progeroid syndromes (Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome, mandibuloacral dysplasia, restrictive dermopathy). Mutations in LMNA, which encodes lamins A and C, account for the largest number of conditions.

Mechanisms

Proposed mechanisms include structural fragility of nuclei under mechanical stress, altered gene-expression programmes through disturbed chromatin–lamina contacts, impaired DNA repair, and accumulation of the toxic mutant protein progerin in progeria, which remains permanently farnesylated and distorts the nuclear envelope.

Treatment

Management is largely supportive and tissue-specific, such as cardiac monitoring and devices in LMNA cardiomyopathy. The farnesyltransferase inhibitor lonafarnib has been approved for Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome, modestly extending survival.
