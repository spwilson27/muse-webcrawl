Promethium

Promethium

Symbol: Pm

Atomic number: 61

Group: Lanthanide

Longest-lived isotope: 145Pm (half-life 17.7 years)

Named after: Prometheus

Promethium is a chemical element with the symbol Pm and atomic number 61. All of its isotopes are radioactive; it is extremely rare, with only about 500–600 grams occurring naturally in the Earth's crust at any time, and is notable as one of only two radioactive elements followed by stable elements on the periodic table (the other being technetium).

History

The existence of an element between neodymium and samarium was predicted by Bohuslav Brauner in 1902. Claims of discovery as "illinium" and "florentium" proved spurious. In 1945, Jacob A. Marinsky, Lawrence E. Glendenin and Charles D. Coryell at Oak Ridge National Laboratory conclusively identified element 61 in fission products of uranium and by neutron bombardment of neodymium, naming it for Prometheus, the Titan who stole fire for humanity.

Properties and isotopes

Promethium is a soft beta emitter. Its most useful isotope, 147Pm, has a half-life of 2.62 years and emits low-energy beta radiation; the longest-lived isotope, 145Pm, has a half-life of 17.7 years. Promethium salts glow pale blue or green in the dark owing to their radioactivity.

Applications

Uses include luminous paint (largely superseded), atomic batteries generating electricity from beta decay for spacecraft and medical devices, portable X-ray sources, and thickness gauges. Because of its scarcity and radioactivity, applications remain specialised.
