Proportional representation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Proportional representation (PR) is a concept in voting systems in which the number of seats won by a party or group of candidates is proportionate to the number of votes received — in contrast to majoritarian/plurality systems, where the largest party may win a majority of seats with a minority of votes. PR is used in the majority of the world's democracies for national legislatures.

Methods

The most common method is party-list PR: voters choose a party list in a multi-member district, and seats are allocated by formulas such as D'Hondt or Sainte-Laguë, under closed lists (the party orders the candidates) or open lists (voters influence which candidates fill the seats). The single transferable vote achieves proportionality through ranked ballots in multi-member districts (used in Ireland and Malta). Mixed-member proportional systems (Germany, New Zealand) combine single-member districts with list seats that compensate to restore overall proportionality. Legal thresholds (e.g. 5% in Germany) exclude very small parties to limit fragmentation.

Consequences

PR tends to produce multiparty legislatures and coalition governments, higher turnout, and greater descriptive representation of women and minorities; its critics point to weaker constituency links (in closed-list forms), party-elite control of candidate selection, and the kingmaker power of small parties. Research generally finds PR governments no less stable or effective than majoritarian ones, with somewhat more redistributive policies — conclusions that remain debated.
