House of LaBeija

The House of LaBeija is generally regarded as the first "house" in ball culture, the African-American and Latino LGBTQ+ subculture centred on competitive balls in New York City. It was founded in the early 1970s by Crystal LaBeija, a drag queen and prominent figure of the Harlem ball scene, following the events dramatised in the 1968 documentary The Queen, in which Crystal protested the racism she perceived in the mainstream drag pageant circuit.

Founding

Houses are chosen families — social structures led by a "mother" or "father" that provide support, belonging and mentorship to queer youth, many of them homeless or rejected by their families of origin. After Crystal LaBeija and fellow queen Lottie founded the first house ball in the early 1970s, billed as hosted by the House of LaBeija, the house system became the organising framework of ball culture, with competing houses such as the House of Xtravaganza and the House of Ninja following.

Leadership and legacy

After Crystal's death, the model and ballroom figure Pepper LaBeija became mother of the house in the late 1970s and led it for over two decades; she was one of the central figures of the 1990 documentary Paris Is Burning. The House of LaBeija remains active in the contemporary ballroom scene, and its founding is widely credited with establishing the template of house-parent leadership, kinship and competitive "walking" that defines ball culture worldwide, later popularised for mainstream audiences by the television series Pose.
