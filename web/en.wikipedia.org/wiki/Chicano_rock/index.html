Chicano rock

Chicano rock is a style of rock music performed by Mexican American (Chicano) musicians, blending American rock and roll and rhythm and blues with Latin American and Mexican influences. It emerged in the United States, particularly in California and the Southwest, in the 1950s and has continued through successive waves — from the East Los Angeles sound of the 1960s to the Chicano punk and alternative bands of later decades.

Origins

The foundational figure of Chicano rock is Ritchie Valens (Richard Valenzuela), whose 1958 recording of the traditional Mexican song "La Bamba" became one of the first Spanish-language rock and roll hits in the United States. In the early 1960s, bands such as Cannibal & the Headhunters ("Land of a Thousand Dances") and Thee Midniters developed the "Eastside sound" of East Los Angeles. Chan Romero ("Hippy Hippy Shake") and other performers contributed to the early scene.

1970s and the Chicano movement

During the Chicano civil rights movement, Chicano rock became a vehicle for cultural identity. Santana, led by Carlos Santana in San Francisco, fused Latin rhythms with rock and blues to international success. In Los Angeles, El Chicano scored a hit with "Viva Tirado" (1970), and bands such as Tierra, Malo and War (with Chicano members) explored Latin-funk-rock fusions. Rubén Guevara and bands like The Zeros later seeded Chicano punk.

Later developments

The 1980s success of Los Lobos — whose version of "La Bamba" topped the charts in 1987 for the film of the same name — brought Chicano rock renewed mainstream attention. Subsequent artists including Los Lonely Boys ("Heaven", 2004, dubbed "Texican rock"), Robert Rodriguez's projects, and Chicano punk and alternative bands such as The Plugz, The Brat, and later Rage Against the Machine's Zack de la Rocha (of partial Chicano heritage) extended the tradition. The genre's legacy is documented in scholarship and in PBS's Latin Music USA series.
