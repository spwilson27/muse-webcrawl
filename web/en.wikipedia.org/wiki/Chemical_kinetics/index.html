Chemical kinetics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Concentration–time curves for reactions of different orders]

Chemical kinetics, also known as reaction kinetics, is the branch of physical chemistry concerned with the rates of chemical reactions and the molecular mechanisms by which they occur. Where thermodynamics (see chemical equilibrium) predicts whether a reaction can happen, kinetics determines how fast it proceeds.

Rate laws and reaction order

The rate of a reaction is commonly expressed by a rate law linking it to the concentrations of the reactants:

rate = k [A]m [B]n

where k is the rate constant and the exponents m and n — the orders with respect to each reactant — are determined experimentally and need not match the stoichiometric coefficients. Integrated rate laws and half-lives follow from the overall order of the reaction.

Factors affecting rate

Reaction rate depends on concentration, temperature, pressure, surface area, solvent, and the presence of a catalyst. The temperature dependence is described by the Arrhenius equation, k = A·e−Ea/RT, in terms of an activation energy Ea and a pre-exponential factor. Collision theory and transition state theory provide molecular interpretations of these quantities: only collisions with sufficient energy and proper orientation surmount the activation barrier through the activated complex.

Mechanisms

Complex reactions proceed through sequences of elementary steps involving intermediates; the slowest, rate-determining step governs the overall rate. The study of mechanisms — through rate laws, isotopic labelling, and spectroscopy — underpins fields from catalysis and enzyme kinetics (Michaelis–Menten) to atmospheric and combustion chemistry.
