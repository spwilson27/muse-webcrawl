Codicology

Codicology is the study of manuscript books — codices — as material objects: their physical structure, materials, production, and history, rather than (or in addition to) the texts they contain. It is sometimes described as the "archaeology of the book". The term was coined in French (codicologie) in the mid-twentieth century, notably by Alphonse Dain, building on the work of scholars such as Charles Samaran.

Scope

Codicologists examine the writing supports (parchment, paper, papyrus), the arrangement of leaves into gatherings or quires, pricking and ruling patterns that govern page layout, inks and pigments, the work of scribes and rubricators, and the binding — boards, sewing, clasps, and decoration. Watermarks in paper can date and localise a manuscript; so can the "flesh and hair" alternation of parchment leaves (Gregory's Rule).

Methods

The discipline developed quantitative methods in the later twentieth century, especially in the French and Italian schools and in the work of Carla Bozzolo and Ezio Ornato, analysing large corpora statistically to reconstruct workshop practices and the economics of book production. Codicological evidence can reveal how a text was compiled, copied, corrected, bound with other texts (miscellanies), owned, and read, complementing palaeography, which studies handwriting itself.

Applications

For manuscripts such as the Codex Sinaiticus and the Codex Argenteus, codicological analysis of scribes' hands, correction campaigns, and materials has been essential to reconstructing their production and provenance. Modern digital imaging and multispectral analysis have extended the field to palimpsests and erased texts.
