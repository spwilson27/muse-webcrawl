Bernoulli's principle

Bernoulli's principle is a key concept in fluid dynamics stating that for an incompressible, inviscid flow, an increase in the speed of a fluid occurs simultaneously with a decrease in static pressure or a decrease in the fluid's potential energy. The principle is named after Daniel Bernoulli, who published it in his book Hydrodynamica in 1738. It can be understood as a statement of the conservation of energy for flowing fluids.

The equation

For steady flow along a streamline, Bernoulli's equation states:

p + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant

where p is pressure, ρ fluid density, v flow speed, g gravitational acceleration and h elevation. The term ½ρv² is the dynamic pressure; the sum expresses that total mechanical energy per unit volume is conserved along a streamline when no work is added or removed.

Applications and misconceptions

The principle underlies the Venturi effect — fluid accelerating through a constriction drops in pressure — used in flow meters, atomisers and carburettors. It is often invoked to explain aerodynamic lift, but popular "equal transit time" explanations of wing lift are incorrect: lift arises from the deflection of airflow and pressure distributions described more completely by the Navier–Stokes equations and circulation theory. Bernoulli's principle applies only under its assumptions; compressible or viscous flows require the extended forms of the equation.
