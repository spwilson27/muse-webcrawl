Horn clause


Structure of a definite Horn clause

In mathematical logic and logic programming, a Horn clause is a clause (a disjunction of literals) with at most one positive literal. Horn clauses are named after the logician Alfred Horn, who first pointed out their significance in 1951. They are the basis of logic programming languages such as Prolog.

Types

A Horn clause with exactly one positive literal is a definite clause and can be written as an implication: if A1 and A2 and … and An, then B—a rule of the form used for rules and facts in Prolog. A Horn clause with no positive literal is a goal clause, representing a query to be proved. A set of definite clauses together with a goal clause can be evaluated efficiently by resolution, and satisfiability of propositional Horn formulas can be decided in linear time.

Use in logic programming

Programs in Prolog consist entirely of Horn clauses, evaluated by SLD resolution using backward chaining: a goal is matched against the heads of definite clauses, and its body atoms become new subgoals. Horn clauses are also central to the database query language Datalog, to description logics, and to automated theorem proving. First-order Horn clause logic is Turing complete, while the propositional fragment is tractable, a combination that makes Horn clauses a key tool in computer science and artificial intelligence.
