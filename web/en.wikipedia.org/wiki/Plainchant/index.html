Plainchant

TypeSacred monophonic chant
Principal traditionsGregorian, Ambrosian, Mozarabic, Byzantine chant

Plainchant or plainsong is a body of chants used in the liturgies of the Western Church. When referring to the term plainsong, it is those sacred pieces that are composed in Latin text. Plainsong is monophonic — a single, unaccompanied melodic line — and sung in free rhythm following the natural accents of the text rather than a regular metre.

Traditions

The best-known plainchant tradition is Gregorian chant, the central tradition of Western plainchant, codified under the Carolingians and traditionally attributed — largely legendarily — to Pope Gregory I. Regional traditions include Ambrosian chant in Milan, the nearly extinct Mozarabic chant of Spain, Old Roman and Beneventan chant in Italy, and Gallican chant in Francia. The chants of the Eastern churches, such as Byzantine chant, are related cousins stemming from the same early Christian practice.

Notation and modes

Plainchant melodies are organised in the system of eight church modes. Early chant was transmitted orally; from about the 9th century scribes developed neumatic notation, mnemonic signs above the text that evolved into square notation on four-line staves, still used in modern chant books such as the Liber Usualis and Graduale Romanum.

History and revival

Plainchant formed the foundation of Western art music: polyphony grew out of organum sung over chant melodies, and chant remained the official music of the Roman Catholic liturgy. Its performance declined after the Middle Ages before a celebrated 19th-century revival led by the monks of Solesmes Abbey in France, whose rhythmic interpretations still dominate. Chant continues to influence composers and popular recordings alike.
