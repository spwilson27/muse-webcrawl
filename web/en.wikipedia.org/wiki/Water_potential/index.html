Water potential

Water moves from higher to lower water potential through the soil–plant–atmosphere continuum.

Water potential is the potential energy of water per unit volume relative to pure water in reference conditions. It quantifies the tendency of water to move from one area to another due to osmosis, gravity, mechanical pressure and matrix effects such as surface tension. Water potential is denoted by the Greek letter Ψ (psi) and is expressed in units of pressure, typically megapascals (MPa). Pure water at atmospheric pressure has a water potential of zero by definition, and water in soils and plants almost always has a negative value.

Components

Total water potential is the sum of component potentials: pressure potential (Ψp), arising from physical pressure — positive in turgid plant cells pressing against their walls, and negative (tension) in the xylem; solute potential or osmotic potential (Ψs), always negative, because dissolved solutes lower the free energy of water; matric potential (Ψm), from the attraction of water to surfaces such as soil particles and cell walls; and gravitational potential (Ψg), significant in tall trees.

Importance

Water potential gradients drive the movement of water through plants: water enters roots from moister soil, rises through the xylem under tension generated by transpiration from the leaves (where potentials are lowest), and exits to the atmosphere. The concept also underlies soil physics — field capacity and wilting point are defined by it — and cell water relations, including turgor pressure and plasmolysis. It is commonly measured with a pressure chamber (Scholander bomb) or psychrometers.
