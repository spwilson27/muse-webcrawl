Herman Snellen (February 19, 1834 – January 18, 1908) was a Dutch ophthalmologist who introduced the Snellen chart to study visual acuity in 1862. The chart, with its rows of shrinking block letters ("optotypes"), and the "Snellen fraction" it yields — 20/20 vision (6/6 in metric) — remain the worldwide standard for eye examinations more than 160 years later.

Life and career

Snellen was born in Zeist and studied medicine at Utrecht University, where he was a pupil of the great physiologist Franciscus Donders. In 1858 he joined the newly founded Netherlands Hospital for Eye Patients (Nederlandsch Gasthuis voor Ooglijders) in Utrecht, becoming its director after Donders, and in 1884 he was appointed professor of ophthalmology at Utrecht. He was an accomplished eye surgeon, publishing on glaucoma, astigmatism, and squint operations, and in 1899 presided over the International Congress of Ophthalmology held in Utrecht.

The Snellen chart

Before Snellen, "vision" was assessed crudely — by whether a patient could read a sample text. Snellen instead defined a standard: the normal eye can distinguish a letter whose strokes subtend one minute of arc, the whole letter fitting a five-minute square. His 1862 chart printed capital letters in a geometric series of sizes on an otherwise blank card, tested at a standard distance of 20 feet (6 metres). Acuity is recorded as a fraction — test distance over the distance at which the smallest line read subtends five minutes of arc — so 20/20 (6/6) denotes standard vision and 20/40 means the patient reads at 20 feet what a normal eye reads at 40. Snellen's colleague Eduard Jäger produced rival reading cards, but the Snellen system prevailed for distance testing worldwide.

Legacy

Snellen died at Utrecht in 1908. His name is embedded in everyday language through the "Snellen fraction," and his chart — now joined by the logMAR and ETDRS charts for research — remains the universal first test of sight, from opticians' rooms to driving-licence examinations.
