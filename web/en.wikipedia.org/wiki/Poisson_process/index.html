Poisson process

In probability theory, a Poisson process is a stochastic process that counts the number of random events occurring in a given interval of time or space, where the events happen with a known constant mean rate and independently of the time since the last event. It is the continuous-time analogue of the Poisson distribution and a fundamental model in queueing theory and statistics.

Definition

A homogeneous Poisson process with rate λ > 0 has: (1) independent increments — counts in disjoint intervals are independent; (2) stationary increments — the distribution of the count in an interval depends only on its length; and (3) counts in an interval of length t following a Poisson distribution with mean λt. The waiting times between successive events (interarrival times) are exponentially distributed, a characterisation equivalent to the counting definition. A non-homogeneous (inhomogeneous) Poisson process generalises the model by allowing the rate λ(t) to vary with time.

Applications

Poisson processes model radioactive decay, telephone call arrivals, insurance claims, photon detection, and many other phenomena. Named after the French mathematician Siméon Denis Poisson, the process is a special case of a renewal process and of a point process; its spatial version is the Poisson point process.
