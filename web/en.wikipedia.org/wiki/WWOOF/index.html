WWOOF (World Wide Opportunities on Organic Farms) is a loose network of national organizations that facilitate work-exchange placements on organic farms. Volunteer workers ("WWOOFers") live and work with host farmers, typically for a few hours of help per day in exchange for food, accommodation, and an opportunity to learn about organic agriculture and sustainable lifestyles. No money changes hands between host and guest.

History

WWOOF was founded in 1971 in England by Sue Coppard, a secretary living in London who wanted access to the countryside and to support the organic movement. The organization began as "Working Weekends on Organic Farms", arranging weekend trial visits to a farm in Sussex. Demand quickly grew, stays lengthened, and the experiment evolved into an international movement. The acronym has been reinterpreted over the years — first as "Willing Workers on Organic Farms", then as "World Wide Opportunities on Organic Farms" in 2000, reflecting the globalization of the network.

How it works

WWOOF is organized through national groups in more than 50 countries, coordinated by the Federation of WWOOF Organisations (FoWO). Volunteers pay a membership fee to their national organization to access lists of hosts in that country. Hosts, in turn, list their farms and the work on offer — which can include gardening, animal care, building, winemaking, cheese-making, and farmers' markets. Arrangements are made directly between hosts and volunteers, and stays range from a few days to several months.

Criticism and legal status

Because WWOOFing sits in a grey area between volunteering, tourism, and employment, its legal status varies by country. In some jurisdictions volunteers on tourist visas may technically be considered workers, and questions have been raised about exploitation and safety. National WWOOF organizations generally set expectations that the exchange be educational and non-commercial in spirit.
