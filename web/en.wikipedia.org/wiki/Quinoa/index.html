Quinoa

Species: Chenopodium quinoa
Family: Amaranthaceae
Origin: Andean region

Quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa) is a flowering plant in the amaranth family grown as a crop primarily for its edible seeds. It is a pseudocereal, not a true grass, closely related to spinach and beets. The seeds are rich in protein, dietary fibre, B vitamins, and minerals, and are unusual among plant foods in containing all nine essential amino acids.

History

Quinoa was domesticated in the Andean region of South America some 5,000 years ago and was a staple of the Inca, who called it the "mother grain". Spanish colonisation suppressed its cultivation in favour of Old World cereals, but it remained central to Andean subsistence farming at altitudes up to 4,000 metres.

Cultivation and use

Peru and Bolivia produce most of the world crop. Seeds are coated in bitter saponins that are removed by rinsing or polishing before cooking; they are prepared like rice, used in salads, soups, and flours, and are naturally gluten-free. International demand surged in the early 21st century, and the United Nations declared 2013 the International Year of Quinoa. Rising prices prompted debate about effects on Andean consumers and on land use.
