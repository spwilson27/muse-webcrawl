Insulin-like growth factor 2

Insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF-2) is one of three protein hormones that share structural similarity to insulin. It is a single-chain polypeptide of 67 amino acids, encoded by the IGF2 gene on human chromosome 11p15.5, and acts through the IGF-1 receptor and the IGF-2/mannose-6-phosphate receptor to mediate growth, especially during gestation. IGF-2 is considered the primary growth factor required for early development, whereas IGF-1 is more important for later growth.

Genomic imprinting

IGF2 is a classic example of genomic imprinting: in most tissues only the copy inherited from the father is expressed, while the maternal copy is silenced. Its expression is coordinated with the neighbouring long non-coding RNA H19, which is expressed from the opposite (maternal) allele, under control of a shared imprinting control region. Loss of imprinting — activation of the normally silent maternal copy — occurs in several cancers and in the overgrowth disorder Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome. Conversely, disruptions reducing IGF-2 signalling are associated with the growth-restriction disorder Silver–Russell syndrome.

Function

IGF-2 is produced mainly by the liver and in many fetal tissues, and circulates bound to IGF-binding proteins, principally IGFBP-3, which regulate its availability. During pregnancy it promotes placental and fetal growth. In adults it persists in the circulation and has roles in tissues including the brain, where it has been linked to memory consolidation in animal studies. Overproduction of incompletely processed "big" IGF-2 by certain tumours can cause non-islet cell tumour hypoglycaemia (Doege–Potter syndrome).
