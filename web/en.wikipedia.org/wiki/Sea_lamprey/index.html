The sea lamprey (Petromyzon marinus) is a parasitic lamprey — a jawless fish of an ancient lineage — native to the Atlantic Ocean and coasts of Europe and North America. Its eel-like body lacks jaws, scales and paired fins; instead it has a round, sucker-like oral disc lined with horny teeth and a rasping tongue, with which the adult attaches to fish and feeds on their blood and tissues.

Life cycle

Sea lampreys are anadromous: adults spawn in freshwater streams in spring and die afterwards. The blind, worm-like larvae (ammocoetes) burrow in stream sediments and filter-feed for several years before metamorphosing into parasitic juveniles, which migrate to the sea (or a lake) and feed on fish for 12–18 months before returning to spawn.

Invasion of the Great Lakes

Sea lampreys entered the upper North American Great Lakes through the improved Welland Canal by the 1930s–40s. Without effective predators, they devastated commercial fisheries — particularly lake trout, burbot and whitefish — driving some stocks to near collapse by the 1950s. Control by the binational Great Lakes Fishery Commission, using the selective lampricide TFM, barriers and trapping, has reduced populations by roughly 90% from their peak, though control is ongoing and permanent. In its native European range, by contrast, the lamprey is prized as a food fish and is now protected in places.

As food

Lamprey has been eaten since Roman times (it reputedly killed King Henry I of England after "a surfeit of lampreys"). It remains a delicacy in parts of France (Bordeaux's lamproie à la bordelaise), Portugal and Finland.
