Overview

Salamanders are a group of amphibians (order Urodela) typically characterised by a lizard-like appearance, with slender bodies, blunt snouts, short limbs and a tail present in adults. About 800 species are known, ranging from the tiny Thorius of Mexico to the Chinese giant salamander, which exceeds 1.5 metres. Most have four toes on the forefeet and five on the hind feet, and moist, permeable skin tied to wet habitats.

Biology

Many salamanders can regenerate limbs, tail, and parts of the spinal cord and eye — a capacity intensely studied for regenerative medicine. The axolotl, a neotenic Mexican salamander that retains larval gills for life, is a laboratory icon now critically endangered in its wild Xochimilco canals. Salamanders face severe threats from habitat loss and the chytrid fungi Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis and B. salamandrivorans (Bsal). The Appalachian Mountains are a global hotspot of salamander diversity (the lungless Plethodontidae).
