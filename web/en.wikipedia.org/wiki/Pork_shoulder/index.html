Pork shoulder

Pork shoulder is a cut of pork comprising the upper part of the pig's front leg and the muscles overlying the shoulder blade. It is a relatively tough, well-marbled, and inexpensive cut, rich in connective tissue, which becomes tender and succulent when cooked slowly at low temperatures. In North American butchery the shoulder primal is usually divided into two subprimals: the upper portion, the Boston butt (or pork butt), and the lower portion, the picnic shoulder (or picnic ham). British butchers typically treat the shoulder (often called the "shoulder" or, when boned and rolled, "spare rib joint") as a single roasting or braising cut.

Boston butt

Despite its name, the Boston butt comes from the front of the pig, not the rear; the name is generally traced to the barrels — called "butts" — in which New England butchers packed the cut for storage and shipment. The butt contains the shoulder blade bone and a group of hard-working muscles with generous intramuscular fat and collagen. It is the cut of choice for pulled pork in American barbecue, where it is typically seasoned with a dry rub, smoked for many hours at around 107–121 °C (225–250 °F) until it reaches about 90–96 °C (195–205 °F) internally, then rested and shredded.

Picnic shoulder

The picnic shoulder extends from below the butt down to just above the front hoof and includes the hock. It usually has more bone and skin and somewhat less uniform meat than the butt, and is correspondingly cheaper. It can be roasted fresh, cured and smoked in the manner of a ham (hence "picnic ham"), or braised. In Latin American and Filipino cooking, the picnic shoulder and related front-leg cuts are used for dishes such as pernil, lechón, and pata dishes.

Cooking methods

Because the shoulder muscles are heavily exercised and threaded with collagen, fast dry-heat cooking leaves the meat tough; slow cooking converts collagen to gelatin, yielding a moist, fork-tender result. Standard techniques include low-and-slow smoking, braising in liquid, slow roasting, pressure cooking, and grinding for sausages and charcuterie — pork shoulder is the principal meat in most fresh and smoked sausages and in salami blends, typically at a fat content of 20–30 percent. Cubed shoulder is also the basis of stews and braises across many cuisines, from Mexican carnitas and chile verde to German Schäufele and British slow-roast shoulder with crackling.
