Prenuptial agreement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Prenuptial agreement
Also calledPrenup, antenuptial agreement, premarital agreement
PurposeDefine property and spousal-support rights on divorce or death

A prenuptial agreement (commonly a "prenup") is a written contract entered into by a couple before marriage that sets out how assets, debts and spousal support will be handled if the marriage ends by divorce or death, in place of the default rules of marital property law.

Contents

Prenups typically classify separate and marital property, protect businesses and inheritances, allocate debts, and may set or waive alimony. In most jurisdictions they cannot determine child custody or child support, and provisions encouraging divorce or waiving support so as to leave a spouse on public assistance may be unenforceable.

Enforceability

Courts in the United States generally enforce prenups if they were signed voluntarily, with full financial disclosure and ideally independent legal counsel for both parties; many states have adopted the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act. Agreements can be challenged for duress (e.g., presentation on the eve of the wedding), unconscionability or lack of disclosure. In England and Wales, historically unenforceable as contrary to public policy, prenups were given decisive weight by the Supreme Court in Radmacher v Granatino [2010] — appeals in that line may reach the Court of Appeal — provided the agreement is freely entered into with full appreciation of its implications and does not prejudice children. Wealthy families, such as business dynasties like the Walton family, frequently use such agreements alongside trusts.

History

Antecedents include the Jewish ketubah and Roman dowry contracts; the modern prenup rose in the US after states adopted no-fault divorce in the 1970s and courts began upholding agreements in cases such as Posner v. Posner (Florida, 1970).
