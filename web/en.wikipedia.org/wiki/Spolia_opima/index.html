Spolia opima

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The spolia opima ("rich spoils") were the armour, arms, and other effects that an ancient Roman general stripped from the body of an opposing commander slain in single combat. They were regarded as the most honourable of all war trophies and were dedicated to Jupiter Feretrius on the Capitoline Hill in Rome. The spoils were considered opima only when taken by a Roman commander fighting under his own auspices who personally killed the enemy leader.

History

Roman tradition recorded only three occasions on which the spolia opima were won. The first was by Romulus, the legendary founder of Rome, who slew Acron, king of the Caeninenses, and dedicated his armour in the Temple of Jupiter Feretrius, said to be the first temple built in Rome. The second was Aulus Cornelius Cossus, who killed Lars Tolumnius, king of Veii, in the fifth century BC — though ancient sources disagreed over whether Cossus held the rank required to qualify. The third and last was Marcus Claudius Marcellus, who slew the Insubrian king Viridomarus (or Britomartus) at the Battle of Clastidium in 222 BC and used the spoils as the subject of his ovation.

The Crassus controversy

In 29 BC Marcus Licinius Crassus, governor of Macedonia and grandson of the triumvir, killed Deldo, king of the Bastarnae, in single combat and sought to dedicate the spolia opima. The emperor Augustus blocked the claim, arguing on the basis of an inscription on Cossus's linen breastplate that only a serving consul commanding under his own auspices could make the dedication — a precedent that excluded Crassus, who had campaigned as a proconsul nominally under Augustus's auspices. Historians have long suspected that Augustus manipulated the antiquarian evidence to reserve the supreme military honour for himself and his family.

Temple of Jupiter Feretrius

The spoils were hung on an oak frame or trunk and deposited in the small Temple of Jupiter Feretrius on the Capitoline. By the reign of Augustus the temple had fallen into disrepair; the emperor restored it, and the discovery there of Cossus's inscribed corselet formed the crux of the legal argument against Crassus.
