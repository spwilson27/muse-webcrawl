Composite order

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. This article is about the classical architectural order. For the weapon, see composite bow.

The Composite order is one of the five classical orders of architecture, developed in ancient Rome. It combines the volutes of the Ionic order with the acanthus leaves of the Corinthian order: its capital has large Ionic scrolls set above two tiers of Corinthian acanthus leaves, with an echinus decorated with egg-and-dart ornament. Renaissance theorists, who systematised the orders, considered the Composite the most ornate and slender of all.

The Composite capital merges Ionic volutes with Corinthian foliage.

History

Although mixed capitals occur earlier, the canonical Composite order appears in imperial Rome — an early prominent example is the Arch of Titus (c. AD 81) in Rome, whose columns are often called the first surviving use of the fully developed order. Sebastiano Serlio's treatise (1537) codified it as the fifth order, ranking it above the Corinthian in elaboration, and it became a favourite of Renaissance, Baroque, and Neoclassical architects seeking maximum richness, as in the work of Michelangelo on the Capitoline Hill.

Characteristics

Composite columns typically have the slender proportions of the Corinthian (about ten diameters high), fluted shafts, and an entablature that may be enriched with modillions and ornament. Because it blends two orders, the Composite was sometimes said to symbolise the union of Ionic grace with Corinthian magnificence, and it was often used on triumphal arches and the uppermost storeys of theatres and amphitheatres, where the sequence of orders rose from Tuscan and Doric below.
