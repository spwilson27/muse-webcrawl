Takings Clause

Takings Clause

SourceFifth Amendment, U.S. Constitution
Text"...nor shall private property be taken for public use, without just compensation."
Related doctrineEminent domain; regulatory takings
Landmark casesPenn Central (1978); Lucas (1992); Kelo (2005)

The Takings Clause is the final clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution: "nor shall private property be taken for public use, without just compensation." It constrains the power of eminent domain in two ways: property may be taken only for a "public use", and the owner must receive "just compensation", generally the fair market value of the property taken.

Incorporation and physical takings

Originally binding only the federal government, the Clause was applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment in Chicago, Burlington & Quincy Railroad Co. v. Chicago (1897). A direct appropriation or physical occupation of property is the clearest case of a taking: in Loretto v. Teleprompter (1982) even a small permanent physical occupation was held compensable, and in Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid (2021) a regulation granting union organisers periodic access to farm property was treated as a per se taking.

Regulatory takings

In Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon (1922), Justice Holmes recognised that a regulation that "goes too far" can amount to a taking even without physical occupation. Modern doctrine applies the ad hoc balancing test of Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City (1978), weighing the regulation's economic impact, its interference with investment-backed expectations, and the character of the government action. In Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council (1992), a regulation destroying all economically beneficial use was held a categorical taking unless background principles of state property law already barred the use.

Public use

The Supreme Court has read "public use" expansively as "public purpose". In Berman v. Parker (1954) and Kelo v. City of New London (2005), takings transferring property to private parties were upheld where they served a public purpose such as economic development — a holding that proved controversial and prompted many states to tighten their own eminent-domain laws.
