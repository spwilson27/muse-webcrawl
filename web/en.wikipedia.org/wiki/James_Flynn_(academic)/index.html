Life

James Robert Flynn FRSNZ (28 April 1934 – 11 December 2020) was an American-born New Zealand researcher in intelligence and political philosophy, professor emeritus at the University of Otago in Dunedin. Trained in political science at the University of Chicago (PhD 1958), he began his career writing on ethics and politics, opposing the Vietnam War, and stood for Parliament in New Zealand on the left. A socialist and civil-liberties campaigner throughout his life, he came to intelligence research as a critic of hereditarian theories of race and IQ.

The Flynn effect

Studying standardisation samples of IQ tests, Flynn documented that raw IQ scores rose steadily throughout the twentieth century — roughly 3 points per decade across dozens of countries, largest on abstract reasoning tests such as Raven's Progressive Matrices. This secular rise, named the Flynn effect by Herrnstein and Murray (who popularised Flynn's data in The Bell Curve), poses a paradox: either earlier generations were drastically less intelligent, or IQ tests measure culturally shifting cognitive skills rather than pure innate capacity. Flynn's own explanation stressed modernity itself: better nutrition, more schooling, smaller families, test familiarity, and above all a culture that teaches people to see the world through "scientific spectacles" of classification and hypothetical reasoning. His 2007 book What Is Intelligence? elaborated the theory; later research has found the effect slowing or reversing (a "negative Flynn effect") in some Western countries.

Other work

Flynn's moral philosophy, including How to Defend Humane Ideals (2000), argued that moral progress rests on debating ideals to reflective equilibrium. His final book, A Book Too Risky to Publish (2020), on free speech, appeared after controversy when a British publisher declined it.
