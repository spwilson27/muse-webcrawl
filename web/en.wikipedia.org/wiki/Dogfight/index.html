Dogfight

A dogfight, or dog fight, is an aerial battle between fighter aircraft conducted at close range. Modern terminology for air-to-air combat is air combat manoeuvring (ACM), or, when considered as a whole, aerial warfare.

History

World War I

Dogfighting first appeared during World War I, as pilots progressed from reconnaissance duties to attacking enemy aircraft with pistols, then with synchronized machine guns firing through the propeller arc. Manoeuvres codified by aces such as Oswald Boelcke — whose Dicta Boelcke laid out rules for attack — and Max Immelmann, namesake of the Immelmann turn, formed the basis of fighter tactics.

World War II

By World War II, faster monoplane fighters fought large battles such as the Battle of Britain. Tactics emphasized energy, altitude advantage, and formation fighting (the "finger-four"); turning fights remained common, especially in the Pacific, where lighter Japanese fighters initially out-turned heavier American types, prompting tactics such as the Thach Weave.

Jet age and missiles

Jet fighters fought dogfights over Korea and Vietnam. The advent of guided air-to-air missiles led many planners to predict the end of the dogfight — the early F-4 Phantom II was built without an internal gun — but combat experience in Vietnam showed missiles were unreliable and close combat persisted, prompting renewed emphasis on guns, agility, and training programs such as the U.S. Navy's TOPGUN school (1969).

Basic fighter manoeuvres

Standard manoeuvres include the barrel roll, split S, Immelmann turn, scissors, high- and low-yo-yo, and the Herbst manoeuvre. Pilots manage energy — trading altitude for speed — and attempt to reach a position behind the opponent (the "six o'clock" position), where weapons employment is easiest and the opponent can least effectively return fire.

Modern era

Modern fighters combine helmet-mounted sights and high-off-boresight missiles, allowing shots at extreme angles, while beyond-visual-range missiles, sensors, and data links have made long-range engagements dominant. Nonetheless, within-visual-range combat remains part of pilot training, and thrust-vectoring aircraft can perform post-stall manoeuvres once considered impossible.
