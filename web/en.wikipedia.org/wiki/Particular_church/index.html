Particular church

In Catholic canon law and ecclesiology, a particular church (Latin: ecclesia particularis) is an ecclesiastical community of the faithful in communion with the pope. The term is used in two principal senses: for a diocese (and similar territorial circumscriptions), and for the autonomous (sui iuris) churches that together constitute the Catholic Church.

Dioceses as particular churches

The Second Vatican Council's decree Christus Dominus and the 1983 Code of Canon Law (canon 368) define a particular church primarily as a diocese: a portion of the people of God entrusted to a bishop, in which the one, holy, catholic and apostolic Church of Christ is truly present and operative. Prelatures, territorial abbacies, apostolic vicariates and similar structures are assimilated to dioceses.

Sui iuris churches

The Catholic Church is a communion of 24 autonomous churches: the Latin Church and 23 Eastern Catholic Churches, each with its own liturgical rite, theology, spirituality and canon law. Major examples include the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church, the Maronite Church, the Syro-Malabar Church and the Melkite Greek Catholic Church. These too are called particular churches sui iuris, each headed either by a patriarch, a major archbishop, a metropolitan or another hierarch, in full communion with the Bishop of Rome.

Theology

The relationship between the universal Church and the particular churches was a major theme of post-conciliar theology, summarised in the formula that the universal Church exists "in and from" the particular churches (in quibus et ex quibus, Lumen gentium 23) — neither a federation of independent churches nor a single organisation of which dioceses are mere branches.
