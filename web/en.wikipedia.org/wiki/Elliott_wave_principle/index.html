Elliott wave principle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


An idealised Elliott wave pattern

The Elliott wave principle, or Elliott wave theory, is a form of technical analysis that finance traders use to analyze financial market cycles and forecast market trends by identifying extremes in investor psychology and price movements.

Ralph Nelson Elliott (1871–1948), an American accountant, developed the concept in the 1930s. Elliott proposed that market prices unfold in specific patterns, which practitioners today call "Elliott waves", or simply "waves". Elliott published his theory of market behavior in the book The Wave Principle in 1938, summarised it in a series of articles in Financial World magazine in 1939, and covered it most comprehensively in his final major work Nature's Laws: The Secret of the Universe in 1946.

Description

The Elliott wave principle is a detailed description of how groups of people behave in financial markets. It reveals that mass psychology swings from optimism to pessimism and back in a natural sequence, creating specific Elliott wave patterns in price movements.

Elliott stated that "because man is subject to rhythmical procedure, calculations having to do with his activities can be projected far into the future with a justification and certainty heretofore unattainable." The patterns are subdivided into motive waves, which move in the direction of the main trend, and corrective waves, which move against it. The basic pattern consists of five waves in the direction of the trend (labelled 1 through 5) followed by three corrective waves (labelled A, B and C).

Wave personality

Elliott wave analysts (or "Elliotticians") hold that each individual wave has its own signature or characteristic, which typically reflects the mass psychology of the moment. Understanding those personalities is key to the application of the Wave Principle; they are defined below: (Definitions assume a bull market in equities)

  • Five wave patterns (dominant trend):
    1. Wave one is rarely obvious at its inception. When the first wave of a new bull market begins, the fundamental news is almost universally negative. The previous trend is considered still strongly in force.
    2. Wave two corrects wave one, but can never extend beyond the starting point of wave one. Typically, the news is still bad.
    3. Wave three is usually the largest and most powerful wave in a trend (although some research suggests that in commodity markets, wave five is the largest). The news is now positive and fundamental analysts raise earnings estimates.
    4. Wave four is typically clearly corrective. Prices may meander sideways for an extended period.
    5. Wave five is the final leg in the direction of the dominant trend. The news is almost universally positive and everyone is bullish.
  • Three wave patterns (corrective trend): After the motive wave, a correction takes place. The three waves of the correction are:
    1. Wave A: Corrections are typically harder to identify than impulse waves.
    2. Wave B: Prices reverse higher, which many see as a resumption of the now long-gone bull market.
    3. Wave C: Price moves impulsively lower in five waves. Volume picks up.
