A fingerprint is an impression left by the friction ridges of a human finger. The recovery of partial fingerprints from a crime scene is an important method of forensic science, and fingerprints are widely used as a biometric identifier. Because the ridge patterns formed before birth are unique to an individual — even between identical twins — and persist unchanged throughout life, fingerprint evidence has been central to personal identification for over a century.

Biology and formation

Friction ridges form on the fingers, palms, toes, and soles during foetal development, influenced by genetics, the growth of volar pads, and random stresses in the womb — the reason twins' prints differ. Ridges improve grip and tactile sensitivity and exude sweat that leaves latent impressions on surfaces touched.

Classification

The Henry Classification System, developed in British India by Edward Henry and his assistants Azizul Haque and Hem Chandra Bose, groups prints by pattern type — arches, loops (ulnar and radial), and whorls. Identification ultimately rests not on the gross pattern but on minutiae: ridge endings, bifurcations, and other fine features whose arrangement is compared between prints using the ACE-V method (analysis, comparison, evaluation, verification).

Forensic use

Latent prints are developed with powders, chemicals such as ninhydrin and cyanoacrylate ("superglue") fuming, and alternate light sources, then compared manually or searched in automated databases such as the FBI's IAFIS/NGI. Fingerprint evidence is powerful but not infallible: the erroneous identification of Brandon Mayfield after the 2004 Madrid train bombings spurred research into examiner bias and error rates, and modern standards emphasise verification and documentation.

History

Fingerprints were used as seals in ancient Babylonia and China. Scientific study began with Nehemiah Grew (1684) and Marcello Malpighi; Jan Evangelista Purkyně classified nine pattern types in 1823. William Herschel used handprints administratively in India from 1858, and Francis Galton's Finger Prints (1892) established the statistical basis for uniqueness. Police forces adopted the Henry system in the early 1900s; the 1911 case of the murderer caught through prints at the Mona Lisa theft investigation helped cement public acceptance.

Biometric systems

Capacitive and optical sensors in phones and laptops scan live fingerprints for authentication, storing mathematical templates rather than images. Concerns persist about spoofing, irrevocability once compromised, and civil-liberties implications of large fingerprint databases.
