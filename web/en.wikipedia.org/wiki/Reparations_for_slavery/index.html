Reparations for slavery

Reparations for slavery is the application of the concept of reparations to victims of slavery and their descendants. In the United States, reparations for African Americans have been proposed as compensation for the institution of slavery, subsequent Jim Crow discrimination, and their continuing effects. Similar claims have been advanced in the Caribbean and against European colonial powers for the transatlantic slave trade.

United States

The idea traces back to the Civil War-era promise of "forty acres and a mule" (Special Field Orders No. 15, 1865), which was reversed after Abraham Lincoln's assassination. In the 20th century, advocates including Queen Mother Moore and organizations such as N'COBRA promoted the cause; Congressman John Conyers repeatedly introduced H.R. 40, a bill to establish a commission to study reparations, from 1989 onward. Ta-Nehisi Coates's 2014 essay "The Case for Reparations" revived national debate. Some U.S. localities, such as Evanston, Illinois (2019–2021), have adopted local reparations programmes, and some universities and institutions have created funds acknowledging their ties to slavery.

International claims

The Caribbean Community (CARICOM) established a Reparations Commission in 2013 pressing European states for apologies and compensation. Haiti's claim against France — for the indemnity Haiti was forced to pay after independence, the so-called "ransom of independence" — is a prominent related case.

Debate

Supporters frame reparations as a matter of corrective justice for stolen labour and compounding disadvantage; opponents question feasibility, responsibility across generations, and the form compensation should take.
