Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo

The posterior semicircular canal, the site most often involved in BPPV.

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is a disorder of the inner ear characterised by repeated brief episodes of vertigo — a spinning sensation — triggered by changes in the position of the head. It is the most common cause of vertigo overall. The name describes the condition: it is harmless (benign), occurs in sudden attacks (paroxysmal), and is provoked by head position (positional).

Signs and symptoms

Episodes typically last less than a minute and are provoked by movements such as rolling over in bed, getting up, looking up, or bending over. Nausea may accompany the vertigo, but hearing loss and tinnitus are usually absent, helping distinguish BPPV from conditions such as Ménière's disease and labyrinthitis.

Cause and mechanism

BPPV results when tiny calcium carbonate crystals called otoconia, normally embedded in the utricle of the inner ear, become dislodged and migrate into one of the semicircular canals — most often the posterior canal. When the head moves, the free-floating crystals shift the fluid in the canal and send a false signal of rotation to the brain. The condition may follow head injury or viral labyrinthitis, but in many cases, particularly in older people, it arises spontaneously from degeneration of the utricle.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is typically made with the Dix–Hallpike test, in which the clinician moves the patient rapidly from sitting to lying with the head turned; in positive cases this provokes a characteristic torsional nystagmus after a short latency. Imaging is reserved for atypical cases to exclude central causes.

Treatment

BPPV is usually treated with repositioning manoeuvres that guide the crystals out of the canal. The best known is the Epley maneuver (canalith repositioning procedure); the Semont maneuver and the Brandt–Daroff exercises are alternatives. Medication such as meclizine may ease nausea but does not treat the cause. Most cases resolve, although recurrence is common.
