Sanacja (Polish for "healing" or "purification") was the political movement and governing regime of the Second Polish Republic from the May Coup of 1926 until the Second World War. Its name, derived from the Latin sanatio, expressed its stated aim of restoring the moral and political "health" of a state its leader, Józef Piłsudski, blamed on corrupt party politics and parliamentary paralysis.

Rise to power

Piłsudski had retired from politics in 1923, disgusted by party strife and the assassination of President Gabriel Narutowicz. In May 1926, amid economic crisis and rapidly changing governments, he marched his loyal troops on Warsaw; after three days of fighting the government resigned, and Piłsudski became the dominant figure of the state, ruling mostly from behind the scenes as Minister of Military Affairs and General Inspector of the Armed Forces.

The regime in power

The Sanacja camp governed through the Nonpartisan Bloc for Cooperation with the Government (BBWR), a broad pro-government formation. The regime progressively curtailed the opposition: leaders of the centrist and leftist Centrolew coalition were imprisoned in the Brest fortress and tried in 1931–32, elections were manipulated, and a detention camp at Bereza Kartuska held political prisoners. The authoritarian April Constitution of 1935 concentrated power in the presidency. Economically the regime pursued state-led development, including the Central Industrial District (COP) and the port of Gdynia.

After Piłsudski

Piłsudski died in May 1935. His successors — the so-called "colonels' regime" around President Ignacy Mościcki, Marshal Edward Rydz-Śmigły and Foreign Minister Józef Beck — campaigned as the Camp of National Unity (OZN) and drifted toward the nationalist right, while maintaining Poland's balancing foreign policy between Germany and the Soviet Union. The regime collapsed with the invasion of Poland in September 1939, its leaders fleeing into exile in Romania.

Legacy

Historians debate the Sanacja as a "soft" authoritarianism: it preserved some parliamentary and judicial forms, never became a totalitarian one-party state, but normalised political violence and weakened the democratic institutions of the republic it claimed to heal.
