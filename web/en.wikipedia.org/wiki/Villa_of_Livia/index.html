Villa of Livia

The Villa of Livia (Latin: Villa Liviae) is an ancient Roman villa at Prima Porta, about 12 kilometres north of Rome along the Via Flaminia. It belonged to Livia Drusilla, wife of the emperor Augustus, and is identified with the estate known in ancient sources as ad Gallinas Albas ("at the white hens"), from an omen in which an eagle dropped a white hen holding a sprig of laurel into Livia's lap.

Discoveries

The site is famous for the statue of Augustus known as the Augustus of Prima Porta, found there in 1863 and now in the Vatican Museums. Excavations revealed a large residence with baths, terraces overlooking the Tiber valley, and service quarters.

The painted garden room

The villa's most celebrated feature is an underground summer dining room (triclinium) whose walls are covered with a continuous fresco of a lush garden — fruit trees, flowering shrubs, and numerous bird species rendered with remarkable naturalism. Painted in the late first century BC, the fresco creates the illusion of dining inside a garden. The fragile paintings were detached and are now displayed in the Museo Nazionale Romano at Palazzo Massimo in Rome, with a replica installed at the villa site.
