Systematic review

A systematic review is a type of review of scientific literature that uses systematic methods to collect secondary data, critically appraise research studies, and synthesize findings qualitatively or quantitatively. Systematic reviews formulate research questions that are broad or narrow in scope, and identify and synthesize data from studies that directly relate to the systematic review question. While some systematic reviews may be specific to a certain area of health care, others may be used to inform policy or practice in other settings.

Systematic reviews are often designed to provide an exhaustive summary of current evidence relevant to a research question. A systematic review of randomized controlled trials is a way of summarizing and providing evidence to inform clinical practice. Systematic reviews are increasingly prevalent in other fields, including education, criminal justice, social welfare, and economics.

Characteristics

A systematic review is characterized by a clearly stated research question, an explicit and comprehensive search strategy designed to locate all relevant studies, predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, critical appraisal of included studies, and a structured synthesis of the results. Many systematic reviews include a meta-analysis, a statistical procedure that combines the results of multiple studies to produce a single estimate of effect, although a meta-analysis is not a required component.

Steps

The conduct of a systematic review generally follows a series of steps: formulating the research question (often framed using the PICO model of population, intervention, comparison and outcome), developing and registering a protocol, searching multiple bibliographic databases and other sources, screening titles, abstracts and full texts against the eligibility criteria, extracting data, assessing the risk of bias in each study, and synthesizing and reporting the findings, conventionally following the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) statement.

History

The term was popularized in the health sciences in the 1980s and 1990s, and the founding of the Cochrane Collaboration in 1993 by Iain Chalmers and colleagues institutionalized the approach in medicine. The Campbell Collaboration, founded in 2000, extended the model to social policy and education. Registration of review protocols in databases such as PROSPERO became standard practice in the 2010s to reduce duplication and selective reporting.

Criticisms

Critics note that systematic reviews are only as reliable as the primary studies they include, and that poor-quality reviews may lend a misleading appearance of rigor. The rapid growth in the number of published reviews, including redundant and commercially influenced reviews, has also been criticized.
