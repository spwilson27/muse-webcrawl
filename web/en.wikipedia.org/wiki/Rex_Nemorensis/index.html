Rex Nemorensis

The Rex Nemorensis (“King of the Wood” or “King of Nemi”) was the priest of Diana at her sanctuary beside Lake Nemi in the Alban Hills of Italy. Ancient accounts describe an extraordinary succession ritual: the priest held office until a challenger killed him in single combat and took his place.

The sanctuary

Diana Nemorensis was worshipped in a wooded sanctuary near Aricia. The cult drew worshippers from Latium and Rome, and the priesthood appears to have been associated in tradition with runaway slaves. Strabo and other writers present the incumbent as a fugitive slave who had won office by slaying his predecessor.

The golden bough

Later tradition linked the challenge to a particular branch or mistletoe-like “golden bough” in the sacred grove. A challenger supposedly had to break the bough before fighting the priest. Virgil’s golden bough in the Aeneid helped keep the image alive, although the precise relationship among the literary episode, local ritual and actual practice is uncertain.

Historical reliability

The violent succession is attested in ancient literary sources, but details may have been embellished. It is difficult to know how often challenges occurred or whether the ritual remained unchanged over centuries. Roman-era emperors and local history also became entangled with the legend; Caligula was said to have arranged for a stronger challenger when an elderly priest had held office too long.

Influence

The strange priesthood at Nemi became the opening mystery of James George Frazer’s The Golden Bough.

Frazer used the Rex Nemorensis as the starting point for a sweeping comparative study of myth, kingship, fertility and ritual. Although many of Frazer’s universal theories are now treated cautiously, his framing made Nemi one of the best-known examples of sacred kingship in classical studies and literature.
