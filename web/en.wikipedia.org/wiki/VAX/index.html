VAX

VAX
Manufacturer: Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC)
Released: 25 October 1977 (VAX-11/780)
Architecture: 32-bit CISC, virtual memory
Operating systems: VMS, Ultrix, BSD Unix
Successor: DEC Alpha

VAX (Virtual Address eXtension) is a family of computers developed and sold by Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) from 1977 into the 1990s. The VAX architecture extended DEC's successful PDP-11 line to a full 32-bit virtual address space, and VAX machines running the VMS operating system became the dominant minicomputers of the late 1970s and 1980s, ubiquitous in universities, laboratories and businesses.

Architecture

The VAX instruction set was famously rich and orthogonal: instructions could operate on many data types (integers, several floating-point formats, packed decimal, strings, queues and bit fields) with flexible addressing modes, and a single instruction could perform tasks — such as polynomial evaluation or queue insertion — that required whole subroutines on other machines. Memory management supported demand-paged virtual memory with a 4 GB virtual address space, large for its time. This complexity made VAX the canonical example of a CISC design, and its instruction set was a favourite subject in computer-architecture textbooks.

Models and history

The first model, the VAX-11/780 of 1977, delivered roughly 1 MIPS — a benchmark unit long known as the "VAX MIPS" — and was nicknamed the "supermini". The range grew to span the MicroVAX desktop systems, mid-range machines such as the VAX 6000 series, multiprocessor systems, and the high-end VAX 9000 mainframe-class systems. VAXclusters, which linked multiple machines into a single system image, were pioneering examples of cluster computing.

On the software side, VMS (later OpenVMS) was the flagship operating system; VAX machines also ran DEC's Ultrix Unix and were the reference platform for Berkeley's 4.x BSD Unix, making them central to the early Internet — many ARPANET and Internet hosts of the 1980s were VAXen.

Decline

In the early 1990s DEC moved its high-end strategy to the 64-bit RISC Alpha processor, and the last VAX models were sold around 2000, though OpenVMS and surviving VAX systems remained in production use for decades afterwards, supported by emulation and a devoted user community.
