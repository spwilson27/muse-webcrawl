Sari

A sari (also spelled saree) is a traditional garment worn by women across the Indian subcontinent, consisting of a single unstitched length of woven fabric — typically 4.5 to 9 metres long — draped over the body, worn with a fitted blouse (choli) and a petticoat. One end, the richly decorated pallu, is usually thrown over the shoulder.

History

Draped garments are depicted in Indus Valley and early historic Indian sculpture, and the three-piece ensemble of lower garment, breast band and shoulder veil is described in ancient Tamil and Sanskrit literature. The modern sari-and-blouse form was consolidated during the colonial period, influenced by Victorian dress norms.

Styles of draping

More than eighty draping styles are documented. The most common is the Nivi drape, with pleats tucked at the front and the pallu over the left shoulder. Regional styles include the Bengali atpourey, the Gujarati seedha pallu, the Maharashtrian nauvari (nine-yard, trouser-like drape), the Tamil madisar and the Kerala kasavu set-mundu style.

Fabrics and regional varieties

Famous varieties include Banarasi silk from Varanasi, Kanchipuram silk from Tamil Nadu, Bandhani tie-dye from Gujarat and Rajasthan, Ikat from Odisha and Andhra Pradesh, Jamdani muslin from Bengal, Chanderi, Maheshwari and the nine-yard Paithani of Maharashtra. Cotton handloom saris remain everyday wear for millions, and handloom weaving is a major rural industry.

Cultural significance

The sari is worn for daily life, festivals, weddings and formal occasions across India, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka and the diaspora, with fabric, colour and border encoding region, community and occasion.
