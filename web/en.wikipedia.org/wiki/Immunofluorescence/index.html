Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence (IF) is a technique used for light microscopy with a fluorescence microscope and is used primarily on biological samples. This technique uses the specificity of antibodies to their antigen to target fluorescent dyes to specific biomolecular targets, and therefore allows visualization of the distribution of the target molecule through the sample. The technique was developed in the 1940s, building on the work of Albert Coons, who first labelled antibodies with a fluorescent dye.

Types

In direct immunofluorescence, the primary antibody that binds the target antigen is itself conjugated to a fluorophore such as FITC or a modern Alexa Fluor dye. In indirect immunofluorescence, an unlabelled primary antibody is detected by a labelled secondary antibody, which amplifies the signal and allows flexibility in labelling. Common applications include immunohistochemistry of tissue sections, immunocytochemistry of cultured cells, detection of autoantibodies (for example the antinuclear antibody test on HEp-2 cells), and diagnosis of skin and kidney diseases from biopsy specimens, where patterns of immunoglobulin and complement deposition are diagnostic.

Procedure and limitations

Samples are typically fixed, permeabilised, blocked to reduce non-specific binding, incubated with antibody solutions, washed, and mounted for examination under a fluorescence or confocal microscope. Limitations include photobleaching of fluorophores, autofluorescence of tissue, and spectral overlap between dyes, addressed with careful controls, spectral unmixing and anti-fade mounting media.
