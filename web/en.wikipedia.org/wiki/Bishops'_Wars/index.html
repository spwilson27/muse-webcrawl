Bishops' Wars

The Bishops' Wars, 1639–1640.
Date1639–1640
LocationScotland and northern England
BelligerentsScottish Covenanters vs. Charles I of England
ResultCovenanter victory; Treaty of London (1641)

The Bishops' Wars (also called the Bellum Episcopale) were two short military campaigns in 1639 and 1640 between Charles I of England and the Scottish Covenanters. The wars arose from Charles's attempt to impose the Anglican Book of Common Prayer and episcopal church government on the Presbyterian Church of Scotland, provoking riots in Edinburgh in 1637 and the signing of the National Covenant in 1638.

The First Bishops' War (1639)

The General Assembly at Glasgow abolished episcopacy in Scotland despite royal opposition. Charles raised an English army and advanced to Berwick-upon-Tweed, but his ill-trained and poorly financed forces faced a well-organised Covenanter army under Alexander Leslie. Unwilling to fight, both sides agreed the Pacification of Berwick (June 1639), which settled nothing definitive.

The Second Bishops' War (1640)

Charles summoned the English "Short Parliament" in April 1640 to raise funds, but dissolved it when it pressed grievances instead. Renewing the campaign, his army was defeated by the Scots, who crossed the River Tweed and routed the English at the Battle of Newburn (28 August 1640), then occupied Newcastle upon Tyne. By the Treaty of Ripon, Charles agreed to pay the Scots £850 per day while their army remained in England. Bankrupt, he was forced to summon what became the Long Parliament — a decisive step toward the English Civil War.

Significance

The Bishops' Wars demonstrated the weakness of Charles's "Personal Rule", empowered his parliamentary and Scottish opponents, and form part of the wider Wars of the Three Kingdoms (1639–1653).
