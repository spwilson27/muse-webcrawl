Galápagos Islands

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Galápagos Islands
Location: Pacific Ocean, about 900 km west of mainland Ecuador
Status: Province of Ecuador; national park and UNESCO site
Famous for: Giant tortoises; Darwin's finches; inspiring the theory of evolution

The Galápagos Islands are an archipelago of volcanic islands in the Pacific Ocean, straddling the equator about 900 km west of continental Ecuador, to which they belong. Formed by a mantle hotspot — young in the west, older and eroding in the east — they are one of the world's great natural laboratories.

Wildlife and Darwin

Isolation stocked the islands with unique species: giant tortoises (for which the islands are named — galápago is an old Spanish word for tortoise), marine iguanas (the world's only sea-going lizards), flightless cormorants, blue-footed boobies and the finches whose beaks vary from island to island. Charles Darwin visited for five weeks in 1835 aboard HMS Beagle; the mockingbirds and tortoises he noted differing by island helped seed his theory of evolution by natural selection, and the finches — later called "Darwin's finches" — became its textbook emblem (see biogeography and adaptation).

Conservation

Some 97% of the land is national park; the surrounding marine reserve is among the largest in the world. Threats include invasive species (goats, rats, the parasitic fly Philornis downsi), fishing pressure offshore, and tourism growth. Lonesome George, the last Pinta Island tortoise, died in 2012 as a global symbol of extinction — while breeding programmes have returned other tortoise populations, including on Española, from the brink.
