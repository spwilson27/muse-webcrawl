Crime mapping

Crime mapping is used by analysts in law enforcement agencies to map, visualize, and analyze crime incident patterns. It is a key component of CompStat and of crime analysis more generally, and permits analysts using a geographic information system (GIS) to identify crime hot spots, along with other trends and patterns.

Methods

Using GIS, crime analysts can overlay other datasets such as census demographics, and the locations of schools, parks and businesses, to understand the underlying social and environmental causes of crime in an area. Common techniques include:

  • Hot spot analysis — statistical identification of areas with significantly higher crime density than expected.
  • Kernel density estimation — producing smooth "heat maps" of incident concentration.
  • Repeat and near-repeat analysis — measuring the elevated short-term risk of crime at and near recent incident locations.
  • Risk terrain modelling — combining spatial layers of environmental features associated with crime risk.

Applications

Crime maps support patrol deployment, problem-oriented policing, situational crime prevention and community engagement. Many police services, including forces in the United Kingdom and the United States, publish public-facing crime maps showing recent incidents by neighbourhood. Critics note that mapping reflects recorded crime and enforcement patterns, which can reinforce bias if used uncritically to direct patrols.

History

The spatial study of crime dates to the nineteenth-century "cartographic school" of criminology, including the maps of André-Michel Guerry and Joseph Fletcher, and to the social ecology of the Chicago School in the early twentieth century. Computerised mapping spread from the 1980s as GIS software became available to police departments.
