Unification of Italy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The unification of Italy (Italian: Unità d'Italia), also known as the Risorgimento ("Resurgence"), was the 19th-century political and social movement that resulted in the consolidation of different states of the Italian Peninsula and its outlying isles into a single state, the Kingdom of Italy, in 1861. The process began with the Congress of Vienna in 1815 and was completed in 1871 when Rome became the capital.

Background

After the fall of the Western Roman Empire, Italy remained divided for over a millennium. By 1815 the peninsula comprised Austrian-dominated Lombardy–Venetia, the Habsburg duchies of Tuscany, Parma and Modena, the Papal States, the Bourbon Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, and the Kingdom of Sardinia (Piedmont), the only Italian state ruled by an Italian dynasty — the House of Savoy — and, after the failed revolutions of 1848, the only one to retain a constitution.

The movement

The Risorgimento combined several strands: the republican nationalism of Giuseppe Mazzini and his Young Italy society; the diplomacy of Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour, prime minister of Piedmont, who secured French alliance and defeated Austria in the Second Italian War of Independence (1859); and the military exploits of Giuseppe Garibaldi, whose Expedition of the Thousand redshirts conquered Sicily and Naples in 1860 and handed them to King Victor Emmanuel II.

The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed on 17 March 1861. Venetia was annexed in 1866 after the Third Italian War of Independence, fought in alliance with Prussia, and Rome was taken on 20 September 1870, when the withdrawal of its French garrison during the Franco-Prussian War left the city exposed; Rome became the capital in 1871.

Aftermath

Unification left deep problems: the "Roman Question" of the Pope's hostility to the new state (resolved only by the Lateran Treaty of 1929), the poverty and brigandage of the south (the questione meridionale), and the unredeemed Italian-speaking lands under Austria — Trento and Trieste — whose recovery ("irredentism") drove Italy's entry into the First World War. The quip attributed to Massimo d'Azeglio — "We have made Italy; now we must make Italians" — summarised the task of nation-building that followed.
