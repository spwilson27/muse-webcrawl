Self-directed learning

Self-directed learners take the initiative in diagnosing their own learning needs

Self-directed learning (SDL) is a process in which individuals take the initiative, with or without the help of others, in diagnosing their learning needs, formulating learning goals, identifying human and material resources for learning, choosing and implementing appropriate learning strategies, and evaluating learning outcomes. This definition, given by Malcolm Knowles in 1975, anchors a research field rooted in adult education (andragogy) and the humanistic psychology of Carl Rogers and Abraham Maslow.

Concepts and models

Researchers distinguish the external management of learning from the learner's internal psychological attributes — confidence, motivation, and willingness to take responsibility. Guglielmino's Self-Directed Learning Readiness Scale (1977) attempted to measure readiness for SDL. Later models, such as Garrison's (1997), integrate self-management (control of tasks), self-monitoring (cognitive and metacognitive responsibility), and motivation. Candy (1991) described four distinct senses of the term: self-direction as a personal attribute, willingness to conduct one's own learning, a mode of organising instruction, and learning that occurs outside institutional settings.

Applications and debate

Self-direction is promoted in professional education (notably medicine and nursing, where continuing competence requires it), workplace learning, open and distance education, and newer formats such as MOOCs and personal learning environments. Critics note that the concept is culturally loaded — emphasising individual autonomy over collective learning — that many learners lack the resources or skills to direct their learning unsupported, and that it risks shifting responsibility for poor provision onto learners. Advocates reply that SDL is best viewed as situational: people are self-directing in some contexts but not others, and teachers can progressively scaffold learners towards autonomy.
