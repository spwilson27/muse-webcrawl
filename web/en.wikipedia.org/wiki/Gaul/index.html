Gaul (Latin: Gallia) was a region of Western Europe first described by the Romans, encompassing present-day France, Belgium, Luxembourg, and parts of Switzerland, the Netherlands, Germany, and Northern Italy. It was inhabited by Celtic and Aquitanian tribes, collectively known as the Gauls.

Roman accounts

Julius Caesar divided Gaul into three parts — Gallia Celtica, Belgica, and Aquitania — in his Commentarii de Bello Gallico. His Gallic Wars (58–50 BC) conquered the region after the great revolt of Vercingetorix ended at the Siege of Alesia in 52 BC. Rome had earlier annexed the south as Gallia Narbonensis (121 BC), the 'Provincia' that gives Provence its name.

Society

Gaulish society was organised in tribes and oppida (fortified towns), with druids exercising religious and judicial authority, a warrior aristocracy, and skilled metalwork — the La Tène culture — including gold torcs and fine coinage. The Gauls had sacked Rome itself around 390 BC under Brennus, and Gallic groups settled as far east as Galatia in Anatolia.

Roman Gaul and legacy

Under Augustus, Gaul was reorganised into provinces including Lugdunensis, Aquitania, Belgica, and the Germanies; Lugdunum (Lyon) became its great capital. Gallo-Roman culture flourished for five centuries before Germanic invasions and the fall of the Western Empire left the region to the Franks, from whom France takes its name. The rooster of Gaul (gallus means both 'Gaul' and 'cockerel' in Latin) remains a French national symbol.
