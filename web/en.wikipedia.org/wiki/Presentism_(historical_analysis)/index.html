Presentism (historical analysis)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Presentism judges or interprets the past through the values and concepts of the present.

In historical analysis, presentism is the anachronistic introduction of present-day ideas, concepts, and perspectives into depictions or interpretations of the past. It is often regarded by historians as a fallacy or a form of bias: the tendency to interpret past events in terms of modern values and concepts, or to judge historical figures by standards that did not exist in their own time.

Definitions and debate

The term is usually used pejoratively by professional historians, in contrast to historicism—the effort to understand past societies in their own terms. The historian David Hackett Fischer identified presentism as a fallacy in his 1970 book Historians' Fallacies, describing it as judging the past by the standards of the present rather than reconstructing the past as it was understood by those who lived in it.

Other scholars have defended forms of present-oriented history. The American Historical Association president Lynn Hunt warned in 2002 against the temptations of presentism while also acknowledging that historians' questions are inevitably shaped by their own time. François Hartog developed the concept of "regimes of historicity", arguing that contemporary Western culture is dominated by a presentist regime in which the present absorbs both past and future.

Moral judgement

A common form of presentism is moral: condemning or praising people of the past solely by present-day ethical standards. Critics of presentism note that historical actors worked within the knowledge, institutions, and norms of their era; defenders of moral judgement reply that refusing ever to evaluate the past can slide into relativism, and that some acts—such as slavery—were contested even in their own time.

Related terms

Presentism is related to, but distinct from, Whig history—the interpretation of history as a story of progress toward present-day arrangements—and from simple anachronism, which concerns factual misplacement of objects or terms in time.
