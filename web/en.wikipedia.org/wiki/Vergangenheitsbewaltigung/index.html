Vergangenheitsbewältigung

Vergangenheitsbewältigung
Literally"Coming to terms with / overcoming the past"
ContextPost-1945 Germany; Nazi crimes and the Holocaust
RelatedDenazification, Wiedergutmachung, Erinnerungskultur

Vergangenheitsbewältigung (German for "struggle of overcoming the past") describes the process by which post-war Germany confronted the crimes of the Nazi regime, especially the Holocaust. It encompasses official acts such as trials, reparations (Wiedergutmachung), education and memorialisation, as well as broader cultural debates about guilt, responsibility and memory.

Development

In the immediate post-war years Allied denazification gave way, in the 1950s Federal Republic, to amnesties and relative silence, against which Theodor W. Adorno's 1959 essay "What Does Working Through the Past Mean?" protested. The Frankfurt Auschwitz trials (1963–65), the student movement of 1968 confronting parents' complicity, Willy Brandt's kneeling in Warsaw (1970), the broadcast of the Holocaust miniseries (1979), President Richard von Weizsäcker's 1985 speech calling 8 May a day of liberation, and the Historikerstreit of 1986 gradually institutionalised remembrance. After reunification the concept was extended to the East German dictatorship, investigated by the Stasi Records Agency, and memorial culture (Erinnerungskultur) came to include projects such as the Stolpersteine stumbling-stones.

Criticism and comparison

Commentators debate whether the past can ever be "mastered", whether ritualised memory substitutes for action, and how the concept applies to Germany's colonial crimes. The German process is frequently cited as a model — and a contrast — in discussions of transitional justice elsewhere, from South Africa's TRC to Canada's residential schools commission.
