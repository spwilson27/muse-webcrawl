Second Viennese School

The Second Viennese School is the name given to the group of composers comprising Arnold Schoenberg and his pupils and close associates in early 20th-century Vienna, above all Alban Berg and Anton Webern. Their music moved from late Romanticism through free atonality to the twelve-tone technique, and profoundly influenced the course of Western classical music.

History

Schoenberg began teaching Berg and Webern in the first decade of the 20th century. Together they moved from the lush chromaticism of works such as Schoenberg's Verklärte Nacht to the abandonment of tonality altogether around 1908–1909, in works like Schoenberg's Three Piano Pieces, Op. 11 and his monodrama Erwartung, and Webern's Five Pieces for Orchestra. Around 1921–1923 Schoenberg formulated the twelve-tone method — composition with twelve tones related only to one another — first used systematically in his Piano Suite, Op. 25.

Style and influence

The school's works are characterised by motivic concentration, "developing variation", and an intense expressionism. Berg's operas Wozzeck and Lulu brought the style to a wide public. After the Second World War, the school's methods — extended by younger composers into integral serialism — dominated the avant-garde for a generation, and its influence reached film music, jazz and beyond.
