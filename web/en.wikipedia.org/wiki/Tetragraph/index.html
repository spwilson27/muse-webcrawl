Tetragraph

A tetragraph (from Greek tetra-, "four", and -graph, "written") is a sequence of four letters used to represent a single sound (phoneme) or a combination of sounds that does not necessarily correspond to the individual values of the letters. Tetragraphs are a type of multigraph, alongside digraphs (two letters) and trigraphs (three letters).

Examples

In English orthography, sequences such as ough in words like "through" and "dough" are sometimes analyzed as tetragraphs, though their pronunciation varies. German uses the tetragraph tsch for the affricate /tʃ/, as in deutsch. Other languages employ tetragraphs in romanization systems; for example, in the Wade–Giles transcription of Chinese, sequences mark aspirated consonants, and various Cyrillic romanizations use multi-letter combinations for single letters.

Use in orthography

Tetragraphs arise where an alphabet lacks enough single letters for the sounds of a language, or where historical spellings have been retained. They are particularly common in transliteration schemes and in the orthographies of languages written with the Latin alphabet.
