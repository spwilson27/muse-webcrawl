Photographic developer

A photographic developer is a chemical solution used in photographic processing to convert the latent image recorded on exposed film or paper into a visible image. The developer reduces the silver halide crystals that were affected by light (or, in chromogenic materials, generates dye alongside reduced silver) to metallic silver, amplifying the latent image enormously.

Composition

A typical black-and-white developer contains: a developing agent (commonly metol, hydroquinone, phenidone, or combinations such as metol–hydroquinone, "MQ"); an alkali accelerator (sodium carbonate, borax, or sodium hydroxide) that sets the pH; a preservative (sodium sulfite) that retards oxidation and dissolves some silver halide; and a restrainer (potassium bromide) that suppresses chemical fog. Well-known commercial formulations include Kodak D-76 (a metol–hydroquinone developer) and Rodinal (based on p-aminophenol).

Process

Development time, temperature, and agitation are controlled carefully, as they determine contrast, grain, and density; development is then arrested with a stop bath (usually dilute acetic acid) and the remaining unexposed silver halide removed by the fixer. In colour processes (C-41 for negatives, E-6 for slides, RA-4 for prints), colour developers reduce silver and simultaneously form dye clouds around developing grains through reactions with dye couplers incorporated in the emulsion or the developer.
