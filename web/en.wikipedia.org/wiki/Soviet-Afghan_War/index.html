The Soviet–Afghan War — often rendered with a hyphen as the Soviet-Afghan War — was a conflict between the Soviet Union and the Soviet-backed Democratic Republic of Afghanistan on one side, and Islamist insurgent groups collectively known as the Afghan mujahideen on the other. It began with the Soviet invasion of December 1979 and ended with the complete withdrawal of Soviet forces in February 1989.

Background and invasion

The Saur Revolution of April 1978 brought the communist People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan to power; its radical reforms and repression provoked widespread rebellion. Fearing the collapse of a friendly regime on its southern border, the Soviet Politburo intervened. Operation Storm-333 killed President Hafizullah Amin in Kabul on 27 December 1979, installing Babrak Karmal, and some 100,000 Soviet troops of the 40th Army deployed across the country.

Course of the war

Soviet forces held the cities and main roads, but the mujahideen — financed and armed through Pakistan's ISI, chiefly by the United States' Operation Cyclone and Saudi funding — controlled much of the countryside. The introduction of US-supplied Stinger anti-aircraft missiles from 1986 eroded Soviet air superiority. The war devastated Afghanistan: millions became refugees in Pakistan and Iran, and landmines and bombing killed and maimed vast numbers of civilians.

Withdrawal and aftermath

Mikhail Gorbachev sought an exit; the Geneva Accords of April 1988 set a withdrawal timetable, and the last Soviet soldier, General Boris Gromov, crossed the Friendship Bridge on 15 February 1989. The Najibullah government survived until 1992, when the mujahideen took Kabul, descending into civil war from which the Taliban emerged. The war's legacy — including networks of foreign fighters such as Osama bin Laden — shaped conflicts for decades, and the conflict is often called the Soviet Union's "Vietnam".
