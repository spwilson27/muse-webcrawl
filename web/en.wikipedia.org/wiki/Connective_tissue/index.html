Connective tissue is one of the four primary types of animal tissue, along with epithelial tissue, muscle tissue, and nervous tissue. It develops mostly from the mesenchyme, derived from the mesoderm germ layer. Connective tissue is found in between other tissues everywhere in the body, including in the nervous system.

Connective tissues are defined by the presence of an extracellular matrix composed of ground substance and protein fibres, chiefly collagen, elastin and reticular fibres, within which relatively few cells are embedded. The most common cell type is the fibroblast, which produces the matrix fibres and ground substance.

Classification

Connective tissues are broadly divided into connective tissue proper and specialised connective tissues.

  • Loose connective tissue — areolar, adipose and reticular tissue; holds organs in place and attaches epithelial tissue to underlying tissues.
  • Dense connective tissue — dense regular tissue forms tendons and ligaments; dense irregular tissue forms the dermis and organ capsules.
  • Specialised connective tissues — cartilage, bone, blood, and lymph, each with a distinctive matrix (solid and pliable in cartilage, mineralised in bone, liquid plasma in blood).

Functions

Connective tissues perform a wide variety of functions: structural support and binding of other tissues, protection of organs, storage of energy (in adipose tissue) and minerals (in bone), transport of substances (blood), and immune defence through macrophages, mast cells, plasma cells and leukocytes within the matrix.

Disorders

Many diseases affect connective tissue, including inherited disorders such as Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, Marfan syndrome and osteogenesis imperfecta, and autoimmune conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus and scleroderma, in which the immune system attacks the body's own connective tissues.
