White House Easter Egg Roll

The White House Easter Egg Roll is a long-running American tradition held each Easter Monday on the South Lawn of the White House in Washington, D.C. Children roll decorated hard-boiled eggs across the grass with long-handled spoons in races, and the event has grown into a day of games, storytelling, and performances attended by tens of thousands of guests selected largely by lottery.

History

Egg rolling on the grounds of the U.S. Capitol was a popular Easter pastime in the 1870s, until an 1876 law protecting the Capitol turf drove the children away. By tradition, President Rutherford B. Hayes in 1878 opened the White House lawn to the egg rollers, beginning the annual custom. Except during the two World Wars, the Civil War-era disruptions, and occasional cancellations for weather or the COVID-19 pandemic, the roll has been held ever since.

Traditions

First Ladies have shaped the event: Lou Hoover introduced folk dancing, Pat Nixon added the egg-rolling race format with spoons, and in 1981 Nancy Reagan's era began the tradition of the wooden commemorative egg signed by the President and First Lady, given to child participants. Since 1977 an official poster and souvenir eggs have marked the occasion. Celebrity storybook readings, military bands, costumed characters, and egg hunts are now standard features.

Significance

The Egg Roll is one of the few regularly scheduled events at which the White House grounds are opened to the general public, and it is overseen by the National Park Service and the White House Visitor Office. Adult egg rolling also survives in its ancestral form in Preston, Lancashire, and elsewhere in England.
