The Sacred and the Profane: The Nature of Religion is a book by the Romanian historian of religion Mircea Eliade, first published in German in 1957 and in English in 1959. One of the most influential works in 20th-century religious studies, it sets out Eliade's account of how religious people experience space, time and nature as divided between the sacred and the profane.

Eliade's classic study of religious experience.

Concepts

Eliade argues that for religious man (homo religiosus) the world is not homogeneous: certain places, times and objects manifest the sacred — an event he calls a hierophany — and thereby become fixed points of orientation in an otherwise formless world. Sacred space centres on an axis mundi, such as a temple or sacred mountain, connecting heaven, earth and the underworld. Sacred time likewise differs from ordinary duration: festivals reactualise mythical events, returning participants to the time of origins (illud tempus). Modern non-religious people, Eliade suggests, retain camouflaged traces of these structures in their behaviour.

Influence and criticism

The book became a standard text in comparative religion and shaped the phenomenological approach to the field. Critics have questioned Eliade's universalising claims, his tendency to flatten historical differences between religions, and the extent to which homo religiosus describes actual believers rather than an ideal type.
