Mathematician

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Mathematicians study quantity, structure, space and change.

A mathematician is someone who uses an extensive knowledge of mathematics in their work, typically to solve mathematical problems. Mathematicians are concerned with numbers, data, quantity, structure, space, models, and change. Unlike scientists in empirical fields, mathematicians primarily advance knowledge by constructing proofs — chains of logical deduction from axioms and previously established theorems.

History

One of the earliest known mathematicians was Thales of Miletus (c. 624 – c. 546 BC), credited with the first deductive proofs in geometry. Pythagoras and his followers explored number theory and the relationships between numbers and harmony. Euclid of Alexandria (c. 300 BC) systematised geometry in his Elements, which remained a standard text for over two thousand years. Archimedes of Syracuse made foundational contributions to geometry, hydrostatics and the method of exhaustion, an ancestor of integral calculus.

During the Islamic Golden Age, scholars such as Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi — from whose name the word "algorithm" derives — developed algebra (from the Arabic al-jabr), while Omar Khayyam classified cubic equations. In early modern Europe, René Descartes united algebra and geometry in analytic geometry, and Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently developed calculus in the late 17th century. The 19th century saw mathematics become increasingly abstract and rigorous, with figures such as Carl Friedrich Gauss, Bernhard Riemann, Évariste Galois, Niels Henrik Abel and Georg Cantor reshaping number theory, geometry, group theory and set theory.

Areas of work

Mathematicians work in pure mathematics, pursued for its own sake, and in applied mathematics, where mathematical methods are used in science, engineering, business and industry. Major branches include algebra, analysis, geometry and topology, number theory, combinatorics, probability and statistics, logic and the foundations of mathematics, and computational mathematics.

Education and careers

Professional mathematicians typically hold a doctorate in mathematics, though many work in industry, finance, computing, cryptography and data science with bachelor's or master's degrees. Academic mathematicians combine research with university teaching. The most prestigious honours in the field include the Fields Medal, awarded every four years to mathematicians under 40, and the Abel Prize.
