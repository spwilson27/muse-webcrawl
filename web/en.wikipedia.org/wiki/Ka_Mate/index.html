"Ka Mate" is a Māori haka composed by Te Rauparaha, war leader of the Ngāti Toa tribe, around 1820. It is the haka most often performed by the New Zealand national rugby union team, the All Blacks, and by other New Zealand sports teams before matches. The words recount Te Rauparaha's escape from pursuing enemies, hiding in a food-storage pit while a chief's wife sat above him, and his emergence back into the sunlight.

Story of the composition

According to tradition, Te Rauparaha, fleeing from enemy warriors after a battle, was hidden by the chief Te Wharerangi in a kūmara pit, with Te Wharerangi's wife seated over the entrance. The haka's lines describe the fear of capture ("Ka mate, ka mate — I die, I die"), the relief as the pursuers passed, and the triumphant climb back into the light: "Whiti te rā! Hī!" — "The sun shines!"

Text

The haka opens with the famous call: Ka mate, ka mate! Ka ora, ka ora! ("I die, I die! I live, I live!"), followed by verses describing "te tangata puhuruhuru" — "the hairy man" who brought the sun and caused it to shine. The performance involves chanting, stamping feet, vigorous posturing (whetero, pukana — wide-open eyes and protruding tongue) and slapping of the body.

Use in rugby

The haka was performed on the 1888–89 New Zealand Native team's tour of Britain and by the 1905 "Originals", whose performances helped fix it in the All Blacks' pre-match ritual. In 2005 the All Blacks introduced a second haka, "Kapa o Pango", composed for the team, which is used for special occasions. New Zealand law, through the Haka Ka Mate Attribution Act 2014, formally recognises Ngāti Toa Rangatira's authorship and guardianship of Ka Mate, following the tribe's Treaty of Waitangi settlement.
