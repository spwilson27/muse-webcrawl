Bioarchaeology

Hero image: excavation and in-field recording of a human burial, the starting point of bioarchaeological analysis.

Bioarchaeology is the scientific study of human remains from archaeological contexts. The term was coined in 1972 by British archaeologist Grahame Clark to refer to zooarchaeology, and was redefined in 1977 by Jane Buikstra as the study of human skeletal remains within archaeology — the usage now standard in North America. In Britain the equivalent field is often called osteoarchaeology. Bioarchaeologists combine methods from physical anthropology, archaeology, and increasingly chemistry and genetics to reconstruct the lives of past people.

Scope and methods

Analysis begins with estimating biological profiles for individuals: age at death, sex, stature, and ancestry, derived from the morphology of the skull and pelvis and the fusion of bones and teeth. Beyond the individual, bioarchaeologists study populations, examining rates of disease (palaeopathology), trauma, nutritional stress markers such as enamel hypoplasia and porotic hyperostosis, and patterns of physical activity recorded in bone shape and joint degeneration. Stable isotope analysis of carbon, nitrogen, strontium, and oxygen in bone and teeth reveals diet and migration, while ancient DNA can establish kinship and population history at burial sites.

Research themes

Major themes include the health consequences of the transition to agriculture — generally associated with declining stature, increased dental caries, and heavier infectious disease loads — the embodiment of social inequality in skeletons, the bioarchaeology of violence and warfare, and the study of care and disability in the past. Clark Spencer Larsen's synthesis Bioarchaeology: Interpreting Behavior from the Human Skeleton is a foundational text.

Ethics and repatriation

Because its subject matter is human remains, bioarchaeology is closely engaged with ethics, descendant communities, and legislation. In the United States, the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA, 1990) reshaped practice by requiring consultation with and repatriation to Indigenous tribes. Many bioarchaeologists now work in collaboration with descendant communities, and destructive analyses such as DNA sampling are increasingly negotiated rather than assumed.
