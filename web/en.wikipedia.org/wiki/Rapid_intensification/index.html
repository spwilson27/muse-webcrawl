Rapid intensification

Rapid intensification
FieldTropical cyclone meteorology
Definition (NHC)Increase in maximum sustained winds of ≥ 30 knots (55 km/h) within 24 hours
ExampleHurricane Wilma (2005), pressure fell 88 hPa in 24 h

Rapid intensification is a meteorological condition in which a tropical cyclone intensifies quickly. The U.S. National Hurricane Center defines it as an increase in maximum sustained winds of at least 30 knots (about 55 km/h, 35 mph) over a 24-hour period, though other thresholds are used in research.

Conditions required

Rapid intensification typically requires very warm sea surface temperatures (at least 26.5 °C, deep warm water), low vertical wind shear, high mid-level moisture, an upper-level anticyclone providing outflow, and an inner core already organised, often marked by an eye forming and bursts of deep convection ("hot towers") near the centre.

Forecasting challenges

Because intensification depends on small-scale inner-core processes, rapid intensification remains one of the hardest problems in tropical-cyclone forecasting; intensity models historically underestimate it, which is hazardous when it occurs near landfall.

Notable cases

Hurricane Wilma (2005) holds the Atlantic record, with pressure dropping 88 hPa in 24 hours to 882 hPa. Other examples include Hurricane Michael (2018), Hurricane Ian (2022), and Pacific typhoons such as Meranti (2016).

Climate change

Research suggests the likelihood of tropical cyclones rapidly intensifying near coastlines has increased in recent decades as ocean heat content has risen, consistent with projections under climate change.
