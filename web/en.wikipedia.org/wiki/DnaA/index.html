DnaA

DnaA is a protein that activates initiation of DNA replication in bacteria. It binds to specific sequences called DnaA boxes in the origin of replication (oriC), melts the DNA duplex there, and recruits the replication machinery. DnaA is conserved across bacteria and is a member of the AAA+ family of ATPases; its activity depends on whether it is bound to ATP (active) or ADP (inactive).

Mechanism

ATP-bound DnaA assembles on DnaA boxes at oriC and wraps the DNA around the complex, straining and unwinding an AT-rich region called the DNA unwinding element. The exposed single strands allow loading of the replicative helicase DnaB (by the loader DnaC), followed by primase and DNA polymerase III holoenzyme, establishing two replication forks.

Regulation

Because initiation must occur once per cell cycle, DnaA activity is tightly controlled: after initiation, the Hda protein and the sliding clamp stimulate ATP hydrolysis by DnaA (a process called RIDA), newly replicated oriC is temporarily sequestered by SeqA, and the dnaA promoter is repressed by DnaA itself. These mechanisms prevent premature re-initiation.
