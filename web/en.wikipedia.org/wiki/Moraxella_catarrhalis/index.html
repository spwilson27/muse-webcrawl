Moraxella catarrhalis is a fastidious, strictly aerobic, oxidase-positive, Gram-negative diplococcus with a conspicuous resemblance (in both morphology and clinical behaviour) to Neisseria species, to which it was once thought related. It was historically classified as Neisseria catarrhalis and then as Branhamella catarrhalis before being placed in the genus Moraxella. Long dismissed as a harmless commensal of the upper respiratory tract, it is now recognised as an important pathogen.

Clinical significance

M. catarrhalis colonises the nasopharynx of a large proportion of young children and a smaller fraction of adults. From this reservoir it causes:

  • Otitis media — it is the third most common bacterial cause in children, after Streptococcus pneumoniae and non-typeable Haemophilus influenzae.
  • Sinusitis in children and adults.
  • Exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) — it is a leading bacterial trigger of exacerbations in adults with chronic bronchitis.
  • Less commonly, pneumonia in the elderly, and rare invasive infections such as bacteraemia and meningitis.

Identification

Colonies on blood or chocolate agar are smooth, opaque and grey-white, and are famously friable: they can be pushed intact across the agar surface with a loop, the so-called "hockey puck" sign. The organism is oxidase- and catalase-positive, produces DNase, reduces nitrate, and — unlike pathogenic Neisseria — fails to produce acid from carbohydrates.

Resistance and treatment

More than 90% of clinical isolates now produce BRO β-lactamases (BRO-1 and BRO-2), conferring resistance to ampicillin and amoxicillin; before the 1970s the species was almost universally penicillin-sensitive. Infections are generally treated with amoxicillin–clavulanate, second- or third-generation cephalosporins, macrolides, tetracyclines, or fluoroquinolones, guided by severity and local resistance patterns. Its outer-membrane proteins (such as UspA1/A2 and Hag) are targets of vaccine research, particularly for COPD and childhood otitis media.
