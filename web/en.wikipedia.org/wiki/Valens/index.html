Valens (Greek: Ουάλης, Ouálēs; c. 328 – 9 August 378) was Roman emperor from 364 to 378. He was appointed co-emperor by his elder brother Valentinian I shortly after the latter's accession, and was given control of the eastern provinces while Valentinian retained the west.

Valens held his brother's eastern government in a largely subservient position, but proved an administrator of some competence. He reduced taxes, showed concern for the poorer classes of Constantinople, and oversaw extensive building works, including the completion of the aqueduct system begun under Constantine. His religious policy favoured Arian Christianity, in contrast to the Nicene orthodoxy dominant in the west.

Early career

Valens was born at Cibalae in Pannonia Secunda around 328, the younger son of Gratianus, a commander of modest origins who rose to high military office. Unlike his brother, Valens had no experience of public office before his accession and received little formal education; contemporaries described him as uncultivated, though loyal and conscientious. He served as a protector domesticus under the emperors Julian and Jovian before Valentinian, proclaimed emperor at Nicaea on 26 February 364, elevated him as co-Augustus at Constantinople on 28 March of the same year.

Rebellion of Procopius

In 365 Valens faced a serious challenge when Procopius, a relative of the emperor Julian, was proclaimed emperor at Constantinople while Valens was campaigning in Syria. Valens initially considered abdication or suicide, but recovered his nerve; Procopius's support melted away, and he was captured and executed in May 366. Valens thereafter persecuted many suspected of complicity, sometimes on flimsy evidence supplied by informers.

Wars on the Danube and in Persia

From 367 to 369 Valens campaigned against the Goths north of the Danube, who had supported Procopius, crossing the river at Noviodunum and defeating the Tervingi under Athanaric. In the east he negotiated with the Sasanian Empire over Armenia and Iberia, though relations deteriorated after the Persian king Shapur II deposed and blinded the Armenian king Arsaces II.

Battle of Adrianople and death

In 376 Valens admitted the Gothic Tervingi, fleeing the Huns, into Roman territory south of the Danube. Mishandling and corruption among Roman officials provoked the refugees into revolt, beginning the Gothic War. After two years of inconclusive fighting, Valens marched from Constantinople to confront the Gothic army near Adrianople. Against the advice of officers who urged him to wait for his nephew Gratian's western reinforcements, Valens attacked on 9 August 378. The Battle of Adrianople was a catastrophic Roman defeat: Valens was killed, either on the field or burned in a farmhouse where he had taken refuge, and his body was never found. The disaster is often regarded as a turning point in the history of the Roman Empire.
