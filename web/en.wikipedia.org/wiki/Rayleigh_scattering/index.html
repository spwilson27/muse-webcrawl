Rayleigh scattering is the elastic scattering of light (or other electromagnetic radiation) by particles much smaller than the wavelength of the radiation, such as the molecules of a gas. Named after the British physicist Lord Rayleigh, who derived the theory in the 1870s, it explains why the daytime sky appears blue and why the sun looks red at sunrise and sunset. Rayleigh scattering is the dominant form of scattering in the lower atmosphere.

Theory

When light passes through a transparent medium, the oscillating electric field induces a dipole moment in each molecule, which then re-radiates at the same frequency. For particles of size much less than the wavelength λ, the scattered intensity I follows the Rayleigh law:

I ∝ (1 + cos²θ) / λ⁴ · (particle size)⁶

The strong inverse-fourth-power dependence on wavelength means short (blue) wavelengths are scattered far more strongly than long (red) ones — roughly five times more for blue (~450 nm) than red (~700 nm). Scattered light at 90° from the incident direction is also strongly polarised, a phenomenon exploited in polarising filters and studied in animal navigation.

Why the sky is blue

Sunlight passing through the atmosphere is scattered by air molecules; the diffusely scattered light reaching the eye from all directions is dominated by blue wavelengths. At sunset, the light's path through the air is much longer, most blue light is scattered away, and the remaining direct light appears orange and red. Scattering by larger particles (comparable to or bigger than the wavelength) is described by Mie scattering and is roughly wavelength-independent, which is why clouds and fog appear white.

Applications

Rayleigh scattering underlies optical techniques including Raman-adjacent diagnostics, lidar, and the characterisation of aerosols and nanoparticles, and it sets limits on signal loss in optical fibres, where the related effect in glass contributes to attenuation.
