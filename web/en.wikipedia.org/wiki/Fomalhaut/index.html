Fomalhaut (Alpha Piscis Austrini, α PsA) is the brightest star in the southern constellation of Piscis Austrinus and one of the brightest stars in the night sky. It is a young A-type main-sequence star about 25 light-years from the Sun. Its name derives from the Arabic fam al-ḥūt, "mouth of the fish" (or "mouth of the whale"). Fomalhaut is notable as one of the first stars found to have an infrared excess indicating a debris disk, and for the directly imaged object Fomalhaut b, announced in 2008 as one of the first exoplanets photographed at visible wavelengths — though later studies reinterpreted it as an expanding cloud of dust from a collision between planetesimals.

Debris disk

In 1983 the IRAS satellite detected a large excess of infrared radiation from Fomalhaut, indicating a disk of cold dust. Hubble Space Telescope images revealed a geometrically striking, sharp-edged ring of debris extending to about 158 AU, offset from the star — evidence that the dust is shepherded by unseen planets. The ring is often compared to the Solar System's Kuiper Belt. The system actually comprises multiple belts, and in 2023 the James Webb Space Telescope revealed nested disk structures including an intermediate belt.

Fomalhaut b (Dagon)

In 2008, astronomers announced the discovery of a planet, Fomalhaut b, in images taken by Hubble in 2004 and 2006 — one of the first exoplanets to be directly imaged. Observations over the following decade showed the object's orbit and its gradual fading and expansion; by 2020 researchers concluded it was most likely not a planet but the slowly dispersing remnant of a collision between two large planetesimals. The object had been given the proper name Dagon in a public naming campaign.

Multiple system

Fomalhaut forms a wide triple system with the orange dwarf TW Piscis Austrini (Fomalhaut B), about 0.9 light-years away, and the red dwarf LP 876-10 (Fomalhaut C) at a distance of about 2.5 light-years, making it one of the widest known multiple star systems.
