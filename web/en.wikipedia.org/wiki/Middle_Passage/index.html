Middle Passage

The Middle Passage was the stage of the triangular trade in which millions of enslaved Africans were forcibly transported across the Atlantic Ocean to the Americas. It was the middle leg of the trade route: European goods went to Africa, enslaved people crossed to the Americas, and colonial produce such as sugar, tobacco, and cotton returned to Europe. Between the 16th and 19th centuries, roughly 12.5 million Africans were embarked on slave ships; about 10.7 million survived the crossing.

Conditions

The voyage typically lasted one to three months. Captives were held below deck in spaces sometimes less than a metre high, chained in pairs, and brought up for food and forced exercise in "dancing". Disease — dysentery, smallpox, scurvy — spread rapidly in the overcrowded, unventilated holds; mortality rates commonly ran between 10 and 20 per cent, and crews also died in large numbers. Sharks were said to follow the ships.

Resistance

Captives resisted through hunger strikes, suicide, and shipboard revolts, which occurred on a significant minority of voyages. The most famous revolt, aboard La Amistad in 1839, ended with the captives winning their freedom in the United States Supreme Court. The 1781 Zong massacre, in which more than 130 sick captives were thrown overboard so the ship's owners could claim insurance, became a notorious symbol for the abolitionist movement.

Abolition

Denmark banned the trade in 1803, Britain and the United States in 1807–1808, and the Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron patrolled against slavers, but an illegal trade to Brazil and Cuba continued into the 1860s. The Middle Passage remains central to historical memory of the Atlantic slave trade and the African diaspora; the historian's name for the crossing echoes in works from abolitionist pamphlets to modern scholarship and memorials.
