Bimetallic strip

A bimetallic strip bends when heated because its two metals expand at different rates.

A bimetallic strip is a mechanical element consisting of two strips of different metals bonded together, which expands or bends in a predictable way when its temperature changes. Because the two metals have different coefficients of thermal expansion, the strip curves toward the side with the lower expansion coefficient when heated, and straightens or curves the opposite way when cooled.

Principle

When two metals such as steel and copper, or invar and brass, are riveted, brazed or welded together along their length, a change in temperature forces the composite strip to bend. The metal with the greater thermal expansion becomes longer, so the strip arcs with that metal on the outside of the curve. The amount of deflection is proportional to the temperature change, which makes the device useful both as a temperature sensor and as a temperature-actuated switch.

History

The principle was exploited in the eighteenth century by the clockmaker John Harrison, who used bimetallic compensation to counteract the effect of temperature on the balance springs and pendulums of his marine chronometers. The bimetallic strip thermometer and the bimetallic thermostat became common in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Applications

Bimetallic strips are used in thermostats for heating and cooling systems, in thermal circuit breakers and overload protectors, in dial thermometers, in automotive choke controls and turn-signal flashers, and as the actuating element in some electric kettles and toasters. Their advantages are simplicity, low cost and the absence of any need for an external power supply; their limitations include relatively slow response and limited accuracy compared with electronic sensors.
