Mohe people

RegionsManchuria: basins of the Amur, Songhua and Mudan rivers (modern Heilongjiang, Jilin, Russian Far East)
Periodc. 4th–10th centuries CE
LanguageTungusic (presumed)
SuccessorsJurchen people

The Mohe (Chinese: 靺鞨; pinyin: Mòhé; Korean: 말갈, Malgal) were a Tungusic people of Manchuria who flourished from roughly the 4th to the 10th century CE. Organised in tribal groups along the Amur, Songhua (Sungari) and Mudan river valleys in what is now Northeast China and the Russian Far East, the Mohe were the historical link between the earlier Sushen–Yilou–Wuji peoples of Chinese records and the Jurchen — ancestors of the Manchus — who rose in the 12th century.

Tribes

Chinese dynastic histories, principally the Book of Sui and the Old and New Books of Tang, divide the Mohe into seven tribes: the Sumo, Baishan ("White Mountain"), Yulou, Wushui, Tieli, Funie and, most powerful in the east, the Heishui ("Black Water") Mohe along the lower Amur. The southern Sumo Mohe, intermixed with Goguryeo populations, were among the founding peoples of the kingdom of Balhae (Bohai) in 698, whose ruling house came from the Sumo Mohe chieftain Dae Joyeong (Da Zuorong).

Society and economy

The histories depict the Mohe as stocky, hardy people skilled in archery and horsemanship, living in semisubterranean dwellings entered from the roof, keeping pigs and dogs, wearing pig- and dog-skin clothing, and practising agriculture — millet, wheat and beans — alongside hunting and fishing. They were famed as archers whose poisoned arrows were feared, and their furs, horses and ginseng were valued tribute goods at Chinese courts. Mohe groups variously paid tribute to, or fought with, the Northern dynasties, Sui, Tang and Goguryeo; Tang briefly organised some of them in the short-lived Heishui commandery in the Amur region (727).

Legacy

After the fall of Balhae to the Khitan Liao dynasty in 926, the Mohe name faded from the records; the unsubdued Heishui Mohe continued as the "wild Jurchen" and, unified by the Wanyan clan, founded the Jin dynasty in 1115. Archaeology of Mohe culture — identified with distinctive pottery, cemeteries and settlement sites in the Amur and Songhua basins — confirms a transition from earlier Wuji remains toward Jurchen material culture.
