Propellant mass fraction

In aerospace engineering, the propellant mass fraction is the portion of a vehicle's mass which does not reach the destination, usually used as a measure of the vehicle's performance. In other words, the propellant mass fraction is the ratio between the propellant mass and the initial mass of the vehicle. In a spacecraft, the destination is usually an orbit, while for aircraft it is their landing location.

Definition

The propellant mass fraction is computed as the mass of propellant divided by the total initial ("wet") mass of the stage or vehicle. Higher propellant mass fractions mean less mass devoted to tanks, engines, and structure, and—through the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation—a greater achievable change in velocity (delta-v) for a given exhaust velocity.

Significance

Achieving a high propellant mass fraction is one of the central challenges of rocket design: tanks and engines must be as light as possible while withstanding launch loads. Single-stage-to-orbit vehicles would require extremely high fractions approaching the practical limits of materials, which is one reason most launch vehicles use staging, discarding empty structure as propellant is consumed.
