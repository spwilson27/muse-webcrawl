Moiety (kinship)

FieldAnthropology of kinship
DefinitionOne of two descent groups into which a society is divided
Typical ruleExogamy — marriage must be with the other moiety

In the anthropology of kinship, a moiety is one of two groups into which an entire society is divided by descent. The word derives from the French moitié, "half". Where moieties exist, every member of the society belongs to one or the other group, membership is usually inherited in a single line (patrilineal or matrilineal), and the two halves stand in a permanent, structured relationship to each other — most famously expressed in the rule of moiety exogamy, by which a person must marry a member of the opposite moiety.

Functions and reciprocity

Moieties typically do far more than regulate marriage. The two halves commonly owe each other reciprocal services: they bury each other's dead, conduct each other's initiation ceremonies, supply ritual partners, and cooperate or compete in games and ceremonies. Among the classic examples are the Raven and Eagle (or Wolf) moieties of the Tlingit and other Northwest Coast peoples, the moiety systems of Aboriginal Australian societies, many Amazonian peoples such as the Bororo studied by Claude Lévi-Strauss, and the Raven and Wolf clans of the Iroquois nations. Moieties are often associated with cosmological dualism — paired symbols such as sky/earth, summer/winter or sun/moon.

Moieties and dual organisation

Lévi-Strauss made dual organisation a centrepiece of structural anthropology, analysing how paired groups structure exchange, myth and social space; later anthropologists qualified his account, noting that dual organisations are frequently asymmetric or mask underlying triadic and hierarchical arrangements. Where society is divided into more numerous groupings, anthropologists speak of clans; when the sub-groups of each moiety are named and numerous they are sometimes termed phratries. Some Australian systems combine moieties with sections or subsections, generating fourfold or eightfold marriage classes.

Terminology

Because members of the opposite moiety are the marriageable category, kinship terminologies in moiety systems commonly classify mother-in-law, cross-cousins and spouses in symmetrical ways, and the moieties may be described as "wife-givers" and "wife-takers" (in asymmetric systems the two functions do not simply alternate). Dual divisions also appear in ceremonial sport and ritual associations beyond strict kinship.
