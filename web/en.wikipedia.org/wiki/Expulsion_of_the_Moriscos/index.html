Expulsion of the Moriscos

Moriscos being embarked for deportation from Spain, 1609.

The Expulsion of the Moriscos (Spanish: Expulsión de los moriscos) was decreed by King Philip III of Spain on April 9, 1609. The Moriscos were the descendants of Muslims who had converted — many forcibly — to Christianity under threat of exile after the fall of Granada in 1492. Over the following five years, roughly 300,000 Moriscos, about 4 percent of Spain's population, were deported, principally to North Africa.

Background

Despite their official conversion, many Moriscos were suspected of secretly practicing Islam, and Old Christian society viewed them as a potential fifth column allied with the Ottoman Empire and Barbary pirates. The bloody Alpujarras revolt (1568–1571) hardened attitudes. Assimilation campaigns failed, and by the early 1600s the Duke of Lerma, the king's favourite, pushed through expulsion, beginning with the Kingdom of Valencia, where Moriscos formed a large share of the agricultural workforce.

The expulsion

Deportations proceeded region by region from 1609 to 1614 — Valencia, then Aragon, Catalonia, Castile, Extremadura, and Andalusia. Exiles were funnelled to ports and shipped to Oran, Algiers, Tunis, and Morocco. Many died in transit or were robbed and killed on arrival; some were attacked by local populations in North Africa, who regarded them as Christians. Limited exceptions were made for small numbers retained for technical skills, and for Morisco children.

Consequences

The expulsion devastated the economy of Valencia, which lost perhaps a third of its population and much of its skilled labour in silk and irrigated agriculture. Historians regard the expulsion as one of the largest forced deportations in early modern Europe and a defining episode in Spain's confessional state-building.
