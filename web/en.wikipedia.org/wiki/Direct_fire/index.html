Direct fire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Direct fire is a method of firing a projectile weapon in which the firer can see the target and aims directly at it, with the projectile following a relatively flat trajectory to the target. It is the opposite of indirect fire, in which the target is not visible from the weapon and fire is aimed by calculation and adjusted by observers or sensors elsewhere.

Characteristics

Because the gunner sights on the target itself — through iron sights, optical sights, telescopic or thermal sights, or laser rangefinder-equipped fire-control systems — direct fire is generally faster to bring to bear and more accurate at short and medium ranges. Its effectiveness is limited by line of sight, terrain, and weather, and the firing position is exposed to return fire because visibility between firer and target is mutual.

Direct-fire weapons include rifles and machine guns, tank guns, anti-tank guns and guided missiles fired at visible targets, autocannon, and recoilless rifles. Historically, field artillery was designed for direct fire over open sights; the widespread adoption of indirect fire in the First World War transformed artillery practice, reserving direct fire for self-defence and anti-tank roles, as with dedicated anti-tank artillery and tank destroyers.

Direct fire in doctrine

In modern combined-arms doctrine, direct fires are planned against enemy positions within line of sight and are integrated with indirect fires, obstacles, and manoeuvre. Infantry anti-armour weapons (such as the RPG-7 and shoulder-fired missiles) and the main armament of armoured fighting vehicles are the principal direct-fire systems on the contemporary battlefield.
