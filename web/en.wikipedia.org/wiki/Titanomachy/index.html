In Greek mythology, the Titanomachy (Greek: Τιτανομαχία, Titanomakhía) was a ten-year series of battles fought in Thessaly between the two camps of Greek gods — the Titans, based on Mount Othrys, and the Olympians, who would come to reign from Mount Olympus. The war was fought to decide which generation of gods would have dominion over the Universe; the Olympians, led by Zeus, emerged victorious.

Background

The Titan Cronus had overthrown his father Uranus (Sky) but, warned that he would in turn be overthrown by his own son, swallowed each of his children by Rhea at birth. Rhea hid the infant Zeus on Crete and gave Cronus a stone in swaddling clothes instead. Grown to adulthood, Zeus forced Cronus to disgorge his siblings — Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades, and Poseidon — and the younger gods declared war.

The war

The war lasted ten years with no decision until Zeus, on Gaia's advice, freed the monstrous hundred-handed Hecatonchires and the Cyclopes from Tartarus. The Cyclopes forged Zeus's thunderbolt, Poseidon's trident, and Hades' helmet of invisibility. With these weapons and the Hecatonchires hurling boulders, the Olympians overwhelmed the Titans. Hesiod's Theogony gives the most complete surviving account; a separate epic poem, the Titanomachy, part of the Epic Cycle, is now lost except for fragments.

Aftermath

The defeated Titans were cast into Tartarus, a deep abyss below the underworld, guarded by the Hecatonchires; the Titan Atlas was condemned to hold up the sky. Zeus and his brothers then divided the cosmos by lot: sky to Zeus, sea to Poseidon, underworld to Hades. The Titanomachy forms part of the succession myth — Ouranos, Cronus, Zeus — that parallels older Near Eastern myths of generational divine conflict.
