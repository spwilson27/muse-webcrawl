Northern Sami (Davvisámegiella) is the most widely spoken of the Sami languages, with roughly 20,000 to 25,000 speakers in the northern parts of Norway, Sweden and Finland. Of some ten living Sami languages, Northern Sami is by far the strongest and serves as a lingua franca among Sami speakers. It belongs to the Uralic language family and is thus related to Finnish, Estonian and Hungarian.

Grammar

Northern Sami is an agglutinative, highly inflected language. Nouns inflect for seven cases and possessive suffixes, and verbs for three persons, two numbers (singular and dual), tense and mood. A characteristic feature is consonant gradation: consonants alternate between "strong" and "weak" grades according to the syllable structure. Word order is relatively free, with subject–verb–object as a common pattern.

Status and revitalisation

Assimilation policies in the 19th and 20th centuries, including boarding schools where Sami was forbidden, sharply reduced speaker numbers. Since the 1970s the language has been revitalised: it is an official language in designated administrative districts of Norway, Sweden and Finland, taught in schools, and used by the Sami parliaments, the broadcaster NRK Sápmi, and the newspaper Ávvir. A common written standard, adopted in 1979, serves all three countries.

Alphabet

Northern Sami uses an extended Latin alphabet including the letters á, č, đ, ŋ, š, ŧ, ž and å, which represent sounds not found in the majority languages.
