Green belt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A green belt is a policy, and a land-use zone designation, used in land-use planning to retain areas of largely undeveloped, wild, or agricultural land surrounding or neighbouring urban areas. Similar concepts are greenways or green wedges, which have a linear character and may run through an urban area instead of surrounding it. In essence, a green belt is an invisible line designating a border around a certain area, preventing development of the area and allowing wildlife to return and be established.

Purposes

The main purposes of green belt policy are to protect the countryside from urban sprawl, to prevent neighbouring towns from merging, to preserve the special character of historic towns, to encourage the recycling of derelict urban land, and to provide access to open countryside for urban residents. Green belts have been adopted in many countries, notably the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, South Korea, Japan and throughout continental Europe.

United Kingdom

In the United Kingdom, the Metropolitan Green Belt around London is the oldest and largest: the concept was promoted by the London Society and others in the 1920s and 1930s, given statutory backing by the Green Belt (London and Home Counties) Act 1938 and the Town and Country Planning Act 1947, and first formally designated from 1955. Fourteen green belts in England cover about 13% of the country's land area. Within them, most development is prohibited except in very special circumstances. The policy is periodically controversial: critics argue it constrains housing supply and pushes development further out ("leapfrogging"), while supporters credit it with preserving open land close to cities.

Elsewhere

Ottawa and other Canadian cities maintain greenbelts; Ontario's Greenbelt, established in 2005, is the world's largest at some 2 million acres. Seoul's development restriction zones, introduced in 1971, have been partially relaxed since the 1990s. Melbourne, Sydney and other Australian cities use green wedges. In the United States, urban growth boundaries such as Portland's serve a similar function.

Criticism

Economists have criticised rigid green belts for raising land and housing prices inside the boundary and encouraging longer commutes from beyond it. Defenders respond that the amenity, ecological and food-growing benefits, and the urban regeneration encouraged by constraining outward expansion, outweigh these costs.
