Stabat Mater

The Stabat Mater is a 13th-century Christian hymn to the Virgin Mary, meditating on her suffering as the mother of Jesus during his crucifixion. Its title is an abbreviation of the first line, Stabat Mater dolorosa ("the sorrowful mother was standing"). The poem, in rhymed, rhythmical Latin tercets, is traditionally attributed to the Franciscan friar Jacopone da Todi (c. 1230–1306), though it has also been ascribed to Pope Innocent III and others. It ranks with the Dies irae among the greatest Latin hymns of the Middle Ages.

Text and devotion

Stabat Mater dolorosa
iuxta crucem lacrimosa,
dum pendebat Filius.

"The sorrowful Mother stood weeping beside the Cross, while her Son was hanging." The hymn invites the believer to share Mary's grief, to ask that her sorrow be imprinted on the heart, and to hope for Paradise through the Passion. It became one of the core texts of Marian devotion, recited especially in Lent and on the Feast of Our Lady of Sorrows, and is the text prayed stanza by stanza in the Stations of the Cross. Suppressed among the sequences at the Council of Trent, it was restored to the Roman Missal in 1727.

Musical settings

The poem has inspired hundreds of composers. The best-known settings include those by Josquin des Prez, Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina, and Alessandro Scarlatti; the youthful setting by Giovanni Battista Pergolesi (1736) for two voices became the most famous of the Baroque era. Later settings include those by Haydn, Rossini (the exuberant Stabat Mater of 1842), Schubert, Verdi, Dvořák, Szymanowski, Poulenc and Arvo Pärt.
