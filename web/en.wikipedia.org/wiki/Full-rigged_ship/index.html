Full-rigged ship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A full-rigged ship, with square sails on fore, main and mizzen masts.

A full-rigged ship (or fully rigged ship) is a sailing vessel with a sail plan of three or more masts, all of them square-rigged. That is, each mast carries horizontal yards from which square sails hang. A full-rigged ship also usually carries a bowsprit and jib sails forward, and a gaff sail aft of the mizzenmast (the spanker).

Terminology

In strict usage, "ship" itself is a technical term: a vessel with three or more masts, all square-rigged. A vessel with two square-rigged masts is a brig or brigantine depending on the after mast; a vessel with a square-rigged foremast and fore-and-aft-rigged mainmast is a barque (also spelled bark). Other names include ship-rigged or ship-rig, and in the age of sail the term "ship" was commonly contrasted with "sloop", "brig" and "schooner".

Sail plan

From the deck upward, the square sails on a typical mast were the course, lower topsail, upper topsail, topgallant and royal, with later vessels adding a skysail above the royal. Stay sails and studding sails (stuns'ls) extended the canvas in fair weather. Working such a ship demanded a large, well-drilled crew: a late 19th-century windjammer might carry 30–40 hands, while a naval ship of the line could require several hundred.

History and surviving examples

Full-rigged ships dominated ocean trade and naval warfare from the 16th through the 19th centuries, reaching their apogee in the fast steel-hulled windjammers of the 1880s–1910s, which carried grain and nitrate around Cape Horn despite competition from steamships. Famous survivors still sailing include the German-built Gorch Fock (1958) and the Norwegian Sørlandet (1927), while museum ships such as Cutty Sark (a clipper, technically a full-rigged ship) and the American USS Constitution preserve the rig.
