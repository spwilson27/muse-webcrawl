Rhins of Galloway

Rhins of Galloway
Location: Dumfries and Galloway, Scotland
Form: Hammer-head peninsula
Main town: Stranraer
Extremity: Mull of Galloway

The Rhins of Galloway is a hammer-head peninsula in Dumfries and Galloway, in the far south-west of Scotland. Its name comes from the Scottish Gaelic rinn, meaning a point or promontory (the plural, na Rinne, gives "Rhins"). The peninsula is attached to the mainland near Stranraer and extends north and south: the North Rhins ends at Corsewall Point, and the South Rhins at the Mull of Galloway, Scotland's most southerly point.

Geography

Loch Ryan borders the peninsula to the east and Luce Bay to the south-east, while the North Channel separates the western coast from Northern Ireland, visible on clear days. The landscape is largely pastoral farmland with small villages such as Portpatrick — a picturesque harbour village on the west coast — Kirkcolm, Leswalt, Drummore and Port Logan, the last noted for its historic fish pond and the nearby Logan Botanic Garden, which benefits from the mild Gulf Stream climate.

History and sights

The area is rich in prehistoric remains, standing stones and early Christian sites. The Mull of Galloway lighthouse, built by Robert Stevenson in 1830, stands on cliffs that form an RSPB nature reserve noted for seabird colonies. The Southern Upland Way long-distance footpath begins at Portpatrick and crosses the Rhins on its way across southern Scotland.
