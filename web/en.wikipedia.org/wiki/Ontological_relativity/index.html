Ontological relativity

Ontological relativity is a thesis in the philosophy of language and ontology developed by Willard Van Orman Quine, most fully in his 1968 lecture "Ontological Relativity" and the book Ontological Relativity and Other Essays (1969). It holds that what objects a theory or a speaker refers to is determinate only relative to a background language or translation scheme: "reference is inscrutable" when considered in absolute terms.

The gavagai argument

The thesis grows out of Quine's earlier doctrine of the indeterminacy of translation in Word and Object (1960). A field linguist hearing a native say "gavagai" as a rabbit passes cannot determine from behaviour alone whether the word refers to rabbits, to undetached rabbit parts, to temporal stages of rabbits, or to manifestations of rabbithood — rival translation manuals can fit all the evidence while assigning different references. Quine argues the same inscrutability applies within one's own language: what our words refer to can be permuted systematically (by "proxy functions") without changing the truth values of anything we say.

Consequences

Quine concludes that ontology — what there is, as a speaker conceives it — makes sense only relative to a coordinate system of language, comparing his doctrine to the relativity of position and velocity in physics. The view underlies his naturalised approach to epistemology and ontology, and has been extensively debated, with Donald Davidson, Hilary Putnam and others drawing related morals about conceptual schemes and model-theoretic arguments against metaphysical realism.
