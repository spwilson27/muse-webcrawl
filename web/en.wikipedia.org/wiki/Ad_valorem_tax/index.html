Ad valorem tax

An ad valorem tax (Latin for "according to value") is a tax whose amount is based on the value of a transaction or of property. It is typically expressed as a percentage of the assessed value and is applied at the time of the transaction or periodically on property owned. The term contrasts with specific taxes, which are levied as a fixed amount per unit regardless of value.

Examples

  • Property tax — the classic ad valorem tax, levied by local governments on real estate (and sometimes personal property) based on assessed market value
  • Value-added tax (VAT) and retail sales taxes — levied as percentages of the price of goods and services
  • Tariffs and customs duties — often charged as a percentage of the declared value of imports
  • Estate and inheritance taxes — based on the value of transferred property
  • Vehicle registration taxes in some jurisdictions, based on the car's value

Features

Because the tax rises with value, ad valorem taxes automatically adjust for inflation and differences in property quality, but they require a mechanism for valuing the taxed property — assessments that taxpayers may contest. Sales and value-added taxes are usually collected by sellers on behalf of the government. Economists note that ad valorem taxation of traded goods is equivalent, under perfect competition, to a corresponding specific tax, though the two diverge under imperfect competition.
