Père Lachaise Cemetery (French: Cimetière du Père-Lachaise) is the largest cemetery in Paris, France, covering 44 hectares (110 acres) in the 20th arrondissement. Named after Père François de la Chaise (1624–1709), confessor to King Louis XIV, who lived on the site, it is the most visited necropolis in the world, receiving more than 3.5 million visitors annually.

History

The cemetery was established by Napoleon in 1804, when new laws forbade burials within the city proper and created several cemeteries outside the old boundaries. Initially Parisians avoided the distant eastern site; in a famous marketing stroke, administrators transferred the remains of Molière, La Fontaine, and the medieval lovers Héloïse and Abélard to Père Lachaise, and burials surged. The Mur des Fédérés (Communards' Wall) in the cemetery marks where 147 fighters of the Paris Commune were shot on 28 May 1871.

Notable burials

Père Lachaise is the resting place of an extraordinary roll of cultural figures, among them Frédéric Chopin, Oscar Wilde, Édith Piaf, Marcel Proust, Gertrude Stein, Colette, Molière, Eugène Delacroix, Georges Bizet, Maria Callas (ashes), Marcel Marceau, and Jim Morrison of the Doors, whose grave is among the most visited. The cemetery also contains numerous war memorials and memorials to victims of the Nazi camps.

Layout

With its rolling hills, some 5,300 trees, and cobbled avenues lined with elaborate sepulchres, the cemetery functions as an open-air museum of funerary sculpture spanning two centuries of French art, from neoclassical tombs to art nouveau and modernist monuments.
