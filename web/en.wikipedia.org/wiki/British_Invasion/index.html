The British Invasion was a cultural phenomenon of the mid-1960s, when rock and pop music acts from the United Kingdom — along with British fashion, film, and style — became popular in the United States. It began with the arrival of the Beatles in February 1964 and launched a wave of British groups that dominated the American charts through the middle of the decade, reshaping American popular music in turn.

Background

American rock and roll's first wave had faded by the early 1960s through deaths, scandal, and military service. British musicians, however, had absorbed American rock and roll, R&B, skiffle, and blues and returned them with a distinctive group format: self-contained bands playing their own instruments and increasingly writing their own songs. In Britain, the Merseybeat scene and London R&B clubs produced dozens of chart acts.

The first wave (1964–1966)

The Beatles' "I Want to Hold Your Hand" reached number one in the US in early 1964; their appearance on The Ed Sullivan Show on 9 February 1964 drew an audience of about 73 million. On the chart dated 4 April 1964 the Beatles held the top five positions. They were followed by the Dave Clark Five, Peter and Gordon, the Animals ("House of the Rising Sun"), Manfred Mann, Petula Clark, Freddie and the Dreamers, Herman's Hermits, Wayne Fontana and the Mindbenders, and the Rolling Stones, whose harder blues-based image contrasted with the Beatles' pop polish.

Impact on American music

The Invasion revived the American recording industry, ended the dominance of solo teen idols and Brill Building pop, and inspired the formation of garage bands across the United States. American acts such as the Byrds and the Monkees adapted the British group model. The flood of British music also reintroduced white American audiences to Black American blues and R&B through British interpreters.

Later waves

Historians sometimes speak of a second British invasion in the early 1980s, when British new wave, synth-pop, and post-punk acts — helped by MTV — charted heavily in the US, and of the global reach of Britpop in the 1990s.
