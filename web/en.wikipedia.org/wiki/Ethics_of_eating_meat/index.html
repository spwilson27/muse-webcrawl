Ethics of eating meat

The ethics of eating meat concerns whether it is morally permissible to eat non-human animals. It is one of the most debated topics in food ethics and animal ethics. Opponents of meat-eating argue that killing or causing suffering to sentient beings for food is wrong when alternatives exist; defenders argue that meat-eating is natural, nutritionally or culturally valuable, or that animals' interests count for less than humans'.

Arguments against eating meat

Peter Singer's utilitarian argument, set out in Animal Liberation (1975), holds that the suffering of factory-farmed animals vastly outweighs the pleasure humans get from eating them, and that discounting animal suffering merely because of species membership — "speciesism" — is unjustifiable. Tom Regan's rights-based view argues that animals are "subjects of a life" with inherent value that may not be sacrificed for others' benefit. Other arguments focus on the environmental damage of animal agriculture, its contribution to climate change and inefficient use of land and crops.

Arguments in favour

Defenders of meat-eating have appealed to human nutrition and tradition, to the ecological role of grazing livestock, and to the claim that moral status depends on capacities — such as rationality or moral agency — that non-human animals lack. Some philosophers distinguish killing from suffering, arguing that painless killing of animals raised well, or the eating of animals that died naturally, roadkill, or cultured (lab-grown) meat, may be permissible even if factory farming is not. Others question the sentience of particular species: debate continues over fish and invertebrates such as oysters and insects.

Related positions

Positions in the debate include vegetarianism, veganism, flexitarianism, pescetarianism and "conscientious omnivorism". Religious traditions also weigh in: Jainism, Hinduism and Buddhism encourage abstinence from meat, while other faiths regulate which meats may be eaten through rules such as kosher and halal.
