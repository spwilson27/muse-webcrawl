Arity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Functions are classified by the number of arguments they take.

In logic, mathematics, and computer science, arity is the number of arguments or operands taken by a function, operation, or relation. In mathematics, arity may also be named rank, but this word can have many other meanings. In logic and philosophy, predicates or relations are also called adicity; in computer science, a closely related notion is the arity of a subroutine or operator.

Terminology

Arities have special names derived from Latin distributives:

  • Nullary (0-ary): takes no arguments; a constant or nullary function.
  • Unary (1-ary): takes one argument, e.g. negation, absolute value, factorial.
  • Binary (2-ary): takes two arguments, e.g. addition, multiplication, exponentiation.
  • Ternary (3-ary): takes three arguments, e.g. the conditional operator ?: in many programming languages.
  • n-ary: takes n arguments for arbitrary n; a function taking an arbitrary or variable number of arguments is called variadic.

Examples

The successor function S(n) is unary; the usual arithmetic operations + and × are binary (infix notation being customary for them); the scalar triple product is ternary. In first-order logic, a predicate symbol's arity determines how many terms it combines into an atomic formula; in universal algebra, the operations of an algebraic structure each have a fixed arity, and the list of arities is the structure's signature.
