The Mozu Tombs (百舌鳥古墳群, Mozu Kofungun) are a group of megalithic burial mounds (kofun) in Sakai, Osaka Prefecture, Japan. Built between the late 4th and early 6th centuries, at the height of the Kofun period, the cluster originally contained more than 100 tombs; about half survive, many now surrounded by the modern city. In 2019 the Mozu Tombs were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List together with the Furuichi Tombs as the "Mozu-Furuichi Kofun Group: Mounded Tombs of Ancient Japan".

The Daisenryō Kofun

The dominant monument of the group, and the largest tomb in Japan, is the Daisenryō Kofun, traditionally attributed to Emperor Nintoku. Its keyhole-shaped mound (zenpō-kōen-fun) is about 486 metres long — the largest grave by area in the world — and is surrounded by three moats and smaller satellite tombs. The mound is now covered in dense forest and is administered by the Imperial Household Agency as an imperial mausoleum, which restricts archaeological excavation; the interior has never been fully investigated, though eroded areas after an 1872 landslide yielded armour and other artefacts.

Contents and context

Excavated smaller tombs in the group have produced grave goods characteristic of the period: iron weapons and armour, horse trappings, gilt-bronze ornaments, Sue ware pottery and haniwa clay figures that once stood in rows on the mounds. The Mozu cluster, on the plain overlooking Osaka Bay, is interpreted as the burial ground of the paramount rulers of the Yamato polity before the centre of tomb-building shifted to the Furuichi group and then the Asuka region. The Sakai City Museum, near Daisen Park, displays material from the tombs.
