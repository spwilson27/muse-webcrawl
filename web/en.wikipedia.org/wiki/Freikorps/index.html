Freikorps (German for "Free Corps" or "Volunteer Corps") were irregular German volunteer units that existed from the eighteenth century but are best known for the paramilitary formations raised after Germany's defeat in the First World War. Composed largely of demobilized soldiers and students, they were employed by the government of the early Weimar Republic to crush communist and socialist uprisings and to fight in border conflicts in the east.

Origins

The name dates to the volunteer corps of the Seven Years' War and the Napoleonic Wars, such as the Lützow Free Corps. After the Armistice of 1918, with the Imperial Army disintegrating, officers including General Ludwig Maercker raised new Freikorps, tolerated and funded by Defence Minister Gustav Noske's government.

Activities

Freikorps units suppressed the Spartacist uprising in Berlin in January 1919 — during which Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht were murdered — as well as the Bavarian Soviet Republic and uprisings in the Ruhr and central Germany. In the Baltic states, German Freikorps fought both Bolshevik and national forces in 1919; others fought Polish insurgents in Upper Silesia.

Dissolution and legacy

The Treaty of Versailles and Allied pressure led to the units' official dissolution by 1920, though some defied the order: the Ehrhardt Brigade's participation in the Kapp Putsch of 1920 was a high point of Freikorps defiance of the republic. Many former members later joined nationalist organizations, and a significant number became early members and leaders of the Nazi SA and SS, a continuity explored in Ernst von Salomon's memoir The Outlaws.
