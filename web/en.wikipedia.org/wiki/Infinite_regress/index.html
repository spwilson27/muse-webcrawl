Infinite regress

An infinite regress is a sequence of reasoning or justification that can never come to an end: each step depends on a further step of the same kind, so the explanation or justification is never completed. Infinite regresses are usually considered a problem for the theories that generate them — a "vicious" regress — although philosophers disagree about whether every regress is vicious.

In epistemology

The classic example is the regress argument for justification, formulated in antiquity and associated with Agrippa the Sceptic and the Münchhausen trilemma: if a belief is justified by another belief, that belief in turn needs justification, and so on. The chain must either go on forever (regress), circle back on itself (circularity), or stop at an unjustified starting point (dogmatism). Foundationalism responds by positing basic beliefs that stop the regress; coherentism accepts a holistic, web-like structure instead; infinitism holds that an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons can justify a belief.

Other examples

Regresses appear in cosmology ("what caused the first cause?"), in the homunculus argument in philosophy of mind (explaining perception by an inner observer who must then perceive in turn), and in Lewis Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," which shows that rules of inference cannot all be written into premises. In mathematics and computer science, recursion resembles a regress but is benign when it terminates at a base case.

Vicious and benign regresses

A regress is generally agreed to be vicious when the task at each step cannot be discharged without completing the whole infinite series — for example, if understanding a sentence required first understanding another, without end. Other infinite series, such as the endless divisibility of a line segment, are usually considered harmless.
