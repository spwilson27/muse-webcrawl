Impeachment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Impeachment is a process by which a legislative body levels charges against a public official, as a prelude to possible removal from office and disqualification. It exists in many constitutional systems and differs from ordinary criminal prosecution: impeachment is a political-legal process directed at fitness for office, though the same conduct may also be prosecuted in the courts.

Procedure and examples

In the United States, the House of Representatives impeaches (adopts articles of impeachment by majority) and the Senate tries the case, conviction requiring two-thirds; the constitution specifies "Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors". Presidents Andrew Johnson (1868), Bill Clinton (1998) and Donald Trump (2019 and 2021) were impeached by the House; none was convicted in the Senate, and Richard Nixon resigned in 1974 facing near-certain impeachment. Other systems use impeachment or analogous procedures: South Korea's National Assembly impeached President Park Geun-hye in 2016 (upheld by the Constitutional Court) and moved against President Yoon Suk Yeol in December 2024 following his martial-law declaration; Brazil removed Dilma Rousseff in 2016; and parliamentary systems accomplish similar ends by no-confidence votes, sometimes making formal impeachment rare (as in the UK, where it is obsolete in practice).

Function

Impeachment exists to remove officials who abuse power where elections or courts cannot act quickly enough; its political character is simultaneously its safeguard (removal is hard, preventing coups by legislature) and its critique (standards are set by partisans). Comparative scholars study it alongside votes of no confidence, recalls and term limits as accountability mechanisms.
