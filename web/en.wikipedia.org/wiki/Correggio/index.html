Antonio da Correggio

Antonio Allegri da Correggio (August 1489 – 5 March 1534), usually known simply as Correggio, was the foremost painter of the Parma school of the Italian High Renaissance. Working largely outside the great artistic centers of Florence and Rome, he developed a luminous, sensuous style famed for soft modeling, dramatic foreshortening, and illusionistic ceiling painting that anticipated the Baroque.

Life

Correggio was born Antonio Allegri in the small town of Correggio, near Reggio Emilia, the son of a merchant. Little is documented about his training; influences of Mantegna, Leonardo da Vinci, and Lorenzo Costa have been detected in his early work. By 1514 he was painting altarpieces for his home town, and from about 1519 he worked chiefly in Parma, where his patrons included the Benedictine abbey of San Paolo, the church of San Giovanni Evangelista, and the cathedral chapter. He died in Correggio in 1534, aged about 44, reportedly after returning home with a large payment in coins.

Works

His first masterpiece is the fresco decoration of the Camera di San Paolo (c. 1519), painted for the abbess Giovanna Piacenza. In San Giovanni Evangelista (1520–1524) he painted the Vision of St John on Patmos in the dome. His crowning work is the Assumption of the Virgin in the dome of Parma Cathedral (1526–1530), a swirling vortex of angels and saints seen di sotto in sù that profoundly influenced later Baroque ceiling painting. Notable easel works include the Mystic Marriage of Saint Catherine, the Madonna of St Francis, La Notte (the Adoration of the Shepherds), and the mythological series painted for Federico II Gonzaga, including Jupiter and Io, The Rape of Ganymede, Danaë, and Leda and the Swan.

Reputation

Neglected by Vasari, who never visited Parma, Correggio's reputation rose sharply in the 17th and 18th centuries, when the Carracci and the Rococo painters admired his grace and luminosity.
