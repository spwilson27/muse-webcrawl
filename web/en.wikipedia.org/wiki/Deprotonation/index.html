Deprotonation

Deprotonation (or dehydrogenation in older usage) is the removal of a proton (a hydrogen cation, H+) from a Brønsted–Lowry acid in an acid–base reaction. The species formed is the conjugate base of that acid. The complementary process, in which a proton is added to a base, is protonation.

Principles

The readiness of a molecule to be deprotonated is measured by the pKa of its acidic proton: the lower the pKa, the more readily the proton is removed. Deprotonation is carried out by a base whose conjugate acid has a higher pKa than the species being deprotonated, so that the equilibrium favours the products. Common bases for deprotonation include hydroxide, alkoxides, amides such as lithium diisopropylamide (LDA), and hydrides such as sodium hydride.

Applications

Deprotonation is fundamental throughout chemistry and biochemistry. In organic synthesis, deprotonation adjacent to a carbonyl group generates enolates used in carbon–carbon bond formation; deprotonation of terminal alkynes gives acetylides. In biochemistry, the protonation state of amino-acid side chains governs protein structure and enzyme catalysis, and buffers maintain the pH at which these equilibria operate. Deprotonation reactions also underlie the operation of many analytical techniques, such as electrospray ionisation in mass spectrometry, where analytes are commonly detected as [M−H]− ions.

Kinetics

Although deprotonation of oxygen and nitrogen acids is usually fast, removal of a proton from carbon can be slow and may require strong, hindered, or specially designed bases, and is subject to kinetic isotope effects when hydrogen is replaced by deuterium.
