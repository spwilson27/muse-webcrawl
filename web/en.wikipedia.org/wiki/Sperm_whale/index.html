Sperm whale

The sperm whale (Physeter macrocephalus) is the largest of the toothed whales and the largest toothed predator on Earth. It is the only living member of the genus Physeter and one of three extant species in the sperm whale family, alongside the pygmy and dwarf sperm whales. Adult males average 16 metres (52 ft) in length and can weigh around 50 tonnes, with a huge head that may account for a third of the animal's length.

Description

The sperm whale is readily recognised by its massive block-shaped head, which contains the spermaceti organ, filled with a waxy substance once prized as a lubricant and illuminant. The lower jaw is narrow and underslung, lined with conical teeth that fit into sockets in the upper jaw. Its wrinkled, dark grey skin, small paddle-like flippers, and triangular tail flukes distinguish it from other large whales. The species possesses the largest brain of any known animal, living or extinct.

Behaviour and ecology

Sperm whales are found in all deep oceans, from the equator to the edges of the polar pack ice. They are prodigious divers, routinely descending 400–1,500 metres—and occasionally beyond 2,000 metres—in dives lasting 45 minutes or more, in search of squid, including the giant and colossal squid, and deep-sea fish. Their echolocation clicks are among the loudest sounds produced by any animal. Females and young live in social pods with strong bonds, cooperatively caring for calves, while adult males are largely solitary or form loose bachelor groups at higher latitudes.

Whaling and conservation

Sperm whales were hunted intensively from the 18th century into the late 20th century for spermaceti, sperm oil, and ambergris, a substance produced in the digestive system and valued in perfumery. Commercial whaling reduced global populations substantially—some estimates suggest from over a million to a few hundred thousand—before the International Whaling Commission's moratorium took effect in the 1980s. The species is now classified as Vulnerable, threatened by entanglement, ship strikes, and ocean noise, and is recovering slowly.

In culture

The sperm whale is the species of Herman Melville's white whale in Moby-Dick (1851), inspired partly by the real albino whale Mocha Dick and the sinking of the whaler Essex in 1820. The species remains an icon of the deep ocean.
