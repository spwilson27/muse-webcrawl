Soil conservation is the prevention of loss of the topmost layer of the soil from erosion or prevention of reduced fertility caused by overuse, acidification, salinisation, or other chemical soil contamination. It encompasses farming and land-management practices that protect soil as a natural resource, maintain its structure and organic matter, and keep it in place against wind and water.

Erosion and its causes

Soil erosion by water and wind removes fertile topsoil far faster than soil formation replaces it. Deforestation, overgrazing, intensive tillage, monoculture, and leaving fields bare between crops accelerate erosion, while climate change is increasing the frequency of heavy rainfall events in many regions. The Dust Bowl of the 1930s in North America — which prompted the creation of the U.S. Soil Conservation Service (now the Natural Resources Conservation Service) under Hugh Hammond Bennett — remains the classic demonstration of erosion's costs.

Practices

Major techniques include contour ploughing and strip cropping along slope contours; terracing of steep land; no-till and reduced-tillage farming that leave residue on the surface; cover crops and crop rotation to keep living roots in the ground; windbreaks and shelterbelts; maintaining grassed waterways and riparian buffer strips; and controlled grazing. At landscape scale, reforestation and watershed management reduce sediment loads in rivers and reservoirs.

Soil health and policy

Modern conservation emphasises soil health — organic matter, soil biology, and structure — alongside erosion control, and recognises soil as a major carbon store. Governments support conservation through extension services, subsidy conditions ("cross-compliance"), conservation reserve programmes that retire marginal land, and international initiatives such as the UN Food and Agriculture Organization's Global Soil Partnership.
