Bernhard Riemann

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Bernhard Riemann, portrait engraving, 1863]

Georg Friedrich Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866) was a German mathematician who made profound contributions to analysis, number theory, and differential geometry, many of them foundational for later developments including Einstein's general theory of relativity.

Born in Breselenz, Hanover, the son of a Lutheran pastor, Riemann studied at Göttingen and Berlin (under Dirichlet, whose lectures shaped him), and succeeded Dirichlet at Göttingen. His 1851 dissertation founded the geometric theory of complex functions (Riemann surfaces); his 1854 habilitation lecture 'On the hypotheses which lie at the bases of geometry' created Riemannian geometry.

Work

The Riemann integral, Riemann zeta function and its 1859 hypothesis, Cauchy–Riemann equations, and the Riemann–Roch theorem all bear his name. His single number-theory paper (1859) — the only one he published on the subject — transformed the study of prime numbers.

Always of fragile health, Riemann died of tuberculosis in Italy in 1866, aged 39, while seeking a warmer climate; his housekeeper reportedly discarded some unpublished manuscripts after his death.

Legacy

Few mathematicians have left so deep a mark in so short a life; his geometry lectures were published posthumously and shaped mathematics for a century.
