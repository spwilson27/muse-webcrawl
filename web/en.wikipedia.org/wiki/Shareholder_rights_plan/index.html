Shareholder rights plan

A shareholder rights plan, colloquially known as a poison pill, is a type of defensive tactic used by a corporation's board of directors against a hostile takeover. The plan typically grants existing shareholders the right to purchase additional shares at a steep discount if a single shareholder — usually the hostile bidder — acquires more than a specified percentage of the company's stock, commonly 10 to 20 percent.

Mechanism

When triggered, the rights attached to existing shares "flip in": every shareholder except the acquirer may buy new shares at a discount, massively diluting the acquirer's stake and making the takeover prohibitively expensive. A related feature, the "flip-over" provision, allows shareholders to buy the acquirer's shares at a discount after a merger is completed. Because the pill can usually be redeemed by the board for a nominal amount before it is triggered, it gives directors leverage to negotiate a higher price or block an unwanted bid entirely.

History

The poison pill was invented in 1982 by corporate lawyer Martin Lipton of the firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz, in response to a wave of hostile tender offers. Its legality was upheld by the Delaware Supreme Court in the landmark 1985 case Moran v. Household International, Inc., and the standards governing its use were refined in Unocal Corp. v. Mesa Petroleum Co. (1985) and Unitrin, Inc. v. American General Corp. (1995).

Criticism and variants

Critics argue that poison pills entrench management and deprive shareholders of takeover premiums; supporters counter that they protect long-term value and force bidders to negotiate. Variants include the "dead hand" pill, which only incumbent directors can redeem (invalidated in Delaware), "no hand" pills, and modern plans adopted to preserve net operating loss carryforwards (NOL pills) with low triggers around 5 percent. High-profile uses include Netflix's 2012 pill against Carl Icahn and Twitter's 2022 pill adopted after Elon Musk's unsolicited bid.
