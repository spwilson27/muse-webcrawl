Micropaleontology

Micropaleontology (American spelling; also spelled micropalaeontology in British usage) is the branch of paleontology that studies microfossils, or fossils that require the use of a microscope to see the organism, its morphology, and its characteristic details.

Microfossils

Microfossils are generally between 0.001 mm and 1 mm in size and include:

  • Foraminifera — single-celled protists with calcium carbonate tests, widely used index fossils.
  • Radiolaria and diatoms — siliceous skeletons.
  • Ostracods — small crustaceans with bivalved carapaces.
  • Calcareous nannofossils (coccoliths) — plates of phytoplankton.
  • Palynomorphs — organic-walled pollen, spores, and dinoflagellate cysts.
  • Conodonts — phosphatic tooth-like elements of extinct chordates.

Methods

Samples of rock or sediment are disaggregated with water, acids, or solvents, sieved, and the residues examined under stereomicroscopes and scanning electron microscopes. Because microfossils occur in huge numbers even in small samples — such as drill cuttings — they are exceptionally useful statistically.

Applications

Micropaleontology is fundamental to biostratigraphy: the petroleum industry uses microfossil assemblages to date and correlate strata while drilling. Microfossils also serve paleoceanography and paleoclimatology, since assemblages and shell chemistry record past sea temperatures, ice volume, and ocean chemistry; deep-sea cores studied by projects such as the Ocean Drilling Program rely heavily on foraminifera and nannofossils.
