Essen Abbey (German: Stift Essen) was a community of secular canonesses for women of high nobility in what is now the city of Essen, Germany. Founded around 845 by Altfrid, Bishop of Hildesheim, it grew into one of the most powerful and wealthy religious houses for women in the Holy Roman Empire, and its abbesses ranked as princesses of the Empire.

History

The abbey's first abbess was Gerswid, a relative of the founder. In the tenth century the house reached the height of its influence under Abbess Mathilde (971–1011), a granddaughter of Emperor Otto I, and her successor Theophanu (1039–1058), granddaughter of the Byzantine princess Theophanu. Under these Ottonian abbesses the abbey commissioned an extraordinary treasury of goldsmith work and illuminated manuscripts, including the Golden Madonna of Essen (c. 980), the oldest known sculptural depiction of the Virgin Mary in Western art, and the Cross of Otto and Mathilde.

The abbey's territory, the Stift Essen, was a small ecclesiastical state of the Empire. The city of Essen grew up around the abbey, and relations between the increasingly self-confident city and the abbess were frequently strained. The abbey was secularised in 1803, its lands passing to Prussia, and the last abbess, Maria Kunigunde of Saxony, left Essen.

The abbey church

The former abbey church, now Essen Minster (Essener Münster), serves as the cathedral of the Diocese of Essen, founded in 1958. Its treasury, the Essen Cathedral Treasury, preserves one of the most significant collections of Ottonian goldsmith art in existence, including four processional crosses of the Ottonian period, the crown sometimes identified with that of the child emperor Otto III, and the Golden Madonna.
