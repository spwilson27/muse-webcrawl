Astronomical year numbering

Astronomical year numbering is a system of numbering years devised by the French astronomer Jacques Cassini in 1740 for use in astronomical calculations. It is based on the familiar Anno Domini (AD/BC) system but includes a year zero, which the traditional system lacks, so that arithmetic across the era boundary works straightforwardly.

Conversion

In the astronomical system, AD years keep their usual numbers. The year 1 BC is numbered 0, 2 BC is −1, and in general the year n BC is numbered −(n − 1). Thus the year 4713 BC, the start of the Julian period, is astronomical year −4712. The system removes the discontinuity caused by the absence of a year 0 between 1 BC and AD 1, simplifying the calculation of intervals: the interval between a date in 10 BC and the same date in AD 10 is simply 19 years either way, but is computed directly as 10 − (−9) in astronomical years.

Usage

Astronomical year numbering is standard in astronomy and is also the convention used by the ISO 8601 date standard, which designates the year 0000 for 1 BC and negative years for earlier dates. Historians generally retain the traditional BC/AD system without a year zero.
