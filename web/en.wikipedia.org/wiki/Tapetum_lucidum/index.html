Tapetum lucidum

Eyeshine produced by light reflected from a cat's tapetum lucidum

The tapetum lucidum (Latin for "bright tapestry") is a layer of tissue in the eye of many vertebrates, and some invertebrates, lying immediately behind or within the retina. It reflects visible light back through the retina, increasing the light available to the photoreceptors and thereby improving vision in low-light conditions. The reflected light produces the phenomenon known as eyeshine, seen when an animal's eyes appear to glow in the dark when illuminated.

Function

Light entering the eye that is not absorbed by the photoreceptors on its first pass is reflected by the tapetum for a second opportunity at absorption. This effectively doubles the path length of light through the photoreceptor layer, improving sensitivity at the cost of some image sharpness, because reflected light is scattered. The adaptation is characteristic of nocturnal and crepuscular animals and of deep-sea fish.

Types

Tapeta differ in structure and location. In many mammals, including cats, dogs and cattle, the tapetum is a layer within the choroid containing crystalline material such as riboflavin or zinc-containing structures arranged in regular patterns that produce iridescent colours. Sharks and other fish have tapeta containing guanine crystals. In some species the tapetum lies within the retinal pigment epithelium itself (retinal tapetum), as in crocodilians and many fish. Spiders possess tapeta of yet another form, contributing to the sensitivity of their secondary eyes.

Occurrence

The tapetum is present in many carnivores, ungulates, rodents, bats and prosimian primates, but absent in humans and other haplorhine primates, most birds, and diurnal animals generally — one reason human night vision is comparatively poor and why human eyes show a dull "red-eye" reflection in flash photographs rather than true eyeshine.
