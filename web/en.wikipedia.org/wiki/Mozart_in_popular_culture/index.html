Mozart in popular culture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Overview

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart (1756–1791) is one of the most widely referenced classical composers in modern popular culture. His image — the child prodigy in a powdered wig who died young and poor — and his music, from Eine kleine Nachtmusik to the Queen of the Night's aria, are instantly recognisable even to audiences who never listen to classical music.

Film, stage and song

The defining portrayal is Peter Shaffer's play Amadeus (1979) and Miloš Forman's film adaptation (1984), which won eight Academy Awards including Best Picture. The film dramatised the (largely fictional) murderous jealousy of Antonio Salieri and fixed in the public mind an image of Mozart as a giggling, foul-mouthed genius. Austrian singer Falco scored an international hit in 1985 with "Rock Me Amadeus". Mozart appears as a character in operas, musicals (including the French rock opera Mozart, l'opéra rock), cartoons, advertisements and countless films — as background music, his works have scored everything from comedies to horror films.

The "Mozart effect"

In 1993 researchers Rauscher, Shaw and Ky reported that listening to Mozart's Sonata for Two Pianos in D major (K.448) briefly improved spatial reasoning in a small student sample. The modest finding was amplified by the media into the "Mozart effect" — the claim that Mozart makes listeners, especially babies, smarter — spawning a multimillion-dollar industry of recordings for infants and even prompting Georgia's governor to distribute classical CDs to newborns. Subsequent research found the effect small, short-lived and probably due to arousal and mood rather than to anything specific to Mozart.

Other references

Mozartkugeln chocolate pralines, the Mozart festivals of Salzburg and Würzburg, the portrayal of Mozart as a punch-drunk character in Bill & Ted spin-offs, and his frequent use as the archetype of innate genius — contrasted with the "hard-working craftsman" — all sustain his afterlife. The apocryphal Salieri poisoning legend, already dramatised by Pushkin in Mozart and Salieri (1830) and set by Rimsky-Korsakov, remains the most durable myth.
