Jacopo Robusti (1518–1594), known as Tintoretto ("little dyer," after his father's trade as a dyer of silk, tintore), was an Italian Renaissance painter of the Venetian school. Celebrated for his speed of execution, dramatic use of perspective, and bold, energetic brushwork, he was called Il Furioso ("the Furious"). His motto, displayed in his studio, was said to be: "The drawing of Michelangelo and the colouring of Titian."

Life and career

According to his early biographers, Tintoretto was briefly apprenticed to Titian, who dismissed him after a few days — a story often read as an early sign of rivalry. Working virtually his whole life in Venice, he built his practice on rapid, inexpensive commissions for churches and confraternities (scuole). His breakthrough came with St Mark's Body Brought to Venice and the Miracle of the Slave (1548). In 1564 he famously won the competition for the Scuola Grande di San Rocco by presenting a finished painting instead of the requested sketch, and spent over two decades decorating the scuola with more than fifty canvases, including a vast Crucifixion.

Major works

His largest painting, Paradise (c. 1588–1592), roughly 22 by 9 metres, covers the wall behind the throne of the Great Council in the Doge's Palace and is among the largest canvases ever painted in oil. His late Last Supper (1592–94) in San Giorgio Maggiore shows the scene diagonally receding into darkness, with Christ distributing communion amid swirling servants and angels — a characteristically dynamic, visionary composition very different from Leonardo's balanced version. Other major works include the Susanna and the Elders, The Origin of the Milky Way (National Gallery, London), and cycles in the Palazzo Ducale and the church of the Madonna dell'Orto, where he is buried.

Style and legacy

Tintoretto pioneered dramatic foreshortening, steep perspectives, and unusual viewpoints, often working out compositions with small wax figures arranged in model stage boxes lit by candles. His loose, rapid technique and ecstatic energy influenced El Greco (who may have worked in his orbit) and later generations of Baroque painters. His workshop continued under his son Domenico and daughter Marietta, herself a noted portraitist.
