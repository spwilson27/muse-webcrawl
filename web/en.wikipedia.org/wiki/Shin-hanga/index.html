Shin-hanga

Shin-hanga (新版画, lit. "new prints") was an art movement in early twentieth-century Japan, roughly from the 1910s to the 1950s, that revitalised the traditional woodblock print (ukiyo-e) by combining its collaborative production system — artist, carver, printer and publisher — with Western-influenced elements such as naturalistic light, shading and perspective.

History

The movement was driven by the publisher Watanabe Shōzaburō, who commissioned designs from artists including Kawase Hasui, known for evocative landscapes; Hiroshi Yoshida; and Tsuchiya Koitsu. Subjects included landscapes (fūkei-ga), beautiful women (bijin-ga), actors, and birds and flowers. Many prints were exported to America and Europe, where they found an enthusiastic market.

Contrast with sōsaku-hanga

Shin-hanga preserved the traditional division of labour, in contrast to the contemporary sōsaku-hanga ("creative prints") movement, in which the artist personally carved and printed their own blocks.
