Egon Sharpe Pearson CBE FRS (11 August 1895 – 12 June 1980) was one of the leading British statisticians of the twentieth century. The son of Karl Pearson, he collaborated with Jerzy Neyman to create the modern theory of statistical hypothesis testing, introducing the framework of Type I and Type II errors and the Neyman–Pearson lemma on most powerful tests.

Life and career

Pearson was educated at Winchester College and Trinity College, Cambridge. After war service he joined his father's Department of Applied Statistics at University College London in 1921, succeeding Karl Pearson as professor and head of department in 1933 and editing the journal Biometrika — which his father had founded — from 1936 to 1966. During the Second World War he organised statistical work on quality control and acceptance sampling for the Ministry of Supply. He served as president of the Royal Statistical Society (1955–56) and received its Guy Medal in Gold in 1955; he was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1966.

The Neyman–Pearson theory

In a series of papers beginning in 1928, Pearson and the Polish mathematician Jerzy Neyman recast hypothesis testing as a choice between two competing hypotheses governed by explicit error probabilities: the probability α of rejecting a true null hypothesis (Type I error) and β of failing to reject a false one (Type II error), with statistical power 1 − β. The Neyman–Pearson lemma (1933) proves that for a simple null against a simple alternative, the likelihood-ratio test is the most powerful test at any given size. Despite a famous feud between Neyman's school and R. A. Fisher's rival philosophy of significance testing, the Neyman–Pearson framework became the standard taught theory of tests and a foundation of industrial quality control.
