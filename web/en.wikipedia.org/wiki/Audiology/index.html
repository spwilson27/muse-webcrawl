Audiology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Audiology is a branch of science that studies hearing, balance, and related disorders. Audiologists treat those with hearing loss and proactively prevent related damage. By employing various testing strategies (e.g. behavioural hearing tests, otoacoustic emission measurements, and electrophysiologic tests), audiologists aim to determine whether someone has normal sensitivity to sounds. If hearing loss is identified, audiologists counsel patients as to which hearing aids, cochlear implants or other technologies may help, and provide aural rehabilitation.

Pure-tone audiometry in a sound-treated booth.

Scope of practice

Audiologists diagnose and manage hearing loss (conductive, sensorineural and mixed), tinnitus, auditory processing disorders and vestibular (balance) disorders. Core diagnostic tools include pure-tone and speech audiometry, tympanometry and acoustic reflex testing, otoacoustic emissions, and auditory brainstem response (ABR) testing — the latter widely used in newborn hearing screening. Treatment and management include fitting hearing aids, programming cochlear implants, dispensing assistive listening devices, cerumen management and counselling.

History

The field emerged during and after the Second World War, when rehabilitation programmes were created for service members with noise- and blast-induced hearing loss; Raymond Carhart is often called the "father of audiology" for coining the term and developing clinical methods in the 1940s. In many countries audiology is now a doctoral-level profession — in the United States the Doctor of Audiology (AuD) became the entry-level degree in the 2000s.

Related fields

Audiology overlaps with otolaryngology (ENT medicine), speech-language pathology, acoustics and hearing science. Audiologists may specialise in paediatrics, geriatrics, educational audiology, occupational hearing conservation or intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring.
