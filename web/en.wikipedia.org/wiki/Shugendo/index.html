Shugendō (Japanese: 修験道) is a Japanese religion centred on ascetic practice in the mountains, formed in the Heian period from the fusion of indigenous mountain worship (Shinto), esoteric Buddhism, Daoist elements and shamanic folk religion. Its practitioners, the yamabushi ("those who lie down in the mountains"), seek spiritual power and enlightenment through severe austerities — mountain pilgrimages, waterfall purification, fasting and fire rituals — performed in sacred peaks regarded as the dwellings of kami and buddhas.

History

Shugendō traces its origin to the semi-legendary seventh-century ascetic En no Gyōja (En no Ozunu), said to have practised on Mount Katsuragi and Mount Ōmine and to have subdued local deities. Through the medieval period, mountain ascetic communities organised around centres such as Ōmine–Kumano, Haguro, and Hiko, and aligned with the Shingon and Tendai schools in two great branches (Honzan-ha and Tōzan-ha). Yamabushi served villages as exorcists, healers and guides for pilgrims. The Meiji government banned Shugendō in 1872 as part of the separation of Shinto and Buddhism; the tradition survived privately and revived after 1945 under freedom of religion.

Practices

Core practices include mineiri — ritualised entry into the mountains structured as a journey through the realms of the Buddhist mandala — waterfall ablution (takigyō), fire rituals (goma and outdoor saitō goma), recitation of sutras and mantras, and feats such as walking over coals. The yamabushi costume is distinctive: a small black cap (tokin), white or saffron garments, a conch trumpet (horagai), staff, and yuigesa stole with pom-poms symbolising Buddhist virtues. Mount Ōmine, a central Shugendō site, is traditionally closed to women, a prohibition that remains controversial.

Beliefs

Shugendō is syncretic and practice-oriented: its goal is to acquire supernatural efficacy (gen) and awakening through the body tested in nature, identifying the practitioner with deities such as Zaō Gongen — the fierce avatar revealed, according to legend, to En no Gyōja on Mount Kinpu — and Fudō Myōō. Its mountain mandalas, such as the Ōmine range read as the Diamond and Womb mandalas, remain living pilgrimage landscapes, now partly inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage in the "Sacred Sites and Pilgrimage Routes in the Kii Mountain Range".
