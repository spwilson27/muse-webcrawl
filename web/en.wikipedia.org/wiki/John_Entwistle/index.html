John Alec Entwistle (9 October 1944 – 27 June 2002) was an English musician, best known as the bass guitarist of the rock band the Who. Nicknamed "The Ox" for his still, composed stage presence amid the band's chaos — and "Thunderfingers" for his technique — he revolutionised the role of the electric bass, playing it as a lead instrument with a full, treble-rich sound, rapid runs and a distinctive typewriter-like fingerstyle. He was repeatedly voted the greatest bassist of all time in critics' and readers' polls and was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame with the Who in 1990.

Early life

Entwistle was born in Chiswick, London. Trained on piano, French horn, trumpet and French horn in school orchestras and the Middlesex Schools Symphony Orchestra, he switched to guitar and then bass, building his own instrument. At Acton County Grammar School he formed a trad jazz band with Pete Townshend; the two soon joined Roger Daltrey's group the Detours, which became the Who.

Career with the Who

Entwistle's virtuosic solo on "My Generation" (1965) — played through successive broken strings' worth of basses — established his style. He contributed darkly comic songs to the band's albums, including "Boris the Spider," "Heaven and Hell" and "My Wife," and sang lead on several. His horn playing coloured tracks such as "5:15," and his compositions were central to Tommy and Quadrophenia. Outside the Who he released solo albums beginning with Smash Your Head Against the Wall (1971) and toured with the John Entwistle Band, while assembling one of the world's finest collections of bass guitars and writing the book Bass Culture.

Death and legacy

Entwistle died in Las Vegas on 27 June 2002, aged 57, on the eve of a Who tour, of a heart attack brought on by cocaine use combined with an existing heart condition. The tour went ahead with bassist Pino Palladino. Entwistle's influence — in technique, tone and in elevating the bass to a featured instrument — is felt throughout rock music; his stage setup of Alembic and Status basses and towering amplifier stacks set standards for decades.
