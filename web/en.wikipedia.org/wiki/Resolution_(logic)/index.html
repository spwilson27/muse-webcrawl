Resolution (logic)

In mathematical logic and automated theorem proving, resolution is a rule of inference leading to a refutation-complete method for sentences in propositional logic and first-order logic. The resolution rule takes two clauses containing complementary literals and produces a new clause containing all the literals of both except the complementary pair.

The resolution rule

In propositional logic, from clauses (a ∨ p) and (¬p ∨ b) one may infer the resolvent (a ∨ b). A proof of unsatisfiability proceeds by repeatedly applying resolution (together with factoring) until the empty clause is derived. In first-order logic the rule is combined with unification: literals may be resolved when they can be made complementary by a substitution, using the most general unifier.

History and applications

The method in its modern form was introduced by John Alan Robinson in a landmark 1965 paper, building on earlier work by Martin Davis and Hilary Putnam. Resolution greatly simplified automated theorem proving by replacing the many rules of earlier systems with a single rule, and it became the basis of the logic programming language Prolog (via SLD resolution) and of many SAT solvers and theorem provers. Refinements such as set-of-support, ordered resolution and binary resolution with factoring control the otherwise explosive search space.
