Tryptophan (symbol Trp or W) is an α-amino acid used in the biosynthesis of proteins. It contains an indole side chain, making it the largest and one of the rarest of the standard amino acids in proteins, and it is essential in humans: it cannot be synthesised by the body and must come from the diet. Tryptophan is famous as the metabolic precursor of the neurotransmitters serotonin and melatonin and of the vitamin niacin.

Function and metabolism

Beyond protein synthesis, most dietary tryptophan is metabolised along the kynurenine pathway in the liver, yielding NAD+ and intermediates implicated in immune regulation. A small fraction is converted by tryptophan hydroxylase to 5-HTP and then serotonin (5-HT), which in the pineal gland is further converted to melatonin, the hormone regulating sleep–wake cycles. Because tryptophan competes with other large neutral amino acids to cross the blood–brain barrier, its availability influences brain serotonin synthesis.

Dietary sources and supplements

Rich sources include milk, eggs, cheese, meat, soy products, seeds, and bananas. The popular claim that turkey makes people sleepy via tryptophan is largely a myth—turkey contains amounts comparable to other meats, and post-meal drowsiness relates more to meal size and carbohydrates. L-tryptophan and 5-HTP are sold as supplements for sleep and mood. In 1989 an outbreak of eosinophilia–myalgia syndrome was traced to a contaminant in L-tryptophan produced by a single manufacturer (Showa Denko), leading to temporary bans; purified products later returned to market.

Chemistry

Tryptophan's indole ring absorbs ultraviolet light (peak ~280 nm), which is why protein concentration is often measured by absorbance at 280 nm, and it shows strong fluorescence used in protein-folding studies. It was first isolated from casein digestion by Frederick Gowland Hopkins and Sydney Cole in 1901.
