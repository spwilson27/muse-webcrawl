Pompe disease

Pompe disease
Also called: Glycogen storage disease type II
Cause: Deficiency of acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA)
Inheritance: Autosomal recessive
Named after: Joannes Cassianus Pompe

Pompe disease is a rare autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by a deficiency of the lysosomal enzyme acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA), which normally breaks down glycogen. It is classified as glycogen storage disease type II and as a lysosomal storage disease. Without functional enzyme, glycogen accumulates in lysosomes, especially in cardiac and skeletal muscle. The disease takes its name from the Dutch pathologist Joannes Cassianus Pompe, who described it in 1932.

Classification and symptoms

Infantile-onset Pompe disease presents in the first months of life with profound muscle weakness ("floppy infant"), poor feeding, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; untreated, it is usually fatal by 1–2 years of age from cardiorespiratory failure. Late-onset Pompe disease (childhood through adulthood) spares the heart to a large degree but causes progressive proximal muscle weakness and respiratory insufficiency, particularly involving the diaphragm.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is confirmed by enzyme assay (e.g., on dried blood spot) and genetic testing of the GAA gene. Pompe disease is included on many newborn screening panels. Enzyme replacement therapy with recombinant human GAA (alglucosidase alfa, marketed as Myozyme/Lumizyme, and the next-generation avalglucosidase alfa, Nexviazyme) has transformed the prognosis for infants and slows progression in late-onset patients. A two-component therapy combining cipaglucosidase alfa with the stabilizer miglustat (Pombiliti + Opfolda) was approved in 2023 for certain late-onset adults. Supportive care includes respiratory support and physical therapy.
