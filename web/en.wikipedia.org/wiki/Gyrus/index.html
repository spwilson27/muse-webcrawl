Latingyrus (pl. gyri)

In neuroanatomy, a gyrus (plural gyri) is a ridge on the cerebral cortex. It is generally surrounded by one or more sulci (depressions or furrows; sg. sulcus). Gyri and sulci create the folded appearance of the brain in humans and other mammals. The folding, called gyrification, greatly increases the surface area of the cortex — roughly two-thirds of the human cortical surface is hidden within sulci — allowing a large sheet of neural tissue to fit inside the skull.

Structure and development

Each gyrus consists of grey matter — the cell bodies and dendrites of cortical neurons — arranged in layers, overlying a core of white matter made of myelinated axons. The pattern of major gyri and sulci is broadly consistent across individuals and is used by anatomists to divide the cortex into lobes and named regions, although the finer folding pattern varies from person to person, much like a fingerprint.

Gyrification takes place during fetal development, accelerating in the third trimester as the cortex grows faster than the space available within the skull. The mechanisms remain debated: mechanical models attribute folding to differential growth of the cortical sheet relative to the underlying white matter, producing buckling, while older theories emphasised axonal tension pulling strongly connected regions together. Abnormal gyrification is associated with a number of neurological conditions, including lissencephaly ("smooth brain"), in which gyri fail to form, and polymicrogyria, in which there are too many abnormally small folds.

Notable gyri

  • Precentral gyrus — the primary motor cortex, immediately in front of the central sulcus.
  • Postcentral gyrus — the primary somatosensory cortex, behind the central sulcus.
  • Superior, middle and inferior frontal gyri — subdivisions of the frontal lobe.
  • Superior and middle temporal gyri, including Heschl's gyri (the primary auditory cortex).
  • Fusiform gyrus — on the ventral surface of the temporal and occipital lobes, involved in high-level vision, including face and word recognition.
  • Parahippocampal gyrus — surrounding the hippocampus, part of the memory system.
  • Cingulate gyrus — an arching gyrus above the corpus callosum, part of the limbic system.
  • Angular gyrus and supramarginal gyrus — around the end of the lateral sulcus, involved in language and number processing.

Clinical significance

Because functions are mapped onto specific gyri, lesions produce characteristic deficits: damage to the posterior inferior frontal gyrus (Broca's area) impairs speech production, while tumours or strokes affecting the precentral gyrus cause contralateral weakness. Cortical stimulation and recording during neurosurgery are charted in terms of gyral anatomy, and modern neuroimaging reports activation peaks by gyrus using standard atlases.
