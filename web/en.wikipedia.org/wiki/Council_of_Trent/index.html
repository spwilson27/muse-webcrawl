The Council of Trent was the 19th ecumenical council of the Catholic Church, held in three periods between 1545 and 1563 in Trent (now Trento, Italy) and briefly in Bologna. Prompted by the Protestant Reformation, it is considered the central council of the Catholic Counter-Reformation, clarifying Catholic doctrine and enacting wide-ranging disciplinary reforms.

A session of the Council of Trent.

Background

Calls for a general council to answer Martin Luther's challenge began in the 1520s, but political conflict between Pope Paul III, the Emperor Charles V, and France delayed it for two decades. The council finally opened on 13 December 1545 with only a few dozen bishops present; attendance grew in later periods, reaching over 200 prelates at the close.

Doctrinal decrees

The council affirmed, against Protestant teachings: that Scripture and unwritten tradition are both sources of revelation, with the Latin Vulgate authoritative; that justification is by faith formed by charity, not by faith alone; that all seven sacraments were instituted by Christ; the doctrine of transubstantiation in the Eucharist and the sacrificial character of the Mass; the existence of purgatory; and the legitimacy of venerating saints, relics, and images and of indulgences (while condemning their sale and abuse).

Reforms

Disciplinary decrees required bishops to reside in their dioceses, restricted pluralism (holding multiple benefices), and ordered seminaries to be established in every diocese for the education of clergy — a lasting institutional legacy. The council also produced a standard catechism (the Roman Catechism, 1566), a revised breviary and missal, and an Index of Prohibited Books.

Legacy

Trent fixed the doctrinal boundaries of Catholicism for four centuries; its decrees were not substantially revisited until the Second Vatican Council (1962–65). The "Tridentine" Mass took its name from the council.
