Anattā

Contemplation of anattā — non-self — is a core insight practice in Buddhism.

Anattā (Pali; Sanskrit: Anātman) is the Buddhist doctrine of "non-self": the teaching that no unchanging, permanent self or essence can be found in any phenomenon. Along with dukkha (suffering/unsatisfactoriness) and anicca (impermanence), anattā is one of the three marks of existence. The doctrine contrasts with the Hindu concept of ātman, an eternal soul or self, and is considered one of Buddhism's most distinctive teachings.

Doctrine

In the early discourses, the Buddha analyses the person into five aggregates (skandhas) — form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness — and shows that none of them, nor any combination, can be regarded as a self: each is impermanent and not fully subject to one's control, so "this is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self." The Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta ("Discourse on the Characteristic of Non-Self") presents this argument to the Buddha's first five disciples. Importantly, the Buddha declined to answer the question of whether a self exists or does not exist at all, treating both eternalism and annihilationism as extremes; anattā is primarily a strategy for releasing attachment rather than a metaphysical denial directed at a person.

Later developments

Abhidharma traditions systematised anattā through the analysis of dharmas. Mahāyāna philosophy, especially Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka, extended the emptiness (śūnyatā) of the self to all phenomena. The Mahāyāna Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra famously speaks of a "True Self" identified with Buddha-nature, a teaching interpreted in different ways by later schools. The doctrine also shapes the theory of rebirth without a transmigrating soul: continuity between lives is causal, like one candle lighting another.
