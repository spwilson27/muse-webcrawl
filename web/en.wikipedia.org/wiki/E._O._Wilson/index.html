Edward Osborne Wilson (10 June 1929 – 26 December 2021) was an American biologist, naturalist, ecologist and entomologist known for developing the field of sociobiology. A specialist in myrmecology, the study of ants, he was called "the father of sociobiology" and "the father of biodiversity", and was regarded as one of the world's leading authorities on ants.

Career

Wilson taught at Harvard University for most of his career. With Robert MacArthur he developed the theory of island biogeography (1967), foundational in ecology and conservation biology. His book The Insect Societies (1971) was a definitive synthesis, and Sociobiology: The New Synthesis (1975) provoked intense controversy by extending evolutionary explanations of social behaviour to humans. He later wrote On Human Nature (1978) and The Ants (1990, with Bert Hölldobler), each winning the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction, and The Diversity of Life (1992), which popularised the term biodiversity.

Later work and advocacy

In his later decades Wilson became a prominent conservation advocate, founding the E. O. Wilson Biodiversity Foundation and proposing in Half-Earth (2016) that half of the planet's surface be reserved for nature. His concept of "biophilia" (1984) hypothesised an innate human affinity for living things. He also initiated the Encyclopedia of Life project.
