Kernel density estimation

In statistics, kernel density estimation (KDE) is the application of kernel smoothing for probability density estimation, i.e., a non-parametric method to estimate the probability density function of a random variable based on kernels as weights. KDE answers a fundamental data smoothing problem in which inferences about a population are made from a finite data sample.

Definition

Let x1, x2, …, xn be independent and identically distributed samples drawn from some univariate distribution with unknown density ƒ. The kernel density estimate at a point x is:

ƒ̂h(x) = (1 / nh) Σ K((x − xi) / h)

where K is the kernel — a non-negative function, typically a symmetric probability density such as the Gaussian — and h > 0 is a smoothing parameter called the bandwidth. A kernel with subscript h is called the scaled kernel. Intuitively, each data point contributes a smooth "bump" to the estimate, and the total area under the estimated density equals one.

Bandwidth selection

The bandwidth controls the trade-off between bias and variance: too small a bandwidth produces a spiky, overfit estimate, while too large a bandwidth oversmooths and obscures structure. Common selection methods include Silverman's rule of thumb, least-squares cross-validation, and plug-in methods. Popular kernels include the Gaussian, Epanechnikov (which is optimal in a mean-square-error sense), uniform, triangular, and cosine kernels.

Applications

KDE is widely used for data visualisation as a smoothed alternative to histograms, for exploratory data analysis, and as an ingredient in classification and clustering methods. It was introduced in the 1950s and 1960s through work by Emanuel Parzen and Murray Rosenblatt, and is sometimes called the Parzen–Rosenblatt window method. Multivariate generalisations exist but suffer from the curse of dimensionality.
