Puerto Ricans

Puerto Ricans (Spanish: puertorriqueños) are the people of Puerto Rico, an unincorporated territory of the United States in the Caribbean, and their diaspora. About 3.2 million people live on the archipelago, while some 5.9 million people of Puerto Rican origin live in the fifty states — more than on the island itself.

Origins and ancestry

Puerto Rican identity formed from the mixture of Indigenous Taíno inhabitants, Spanish colonists (including many Canary Islanders), enslaved and free Africans, and later Corsican, French, German, Irish, Chinese, Lebanese and other immigrants. Genetic studies find average ancestry of roughly two-thirds European, with substantial West African and Indigenous American components. The island was a Spanish colony from 1493 until 1898, when it was ceded to the United States after the Spanish–American War; the Jones–Shafroth Act of 1917 granted Puerto Ricans US citizenship.

Diaspora

Large-scale migration to the mainland, especially New York City, followed the Second World War, driven by industrial recruitment and cheap air travel; stateside Puerto Ricans (often called Nuyoricans in New York) built major cultural and political communities in New York, Florida, Pennsylvania, Illinois and Massachusetts. After Hurricane Maria in 2017, Florida's Puerto Rican population grew sharply; Orlando and other Florida metros now rival traditional centres.

Culture

Puerto Rican culture blends Spanish, African and Taíno elements: Spanish (with a distinctive island dialect) and English are both official. Music ranges from bomba and plena to salsa and reggaetón; cuisine centres on dishes such as mofongo, arroz con gandules and lechón. The island's political status — statehood, independence or the current commonwealth arrangement — remains the central question of its politics, debated in a series of non-binding referendums.
