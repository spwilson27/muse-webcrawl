Proto-city

A proto-city is a large, dense Neolithic settlement that displays some characteristics of a city but lacks others, such as a system of writing, a centralised temple or palace administration, or a clear class structure with a ruling elite. The term is used by archaeologists for settlements that were far larger than ordinary villages yet had not crossed the threshold into true urbanism.

Characteristics

Proto-cities typically arose after the development of agriculture allowed food surpluses. They often feature thousands of inhabitants, densely packed housing, craft specialisation, and communal or ceremonial buildings, but lack clear evidence of central government, monumental palaces, standing armies or written administration. Some archaeologists prefer the term "large village" or use the German word Tell for such sites, and the proto-city label remains debated.

Examples

  • Çatalhöyük (c. 7500–5700 BC) in Anatolia — thousands of people in honeycomb-like houses entered through the roof, with elaborate wall paintings and burials beneath floors, but no streets, temples or palaces distinguishable from houses.
  • Jericho (from c. 9000 BC) — among the oldest continuously inhabited settlements, with a massive stone tower and wall.
  • 'Ain Ghazal in Jordan — a large Neolithic settlement with plaster statues.
  • Tell Brak and Hamoukar in Mesopotamia — early large settlements that later became true cities.
  • The Cucuteni–Trypillia mega-sites of Ukraine (c. 4000 BC), such as Talianki, which may have held over 10,000 inhabitants in planned concentric layouts yet show little social stratification.

Significance

Proto-cities challenge simple models of urban origins, such as V. Gordon Childe's "urban revolution" checklist. Sites like Çatalhöyük and the Trypillia mega-settlements suggest that large, dense, sedentary communities could persist for centuries in a relatively egalitarian fashion without developing into states, showing that urbanisation was not an inevitable consequence of population growth.
