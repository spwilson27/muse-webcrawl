Corporate tax in the United States

Corporations in the United States are subject to income tax at the federal level and in most states. Since 2018, the federal corporate income tax has been levied at a flat rate of 21 percent on taxable income, replacing the earlier graduated rate schedule that topped out at 35 percent. The tax is governed primarily by Subchapter C of the Internal Revenue Code.

History

A federal tax on corporate income began as an excise tax in 1909, held constitutional as a tax on the privilege of doing business in corporate form. After ratification of the Sixteenth Amendment in 1913, a straightforward corporate income tax was imposed, initially at 1 percent. Rates rose sharply during the two World Wars and remained high through the mid-20th century; corporate tax supplied roughly a third of federal revenue in the early 1950s, a share that has since declined to around a tenth as payroll and individual income taxes grew.

Structure

Taxable income is gross income minus deductions for ordinary and necessary business expenses, depreciation, and interest. Dividends received from other corporations receive a partial deduction. Shareholders are taxed again on dividends and capital gains, producing the classic “double taxation” of C corporations, whereas S corporations and partnerships generally pass income through to owners. An alternative minimum tax on very large corporations (a 15 percent book minimum tax) was enacted in the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022.

International aspects

Before 2018 the United States taxed the worldwide income of its corporations, with deferral for unrepatriated foreign profits. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act moved toward a territorial system, imposing a one-time transition tax on accumulated foreign earnings and introducing regimes such as GILTI (a minimum tax on global intangible low-taxed income) and BEAT (base erosion and anti-abuse tax). Multinationals' use of transfer pricing and tax havens has been a persistent policy concern, and the United States participated in negotiations toward a 15 percent global minimum corporate tax under the OECD framework.

State taxes

Most states levy their own corporate income taxes, with rates varying widely, and some cities, notably New York City, impose additional taxes. A few states instead use gross receipts taxes.
