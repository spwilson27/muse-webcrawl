Lever action

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A lever action is a type of action for repeating firearms in which a manually operated cocking handle, located beneath the receiver, is worked in a downward-and-upward arc. Operating the lever ejects the spent cartridge case, cocks the hammer, and feeds a fresh round from a magazine—usually a tube beneath the barrel—into the chamber. Lever-action rifles became icons of the American frontier and remain popular for hunting and sport shooting.

History

Early lever-action designs appeared in the 1840s and 1850s, including the Volcanic repeating arms. Oliver Winchester's company refined the concept in the Henry rifle of 1860, which saw service in the American Civil War. Winchester's Model 1866 and especially the Model 1873—marketed as "the gun that won the West"—made the design famous. Later designs by John Browning, including the Winchester Model 1894, could handle more powerful smokeless-powder cartridges. John Marlin's competing Marlin Firearms designs and the Savage Model 99, with its rotary magazine and ability to use pointed bullets, were the other major branches of the design.

Characteristics

Traditional lever actions use tubular magazines, which require flat- or round-nosed bullets to avoid a pointed bullet tip detonating the primer of the cartridge ahead of it under recoil; some modern designs use detachable box magazines instead. The action is generally regarded as offering a fast rate of fire for a manually operated rifle and an easy one-hand-carrying balance, though it is usually less inherently accurate and less suited to very high-pressure cartridges than a bolt action.

Use and popularity

Lever-action rifles in calibres such as .30-30 Winchester and .45-70 Government are classic deer-hunting arms in North America. Lever-action shotguns and rimfire rifles are also made, and the type is a staple of cowboy action shooting competitions and of Western films and television.
