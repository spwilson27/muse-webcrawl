Thoracentesis

Other namesThoracocentesis, pleural tap, needle thoracostomy (in emergency contexts)
FieldPulmonology, interventional radiology, emergency medicine
PurposeRemoval of fluid or air from the pleural space

Thoracentesis (/ˌθɔːrəsɛnˈtiːsɪs/), also known as thoracocentesis, pleural tap, needle thoracostomy, or needle decompression, is an invasive medical procedure to remove fluid or air from the pleural space for diagnostic and/or therapeutic purposes. A cannula, or hollow needle, is carefully introduced into the thorax — generally after administration of local anaesthesia. The recommended location varies by source, but is most commonly the mid-axillary line, in the seventh, eighth or ninth intercostal space. Whenever possible, the procedure should be performed under ultrasound guidance, which substantially reduces complications.

Indications

Thoracentesis is performed to determine the cause of a pleural effusion (fluid accumulated between the lung and chest wall) and to relieve breathlessness caused by a large effusion. Fluid is sent for analysis — protein and lactate dehydrogenase levels (to apply Light's criteria distinguishing exudates from transudates), cell counts, cytology, Gram stain and culture, and pH. Emergency needle decompression is also the first-line field treatment for a tension pneumothorax, typically performed in the second intercostal space in the midclavicular line, followed by insertion of a chest tube.

Procedure

The patient usually sits upright, leaning forward over a table. After locating the effusion by examination and ultrasound, the skin is cleaned and local anaesthetic infiltrated. The needle or catheter is advanced just above the upper border of the rib below the selected space — avoiding the neurovascular bundle that runs along the rib's lower edge — until fluid is aspirated. For therapeutic drainage, fluid is drawn off slowly; removing very large volumes quickly (generally more than 1–1.5 litres) is avoided because it may precipitate re-expansion pulmonary oedema.

Complications

The most important complication is pneumothorax (air entering the pleural space and collapsing the lung), historically reported in up to 20–30% of unguided procedures but markedly reduced by ultrasound guidance. Others include bleeding (haemothorax), infection, pain, coughing, low blood pressure, and injury to the liver or spleen if the needle is placed too low.

Related procedures

Thoracentesis differs from tube thoracostomy (chest drain insertion), which leaves a drain in place for continuing drainage, and from pleurodesis, in which an irritant is instilled to obliterate the pleural space and prevent recurrent effusions.
