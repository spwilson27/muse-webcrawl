Sewage treatment

Sewage treatment (or domestic wastewater treatment, municipal wastewater treatment) is a type of wastewater treatment which aims to remove contaminants from sewage to produce an effluent that is suitable to discharge to the surrounding environment or an intended reuse application, thereby preventing water pollution from raw sewage discharges. Treatment is normally carried out in a sewage treatment plant using a sequence of physical, biological, and chemical processes.

Process overview

Preliminary and primary treatment

Incoming sewage passes through screens and grit chambers that remove rags, plastics, sand, and gravel. In primary sedimentation tanks, settleable solids sink as sludge and oils and grease float for skimming, removing roughly half of the suspended solids and a third of the BOD.

Secondary treatment

Biological processes — most commonly the activated sludge process, trickling filters, or rotating biological contactors — use microorganisms to consume dissolved and colloidal organic matter. In aeration basins, bacteria flocculate and are then separated in secondary clarifiers, with part of the biomass recycled. Secondary treatment typically removes 85% or more of BOD and suspended solids.

Tertiary treatment and disinfection

Where effluent standards require it, tertiary steps remove nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus), filter the water, and disinfect it by chlorination, ultraviolet light, or ozonation to inactivate pathogens before discharge or reuse.

Sludge treatment

Sludge from primary and secondary treatment is thickened, stabilised (often by anaerobic digestion, which yields biogas), dewatered, and then disposed of or reused as biosolids.

History

Systematic sewage treatment developed in the later 19th century as cities built sewer networks — London's system, engineered by Joseph Bazalgette after the Great Stink of 1858, being the most famous — and shifted from merely conveying waste to treating it, spurred by germ theory and waterborne-disease control. The activated sludge process was developed in England in 1913–1914 by Edward Ardern and W. T. Lockett.
