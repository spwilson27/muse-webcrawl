Semiconductor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A silicon wafer gleaming with rainbow reflections in a cleanroom]

A semiconductor is a material whose electrical conductivity lies between a conductor and an insulator and can be precisely controlled — most importantly silicon, the basis of nearly all modern electronics. Doping silicon with traces of other elements creates n-type (electron-rich) and p-type (hole-rich) regions; their junctions make diodes and transistors.

The semiconductor industry grew from the 1947 transistor at Bell Labs through the integrated circuit (1958–59) to today's fabrication plants, among the most expensive factories ever built, etching features a few nanometres wide.

Materials

Germanium served the first transistors before silicon's abundance and stable oxide won out. Compound semiconductors (gallium arsenide, gallium nitride, silicon carbide) dominate where silicon is weak: radio-frequency amplifiers, LEDs and power electronics.

Band theory — electrons occupying energy bands separated by a gap — is the quantum-mechanical picture explaining the behaviour, and the gap's size is the material's defining constant.
