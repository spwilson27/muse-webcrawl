Vasodilation

Vasodilation is the widening of blood vessels. It results from relaxation of smooth muscle cells within the vessel walls, in particular in the large veins, large arteries, and smaller arterioles. The process is the opposite of vasoconstriction, which is the narrowing of blood vessels.

When blood vessels dilate, the flow of blood is increased due to a decrease in vascular resistance and an increase in cardiac output. Therefore, dilation of arterial blood vessels (mainly the arterioles) decreases blood pressure.

Function

Vasodilation increases blood flow to tissues that need it most, for example during exercise, inflammation, or in response to local metabolic demand. In thermoregulation, dilation of cutaneous vessels promotes heat loss from the body surface, whereas vasoconstriction conserves heat.

Mechanism

Smooth muscle relaxation in vessel walls can be produced by several mechanisms, including:

  • Release of nitric oxide from endothelial cells, which raises cyclic GMP in smooth muscle cells.
  • Beta-2 adrenergic stimulation by adrenaline.
  • Decreased sympathetic nervous system activity.
  • Local factors such as increased carbon dioxide, adenosine, potassium ions, and decreased pH.
  • Substances such as histamine, prostaglandins, and bradykinin released during inflammation.

Vasodilator drugs

Drugs that cause vasodilation are called vasodilators and are used to treat hypertension, angina, and heart failure. Examples include nitrates such as nitroglycerin, calcium channel blockers such as amlodipine, ACE inhibitors, hydralazine, and minoxidil. A common side effect is flushing and headache.
