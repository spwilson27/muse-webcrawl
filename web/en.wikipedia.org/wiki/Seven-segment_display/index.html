Seven-segment display

A seven-segment display is a form of electronic display device for displaying decimal numerals that is an alternative to the more complex dot matrix displays. It consists of seven segments, conventionally labelled a through g, arranged in a figure-eight pattern; by illuminating selected combinations of segments, the digits 0–9 and some letters can be formed.

History

The concept dates to a 1903 patent by Frank W. Wood, but practical devices arrived with incandescent and gas-discharge technology in the mid-twentieth century. The widespread adoption of light-emitting diodes in the 1970s made seven-segment displays cheap and ubiquitous in calculators, digital clocks, meters and appliances; liquid-crystal versions followed for low-power devices.

Operation

Each segment is an individual LED (or LCD element), wired either in common-anode or common-cathode configuration. Decoder/driver integrated circuits such as the 74LS47 translate binary-coded decimal input into the segment patterns. Digits are often multiplexed: several digits share segment lines and are lit in rapid sequence so that persistence of vision merges them into a steady display. An eighth element, a decimal point, is commonly added.

Character set

Beyond the ten digits, hexadecimal letters A, b, C, c, d, E and F are standard, and a limited set of other symbols (such as a minus sign) can be represented, though some letters are ambiguous or impossible.
