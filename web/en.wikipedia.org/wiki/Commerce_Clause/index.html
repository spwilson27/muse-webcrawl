Commerce Clause

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Commerce Clause describes an enumerated power in the United States Constitution (Article I, Section 8, Clause 3). The clause states that the United States Congress shall have power "[t]o regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, and among the several States, and with the Indian Tribes." It is the foundation of a large share of federal regulatory authority in the United States, and its interpretation has been one of the most consequential and contested questions in American constitutional law.

Scope of the power

Since the New Deal era, the Supreme Court has recognised three categories of activity Congress may regulate under the clause: (1) the channels of interstate commerce; (2) the instrumentalities of interstate commerce, and persons or things in it; and (3) activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. The third category — elaborated in cases such as NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp. (1937) and Wickard v. Filburn (1942), where even a farmer's home-grown wheat was held reachable because such production in the aggregate affected the national market — long supported a very broad federal power, underpinning statutes from the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (upheld in Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States) to federal criminal and environmental law.

Limits

Beginning in the 1990s, the Rehnquist Court for the first time in nearly sixty years invalidated federal statutes as exceeding the commerce power: in United States v. Lopez (1995) it struck down the Gun-Free School Zones Act, and in United States v. Morrison (2000) parts of the Violence Against Women Act, emphasising that the regulated activity must be economic in nature. In NFIB v. Sebelius (2012), five justices concluded that the individual health-insurance mandate could not be sustained under the Commerce Clause because it compelled individuals to enter commerce rather than regulating existing activity — though the mandate's penalty was upheld as a tax.

The dormant Commerce Clause

Courts have also inferred from the clause a "dormant" or "negative" dimension: even where Congress has not acted, states may not discriminate against or unduly burden interstate commerce. State laws that facially discriminate against out-of-state goods are virtually per se invalid, while even-handed laws are balanced under the test of Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc. (1970). The doctrine polices economic protectionism among the states, one of the central defects of the Articles of Confederation that the Constitution was written to cure.

Original meaning debates

Scholars dispute the original scope of "commerce" — some arguing it meant chiefly trade and exchange rather than all gainful activity — and of "among the several States". Chief Justice John Marshall's opinion in Gibbons v. Ogden (1824), the foundational case, defined commerce broadly as "intercourse" and the federal power over it as plenary within its sphere, a reading that has framed debate ever since.
