Thoracic diaphragm

SystemMusculoskeletal / respiratory
LocationFloor of the thoracic cavity
NervePhrenic nerve (C3, C4, C5)
ActionsInspiration (principal muscle of breathing)

The thoracic diaphragm, or simply the diaphragm, is a sheet of internal skeletal muscle in humans and other mammals that extends across the bottom of the thoracic cavity. The diaphragm is the most important muscle of respiration, and separates the thoracic cavity (containing the heart and lungs) from the abdominal cavity; it performs an important function in breathing: as the diaphragm contracts, the volume of the thoracic cavity increases, creating a negative pressure that draws air into the lungs, and as it relaxes, air is expelled.

Structure

The diaphragm is a double-domed sheet of muscle and fibrous tissue. Its peripheral muscular part arises from the lower sternum (xiphoid process), the lower six costal cartilages and ribs, and the lumbar vertebrae by way of two tendinous pillars, the right and left crura, and the medial and lateral arcuate ligaments. The fibres converge on a trefoil-shaped aponeurosis, the central tendon, which has no bony attachment. The right dome typically reaches as high as the fifth intercostal space, the left slightly lower, and the central tendon sits at about the level of the eighth thoracic vertebra.

Openings

Three major structures pass between the thorax and abdomen through openings in the diaphragm: the inferior vena cava, through the caval opening at T8; the oesophagus and vagus nerves, through the oesophageal hiatus at T10; and the aorta, thoracic duct and azygos vein, passing behind the diaphragm at T12 — a sequence often remembered by the mnemonic "I 8 10 eggs at 12" (IVC at T8, oesophagus at T10, aorta at T12).

Nerve supply and function

Motor supply is from the right and left phrenic nerves (cervical roots C3–C5 — "C3, 4, 5 keep the diaphragm alive"). Contraction flattens the domes and drives inspiration; in quiet breathing the diaphragm descends 1–2 cm, and up to 6–10 cm in deep breathing. It also assists in raising intra-abdominal pressure for coughing, vomiting, defecation and childbirth. The hiccup is an involuntary spasm of the diaphragm.

Clinical significance

The diaphragm may be weakened or pierced in hiatal hernia, in which part of the stomach slides into the thorax, and in congenital diaphragmatic hernia. Paralysis of one half (hemidiaphragm) follows phrenic nerve injury, and eventration describes abnormal elevation of the diaphragm. Diaphragmatic breathing is taught in physiotherapy, singing and meditation.
