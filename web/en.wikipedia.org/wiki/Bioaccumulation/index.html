Bioaccumulation

Bioaccumulation is the gradual accumulation of substances, such as pesticides, heavy metals or other persistent chemicals, in an organism. It occurs when an organism absorbs a substance faster than it can be lost by metabolism or excretion, so that concentrations in tissues rise over the organism's lifetime.

Mechanism

Bioaccumulation is favoured by substances that are lipophilic (fat-soluble) and resistant to degradation, because they are stored in fatty tissue rather than excreted in urine. The rate of accumulation depends on uptake from water, food and sediment, the organism's feeding rate, and its capacity to metabolise or eliminate the compound. The ratio of concentration in the organism to that in the surrounding water at steady state is expressed as a bioconcentration factor.

Biomagnification

Bioaccumulation within a single organism is distinguished from biomagnification, the increase in concentration across trophic levels of a food chain. Classic examples include DDT concentrating in birds of prey, where it caused eggshell thinning publicised by Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962), and methylmercury accumulating in predatory fish and poisoning fishing communities at Minamata, Japan.

Notable substances

Persistent organic pollutants such as PCBs, dioxins and organochlorine pesticides, together with metals including mercury, cadmium and lead, are of greatest concern. Regulators screen new chemicals for persistence, bioaccumulation and toxicity (PBT criteria), and international controls are set out in the Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants.

Monitoring

Because tissue residues integrate exposure over time, sentinel organisms such as mussels and oysters are used in "Mussel Watch" monitoring programmes to track coastal contamination.
