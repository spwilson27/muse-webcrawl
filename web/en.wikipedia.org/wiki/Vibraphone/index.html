Vibraphone

A modern vibraphone.

The vibraphone (also called the vibraharp) is a percussion instrument in the metallophone family. It consists of tuned metal bars, typically aluminium, arranged like a piano keyboard, with motor-driven rotating discs inside resonator tubes beneath each bar. The discs alternately open and close the resonators, producing a pulsing vibrato effect from which the instrument takes its name. A foot-operated damper pedal sustains or cuts off the sound.

History

The vibraphone was developed in the United States in the early 1920s; Leedy Manufacturing produced the first instruments under the name "vibraphone" in 1921, and the Deagan company refined the design with the pedal damper and electric motor. It was quickly adopted by dance bands and then by jazz musicians, notably Lionel Hampton, who popularised it as a solo instrument, and Milt Jackson of the Modern Jazz Quartet.

Technique and use

Players strike the bars with mallets, commonly two, but advanced technique uses four mallets, allowing chords and independent melodic lines. The standard range is three octaves, F3 to F6. The vibraphone is a staple of jazz and appears widely in orchestral, chamber, and contemporary music; composers such as Darius Milhaud and Olivier Messiaen wrote prominent parts for it. In jazz, notable exponents include Red Norvo, Gary Burton (a pioneer of the four-mallet grip), Bobby Hutcherson, and Stefon Harris.
