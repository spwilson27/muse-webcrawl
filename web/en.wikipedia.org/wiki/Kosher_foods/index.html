Kosher foods

Jewish lawKashrut
Key sourcesTorah, Talmud, Shulchan Aruch
SupervisionMashgiach; kosher certification agencies

Kosher foods are foods that conform to the Jewish dietary regulations known as kashrut. Food that does not accord with Jewish law is called treif. The laws derive from commands in the Torah as interpreted by rabbinic tradition in the Talmud and later codes such as the Shulchan Aruch.

Basic rules

Permitted land animals must chew the cud and have fully cloven hooves; of these, only animals slaughtered by ritual slaughter (shechita) may be eaten, and certain parts are prohibited. Sea creatures must have both fins and scales, so shellfish and many other sea animals are forbidden. Most birds of prey are prohibited, while common domesticated poultry are permitted. Meat and dairy products must not be cooked, served, or eaten together, and separate utensils, dishes, and waiting periods are observed. Blood must be removed from meat by salting and soaking or by broiling. Fruits, vegetables, and grains are inherently kosher (pareve, neutral) but must be checked for insects, and grape products require supervision because of their role in religious ritual.

Passover rules

During Passover, additional restrictions apply: leavened products (chametz) are prohibited, and Ashkenazi tradition also avoids kitniyot such as rice, corn, and legumes. Such items are certified "Kosher for Passover" under special supervision.

Certification

In modern food production, kosher status is verified by a supervisor (mashgiach) and indicated by a symbol called a hechsher, issued by certification agencies such as the Orthodox Union (OU), OK, Star-K, and Kof-K. A substantial share of prepackaged foods in the United States carry kosher certification, and kosher food is purchased by many non-Jewish consumers for reasons of perceived quality, as well as by some vegetarians, Muslims, and people with certain allergies—though kosher certification is not a guarantee of allergen status.
