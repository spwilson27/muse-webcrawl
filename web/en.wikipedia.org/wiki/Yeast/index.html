Yeast

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A block of fresh baker's yeast and active dry yeast granules]

Yeasts are eukaryotic, single-celled microorganisms classified as members of the fungus kingdom. The species most important to food is Saccharomyces cerevisiae, used for millennia to leaven bread and ferment beer and wine: it converts sugars into carbon dioxide and ethanol, the gas raising dough and the alcohol defining the drinks. Other yeasts ferment cider, kefir and soy products, and yeast extracts are eaten as foods and flavourings in their own right.

In bread making

Baker's yeast is sold as fresh (compressed) cakes, active dry yeast, and instant yeast. In dough, yeast ferments sugars from the flour, inflating a network of gluten with gas bubbles; the yeast is killed during baking, leaving the set crumb. Wild yeasts (with lactobacilli) drive sourdough starters. Louis Pasteur's work in the 1850s–60s proved fermentation was caused by living yeast, founding modern microbiology and the pure-culture yeast industry.

In brewing and winemaking

Ale yeasts (S. cerevisiae) ferment warm and rise to the top; lager yeasts (S. pastorianus) ferment cold at the bottom. Winemakers may rely on ambient wild yeasts or inoculate selected strains to control flavour. Spent brewer's yeast is processed into spreads such as Marmite and Vegemite.

As food

Nutritional yeast — deactivated S. cerevisiae flakes — is a savoury, cheese-flavoured seasoning popular in vegan cooking and a source of B vitamins. Yeast is also a model organism of biology, the first eukaryote to have its genome fully sequenced (1996).
