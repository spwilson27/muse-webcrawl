New-York Tribune

The New-York Tribune was an American newspaper founded in 1841 by editor Horace Greeley. It bore the hyphenated title until 1914. Long the dominant newspaper of the Whig Party and then the Republican Party, it was widely read nationally and was influential in shaping public opinion in the decades before and during the American Civil War.

History

Greeley founded the Tribune as a penny daily but built it into a serious journal of news, reform, and literature; its weekly edition reached hundreds of thousands of subscribers across the rural North. The paper employed notable contributors, including Charles Anderson Dana, George William Curtis, and—for a time—Karl Marx as a European correspondent. Greeley's editorials promoted abolition, temperance, labor reform, and westward expansion ("Go West, young man"). Greeley ran for president in 1872 and died shortly after his defeat.

Under his successor Whitelaw Reid, the Tribune remained a leading Republican voice. In 1924 it merged with the New York Herald to form the New York Herald Tribune, which continued until 1966.
