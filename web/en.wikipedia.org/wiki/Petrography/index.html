Petrography is the branch of petrology concerned with the detailed description and systematic classification of rocks, especially by microscopic examination of thin sections. Whereas petrology seeks to explain the origin and evolution of rocks, petrography provides the descriptive foundation: mineral composition, texture, grain size, fabric, and alteration.

Methods

The central technique is optical mineralogy: a slice of rock is ground to a standard thickness of about 30 micrometres, mounted on a glass slide, and examined in transmitted polarised light with a petrographic microscope. Under plane- and cross-polarised light, minerals display characteristic colours, birefringence, extinction behaviour, and twinning that allow identification and modal analysis (point counting). Petrographers also use hand-specimen description, X-ray diffraction, electron microprobe analysis, cathodoluminescence, and scanning electron microscopy.

History and applications

Thin-section petrography was pioneered in the mid-19th century by Henry Clifton Sorby, who showed that microscopic study could reveal a rock's history, and was developed by German and British schools into a cornerstone of geology. Today petrography is used in igneous, metamorphic, and sedimentary studies; in petroleum geology (reservoir sandstone analysis); in archaeology and art history (ceramic petrography and stone provenance); and in engineering and conservation, for example in assessing concrete deterioration.
