Dieu et mon droit (French pronunciation: [djø e mɔ̃ dʁwa], meaning "God and my right") is the motto of the monarch of the United Kingdom outside Scotland. It appears on a scroll beneath the shield of the version of the coat of arms of the United Kingdom used outside Scotland. The motto is French for "God and my right", referring to the monarch's divine right to govern.

Origin

The phrase is traditionally attributed to Richard I of England, who is said to have adopted it as a battle cry in 1198 during the Battle of Gisors against Philip II of France, signifying that Richard owed his kingship to God alone and not to any earthly overlord. The motto took on a sharper meaning in 1340, when Edward III of England claimed the throne of France: the "right" in question became the English monarch's asserted hereditary right to the French crown. From that time, the motto was used in connection with the English (later British) claim to France, and the royal arms quartered the arms of France with those of England until the claim was formally renounced in 1801.

Usage

Despite the abandonment of the French claim, the motto was retained and remains part of the royal arms used in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. In Scotland, the monarch uses the Scottish mottoes In Defens and Nemo me impune lacessit. Dieu et mon droit has also appeared on British passports, coinage, and official insignia, and it is the motto of several military units and institutions. The persistence of a Norman French motto reflects the historically francophone culture of the medieval English court.
