Kombucha

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A glass jar of kombucha with the pale SCOBY floating on top]

Kombucha is a fizzy, tangy fermented tea, brewed by a SCOBY — a symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast that forms a pale, floating mat — which converts sweetened tea partly into acetic and gluconic acids, trace alcohol and carbon dioxide. Its origins lie in northeast China and Manchuria some two thousand years ago by tradition, whence it travelled to Russia (as "tea mushroom", chayny grib) and, in the 1990s, to American health-food fame through brands such as GT's. A Japanese legend credits a Dr Kombu who carried the tea (cha) to an emperor — a story that gives the drink its name in Japan, where, confusingly, "kombucha" otherwise means kelp tea.

1. Brew and business

Brew and business

Home brewers keep SCOBY "hotels", debate second ferments with ginger or fruit, and occasionally produce something closer to vinegar or to beer. Commercial kombucha must stay under 0.5% alcohol to be sold as a soft drink in the US — a line the industry crossed publicly in 2010, when products were pulled for testing and reformulated, and a separate "hard kombucha" category now owns the stronger brews. The health claims — probiotics, antioxidants, detox — run well ahead of the clinical evidence, which remains thin; the drink's demonstrable virtues are flavour, ritual and a pleasant acidity that stands in for alcohol at the table.
