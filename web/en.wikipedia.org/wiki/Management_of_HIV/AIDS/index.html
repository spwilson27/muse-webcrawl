Management of HIV/AIDS

The management of HIV/AIDS normally includes the use of multiple antiretroviral drugs in combination, known as antiretroviral therapy (ART) or highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART). Since its introduction in the mid-1990s, combination therapy has transformed infection with the human immunodeficiency virus from a near-universally fatal condition into a chronic, manageable disease, with life expectancy approaching that of the general population where treatment is available and adherence is maintained.

Principles of treatment

Standard regimens combine three drugs from at least two classes — most commonly two nucleoside reverse-transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) as a "backbone" plus an integrase inhibitor (such as dolutegravir), a protease inhibitor or a non-nucleoside reverse-transcriptase inhibitor. The goals of therapy are maximal suppression of viral load to undetectable levels (below assay limits, typically 50 copies/mL), restoration of immune function as measured by CD4 cell counts, and prevention of transmission: sustained undetectable viral load means the virus is not sexually transmissible ("U=U", undetectable = untransmittable).

When to start and monitoring

Current WHO and national guidelines recommend starting ART as soon as possible after diagnosis, regardless of CD4 count, based on trials such as START and TEMPRANO showing reduced mortality with immediate treatment. Patients are monitored with regular viral-load and CD4 testing, screening for drug resistance where viral suppression fails, and surveillance for metabolic, renal and hepatic side effects.

Prevention alongside treatment

Management also includes pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) for HIV-negative people at risk, post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) within 72 hours of exposure, treatment of opportunistic infections, prevention of mother-to-child transmission, and counselling. Long-acting injectable regimens (cabotegravir/rilpivirine) offer an alternative to daily pills for some patients.

Challenges

There is still no cure or licensed vaccine. Lifelong adherence is required to prevent resistance, and global access remains uneven, though programmes such as PEPFAR and the Global Fund have expanded treatment to more than 29 million people worldwide.
