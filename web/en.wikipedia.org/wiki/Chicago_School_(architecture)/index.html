Chicago school (architecture)

The Chicago school was a school of architecture that flourished in the city of Chicago between the 1880s and the First World War. Its architects pioneered the steel-frame construction that made the first skyscrapers possible, and developed a distinctive style of commercial building characterised by large plate-glass windows, restrained ornament and an honest expression of structure. Leading figures included William Le Baron Jenney, Daniel Burnham, John Wellborn Root, William Holabird, Martin Roche and Louis Sullivan.

Characteristics

Chicago school buildings typically rest on a steel skeleton frame clad in masonry or terra cotta, freeing the facade from structural duties and allowing wide bands of windows. The signature "Chicago window" consists of a large fixed central pane flanked by narrower operable sash. Facades were often organised in a tripartite scheme echoing a classical column — base, shaft and capital — an approach articulated by Sullivan, whose dictum "form follows function" became a creed of modern architecture.

Major works

Key buildings include Jenney's Home Insurance Building (1885), the Tacoma Building (1889) by Holabird & Roche, Burnham and Root's Monadnock Building (1891) and Reliance Building (1890–95, completed by Charles B. Atwood), and Sullivan's Wainwright Building in St. Louis (1891) and Carson, Pirie, Scott Building in Chicago (1899–1904).

Legacy

The Chicago school's structural innovations laid the groundwork for the modern skyscraper and influenced the development of modernism in Europe. A "second Chicago school" emerged after the Second World War around Ludwig Mies van der Rohe and firms such as Skidmore, Owings & Merrill, applying steel-and-glass construction to a new generation of towers.
