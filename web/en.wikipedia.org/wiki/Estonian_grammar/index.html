Estonian grammar

Estonian grammar is the grammar of the Estonian language, a Finnic language spoken in Estonia. Estonian is an agglutinative language with strong fusional tendencies, known for its fourteen grammatical cases, three degrees of phonemic vowel and consonant length, and the absence of grammatical gender.

Nouns and cases

Estonian nouns decline for number (singular and plural) and fourteen cases, including the nominative, genitive, partitive, illative, inessive, elative, allative, adessive, ablative, translative, terminative, essive, abessive, and comitative. The genitive stem forms the basis of most other case forms, and the partitive is used for partial objects and after numerals greater than one.

Quantity

Estonian famously distinguishes three lengths — short, long, and overlong — for both vowels and consonants, a system reflected in spelling only partially (double letters mark long sounds, while overlong sounds are not distinguished in writing). Quantity interacts with a process of consonant gradation, in which stop consonants alternate between strong and weak grades across inflected forms.

Verbs

Verbs conjugate for person and number in the present and past tenses, and the language has no future tense, using the present instead. Estonian has four moods: indicative, conditional, imperative, and quotative (also called the oblique mood), the latter used to report hearsay. Negation uses a separate negative verb ei that does not conjugate for person.

Word order

The basic word order is subject–verb–object, but it is relatively free and is used to mark topic and focus. Estonian has no articles; definiteness is inferred from context and case choice.
