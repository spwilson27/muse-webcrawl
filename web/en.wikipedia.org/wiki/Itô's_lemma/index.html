In mathematics, Itô's lemma or Itô's formula is an identity used in Itô calculus to find the differential of a time-dependent function of a stochastic process. It is the stochastic-calculus counterpart of the chain rule in ordinary calculus. The lemma was discovered by the Japanese mathematician Kiyosi Itô and is one of the most widely used results in stochastic calculus, with central applications in mathematical finance, notably in the derivation of the Black–Scholes equation.

Statement

Let Xt be an Itô process satisfying the stochastic differential equation

dXt = μt dt + σt dBt,

where Bt is a Wiener process. If f(t, x) is a twice-differentiable scalar function, then

df = ( ∂f/∂t + μ ∂f/∂x + (σ²/2) ∂²f/∂x² ) dt + σ ∂f/∂x dBt.

The extra second-order term (σ²/2) ∂²f/∂x², absent from the ordinary chain rule, arises because a Wiener process has non-zero quadratic variation: informally, (dBt)² = dt.

Informal derivation

A formal proof proceeds by expanding f in a Taylor series and retaining terms up to order dt, using the multiplication rules dt² = 0, dt·dBt = 0 and (dBt)² = dt, which can be justified using the quadratic variation of Brownian motion. Unlike ordinary calculus, the second-order spatial derivative therefore contributes to the drift.

Applications

Itô's lemma is used to derive the dynamics of derivative prices from an assumed process for the underlying asset. Applying it to the option value function in the Black–Scholes model yields the Black–Scholes partial differential equation. It is also used to solve stochastic differential equations — for example, applying the lemma to log St solves the geometric Brownian motion equation — and underlies the Girsanov theorem and the Feynman–Kac formula's stochastic representation of partial differential equations.

Generalizations

The formula extends to functions of several Itô processes (with cross-variation terms), to processes with jumps (where a correction involving the jump sizes appears), and to the Itô–Stratonovich conversion between the Itô and Stratonovich interpretations of stochastic integration.
