Basileus

Basileus (Greek: βασιλεύς) is a Greek term and title that has signified various types of monarchs in history. In the English-speaking world it is most widely understood to mean a king or emperor. The title was used by sovereigns in the Hellenistic world and the Byzantine Empire, and in modern Greece for the country's kings until 1973.

Origins

The word appears in Mycenaean Greek Linear B as qa-si-re-u, where it denoted a local chief or official rather than a great king (who was the wanax). In Homer and the archaic period, basileus referred to any of the leading aristocratic chieftains. In classical Athens, the archon basileus was a magistrate who retained the religious functions of the old kingship.

Hellenistic and Byzantine use

After Alexander the Great, basileus became the standard title of Hellenistic monarchs. In the Roman and Byzantine world it was applied to the Persian king and later, especially from the reign of Heraclius in the 7th century, became the official Greek title of the Byzantine emperor — basileus kai autokratōr tōn Rhōmaiōn, "emperor and autocrat of the Romans".

Modern use

The kings of modern Greece bore the title Basileus from 1832 until the abolition of the monarchy in 1973. The word also survives in names — Basil derives from basileus — and in terms such as basilica, originally a royal hall.
