Bagna Càuda

Bagna càuda (Piedmontese for "hot dip" or "hot sauce") is a warm sauce from Piedmont in north-west Italy, made from garlic, anchovies, and olive oil — in some versions with butter or cream — served as a dip for raw and cooked vegetables. Like fondue, it is kept warm in a communal pot at the table (in a traditional earthenware fujot), and diners dip vegetables into it.

History

The dish developed in the vine-growing countryside of Lower Piedmont from the Middle Ages onward, when anchovies were carried inland from Liguria along the salt roads and traded for wine and other goods. Bagna càuda became the centrepiece of convivial autumn and winter meals at the end of the grape harvest, and by the 19th and 20th centuries it was codified as a symbol of Piedmontese identity and hospitality.

Preparation and serving

Recipes vary by locality and family; garlic is sliced or crushed and gently cooked in olive oil until soft without browning, desalted anchovies are melted into the oil, and many versions finish with butter, sometimes walnut oil, milk, or cream. The dip is served with seasonal vegetables such as cardoons (a local speciality), peppers, cabbage, Jerusalem artichokes, carrots, and bread. A traditional saying holds that the meal should be eaten communally and that the dip should be "hot" both in temperature and convivial spirit.
