Graft-versus-tumor effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Donor immune cells can recognise and destroy residual malignant cells in the recipient.

The graft-versus-tumor effect (GvT), also called the graft-versus-leukemia effect (GvL) in the context of blood cancers, is an immune-mediated anti-cancer response that can occur after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Donor T cells and natural killer cells recognise antigens on the recipient's residual malignant cells and attack them, helping to eliminate disease that survived the conditioning chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

Evidence and mechanism

The effect was inferred from clinical observations that patients who developed graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) had lower relapse rates, that relapse was more common after transplants from identical twins or after T-cell-depleted grafts, and that infusions of donor lymphocytes could re-induce remission in patients who relapsed after transplantation. The targets include minor histocompatibility antigens expressed on hematopoietic cells and leukemia-associated antigens such as WT1 and PR1.

Clinical exploitation

GvT is the principal therapeutic benefit of allogeneic transplantation for many leukemias and lymphomas. Clinicians harness it through donor lymphocyte infusions and through reduced-intensity ("mini") conditioning regimens, which rely on the immune effect rather than maximal chemo-radiation to eradicate disease, making transplantation feasible for older patients. The central clinical challenge is separating beneficial GvT from harmful GvHD, in which the same donor immunity attacks the recipient's skin, gut, and liver.
