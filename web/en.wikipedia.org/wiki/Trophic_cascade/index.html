Trophic cascade

A trophic cascade is an ecological phenomenon in which changes at the top of a food web — the addition or removal of apex predators — propagate downward through trophic levels, producing inverse patterns in the abundance or biomass of successive levels.

Classic examples

The concept developed from studies such as Hairston, Smith, and Slobodkin's "green world" hypothesis (1960) and Robert Paine's experiments with starfish on rocky shores. The best-known case is the sea otter–urchin–kelp system: where orcas prey on sea otters, urchins multiply and destroy kelp forests; where otters thrive, kelp flourishes. The reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone National Park in 1995 is widely cited as triggering a cascade — elk browsing declined, willow and aspen recovered, beavers and songbirds returned, and riverbank erosion lessened — though the strength and causes of each link remain debated by ecologists.

Significance

Trophic cascades show that predators shape ecosystems far beyond their direct prey, influencing vegetation, nutrient cycling, carbon storage, and disease dynamics. They underpin arguments for predator conservation and rewilding.
