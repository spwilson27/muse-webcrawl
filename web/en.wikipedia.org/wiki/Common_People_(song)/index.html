Common People (song)

"Common People" is a song by English alternative rock band Pulp, released in May 1995 as the lead single from their fifth studio album, Different Class. Written by the band members Nick Banks, Jarvis Cocker, Candida Doyle, Steve Mackey and Russell Senior, the song reached number two on the UK Singles Chart and became one of the defining tracks of the Britpop era.

Background and composition

The song was inspired by a Greek art student whom frontman Jarvis Cocker met while studying film at Central Saint Martins College of Art and Design in London in 1988. The woman told Cocker that she wanted to move to Hackney and "live like the common people". Cocker found the phrase patronising, and the encounter formed the seed of the song, whose narrator rebukes a wealthy student who wants to slum it for amusement while lacking any real understanding of working-class life.

The track builds from a quiet, keyboard-led verse into a sweeping, anthemic chorus. Its lyrics contrast the lives of the genuinely poor with "poverty tourism", culminating in the warning that the rich girl will "never understand how it feels to live your life with no meaning or control".

Release and reception

"Common People" was released on 22 May 1995 and peaked at number two in the UK, held off the top spot by Robson & Jerome's "Unchained Melody". It became Pulp's biggest hit and helped propel Different Class to the top of the UK Albums Chart. The song was widely praised by critics and has frequently appeared on lists of the greatest songs of all time; in 2015 it was voted the greatest Britpop song in a BBC Radio 6 Music poll.

Live performances and legacy

Pulp performed the song at the 1995 Glastonbury Festival, where they headlined at short notice, in a set widely regarded as one of the festival's finest. A famous cover version by William Shatner, produced by Ben Folds, appeared on Shatner's 2004 album Has Been and introduced the song to a new audience. The song remains a staple of British popular culture and is frequently cited as an anthem about class division in Britain.
