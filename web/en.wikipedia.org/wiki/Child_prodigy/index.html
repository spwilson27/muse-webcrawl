A child prodigy is a child, generally under the age of ten, who produces meaningful work in a domain at the level of an adult expert. Prodigies are most often identified in rule-based, structured fields such as music, chess, mathematics, and art.

Characteristics

Psychological research, notably by Ellen Winner and Joanne Ruthsatz, finds that prodigies typically combine high general intelligence with extraordinary working memory and an intense, self-driven preoccupation with their domain, sometimes called a "rage to master". Prodigies differ from savants in that their abilities are broad within the domain and accompanied by high overall cognitive ability. Many show heightened attention to detail and some traits overlapping with autism spectrum profiles in their families.

Famous examples

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart composed from age five and toured Europe as a child performer. Blaise Pascal wrote a treatise on conic sections at sixteen; John von Neumann could divide eight-digit numbers mentally at six; Bobby Fischer became a chess grandmaster at fifteen; mathematician Terence Tao scored over 760 on the SAT mathematics section at age eight.

Later outcomes

Only some prodigies become eminent adult creators; researchers note that transitioning from mastering an existing domain to transforming it requires different capacities, and that intensive childhood practice and family support shape outcomes as much as innate ability.
