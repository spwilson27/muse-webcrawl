Benford's law

Benford's law
Also called: Newcomb–Benford law; first-digit law
Observed by: Simon Newcomb (1881); Frank Benford (1938)
Probability first digit 1: about 30.1%
Probability first digit 9: about 4.6%

Benford's law, also known as the Newcomb–Benford law or the first-digit law, is an observation that in many real-life sets of numerical data, the leading digit is likely to be small. The digit 1 occurs as the leading significant digit about 30% of the time, while 9 occurs less than 5% of the time. The probability that the first digit is d is log10(d + 1) − log10(d) = log10(1 + 1/d).

History

Astronomer Simon Newcomb noticed in 1881 that the early pages of logarithm tables — used for numbers beginning with 1 — were far more worn than later pages. Physicist Frank Benford rediscovered the phenomenon in 1938 and tested it against some 20,000 numbers drawn from diverse sources such as river areas, street addresses, atomic weights, and newspaper figures. The law has since been shown to arise in data that span several orders of magnitude and is related to the scale-invariance of logarithmic distributions.

Applications

Because fabricated numbers often fail to follow Benford's law, it is used in forensic accounting and auditing to flag possible fraud in tax returns, financial statements, and election data, though it is not by itself proof of wrongdoing. The law does not apply to data that are assigned (such as telephone numbers), constrained to a narrow range, or influenced by psychological pricing.
