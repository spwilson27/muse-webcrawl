European witchcraft

Early modern depictions fused folk magic with fears of a diabolical conspiracy.

European witchcraft refers to the beliefs, practices and persecutions surrounding witchcraft in Europe from late antiquity through the early modern period. It encompasses widespread folk traditions of magic and healing, and the learned concept—crystallised in the fifteenth century—of the witch as a member of a devil-worshipping sect, which underlay the witch trials of c. 1450–1750.

Folk traditions and the learned synthesis

European villages long knew cunning folk, charmers and healers whose magic (maleficium when harmful) was ambivalent but local. Medieval theology initially discouraged belief in witchcraft—the early medieval Canon Episcopi treated night-flight beliefs as delusions. In the fifteenth-century Alpine region, inquisitors and judges fused folk beliefs with diabolism: the pact with the Devil, the witches' sabbath, flight and harmful sorcery, codified in treatises such as the Malleus Maleficarum (1486) attributed to Heinrich Kramer.

The witch trials

Persecution peaked between about 1560 and 1650, amid religious conflict, war, and climatic hardship during the Little Ice Age. Across Europe perhaps 100,000 people were tried and 40,000–60,000 executed, around three-quarters of them women. Massive hunts in the German prince-bishoprics, Scotland's trials under James VI's Daemonologie (1597), and the Salem trials in colonial Massachusetts (1692–93) are among the best known. Torture-generated denunciations drove chain-reaction panics.

Decline

Judicial scepticism, stricter rules of evidence, and Enlightenment thought ended prosecutions during the eighteenth century; Britain's Witchcraft Act of 1735 reclassified pretended witchcraft as fraud. The last executions are generally dated to the 1780s (Anna Göldi in Switzerland, 1782). Modern historians debate combinations of gender, religion, law and social stress in explaining the hunts, while the term "witch" has been reclaimed by modern pagan movements such as Wicca.
