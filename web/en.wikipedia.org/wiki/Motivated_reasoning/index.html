Motivated reasoning

Motivated reasoning is a phenomenon in cognitive science and social psychology in which individuals use emotion-biased reasoning to produce justifications or make decisions that are most desired rather than those that accurately reflect the evidence. In other words, motivated reasoning is the "tendency to find arguments in favor of conclusions we want to believe to be stronger than arguments for conclusions we do not want to believe."

The phenomenon is driven by directional goals — the motivation to arrive at a particular conclusion — as opposed to accuracy goals, the motivation to arrive at the most defensible conclusion regardless of one's preferences. Psychologist Ziva Kunda's influential 1990 paper, "The Case for Motivated Reasoning," synthesized the literature and argued that motivation biases cognitive processes including memory search, hypothesis generation, and evidence evaluation.

Mechanisms

Motivated reasoning operates through several mechanisms. People engage in biased assimilation, interpreting mixed evidence as confirming their prior beliefs, and disconfirmation bias, subjecting uncongenial evidence to harsher scrutiny than congenial evidence. They also deploy selective exposure, preferring information sources likely to agree with them, and biased memory recall, remembering supporting evidence more readily.

Importantly, motivated reasoners typically do not feel they are being biased; they construct justifications that appear objective to themselves. Kunda noted that people "are more likely to arrive at conclusions that they want to arrive at, but their ability to do so is constrained by their ability to construct seemingly reasonable justifications for these conclusions."

Political applications

Motivated reasoning is heavily studied in political psychology, where it helps explain partisan polarization, resistance to factual corrections, and asymmetric responses to scientific consensus on topics such as climate change and vaccination. Research by Dan Kahan and colleagues on identity-protective cognition suggests that individuals process information in ways that protect their standing within valued cultural groups, and that higher numeracy and cognitive sophistication can actually increase polarization on identity-laden issues.
