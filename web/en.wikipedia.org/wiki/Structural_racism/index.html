Structural racism (also called systemic racism or institutional racism) is a concept in which a society's institutions, policies, practices and cultural norms interact in ways that produce and reinforce inequitable outcomes for different racial and ethnic groups. Unlike individual prejudice, structural racism does not require the racist intent of any particular person; disadvantages are produced by the normal functioning of interlocking systems such as housing, education, employment, criminal justice and health care.

Definitions

Scholars distinguish structural racism from purely interpersonal racism by its focus on how systems interact: for example, historically segregated housing (redlining) shapes the tax base for local schools, which shapes educational attainment, which shapes employment and exposure to the criminal justice system, which in turn shapes health and wealth. The sociologist Joe Feagin's concept of the "white racial frame" and Eduardo Bonilla-Silva's theory of "racism without racists" are related attempts to describe how racial hierarchies persist without overt bigotry.

Examples by domain

  • Housing: twentieth-century redlining maps in the United States denied mortgages in Black neighbourhoods; the resulting wealth gap persists in home-ownership rates and neighbourhood disinvestment.
  • Health: researchers have documented disparities in maternal mortality, exposure to pollution (environmental racism) and access to care.
  • Criminal justice: sentencing disparities, drug-law enforcement and cash bail systems have been analysed as mechanisms that reproduce racial inequality even when statutes are formally race-neutral.
  • Education: funding tied to local property taxes and disciplinary practices that disproportionately suspend minority students.

Debate

The concept is debated. Supporters argue it explains persistent outcome gaps that individual-prejudice models cannot. Critics argue the term is too broad, difficult to falsify, or that class and other factors better explain some disparities; others object to policy remedies derived from it. Public discussion intensified after 2020, and the term entered official usage in public-health declarations by bodies such as the American Public Health Association, while some governments have restricted its teaching in public institutions.
