Sodium hypochlorite

Sodium hypochlorite is an inorganic compound with the formula NaOCl (also written NaClO). In aqueous solution it is the active ingredient of household bleach and is widely used as a disinfectant and bleaching agent. The solution — pale greenish-yellow, with a characteristic chlorine-like odour — is unstable and decomposes slowly, so commercial products are typically supplied at 3–8% concentration for household bleach and 10–15% for industrial and pool use ("liquid chlorine"). Sodium hypochlorite has been produced industrially since the late 18th century, when it was marketed in France as eau de Javel.

Production and chemistry

Sodium hypochlorite is manufactured by passing chlorine gas into cold dilute sodium hydroxide solution (the chloralkali process): Cl2 + 2 NaOH → NaCl + NaOCl + H2O. In water it forms hypochlorous acid (HOCl), the species chiefly responsible for its rapid microbicidal action; the balance between HOCl and the hypochlorite ion depends on pH. Solutions degrade with heat, light and metal contamination, releasing oxygen or forming chlorate, and are stored cool, dark and slightly alkaline.

Uses

Household bleach disinfects surfaces and whitens laundry by oxidising coloured compounds. Municipal water treatment and swimming pools use sodium hypochlorite for disinfection; it is also used in dentistry as an irrigant in root-canal treatment, in food processing sanitation, and in dilute form (Dakin's solution) historically for wound care. During public health emergencies it is a standard surface disinfectant effective against bacteria, viruses and fungi.

Safety

Sodium hypochlorite is corrosive to skin and eyes and damaging if swallowed. Mixing bleach with acids releases toxic chlorine gas, and mixing with ammonia or ammonia-based cleaners produces chloramines; bleach must never be combined with other cleaning products. Concentrated solutions can damage fabrics and metals.
