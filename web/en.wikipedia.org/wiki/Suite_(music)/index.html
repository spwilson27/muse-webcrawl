Suite (music)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Baroque suite was a sequence of stylised dance movements.

A suite, in Western classical music, is an ordered set of instrumental or orchestral pieces. It originated in the late 14th century as a pairing of dance tunes and developed in form throughout the 16th, 17th and 18th centuries, most fully in the Baroque suite (also called partita or, in France, ordre), a sequence of stylised dances in the same key.

The Baroque suite

The standardised Baroque suite, as cultivated by composers such as Froberger, Bach and Handel, consists of four core movements:

  • Allemande — a stately German dance in moderate 4/4 time;
  • Courante (or corrente) — a lively French (or Italian) dance in triple time;
  • Sarabande — a slow, solemn Spanish-derived dance in triple time, stressing the second beat;
  • Gigue — a fast, sprightly dance, usually in compound time.

Between the sarabande and gigue, composers often inserted optional dances (galanteries) such as the minuet, gavotte, bourrée, passepied or air, and the suite might open with a prelude or overture. J.S. Bach's six suites for solo cello, his English Suites and French Suites for keyboard, and Handel's Water Music and Music for the Royal Fireworks are celebrated examples.

Later uses

In the 19th and 20th centuries the term broadened to cover collections of movements extracted from ballets, operas and incidental music — for example Tchaikovsky's Nutcracker Suite and suites from Bizet's Carmen — and free sequences of character pieces, such as Holst's The Planets or suites compiled from film scores. "Suite" is also used for ordered sets of pieces in popular music and jazz.
