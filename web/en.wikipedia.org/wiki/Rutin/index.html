Rutin

Rutin
IUPAC name2-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-3,5,7-trihydroxy-4H-1-benzopyran-4-one 3-O-(6-deoxy-α-L-mannopyranosyl-(1→6)-β-D-glucopyranoside)
FormulaC27H30O16
Molar mass610.52 g/mol
Other namesRutoside, quercetin-3-O-rutinoside, sophorin, vitamin P

Rutin, also called rutoside and quercetin-3-O-rutinoside, is a flavonol glycoside found in a wide variety of plants, including buckwheat, citrus fruits, apples, asparagus, capers, and the Japanese pagoda tree (Styphnolobium japonicum). It consists of the flavonol quercetin bound to the disaccharide rutinose (α-L-rhamnopyranosyl-(1→6)-β-D-glucopyranose).

Occurrence

Rutin was first isolated from the herb Ruta graveolens (rue), from which it takes its name. Buckwheat is one of the richest dietary sources; tartary buckwheat in particular can contain substantial amounts. It is also abundant in capers, black olives, asparagus, and the flowers and leaves of the pagoda tree, which is used as a commercial source.

Chemistry

Rutin is a yellow-to-greenish crystalline powder, sparingly soluble in water but more soluble in hot water and alcohol. Its aglycone, quercetin, is released by hydrolysis of the glycosidic bond, which can occur in the gut through microbial enzymes. Like other flavonoids, rutin has antioxidant activity in vitro and has been investigated for anti-inflammatory and vasoprotective effects.

Medical use

Rutin and its semisynthetic derivatives, the hydroxyethylrutosides (such as troxerutin), have been used in some countries as venotonic agents for chronic venous insufficiency and capillary fragility. Along with related citrus flavonoids, it was once promoted under the historical name "vitamin P," though it is not recognized as a vitamin. Evidence for clinical benefit is considered limited, and rutin is mainly sold as a dietary supplement.
