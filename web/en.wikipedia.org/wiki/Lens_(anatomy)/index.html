Lens (anatomy)

The crystalline lens of the human eye
Part ofHuman eye
FunctionFocuses light onto the retina
DiameterAbout 10 mm (adult)
ThicknessAbout 4 mm

The lens, or crystalline lens, is a transparent biconvex structure in the eye that, along with the cornea, refracts light to be focused on the retina. By changing its shape, the lens adjusts the eye's focal length so that images at different distances can be brought into focus — a process called accommodation.

Structure

The lens is suspended behind the iris by a ring of fibrous strands, the zonules of Zinn, which connect it to the ciliary body. It consists of an outer capsule, an epithelium, and an inner mass of elongated lens fibre cells packed with transparent proteins called crystallins. The lens has no blood supply or nerves after fetal development; it is nourished by the aqueous humour. It grows throughout life as new layers of fibres are added at its equator.

Accommodation

When the ciliary muscle contracts, tension on the zonules relaxes and the elastic lens rounds up, increasing its optical power for near vision. When the muscle relaxes, the lens flattens for distance vision. With age the lens hardens and this ability declines, producing presbyopia, usually noticeable in the mid-forties.

Diseases and disorders

Clouding of the lens is a cataract, the commonest cause of treatable blindness worldwide; it is treated by surgically removing the lens and replacing it with an artificial intraocular lens. Dislocation of the lens (ectopia lentis) may occur after trauma or in connective-tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome. Absence of the lens is called aphakia.
