Weimar cinema
Period:c. 1919–1933
Major studio:UFA (Universum Film AG)

Weimar cinema refers to the film industry of Germany during the Weimar Republic (1919–1933), one of the most artistically innovative periods in film history. Centred on the UFA studios at Babelsberg, German filmmakers pioneered expressionist design, mobile camera work and psychological storytelling, profoundly influencing world cinema and later Hollywood film noir.

Historical context

France, Britain and the United States had restricted German films during World War I, and the Weimar government's import limits accidentally sheltered the domestic industry. Inflation made German productions cheap abroad while the collapsing mark fuelled lavish spending; by the mid-1920s UFA was Europe's most advanced studio.

Expressionism

German Expressionist cinema used distorted sets, painted shadows and stylised acting to externalise inner states. Landmark films include Robert Wiene's The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920), Paul Wegener's The Golem (1920), F. W. Murnau's Nosferatu (1922) and Fritz Lang's Dr. Mabuse the Gambler (1922). Siegfried Kracauer's From Caligari to Hitler famously read these films as prefiguring Nazism — an interpretation still debated.

Later developments

Murnau's The Last Laugh (1924) showcased the "unchained camera"; Lang's Metropolis (1927) was the most expensive European film of its era. The Kammerspiel (chamber drama) and "street film" turned toward intimate realism. With sound came Lang's M (1931) and the musical The Three from the Filling Station. G. W. Pabst's Pandora's Box (1929), starring Louise Brooks, exemplified the New Objectivity.

Emigration and legacy

After 1933, Jewish and left-wing filmmakers fled the Nazis; Lang, Billy Wilder, Fred Zinnemann, Robert Siodmak and others reshaped Hollywood, carrying expressionist lighting and themes into film noir.
