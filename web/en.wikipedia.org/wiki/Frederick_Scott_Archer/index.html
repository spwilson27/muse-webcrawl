Frederick Scott Archer

Born1813, United Kingdom
Died1 May 1857, London
Known forWet plate collodion process

Frederick Scott Archer (1813 – 1 May 1857) was an English photographer and sculptor who invented the photographic collodion process, which preceded the modern gelatin emulsion. Published in 1851, his wet plate process dramatically reduced exposure times compared with the calotype and daguerreotype and made photography far more practical and widespread.

Life and work

Archer was a silversmith's apprentice and later a sculptor in London. Seeking a better way to record his sculpture, he experimented with photographic processes. In March 1851 he published his method in The Chemist: a glass plate coated with collodion (guncotton dissolved in ether and alcohol) containing iodide salts, sensitised in a silver nitrate bath, exposed while still wet, and developed immediately. The result was a sharp glass negative from which many paper prints could be made.

Impact and later life

Archer did not patent his invention, freely giving it to the world. The collodion process dominated photography for three decades, enabling portrait studios, war photography and the mass production of images. Despite the importance of his discovery, Archer died in poverty in 1857; a government pension was granted to his children after a public campaign, and a memorial fund was raised by the photographic community.
