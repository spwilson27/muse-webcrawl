Hyperbolic discounting (steep near the present) versus exponential discounting.

Hyperbolic discounting is a model of time preference in behavioural economics in which people discount the value of future rewards at a rate that declines with the length of the delay. Unlike the standard exponential discounting assumed by neoclassical economics — where the discount rate is constant — hyperbolic discounting implies that people are especially impatient about the immediate future but more patient when comparing two distant dates.

The model

A common formulation, after Mazur, gives the present value of a reward of amount A delayed by D as:

V = A / (1 + kD)

where k measures impatience. Because the implied discount rate falls as D grows, preferences can reverse over time: a person may prefer £110 in 31 days to £100 in 30 days, yet when day 30 arrives prefer £100 immediately to £110 tomorrow. Such preference reversals violate the time consistency assumed by exponential discounting.

Evidence and explanations

Hyperbolic-like discounting has been demonstrated in experiments with humans and other animals, including pigeons and rats. Psychologists relate it to present bias and the β–δ (quasi-hyperbolic) model of Laibson, in which a present-bias parameter β down-weights all future periods relative to the present. Uncertainty about whether future rewards will be received can also make rational discounting appear hyperbolic.

Consequences

Hyperbolic discounting helps explain procrastination, under-saving for retirement, addiction and other self-control problems, and motivates commitment devices — from pension auto-enrolment to deadline-setting — that let people bind their future selves.
