Extrajudicial killing

An extrajudicial killing (also known as an extrajudicial execution or an extralegal killing) is the deliberate killing of a person or group of people by governmental authorities without the sanction of any judicial proceeding or legal process. Extrajudicial punishments are by their nature illegal, since they do not flow from a legal process and thus violate the usual protections of due process.

Extrajudicial killings bypass courts and due process entirely.

Legal framework

The right to life is protected by the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Article 3) and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (Article 6), which prohibits the arbitrary deprivation of life. Killings by police or security forces are lawful only in narrow circumstances, such as absolute necessity in self-defence; deliberate killings outside those bounds — including "death squad" murders and targeted killings outside armed conflict — are treated in international law as extrajudicial executions.

Contexts

Extrajudicial killings have been documented in many settings: counter-insurgency campaigns, "dirty wars" such as those in Latin America in the 1970s–80s (where victims often became desaparecidos, the forcibly disappeared), anti-drug campaigns in which police or vigilantes kill suspected dealers, and the targeted killing of suspected terrorists by drone strike, whose legality is contested. The United Nations has maintained a Special Rapporteur on extrajudicial, summary or arbitrary executions since 1982, and the 1989 UN Principles on the Effective Prevention and Investigation of Extra-legal, Arbitrary and Summary Executions set standards for investigation.

Distinctions

The term is distinguished from lawful lethal force (which is judicially reviewable), from judicially imposed capital punishment (which follows a trial, however flawed), and from killings by non-state actors, which are generally classified as murder or terrorism rather than extrajudicial killing.
