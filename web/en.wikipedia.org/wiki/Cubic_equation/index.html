In algebra, a cubic equation is an equation of the form

ax³ + bx² + cx + d = 0,

in which a is nonzero. The solutions of this equation are called roots of the cubic function defined by the left-hand side. Every cubic equation with real or complex coefficients has at least one real root and, counting multiplicity, exactly three roots in the complex numbers, by the fundamental theorem of algebra.

History

Cubic equations were known to the ancient Babylonians, Greeks, Chinese, Indians and Egyptians, usually in geometric or specific numerical forms. Omar Khayyam (1048–1131) gave a systematic geometric treatment, solving cubics by intersecting conic sections, and correctly noted that a cubic may have more than one solution, while denying that it could be solved by radicals alone. The algebraic breakthrough came in Renaissance Italy: Scipione del Ferro discovered a method for the depressed cubic around 1515; Niccolò Tartaglia rediscovered and extended it; and Gerolamo Cardano published the general solution in his Ars Magna (1545), crediting both men — an act that provoked a famous priority dispute. Cardano's student Lodovico Ferrari extended the work to the quartic equation. The cubic's irreducible case, in which three real roots are expressed through complex cube roots, forced mathematicians to take complex numbers seriously, notably through Rafael Bombelli's work.

Cardano's formula

Substituting x = t − b/(3a) removes the quadratic term, giving the depressed cubic t³ + pt + q = 0. Cardano's formula then gives a root

t = ∛(−q/2 + √((q/2)² + (p/3)³)) + ∛(−q/2 − √((q/2)² + (p/3)³)).

The expression under the square root is related to the discriminant Δ = −4p³ − 27q² of the depressed cubic: if Δ > 0 the equation has three distinct real roots; if Δ = 0 there is a repeated root; if Δ < 0 there is one real root and a pair of complex conjugate roots. In the case of three real roots (the casus irreducibilis), the roots are most conveniently expressed trigonometrically, as shown by François Viète.

Applications

Cubic equations arise in computing volumes and areas, in the geometry of conic sections, in cubic splines used in computer graphics and interpolation, in the van der Waals equation of state for real gases, and in numerous optimization problems where a cubic is the lowest-degree polynomial able to model an inflection point.
