Light tank

A light tank is a tank variant initially designed for rapid movements in and out of combat, to outmaneuver heavier tanks. It is smaller in size with thinner armor and a less powerful main gun, tailored mainly for reconnaissance, screening, and supporting roles where speed and strategic mobility matter more than protection or firepower.

History

Light tanks emerged in the First World War — the French Renault FT, weighing under seven tonnes, set the pattern with its rotating turret. Between the wars, light tanks were produced in large numbers, including the Soviet T-26 and BT series and the German Panzer I and II. In the Second World War they were widely used for reconnaissance; notable examples include the American M3/M5 Stuart and M24 Chaffee, the Soviet T-70, and the British Tetrarch, which was light enough to be delivered by glider.

As the war progressed, anti-tank weapons outclassed light armor, and many light tank roles passed to armored cars. Post-war designs such as the American M41 Walker Bulldog and the amphibious Soviet PT-76 continued the type, while the M551 Sheridan combined a light hull with a large-calibre gun-missile launcher. In the late Cold War, the British FV101 Scorpion — among the fastest tanks ever fielded — exemplified the reconnaissance role.

Modern role

Today few armies field vehicles formally called light tanks, with wheeled reconnaissance vehicles and infantry fighting vehicles performing many of their tasks. However, interest in the category persists for expeditionary and mountain warfare: examples include China's Type 15 tank, designed for high-altitude terrain, and various armored gun systems intended to give light forces direct fire support. Their advantages remain strategic mobility — transportability by air and the ability to cross bridges that heavier main battle tanks cannot use.
