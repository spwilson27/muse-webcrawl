Storm shelter

A storm shelter is a structure designed to protect its occupants from severe weather, particularly tornadoes and hurricanes. Storm shelters are most common in regions of the United States prone to tornadoes, such as the Great Plains and the Southeast. Modern standards for storm shelters in the United States are set out in FEMA publications (notably FEMA P-320 for residential shelters and FEMA P-361 for community shelters) and the ICC/NSSA 500 standard.

Types

  • Storm cellars — traditional underground rooms, often entered from outside the home, common in the early and mid-20th century.
  • Below-ground shelters — reinforced rooms installed under a garage slab or in a yard.
  • Above-ground safe rooms — steel or concrete rooms, often installed in a garage or interior closet, engineered to resist extreme wind pressures and debris impact.
  • Community shelters — large public shelters serving schools, mobile home parks, and neighbourhoods.

Design criteria

Shelters are designed to withstand winds of up to 250 mph (400 km/h) in the most protective category and to resist penetration by wind-borne debris, tested using a standard missile of a 15-pound 2×4 timber travelling at 100 mph horizontally. Doors, ventilation, and anchoring against uplift are critical elements of design and are subject to third-party testing and certification.

Usage

Occupants enter a shelter on the issuance of a tornado warning and remain until the storm passes. Because warning lead times are short, shelters are typically stocked with water, flashlights, a weather radio, and first-aid supplies.
