Achondroplasia is a genetic disorder and the most common cause of dwarfism, characterised by disproportionate short stature in which the limbs are short relative to the trunk. The word derives from Greek roots meaning "without cartilage formation", although the problem lies in the conversion of cartilage to bone (endochondral ossification) rather than in cartilage formation itself.

Cause

Achondroplasia results from a gain-of-function mutation in the gene for fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) on chromosome 4, in nearly all cases a single specific amino-acid substitution (Gly380Arg). The overactive receptor inhibits the growth of long bones. Inheritance is autosomal dominant — a person with the condition has a 50% chance of passing it to each child — but about 80% of cases arise from new mutations, associated with increased paternal age. Having two copies of the mutation (homozygous achondroplasia) is usually lethal in infancy.

Features and complications

Typical features include short limbs with rhizomelic shortening, a relatively large head with frontal bossing, midface retrusion, a trident configuration of the hands, and bowed legs. Intelligence is normal and life expectancy is near normal. Complications can include narrowing of the foramen magnum in infancy, obstructive sleep apnoea, recurrent ear infections, obesity and symptomatic spinal stenosis in adults.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is usually clinical and radiographic and can be confirmed by genetic testing; it is sometimes suspected on prenatal ultrasound. There is no cure, and management focuses on monitoring growth and complications and on surgical treatment where needed. The drug vosoritide, a C-type natriuretic peptide analogue that counteracts FGFR3 signalling, was approved in 2021 to increase growth velocity in children with achondroplasia.
