Neanderthal genetics

Neanderthal genetics is the study of the genome of Neanderthals, the archaic humans who lived in Europe and western Asia until about 40,000 years ago. The sequencing of Neanderthal DNA from fossil bones, beginning with mitochondrial DNA in 1997 and culminating in a draft nuclear genome in 2010, has transformed understanding of human evolution.

Admixture with modern humans

The genome project led by Svante Pääbo showed that between roughly 1 and 4 percent of the genomes of present-day people of non-African ancestry derive from Neanderthals, evidence of interbreeding after modern humans left Africa. Introgressed genes influence traits including skin and hair biology, immune responses and susceptibility to certain diseases. Svante Pääbo received the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this work.

Genetic findings

Neanderthals carried the derived variant of the FOXP2 gene associated with speech and language in modern humans, and genome comparisons have identified regions of the modern genome conspicuously depleted of Neanderthal ancestry, suggesting selection against hybrid incompatibilities.
