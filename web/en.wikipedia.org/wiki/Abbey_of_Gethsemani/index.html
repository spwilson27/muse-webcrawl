The Abbey of Our Lady of Gethsemani is a Trappist monastery near Bardstown, Kentucky, in Nelson County. Founded in 1848 by monks of Melleray Abbey in France, it is the oldest monastery in the United States still in continuous operation, and the motherhouse of several American Trappist foundations. It is best known internationally as the home of the writer and monk Thomas Merton, whose 1948 autobiography The Seven Storey Mountain brought the abbey widespread fame.

History

In 1805 the DeHaven family donated land in Kentucky that had earlier hosted a community of Sisters of Loretto. In October 1848, Dom Eutropius Proust led 44 monks from Melleray Abbey in Brittany across the Atlantic and down the Ohio River to establish the community; the first Mass was celebrated on 21 December 1848, and Gethsemani was raised to an abbey in 1851. The monks built a farm, mills, and the present church (consecrated in 1866), and supported themselves through agriculture and, later, mail-order sales of cheese, fruitcake, and fudge.

During the American Civil War the monastery sheltered both Union and Confederate soldiers. In the twentieth century the abbey founded daughter houses including the Monastery of the Holy Spirit in Conyers, Georgia, and communities in Utah, South Carolina, and elsewhere. Gethsemani also hosted historic interreligious encounters: the Dalai Lama visited in 1996 and met the monks in the chapter room where Merton had spoken with Buddhist scholars decades earlier.

Thomas Merton

Thomas Merton entered Gethsemani on 10 December 1941 and lived there until his death in 1968. His autobiography became a bestseller and drew a flood of postulants; under Abbot James Fox the community swelled to over 200 monks and built new accommodation for retreatants. Merton's writings on contemplation, peace, and interreligious dialogue — much of it written in a hermitage on the abbey grounds — made Gethsemani a centre of spiritual tourism, and his grave in the abbey cemetery remains a place of pilgrimage.

Life today

The community, typically around 40 monks, follows the Rule of St Benedict under the Cistercian constitutions, with days structured around the Liturgy of the Hours, manual labour, and lectio divina. The abbey offers silent retreats, sells its food products under the "Gethsemani Farms" label, and maintains some 2,000 acres of farmland and forest in the Kentucky knobs.
