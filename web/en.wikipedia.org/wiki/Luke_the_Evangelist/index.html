Luke the Evangelist is one of the Four Evangelists — the four traditionally ascribed authors of the canonical gospels. Church tradition attributes to him the Gospel of Luke and the Acts of the Apostles, which together comprise more than a quarter of the New Testament. The New Testament mentions a Luke several times: Paul calls him "the beloved physician" (Colossians 4:14) and lists him among his fellow workers, leading to the traditional picture of Luke as a physician and companion of Paul on his missionary journeys.

Identity and authorship

The "we passages" in Acts, in which the narration shifts to the first person plural, were long read as evidence that the author travelled with Paul. Early church writers such as Irenaeus and the Muratorian fragment identify this author as Luke. Most critical scholars date Luke–Acts to around 80–110 AD and debate whether the author was personally a companion of Paul; the works themselves are formally anonymous, though the traditional attribution remains influential.

Life in tradition

Later tradition makes Luke a native of Antioch, a Gentile — often regarded as the only non-Jewish author of a New Testament book — and, in Byzantine and medieval legend, the first icon painter, credited with portraits of the Virgin Mary painted from life. He is said to have died at an advanced age in Boeotia, Greece. His relics were translated to Constantinople in 357 and later, according to tradition, to Padua, where a tomb in the Basilica of Santa Giustina is venerated.

Symbol and patronage

Luke's evangelist symbol is the winged ox or bull, associated with sacrifice and with the sacrificial emphasis in his Gospel's opening scene in the Temple. As a physician and (by legend) an artist, he is the patron saint of doctors, surgeons, painters, and students, and guilds of painters across Europe were commonly named for St Luke.
