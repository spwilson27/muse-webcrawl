Inverse system

An inverse system consists of objects connected by morphisms directed from larger to smaller indices.

In mathematics, an inverse system (also called a projective system) is a collection of objects (for example, groups, sets, or topological spaces) indexed by a directed set, together with morphisms between them that go from objects with larger indices to objects with smaller indices, satisfying natural compatibility conditions.

Definition

Let I be a directed set. An inverse system in a category C consists of a family of objects {Xi} indexed by I and morphisms fij : Xj → Xi for all i ≤ j, such that fii is the identity and fik = fij ∘ fjk whenever i ≤ j ≤ k.

Inverse limit

The inverse limit of such a system, when it exists, is an object together with projection maps to each Xi that is universal among objects mapping compatibly into the system. A classic example is the ring of p-adic integers, constructed as the inverse limit of the rings Z/pnZ.

Applications

Inverse systems and their limits appear throughout algebra and topology, for instance in the construction of profinite groups, the Čech cohomology of a topological space, and adelic constructions in number theory.
