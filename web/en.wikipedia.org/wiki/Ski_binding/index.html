Ski binding

A ski binding is a device that connects a ski boot to a ski. Bindings differ substantially between disciplines: cross-country bindings hold only the toe of the boot, leaving the heel free for striding, whereas alpine (downhill) bindings fix both toe and heel to the ski and are designed to release the boot during a fall to reduce the risk of leg injuries.

Alpine bindings

Modern alpine bindings consist of a toe piece and a heel piece mounted on the ski. The boot is held under adjustable spring tension; when a twisting or forward force exceeds a set threshold — calibrated by the standardised release ("DIN") setting according to the skier's weight, height, boot length, age, and ability — the binding releases. The introduction of releasable bindings in the mid-20th century, following designs such as the Look Nevada and the Marker toe piece, greatly reduced lower-leg fractures.

Cross-country and telemark bindings

Cross-country bindings clamp the toe of a flexible boot to the ski, allowing the heel to lift. Common systems include the NNN (New Nordic Norm) and SNS (Salomon Nordic System) standards. Telemark bindings similarly free the heel but use sturdier boots and bindings to support the telemark turn; modern NTN (New Telemark Norm) systems can include step-in and release features. Ski touring (alpine touring) bindings use pivoting tech ("pin") toe pieces for climbing with the heel free, then lock down for descent.

History

Early bindings were simple leather straps; cable bindings appeared in the early 20th century. Norwegian skier Sondre Norheim pioneered stiffer bindings in the 19th century. Safety-release toe pieces were developed in the 1930s–1950s, notably by Hjalmar Hvam, and release bindings became standard equipment with the rise of recreational alpine skiing.
