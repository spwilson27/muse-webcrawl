United States military ration

United States military rations are the food provisions provided to members of the U.S. Armed Forces for consumption in the field, at sea, or where organized food facilities are unavailable. They have evolved from salt pork and hardtack to shelf-stable, self-heating packaged meals.

History

During the Revolutionary War, the Continental Congress prescribed rations of beef or pork, bread or flour, rum, salt, and vinegar. The Civil War added coffee, sugar, and desiccated vegetables. World War I brought canned "reserve rations," and World War II produced the famous K-ration and C-ration, along with the D-ration chocolate bar.

The C-ration and MCI

Canned wet rations remained standard through Korea and Vietnam, where the Meal, Combat, Individual (MCI) was issued from 1958 to 1980. Troops often called them "C-rats."

The MRE

The Meal, Ready-to-Eat (MRE) was adopted in 1981 and first widely deployed in the 1990s. Each MRE provides about 1,250 calories, is packaged in a retort pouch, and includes a flameless ration heater. Variants include the First Strike Ration, the Meal, Cold Weather, and humanitarian daily rations.

Development

New rations are developed by the Combat Capabilities Development Command Soldier Center in Natick, Massachusetts, with menus rotating on a multi-year cycle based on field testing and soldier feedback.
