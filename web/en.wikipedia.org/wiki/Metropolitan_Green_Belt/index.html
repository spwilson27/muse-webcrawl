The Metropolitan Green Belt is a statutory green belt around London, England. It comprises some 486,000 hectares of designated countryside and was the first and largest of England's green belts, whose fundamental aim is to prevent urban sprawl by keeping land permanently open.

History

The idea of a green girdle around London was advocated by Ebenezer Howard's garden city movement and the London Society. The Green Belt (London and Home Counties) Act 1938 allowed local authorities to buy land for protection. The Greater London Plan of 1944 by Patrick Abercrombie proposed a continuous belt, and a 1955 government circular by Duncan Sandys invited local authorities nationwide to establish green belts; London's belt was progressively approved through county development plans.

Extent and rules

The belt surrounds Greater London, extending into Hertfordshire, Surrey, Kent, Buckinghamshire, Berkshire and Essex, typically 10–25 km deep. National planning policy presumes strongly against "inappropriate development" in the green belt, permitting it only in very special circumstances. About 60% of the belt is farmland.

Debate

Supporters credit the belt with preserving countryside and encouraging urban regeneration on brownfield sites. Critics argue it inflates housing costs in the South East, forces development to leapfrog into longer commutes, and protects low-quality farmland while blocking needed homes — a recurring controversy in British planning politics.
