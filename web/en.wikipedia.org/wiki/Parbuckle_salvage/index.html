Parbuckle salvage

Parbuckle salvage, or parbuckling, is the righting of a sunken vessel using rotational leverage: cables or chains passed under the hull are pulled so that the ship rolls upright, like a barrel rolled by a rope passed around it (the parbuckle). The technique is used when a vessel has capsized and rests on its side or upside down on the seabed.

Method

To parbuckle a wreck, salvors first make the hull watertight and fit attachment points, often large sponsons — steel buoyancy tanks welded to the raised side of the ship. These serve both as lifting points for the pulling cables and as flotation that helps the vessel remain upright once righted. Winches on barges or on shore then haul on the cables in a carefully controlled sequence, rotating the hull slowly about its keel until it floats upright, after which it can be pumped out and refloated.

The torque required is greatest at the start of the rotation, so the sponsons and the geometry of the pull are designed to maximise leverage. Hold-back cables are fitted on the opposite side to prevent the ship from rolling too far once it passes the upright position.

Notable examples

Famous parbuckling operations include the righting of the USS Oklahoma at Pearl Harbor in 1943, in which 21 electric winches on Ford Island rolled the capsized battleship upright, and the salvage of the cruise ship Costa Concordia off Giglio Island, Italy. The Costa Concordia parbuckling in September 2013 — a rotation lasting some 19 hours using strand jacks, cables and sponson tanks — was described as the largest such operation ever attempted on a ship of that size (114,000 gross tons).
