The Home Army (Polish: Armia Krajowa, abbreviated AK) was the dominant resistance movement in German-occupied Poland during World War II. Formed in February 1942 from the earlier Union of Armed Struggle (ZWZ), it was loyal to the Polish government-in-exile in London and became one of the largest underground armies in occupied Europe, with a sworn membership estimated at 350,000 to 400,000 by 1944, in addition to many more supporters.

Home Army soldiers in Warsaw during the 1944 uprising.

Operations

The Home Army conducted sabotage, intelligence, and guerrilla operations against the German occupation. Its intelligence network supplied the Western Allies with valuable information, including reports on the Holocaust — such as those smuggled out by Witold Pilecki — and on German secret weapons. AK units carried out assassinations of Gestapo officers, derailed transports, and freed prisoners. Its counter-intelligence and diversionary arm, Kedyw, handled special operations.

Warsaw Uprising

The AK's largest operation was the Warsaw Uprising, launched on 1 August 1944 as part of Operation Tempest, an attempt to liberate the capital before the arrival of the Red Army. After 63 days of fighting, and with Soviet forces halted across the Vistula, the uprising was crushed; much of Warsaw was systematically destroyed and a large share of its civilian population killed or expelled.

Dissolution and aftermath

As Soviet forces advanced into Poland, AK soldiers were often arrested by the NKVD despite having fought the Germans. The last commander, Leopold Okulicki, ordered the Home Army's dissolution on 19 January 1945. Many former members were persecuted under the post-war communist authorities, and some continued resistance in anti-communist formations ("cursed soldiers"). The Home Army's role was officially rehabilitated in Polish public memory after 1989.
