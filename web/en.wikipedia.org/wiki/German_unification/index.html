German unification

This article is about the unification of Germany in 1871. For the reunification of East and West Germany in 1990, see German reunification.

The unification of Germany was a process of building the first nation-state for Germans with federal features, based on the concept of Lesser Germany (excluding Austria). It commenced on August 1, 1864, with the formation of a confederation and ended with the proclamation of the German Empire on January 18, 1871, in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles.

Background

After the Napoleonic Wars, the Congress of Vienna (1815) created the German Confederation of 39 states, dominated by the rivalry between Austria and Prussia. The Zollverein customs union (1834) integrated much of the German economy under Prussian leadership, excluding Austria. Liberal nationalists attempted unification during the Revolutions of 1848, but the Frankfurt Parliament's offer of a crown was refused by Frederick William IV of Prussia, and the effort collapsed.

Bismarck and the wars of unification

Appointed Minister President of Prussia in 1862, Otto von Bismarck pursued unification through "blood and iron" — diplomacy backed by military force. Three wars accomplished it:

  • Second Schleswig War (1864): Prussia and Austria jointly defeated Denmark and took Schleswig and Holstein.
  • Austro-Prussian War (1866): Prussia's victory at Königgrätz excluded Austria from German affairs; the North German Confederation was formed under Prussian leadership.
  • Franco-Prussian War (1870–1871): Provoked in part by the Ems Dispatch, the war united the southern German states with the north; Napoleon III was captured at Sedan, and German princes proclaimed Wilhelm I German Emperor at Versailles.

Consequences

The German Empire, a federal state of 25 constituent states dominated by Prussia, rapidly became Europe's leading industrial and military power, upsetting the continental balance of power and setting the conditions that eventually led to World War I.
