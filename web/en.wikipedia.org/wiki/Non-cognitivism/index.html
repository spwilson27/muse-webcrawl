Non-cognitivism

Non-cognitivism is the meta-ethical view that ethical sentences do not express propositions and therefore cannot be true or false (they are not truth-apt). A noncognitivist denies that moral judgments are beliefs about moral facts; instead they express attitudes, emotions, prescriptions, or other non-cognitive mental states. Non-cognitivism stands in contrast to cognitivism, which holds that moral sentences do express propositions that can be true or false.

Varieties

  • Emotivism — associated with A. J. Ayer and C. L. Stevenson — holds that moral utterances express the speaker's feelings and aim to influence others: "Stealing is wrong" amounts roughly to "I disapprove of stealing; do so as well."
  • Prescriptivism — developed by R. M. Hare — treats moral judgments as universalizable imperatives or prescriptions.
  • Expressivism — in the work of Simon Blackburn (quasi-realism) and Allan Gibbard (norm-expressivism) — explains moral language as expressing states of mind such as plans, attitudes, and acceptance of norms, while trying to earn the right to talk of moral "truth" in a deflated sense.

Arguments and objections

Non-cognitivists appeal to the motivational force of moral judgment (the link between judging an act right and being moved to do it), to Hume's observation that reason alone does not motivate, and to the "open question" intuitions against defining moral terms naturally. The chief technical objection is the Frege–Geach problem: moral sentences occur in unasserted contexts — in conditionals ("If stealing is wrong, then getting your brother to steal is wrong") and negations — where no attitude is being expressed, yet they must keep a constant meaning for moral arguments to be valid. Expressivists have devoted much work to solving this problem of embedding.

The distinction between cognitivism and non-cognitivism also arises outside ethics, in discussions of modality, probability, and conditionals.
