Science of reading

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The science of reading is an interdisciplinary body of research from cognitive psychology, linguistics, education and neuroscience concerning how humans learn to read and how reading should be taught. In education policy — especially in the United States — the term also denotes a reform movement advocating instruction grounded in this research, in particular systematic phonics in the early grades, in contrast to approaches such as whole language and balanced literacy.

Overview

Reading research converges on several findings:

  • Decoding and comprehension are distinct but interdependent. The "Simple View of Reading" (Gough and Tunmer, 1986) models reading comprehension as the product of decoding (word recognition) and language comprehension: weakness in either limits the whole.
  • Word recognition depends on phonological awareness and letter–sound knowledge. Learning that spoken words are made of phonemes, and mapping letters to those sounds (the alphabetic principle), is the gateway to independent reading in alphabetic languages.
  • Skilled readers process nearly every letter of words. Eye-movement research shows fluent readers do not guess words from pictures or context; context supports comprehension, not word identification.
  • Orthographic mapping (Ehri) describes how repeated decoding bonds spellings to pronunciations and meanings, building the sight vocabulary that makes reading fluent.

Scarborough's "Reading Rope" (2001) is a widely used model weaving together word-recognition strands (phonological awareness, decoding, sight recognition) with language-comprehension strands (background knowledge, vocabulary, language structures, verbal reasoning, literacy knowledge).

The "reading wars"

Disputes over beginning reading instruction — the so-called reading wars — stretch back more than a century. Whole-language approaches, dominant in many schools from the 1970s, treated reading as a natural process best learned through immersion in authentic texts, with phonics taught incidentally. Research reviews, including the U.S. National Reading Panel (2000) and the Rose Review in England (2006), concluded that systematic synthetic phonics produces better outcomes, especially for at-risk readers. Nevertheless many classrooms continued to use "three-cueing" (guessing from meaning, structure and visual cues), popularised by widely used curricula.

Policy movement

From the 2010s, journalism — notably Emily Hanford's reporting for APM, including the 2022 podcast Sold a Story — brought the research consensus to wide public attention. Dozens of U.S. states passed "science of reading" laws mandating evidence-based curricula, phonics screening, teacher retraining and bans on three-cueing; Mississippi's 2013 Literacy-Based Promotion Act and its subsequent rise in national fourth-grade reading scores were widely cited as a model. Similar shifts occurred in parts of Canada, Australia and elsewhere. Critics of the movement caution that it can narrow instruction, neglect comprehension and writing, or become a marketing label; advocates reply that the research base is broad and includes vocabulary, fluency and knowledge-building.
