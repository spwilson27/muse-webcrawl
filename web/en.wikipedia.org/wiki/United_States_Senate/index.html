United States Senate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United States Senate is the upper chamber of the United States Congress, the legislature of the federal government of the United States, alongside the lower chamber, the House of Representatives. The Senate is composed of senators, each of whom represents a single state in its entirety. Each of the 50 states is equally represented by two senators who serve staggered terms of six years, for a total of 100 senators — a design (equal state suffrage) that was the Connecticut Compromise's price for union and that the Constitution uniquely entrench­es against amendment without the affected state's consent.

Powers and procedure

Beyond sharing ordinary legislation with the House, the Senate holds exclusive powers: advice and consent to treaties (by two-thirds) and to presidential appointments (judges, cabinet officers, ambassadors), and the trial of impeachments (conviction by two-thirds). The Vice President serves as its president, voting only to break ties; day-to-day presiding falls to the president pro tempore and junior majority members. The Senate's rules prize individual prerogative — unlimited debate checked only by cloture (the filibuster), unanimous-consent agreements, and individual holds — making it the more deliberative, and often the more obstructive, chamber. Senators were chosen by state legislatures until the Seventeenth Amendment (1913) mandated direct election. Its small size, longer terms and statewide constituencies give it a distinctive political culture and make it the usual springboard to presidential candidacies.
