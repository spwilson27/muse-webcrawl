Seon

Seon meditation at a Korean Buddhist temple.

Seon (Korean: 선, 禪) is the Korean form of Zen Buddhism, the Mahayana Buddhist tradition emphasizing meditation and direct insight into one's true nature. It derives from Chinese Chan Buddhism, itself tracing its lineage to the Indian practice of dhyana (meditation). Introduced to Korea during the Silla period, Seon became one of the principal schools of Korean Buddhism and remains its dominant form today, principally through the Jogye Order.

History

Chan teachings first reached Korea in the seventh to ninth centuries, when Korean monks who had studied in Tang-dynasty China returned home. During the late Silla period, the Nine Mountains school (Gusan Seonmun) arose, named for the mountain monasteries where the various Seon lineages were based. Seon flourished alongside the doctrinal (Gyo) schools, though relations between meditation and scholastic traditions were often tense.

The Goryeo-dynasty monk Jinul (1158–1210) is regarded as the great systematizer of Korean Seon. In response to corruption and doctrinal division, he founded the Samadhi and Prajna Society and advocated a synthesis of Seon meditation with scriptural study, teaching "sudden awakening followed by gradual cultivation." His methods, including the hwadu (critical phrase) meditation derived from Chinese huatou practice, became characteristic of Korean Seon.

During the Joseon dynasty, Buddhism was suppressed in favor of Neo-Confucianism, and monks were largely confined to mountain temples. Figures such as Hyujeong (Seosan Daesa), who organized monk militias during the Japanese invasions of the 1590s, kept the tradition alive. Under Japanese colonial rule (1910–1945), Korean Buddhism was reorganized along Japanese lines, including the introduction of married clergy; after liberation, a celibate monastic movement re-established itself as the Jogye Order (founded 1962), now the largest Buddhist order in South Korea.

Practice

The heart of Seon practice is meditation, pursued in intensive retreats (gyeoljae) during the summer and winter seasons at training temples. The characteristic Korean method is Ganhwa Seon, the investigation of a hwadu — a phrase or question such as "What is this?" — given by a teacher and contemplated until doubt and effort culminate in awakening. Masters such as Seongcheol (1912–1993) emphasized rigorous, uninterrupted practice and sudden enlightenment.

Lay participation has grown through temple stay programs, which introduce visitors to meditation, monastic meals, and temple life, and Seon teachers including the monk Pomnyun have achieved wide public followings. Korean Seon has also been transmitted abroad, notably through Seungsahn, founder of the international Kwan Um School of Zen.
