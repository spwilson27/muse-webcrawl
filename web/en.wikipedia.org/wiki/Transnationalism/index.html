Transnationalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transnationalism is a social phenomenon and scholarly research agenda grown out of the heightened interconnectivity between individuals, communities and institutions across national borders, driven by globalisation and international migration. The term is commonly used for the sustained ties of migrants to their countries of origin — economic, social and political — maintained alongside integration into the countries where they settle.

Origins of the concept

Although the word was used earlier, notably by Randolph Bourne in 1916, the modern research programme was launched by anthropologists Nina Glick Schiller, Linda Basch and Cristina Szanton Blanc in the early 1990s, who defined transnational migration as the process by which immigrants forge and sustain simultaneous multi-stranded social relations linking their societies of origin and settlement.

Forms

Economic transnationalism includes remittances — which now exceed foreign aid several times over for many countries — migrant entrepreneurship and "circular" trade. Political transnationalism includes dual citizenship, diaspora voting, and hometown associations funding public works in origin communities. Cultural transnationalism covers media flows, religious networks and the circulation of ideas and identities.

Debates

Critics ask whether transnational practices are genuinely new or simply made more visible by cheap communication and travel, and whether they persist into the second generation. Scholars distinguish transnationalism "from above" — corporations, states and global institutions — from transnationalism "from below" among migrants and grassroots movements.
