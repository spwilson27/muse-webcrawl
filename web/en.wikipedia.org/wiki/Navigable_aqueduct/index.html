Navigable aqueduct

A boat crossing the Pontcysyllte Aqueduct, Wales

Navigable aqueducts (sometimes called water bridges) are bridge structures that carry navigable waterways — canals or channels — across rivers, valleys, roads or other canals, so that boats and barges can pass. Unlike classical aqueducts, which merely convey water for supply, a navigable aqueduct must support the full weight of the water channel and the vessels using it, usually in an iron, steel or masonry trough.

Notable examples

  • Pontcysyllte Aqueduct (Wales, 1805): Thomas Telford's cast-iron trough carried 336 yards (307 m) at 126 ft (38 m) above the River Dee; a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
  • Barton Swing Aqueduct (England, 1893): a swing bridge carrying the Bridgewater Canal over the Manchester Ship Canal — the only swing aqueduct in the world.
  • Magdeburg Water Bridge (Germany, 2003): at 918 m the world's longest navigable aqueduct, carrying the Mittelland Canal over the Elbe.
  • Briare Aqueduct (France, 1896): Gustave Eiffel-associated metal trough carrying the Canal latéral à la Loire over the Loire.
  • Edstone Aqueduct (England, 1816): the longest cast-iron aqueduct in England, on the Stratford-upon-Avon Canal.
  • Håverud Aqueduct (Sweden): a riveted steel trough on the Dalsland Canal, built in 1868.

Design

The defining engineering trick is that a boat displaces its own weight of water, so the trough carries roughly the same load whether or not a vessel is crossing. Aqueducts may be fixed or movable (swing or lift) and are usually built with towpaths for horses or pedestrians alongside the channel.
