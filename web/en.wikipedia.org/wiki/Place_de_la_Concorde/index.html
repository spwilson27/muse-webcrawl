Place de la Concorde

The Place de la Concorde (French pronunciation: [plas də la kɔ̃kɔʁd]; lit. "Concord Square") is one of the major public squares in Paris, France. Measuring 7.6 ha (19 acres) in area, it is the largest square in the French capital. It is located in the city's 8th arrondissement, at the eastern end of the Champs-Élysées. It was the site of many notable public executions, including those of the deposed King Louis XVI, Marie Antoinette and Maximilien Robespierre in the course of the French Revolution, during which the square was temporarily renamed the Place de la Révolution ("Revolution Square").

History

Design and construction

The square was designed by the architect Ange-Jacques Gabriel in 1755 as a moat-skirted octagon between the Champs-Élysées to the west and the Tuileries Garden to the east. Decorated with statues and fountains, it was named the Place Louis XV to honour the king. At its centre stood an equestrian statue of Louis XV, commissioned in 1748 and unveiled in 1763. The statue was torn down during the Revolution.

At the north end, two magnificent identical stone buildings were constructed, separated by the Rue Royale. Designed by Gabriel, they were initially royal storehouses; the eastern one became the Hôtel de la Marine, long the headquarters of the French Navy, while the western one is the Hôtel de Crillon, today a luxury hotel. A matching building to the south was never built; instead, across the Seine, the square opens toward the Palais Bourbon, seat of the French National Assembly.

The French Revolution

During the French Revolution the square was renamed the Place de la Révolution and a guillotine was erected at its centre. It was here that King Louis XVI was executed on 21 January 1793, and Marie Antoinette on 16 October 1793. During the Reign of Terror, more than 1,100 people were guillotined in the square, including Maximilien Robespierre himself on 28 July 1794. In 1795, seeking reconciliation after the Terror, the government renamed the square Place de la Concorde — "Concord Square" — a name it has kept, with interruptions, ever since.

Nineteenth century

In 1836, the Luxor Obelisk, a gift from Muhammad Ali Pasha, the Ottoman viceroy of Egypt, was raised at the centre of the square. The obelisk, once marking the entrance to the Luxor Temple in Egypt, is covered with hieroglyphs exalting the reign of Pharaoh Ramesses II. It is 23 metres (75 ft) high, weighs over 250 tonnes, and is topped by a gold-leafed pyramidion added in 1998. Between 1836 and 1846 the architect Jacques Ignace Hittorff added the square's two monumental fountains — the Fountain of the Seas and the Fountain of the Rivers — modelled on those of St. Peter's Square in Rome, as well as the ornate lampposts that still light the square. Eight statues representing French cities (Brest, Rouen, Lille, Strasbourg, Lyon, Marseille, Bordeaux and Nantes) stand at the corners of the octagon.

Modern era

The square remains a focal point of Parisian life and ceremony. It lies on the historic east–west axis of Paris, running from the Louvre through the Tuileries and the Champs-Élysées to the Arc de Triomphe and La Défense. For the 2024 Summer Olympics, the square hosted the urban sports venue for skateboarding, BMX freestyle, breaking and 3x3 basketball. In the 2020s, plans advanced to pedestrianise and green much of the square, reducing traffic around its edges.

Description

The square is organised around the Luxor Obelisk. To the north and south of it stand Hittorff's two fountains. The Fountain of the Seas (Fontaine des Mers), to the south, commemorates maritime commerce and industry; the Fountain of the Rivers (Fontaine des Fleuves), to the north, celebrates river navigation and agriculture. Around the square, the moat of the original design has been filled in, and the square is divided by some of Paris's busiest traffic arteries.
