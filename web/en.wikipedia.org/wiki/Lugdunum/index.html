Lugdunum (also Lugudunum) was an important Roman city in Gaul, founded in 43 BC on the Fourvière hill above the confluence of the Rhône and Saône rivers. It is the ancestor of the modern French city of Lyon. The name is generally interpreted as "fortress of Lugus," after the Celtic god Lugus.

History

Lucius Munatius Plancus founded the colony for Roman refugees expelled from Vienne. Lugdunum quickly became the capital of the province of Gallia Lugdunensis and the administrative and commercial hub of the Three Gauls. Augustus made it the seat of the federal sanctuary of the Three Gauls, where delegates of sixty Gallic peoples met annually from 12 BC around the altar of Rome and Augustus. The emperors Claudius and Caracalla were born there; Claudius's speech admitting Gauls to the Senate was delivered at Lugdunum and survives in part on the bronze Lyon Tablet.

The city

At its height in the 2nd century AD, Lugdunum had perhaps 50,000–80,000 inhabitants. It possessed a theatre and odeon on Fourvière, an amphitheatre, baths, aqueducts (including the great Gier aqueduct), an imperial mint, and a famous temple of Cybele. It was also an early centre of Christianity in Gaul: in AD 177, Christians of Lugdunum and Vienne suffered a notorious persecution in which the slave Blandina and bishop Pothinus were martyred. Irenaeus, Pothinus's successor, became one of the early Church's leading theologians.

Decline

The city suffered in the civil wars of the 3rd century — it was sacked after the Battle of Lugdunum in AD 197, one of the largest battles of the Roman civil wars, won by Septimius Severus — and declined as the aqueducts failed and settlement shifted to the river plains, though it remained a bishopric into the medieval period.
