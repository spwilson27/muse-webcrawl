Galloway (Scottish Gaelic: Gall-Ghàidhealaibh [ˈkal̪ˠ ɣaːl̪ˠəv]; Scots: Gallowa; Latin: Gallovidia) is a region in southwestern Scotland comprising the historic counties of Wigtownshire and Kirkcudbrightshire. It is administered as part of the council area of Dumfries and Galloway. The region's name derives from the Gall-Ghàidheil, the "foreign Gaels" — Norse-Gaelic settlers who dominated the area in the early medieval period.

Geography

Galloway stretches from the Rhins of Galloway peninsula in the west — terminating at the Mull of Galloway, Scotland's most southerly point — to the Nith estuary in the east. The interior is a landscape of rolling moorland, lochs and granite hills (the Galloway Hills, including the Merrick), while the Solway Firth coast features cliffs, bays and the Machars and Stewartry lowlands. The Galloway Forest Park, designated in 2009 as a Dark Sky Park, covers much of the upland core.

History

Early medieval Galloway was a semi-independent lordship ruled by figures such as Fergus of Galloway (d. 1161), whose descendants contested Scottish royal authority into the 13th century. The region was the cradle of early Christianity in Scotland: St Ninian founded Candida Casa at Whithorn around 397, traditionally Scotland's first church. Galloway retained a distinctive Gaelic dialect until the 18th century. In the 20th century the Galloway hydro-electric scheme dammed the River Ken and Dee, and commercial forestry transformed the uplands.

Economy and culture

Agriculture (dairy and beef — the Belted Galloway cattle breed originates here), forestry, fishing and tourism dominate. Wigtown is Scotland's National Book Town; Kirkcudbright is known as the "Artists' Town". The region gave its name to the Galloway pony and features in the novels of S. R. Crockett and John Buchan.
