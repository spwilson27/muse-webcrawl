Juniper berry

TypeModified female seed cone (galbulus)
UsesSpice; flavouring for gin
Principal source regionsItaly, Balkans, Scandinavia, North America

A juniper berry is the female seed cone produced by the various species of juniper. It is not a true berry, but a cone with unusually fleshy and merged scales, which give it a berry-like appearance. The cones of most species are inedible or too bitter to eat; those of Juniperus communis (common juniper) are used as a spice, and are the essential flavouring of gin.

Description

The berries of J. communis are 4–12 mm in diameter, green when young, ripening over 18 months to a dark blue-black with a waxy bloom, and contain usually three hard seeds. Other species ripen more quickly, in 8–10 months.

Uses

Juniper berries flavour gin — the spirit's name derives from the Dutch jenever and the French genièvre, both meaning juniper — as well as some beers, including Finnish sahti. In the kitchen their sharp, resinous, pine-like taste seasons game, pork, sauerkraut and cabbage dishes in German, Austrian and Scandinavian cooking, and they are a component of marinades for wild boar and venison.

Traditional medicine and toxicity

The berries and their essential oil have been used in folk medicine as a diuretic and digestive aid. Juniper berries of some species, notably J. sabina (savin), are toxic and should not be consumed. Culinary use of common juniper berries is considered safe in food amounts, though large doses and use during pregnancy are advised against.
