Backstrap loom

A Maya weaver using a backstrap loom
TypeHand loom
RegionsMesoamerica, Andes, Southeast Asia, Japan
Other namesHip loom, body-tension loom

A backstrap loom, also called a hip loom or body-tension loom, is a simple, portable loom in which one end of the warp is attached to a fixed support such as a post or tree and the other end is held by a strap passing around the weaver's lower back or hips. The weaver controls warp tension by leaning backward or forward, and opens the shed with heddle rods and a shed stick. The design is ancient and remains in use in Mesoamerica, the Andes, Southeast Asia, and parts of East Asia.

Construction and use

The loom consists of little more than sticks, cords, a sword beater, and the backstrap itself, yet it can produce finely patterned cloth. Warp threads are wound between two bars; a heddle rod lifts alternate threads to form one shed, while a shed roll forms the counter-shed. Weft is inserted with a shuttle or bobbin and beaten in with a wooden sword. By leaning back, the weaver tensions the warp for beating; leaning forward slackens it to change sheds. Brocade and warp-faced patterning techniques allow complex designs.

History and distribution

Backstrap weaving predates the Spanish conquest in the Americas: tomb paintings and ceramics show Maya weavers using the loom, and it remains central to textile traditions in Guatemala, Mexico, Peru, and Bolivia, where garments such as the huipil are woven in panels and sewn together. In Asia, similar looms are used by peoples of southern China, Vietnam, Indonesia, and the Philippines, and historically in Japan for narrow bands. Because the loom is cheap, light, and easily dismantled, it suits household production; its width, however, is limited by the weaver's reach, so wide cloth must be joined from several panels.

Cultural significance

In many communities weaving on the backstrap loom is women's work, passed from mother to daughter, and the patterns woven encode community identity and cosmology. Maya weaving cooperatives market backstrap-woven textiles internationally, and the craft is recognized as intangible cultural heritage in several countries.
