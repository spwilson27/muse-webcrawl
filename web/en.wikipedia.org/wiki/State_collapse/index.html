State collapse

State collapse (or state failure, in the extreme case) is a process in political science describing the disintegration of a state's governmental authority, in which the central government loses the ability to exercise effective control over its territory, enforce law, provide basic services and maintain a monopoly on the legitimate use of force. A collapsed state differs from a merely weak or "fragile" state in that the institutions of government effectively cease to function, leaving a vacuum often filled by warlords, insurgencies, criminal networks, clan or communal authorities, or foreign actors.

Causes

Scholars attribute collapse to combinations of factors: prolonged economic decline and fiscal crisis; loss of legitimacy and elite fragmentation; ethnic, religious or regional polarisation exploited by armed entrepreneurs; the legacy of colonial borders and institutions; dependence on a single export commodity or foreign patron whose support disappears (as happened to several Cold War client states after 1989); and civil war or external intervention. In his classic study, William Zartman framed collapse as a situation in which the structure, authority, law and political order of the state have fallen apart and must be reconstituted in some form.

Examples

The canonical modern case is Somalia, where the fall of Siad Barre in 1991 destroyed central government for two decades, producing famine, piracy and the eventual emergence of the self-declared (unrecognised) Republic of Somaliland and a fragile federal government. Other frequently discussed cases include Liberia and Sierra Leone in the 1990s, the Democratic Republic of the Congo after the fall of Mobutu, Afghanistan at various points, Yemen after 2014, Libya after 2011, and Haiti in the 2020s. Historical cases include the disintegration of Yugoslavia and Lebanon's civil-war paralysis (1975–1990).

Consequences and responses

State collapse is associated with humanitarian emergencies, mass displacement, transnational crime and terrorism havens, and regional instability. International responses have included peacekeeping, humanitarian intervention, trusteeship-like administrations (as in Kosovo and East Timor) and long-term state-building programmes, whose record is widely debated. Analysts caution that the label "failed state" can conceal resilient informal institutions and local forms of order that persist beneath the collapse of the formal state.
