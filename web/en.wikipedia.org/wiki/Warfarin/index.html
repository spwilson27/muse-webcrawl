Warfarin, sold under the brand name Coumadin among others, is an anticoagulant medication. It is commonly used to prevent blood clots such as deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, and to prevent stroke in people who have atrial fibrillation, valvular heart disease or artificial heart valves.

Medical uses

Warfarin decreases blood clotting and is widely used for long-term anticoagulation. Because the appropriate dose varies widely between individuals, therapy is guided by measuring the international normalized ratio (INR), typically targeting a range of 2.0–3.0 for most indications.

Mechanism of action

Warfarin inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1), preventing regeneration of reduced vitamin K. This depletes the active forms of clotting factors II, VII, IX and X, as well as proteins C and S, thereby reducing the blood's ability to form clots.

Adverse effects

The principal risk is bleeding, which is increased by many drug and dietary interactions. Warfarin crosses the placenta and can cause birth defects, so it is generally avoided in pregnancy. Its effects can be reversed with vitamin K, fresh frozen plasma or prothrombin complex concentrate.

History

The drug's origins lie in the investigation of a haemorrhagic disease of cattle in the 1920s, traced to mouldy sweet clover hay. Karl Paul Link's laboratory at the University of Wisconsin isolated the anticoagulant dicoumarol, and in 1948 synthesised the more potent warfarin — named for the Wisconsin Alumni Research Foundation (WARF) plus "-arin" from coumarin. It entered clinical use in 1954; one of its most famous early patients was U.S. President Dwight D. Eisenhower.

Use as a rodenticide

Warfarin was first marketed as a rat poison, exploiting its slow, cumulative anticoagulant action. Resistance in rodent populations later led to "superwarfarins" such as brodifacoum.
