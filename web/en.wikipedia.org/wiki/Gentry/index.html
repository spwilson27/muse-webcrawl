Gentry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The gentry (from Old French genterie, based on Latin gens, "clan, family") are "well-born, genteel and well-bred people" of high social class, especially in the past. In England, the term came to denote the class of landowners below the peerage — the landed gentry — who lived on the income of their estates without needing to work, and who were entitled to bear coats of arms. Together with the peerage they formed the traditional ruling class of the countryside.

Structure in England

Below the titled nobility, the gentry were conventionally ranked as baronets, knights, esquires, and gentlemen. Their wealth lay in agricultural land let to tenant farmers. From the sixteenth century the gentry grew in numbers and power as monastic and crown lands were sold off after the Dissolution of the Monasteries, and their representatives filled the House of Commons, served as justices of the peace, and administered local government. Historians have long debated a supposed "rise of the gentry" before the English Civil War — the "storm over the gentry" controversy associated with R. H. Tawney, Lawrence Stone, and their critics.

Comparable classes

Similar strata existed elsewhere: the Polish szlachta, the French provincial nobility and noblesse de robe, and the Prussian Junkers. In late imperial China, the gentry (shenshi) were scholar-officials and degree-holders — families that had passed the imperial examinations — who mediated between the state and local society, managed public works, and enjoyed legal privileges; Western historians apply the word to them by analogy with the English class. In the antebellum American South, the planter class is sometimes described as a gentry.

Decline

The British landed gentry's economic position eroded from the late nineteenth century with the agricultural depression, death duties, and the great land sales of 1918–1921, in which millions of acres changed hands. The term survives in works such as Burke's Landed Gentry and in the broader concept of "gentry" as a polite, propertied social stratum; "gentry" also gives us "gentrification", the displacement of poorer residents by wealthier newcomers to a district.
