Learning theory (education)

Learning theory describes how students receive, process, and retain knowledge during learning. Cognitive, emotional, and environmental influences, as well as prior experience, all play a part in how understanding, or a world view, is acquired or changed and knowledge and skills retained. Learning theories are conceptual frameworks that structure educational research and practice.

Behaviourism

Behaviourism, associated with John B. Watson, B. F. Skinner, and Ivan Pavlov, treats learning as a change in observable behaviour produced by conditioning: classical conditioning links a stimulus to a response, while operant conditioning shapes behaviour through reinforcement and punishment. Instructional descendants include programmed instruction, mastery learning, and direct instruction with immediate feedback.

Cognitivism

Cognitivism shifted attention to internal mental processes — perception, memory, and problem solving — viewing the learner as an information processor. Key figures include Jean Piaget, whose stage theory described children's developing schemas, and Jerome Bruner, who emphasised discovery and the spiral curriculum. Cognitive load theory and information-processing models inform modern instructional design.

Constructivism

Constructivism holds that learners actively build knowledge from experience rather than passively receiving it. Lev Vygotsky's social constructivism stressed language, culture, and the zone of proximal development — the gap between what a learner can do alone and with guidance — from which the concept of scaffolding derives. Constructivist approaches include inquiry-based and problem-based learning.

Other perspectives

Humanistic theories (Maslow, Rogers) emphasise self-direction and the learner's whole experience. Social learning theory (Bandura) adds observation and modelling. More recent frameworks include connectivism, which describes learning in networked digital environments, and experiential learning theory, summarised in Kolb's learning cycle.
