Kautilya, also identified with Chanakya and Vishnugupta, was an ancient Indian teacher, philosopher, economist, jurist, and royal adviser. He is traditionally credited as the author of the Arthashastra, the ancient Indian treatise on statecraft, economic policy, and military strategy, and — in later legend — as the strategist who destroyed the Nanda dynasty and raised Chandragupta Maurya to power, founding the Mauryan Empire around 321 BC.

The Arthashastra

The Arthashastra is a comprehensive manual of government: the training and protection of the king, the organisation of ministries and the civil service, taxation and the treasury, law and punishment, foreign policy (the "circle of states", mandala theory, in which the neighbour is the natural enemy and the neighbour's neighbour the natural friend), espionage networks, and the conduct of war and diplomacy. The text is strikingly pragmatic, treating the welfare and security of the state as the supreme good. The work was lost for centuries and dramatically rediscovered in 1905, when a manuscript was identified in Mysore and published by R. Shamasastry. Modern scholarship generally treats the text as layered, compiled and redacted over several centuries, while preserving a core associated with the Kautilya tradition.

The Chanakya legend

In the later Sanskrit drama Mudrarakshasa and in Jain and Buddhist traditions, Chanakya appears as the wily Brahmin minister who, insulted by the Nanda king, vows revenge, discovers and educates the young Chandragupta, and engineers the Nandas' overthrow through intrigue. Sayings on politics and conduct attributed to him (Chanakya Niti) remain popular in India, where he is frequently compared to Machiavelli as a theorist of realpolitik.
