Canoe

A canoe is a lightweight, narrow watercraft, typically pointed at both ends and open on top, propelled by one or more seated or kneeling paddlers facing the direction of travel and using a single-bladed paddle. Canoes are among the oldest boat types in the world and remain widely used for recreation, sport, transport, and fishing.

History

The oldest known canoe, the Pesse canoe found in the Netherlands, dates to around 8000 BC. Dugout canoes were developed independently by Indigenous peoples worldwide. In North America, the birchbark canoe of the Algonquian peoples — a light frame covered with birch bark, sewn with spruce root and sealed with gum — became the workhorse of the fur trade, scaled up in the great canots de maître of the voyageurs. Pacific peoples developed sophisticated outrigger canoes capable of ocean voyaging and settlement of distant islands.

Design and construction

Traditional canoes were dugouts carved from a single log or skin-and-frame boats. Modern canoes are built from aluminium, moulded plastic, fibreglass, Kevlar, and carbon fibre, or from wood using strip, stitch-and-glue, or wood-and-canvas construction. Hull shapes vary: flat bottoms favour stability for fishing, rounded and V-shaped hulls trade initial stability for speed and tracking, and pronounced "rocker" improves manoeuvrability in whitewater.

Use

Recreational canoeing includes flatwater touring and canoe camping, whitewater canoeing, and marathon racing. Canoe sprint and canoe slalom are Olympic disciplines governed by the International Canoe Federation; in Olympic terminology "canoe" denotes the kneeling, single-blade disciplines, as distinct from kayaking. The distinction in common usage is straightforward: in a canoe the paddler kneels or sits and uses a single-bladed paddle, while in a kayak the paddler sits with legs extended and uses a double-bladed paddle.
