David Kellogg Lewis (28 September 1941 – 14 October 2001) was an American philosopher widely regarded as one of the most important analytic philosophers of the late 20th century. He taught first at UCLA and then for most of his career at Princeton University.

Work

Lewis is best known for modal realism, defended in On the Plurality of Worlds (1986): the thesis that all possible worlds are as real as the actual world, and that "actual" is merely indexical, like "here". His counterpart theory analysed modal claims in terms of counterparts in other worlds rather than trans-world identity. In metaphysics he championed Humean supervenience — roughly, that all there is in the world is a spatiotemporal mosaic of local matters of fact.

His first book, Convention (1969), gave a game-theoretic analysis of social convention that reshaped philosophy of language. His paper "Causation" (1973) founded the counterfactual theory of causation. Lewis also wrote on semantics, philosophy of mind, decision theory, and the philosophy of probability, including the Principal Principle linking chance and credence.

Legacy

Lewis died suddenly of complications from diabetes in 2001. His systematic, bold style set an agenda for contemporary analytic metaphysics; polls of philosophers regularly rank him among the most influential philosophers since the Second World War.
