Great Books of the Western World is a series of books originally published in the United States in 1952 by Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc., presenting the Western canon in a single package of 54 volumes. The series grew out of the great books movement championed by Robert Maynard Hutchins, president of the University of Chicago, and philosopher Mortimer J. Adler, who served as associate editor and compiled the Syntopicon, a two-volume topical index to the ideas in the set.

Contents

The first edition collected works by 74 authors ranging from Homer, the Bible, Aeschylus, Plato and Aristotle through Virgil, Augustine, Aquinas, Dante, Chaucer, Machiavelli, Shakespeare, Cervantes, Galileo, Newton, Locke, Hume, Rousseau, Kant, Gibbon, Darwin, Marx, Tolstoy and Freud. The first volume, The Great Conversation, was Hutchins's essay on the value of the canon; volumes 2–3 contained the Syntopicon, organising the contents under 102 "Great Ideas" such as Truth, Good and Evil, God, and Liberty.

Second edition (1990)

A revised 60-volume edition appeared in 1990 under Adler's editorship, expanding to 130 authors. Six volumes of additional 20th-century material added works by authors including James Joyce, Virginia Woolf, Franz Kafka, Bertolt Brecht, Willa Cather and others, and some earlier translations were replaced. Critics have long debated the selection's Eurocentrism and the near-absence of women and non-Western authors, a controversy the second edition tried, partly, to address.

Reception and sales

The set was marketed door-to-door and to middle-class households as an instrument of self-education, and sold widely despite mixed scholarly reviews. It remains a landmark publishing project and the most ambitious popular presentation of the Western literary and philosophical canon.
