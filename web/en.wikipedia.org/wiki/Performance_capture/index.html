Performance capture is a form of motion capture in which an actor's full performance — body movement, facial expressions and voice — is recorded simultaneously and used to drive a digital character. The term is used to emphasise that what is captured is not merely movement but the actor's complete performance, in contrast to traditional animation, where a character's motion and expressions are created by animators keyframe by keyframe.

Technique

Actors wear suits fitted with reflective or LED markers and perform in a capture volume surrounded by cameras that triangulate marker positions in three dimensions. A head-mounted camera rig records facial markers or markerless facial data, allowing expressions, lip movements and eye lines to be mapped onto the digital character. The performance is recorded together with the actor's voice, and reference cameras document the take for animators, who refine the raw solve in post-production.

History

Motion capture was used in film from the 1990s, but performance capture as a named practice rose to prominence with Andy Serkis's portrayal of Gollum in Peter Jackson's The Lord of the Rings trilogy (2001–2003), developed with Weta Digital. Robert Zemeckis used the technique extensively in The Polar Express (2004) and Beowulf (2007). James Cameron's Avatar (2009) introduced a head-mounted facial camera and virtual cinematography, letting directors view actors as their characters in the digital environment in real time. Serkis's Caesar in the Planet of the Apes films (2011–2017) marked a further step, with capture performed on location rather than in a studio volume.

Debate

The technique has prompted debate about authorship and awards: performers such as Serkis argue that performance capture is acting and should be eligible for acting awards, while some animators stress their own substantial creative contribution in translating captured data into the final character.
