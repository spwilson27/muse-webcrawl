Scientometrics

Scientometrics is a subfield of informetrics that studies the quantitative characteristics of science, scientific research, technical progress and innovation. Major research issues include the measurement of the impact of research, reference sets of articles to investigate the citation impact of journals and institutes, understanding of scientific citations, mapping of scientific fields and the production of indicators for use in policy and management contexts.

Scientometrics has overlapping interests with bibliometrics — the quantitative study of written publications — and is closely related to the sociology of science and science policy studies.

History

The term scientometrics was coined in the Soviet Union, where Vassily V. Nalimov and Z. M. Mulchenko used it (as naukometriya) in the 1960s for the quantitative study of science. The modern discipline crystallised with the work of Eugene Garfield, founder of the Institute for Scientific Information, whose Science Citation Index (1964) made large-scale citation analysis possible, and of Derek J. de Solla Price, whose 1963 book Little Science, Big Science documented the exponential growth of scientific literature.

The journal Scientometrics was founded in 1978 by Tibor Braun in Hungary. The International Society for Scientometrics and Informetrics (ISSI) was established in 1993.

Methods and indicators

Scientometric studies commonly draw on large bibliographic databases such as Web of Science, Scopus and Google Scholar. Typical indicators include:

  • Publication counts, by author, institution, country or field.
  • Citation counts and derived measures such as the h-index, which attempts to balance productivity and impact.
  • Journal impact factors, based on average citations to recent articles.
  • Co-authorship and citation networks, used to map the structure and evolution of research fields.
  • Altmetrics, which track online attention such as downloads, mentions and social media references.

Applications

Governments and funding agencies use scientometric indicators in research assessment exercises, university rankings and science policy. Universities employ them in hiring, promotion and tenure decisions. Critics warn against the mechanical use of indicators: the San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA, 2012) and the Leiden Manifesto (2015) argue that quantitative measures should support, not supplant, expert judgement, because indicators can be gamed and can distort research behaviour.
