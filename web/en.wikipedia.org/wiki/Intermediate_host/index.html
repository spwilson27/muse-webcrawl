Intermediate host

An intermediate host is an organism that harbours a parasite during an immature or asexual stage of its life cycle, before the parasite reaches its definitive host, in which it matures and reproduces sexually. Many parasites, including trematodes, cestodes and some nematodes, require one or more intermediate hosts to complete their development.

Examples

Snails serve as intermediate hosts for the blood flukes that cause schistosomiasis: larvae develop inside the snail and emerge as free-swimming cercariae that infect humans in water. In the life cycle of the pork tapeworm, pigs are intermediate hosts carrying larval cysts in muscle, while humans are the definitive hosts. Mosquitoes, by contrast, are usually described as vectors, though for malaria parasites the mosquito is technically the definitive host because sexual reproduction occurs there.

Terminology

A paratenic host or transport host carries a parasite without any development occurring, bridging an ecological gap in the life cycle. Control of parasitic diseases often targets intermediate hosts — draining snail habitats, for instance — to break transmission.
