Bacchus

Bacchus is the Roman name of the Greek god Dionysus, god of wine, vegetation, fertility, festivity, ecstasy and theatre. The name derives from the Greek Bakkhos, an epithet of Dionysus associated with the frenzied state of his worshippers. In Rome his cult merged with that of the Italic wine god Liber Pater, and the secret rites of the Bacchanalia became so notorious that the Senate suppressed them in 186 BCE.

Mythology

Bacchus was the son of Jupiter and the mortal Semele, who died after demanding to see the god in his full glory; the infant was sewn into Jupiter's thigh and born a second time. Raised by Silenus and the nymphs of Mount Nysa, he wandered the world teaching viticulture, attended by ecstatic female followers (maenads) and satyrs. Those who resisted his divinity — Pentheus of Thebes, Lycurgus, the daughters of Minyas — were driven mad or destroyed, a theme dramatised in Euripides' tragedy The Bacchae. He rescued the abandoned Ariadne on Naxos and made her his wife.

The Bacchanalia affair

In 186 BCE the Roman Senate, alarmed by reports of the secret, mixed-sex nocturnal rites of the Bacchanalia, passed the Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus, restricting the cult throughout Italy. Livy's sensational account of the affair remains a key source. The English word "bacchanal" survives for any drunken revelry, and Bacchus has been a frequent subject in art, from Michelangelo's drunken Bacchus statue to Caravaggio's paintings.
