Modern Chinese literature refers to Chinese-language literature produced from the Literary Revolution of 1917 onward, written predominantly in the vernacular (baihua) rather than in Classical Chinese (wenyan). It emerged amid the New Culture and May Fourth movements, when intellectuals sought forms adequate to a modernising nation, and it developed through sharply different periods — Republican-era experimentation, the literature of war and revolution, socialist realism after 1949, and the diversification that followed the reform era. Modern Chinese literature includes writing from mainland China, Taiwan, Hong Kong and the Chinese diaspora.

Origins: the Literary Revolution and May Fourth

In 1917 Hu Shih published his call for literary reform, and Chen Duxiu's journal New Youth championed writing in baihua. The May Fourth Movement of 1919 accelerated the shift. Lu Xun's "Diary of a Madman" (1918) is conventionally regarded as the first modern Chinese short story, and his novella The True Story of Ah Q (1921–22) became the era's emblematic critique of national character. Lu Xun is widely considered the founder of modern Chinese literature.

Republican era (1920s–1949)

The 1920s and 1930s saw a flowering of schools and societies: the Literary Research Association advocateded "art for life's sake", while the Creation Society favoured romantic self-expression. Major writers included novelist Ba Jin (the Family trilogy), Lao She (Rickshaw Boy), Shen Congwen, who wrote lyrically of his native west Hunan, Mao Dun (Midnight), and the poet Xu Zhimo. The 1930s also saw the League of Left-Wing Writers push literature toward social and revolutionary purposes. After the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War in 1937, much writing turned to resistance themes; in Shanghai, Eileen Chang (Zhang Ailing) achieved fame in the 1940s with psychologically acute stories of city life.

1949–1976

Following the founding of the People's Republic in 1949, and guided by Mao Zedong's 1942 Yan'an Talks, literature was expected to serve workers, peasants and soldiers, adopting socialist realism. During the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976), literary production narrowed drastically to a handful of officially approved "model works" (yangbanxi) and many writers were persecuted or silenced.

Reform era and after

After 1978, "scar literature" confronted the Cultural Revolution's traumas, followed by root-seeking (xungen) fiction, avant-garde writers such as Yu Hua, Su Tong and Ge Fei, and the reflective work of Ah Cheng and Wang Zengqi. In 2000 Gao Xingjian became the first Chinese-language writer to receive the Nobel Prize in Literature (he had become a French citizen), and in 2012 Mo Yan received the prize, cited for his "hallucinatory realism". Contemporary Chinese literature ranges from the historical epics of Mo Yan and Yu Hua's To Live to the global success of science fiction, notably Liu Cixin's The Three-Body Problem. In Taiwan and Hong Kong, writers such as Bai Xianyong, Li Ang and Xi Xi developed distinct modernist and postcolonial traditions.
