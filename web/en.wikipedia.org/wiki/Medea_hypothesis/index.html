Medea hypothesis

The Medea hypothesis is a term coined by paleontologist Peter Ward for the anti-Gaian view that multicellular life, understood as a superorganism, is self-destructive. It proposes that life itself periodically triggers mass extinctions, including its own, through disruptive changes to the environment.

The hypothesis is named after Medea, the mythological figure who killed her own children, in deliberate contrast to the Gaia hypothesis, named after the benevolent Earth goddess Gaia.

Examples

Ward cites events such as the Great Oxidation Event, in which photosynthetic bacteria flooded the atmosphere with oxygen poisonous to most anaerobic life, and episodes of runaway climate change driven by biological methane production, as cases where life destabilised the conditions it depended on. On this view, Earth history is punctuated by life-induced catastrophes, and complex life may ultimately be self-limiting.

Reception

The hypothesis, set out in Ward's 2009 book The Medea Hypothesis, has been discussed as a counterweight to optimistic readings of Gaia, though critics note that the same events can be framed as life adapting and diversifying after crises.
