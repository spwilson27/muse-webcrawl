Pharmacoeconomics

Pharmacoeconomics is the branch of health economics that identifies, measures, and compares the costs and consequences (outcomes) of pharmaceutical products and services. It applies economic evaluation methods — cost-effectiveness analysis, cost-utility analysis, cost-benefit analysis, and cost-minimization analysis — to inform decisions about drug pricing, reimbursement, formulary placement, and clinical policy.

Methods

Pharmacoeconomic studies adopt a stated perspective (patient, payer, health system, or society) and compare an intervention with relevant alternatives over a defined time horizon. Principal methods include:

  • Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA): outcomes measured in natural units (e.g., life-years gained, cases prevented); results expressed as an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER).
  • Cost-utility analysis (CUA): outcomes weighted by health-related quality of life, typically quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) or disability-adjusted life years (DALYs).
  • Cost-benefit analysis (CBA): both costs and outcomes expressed in monetary terms.
  • Cost-minimization analysis (CMA): used when outcomes are demonstrably equivalent, comparing only costs.
  • Budget impact analysis: estimates the financial consequences of adopting a therapy for a defined population.

Applications

Health technology assessment bodies such as the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in England use pharmacoeconomic evidence — for instance, cost per QALY thresholds — in coverage recommendations. Pharmaceutical companies generate such analyses to support pricing and market access; payers use them in formulary and prior-authorization decisions. The field emerged as a distinct discipline in the late 1970s and 1980s, with the term popularized in the late 1980s, and it has its own professional society, ISPOR (the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research), and journals such as PharmacoEconomics and Value in Health.
