Pathwidth

In graph theory, the pathwidth of an undirected graph is a measure of how closely the graph resembles a path. It is defined through path decompositions: a path decomposition of a graph is a sequence of "bags" of vertices such that every edge has both endpoints in some bag, and each vertex appears in a contiguous subsequence of bags. The width of a decomposition is the size of its largest bag minus one, and the pathwidth of the graph is the minimum width over all path decompositions.

Properties

Pathwidth is a special case of treewidth, in which the bags are arranged in a tree rather than a path; consequently pathwidth is always at least treewidth. Paths have pathwidth 0 or 1 depending on convention details, trees have pathwidth O(log n) in the worst case, and complete graphs on n vertices have pathwidth n−1. Graphs of pathwidth one are exactly the caterpillar forests. Pathwidth is related to other parameters such as vertex separation number, node searching number, and interval graph completion: the pathwidth of a graph equals the minimum clique number minus one over its interval supergraphs.

Algorithms

Many NP-hard problems, such as graph coloring and Hamiltonian path, become tractable on graphs of small pathwidth through dynamic programming over a path decomposition. Computing pathwidth exactly is NP-hard in general, but fixed-parameter tractable algorithms and good approximation and heuristic methods exist, making the parameter practical in compiler design (register allocation), VLSI layout, and network analysis.
