Musical set theory

Musical set theory provides concepts for categorising musical objects and describing their relationships, especially in atonal and post-tonal music. It adapts the language of mathematical set theory to pitch, treating notes as elements of sets of pitch classes — notes identified across octaves and enharmonic spellings. Although particularly associated with the analysis of atonal music, the concepts are also applied to tonal and rhythmic structures.

A pitch-class set plotted on a mod-12 "clockface".

Basic concepts

Pitches are assigned integers 0–11 by their position in the chromatic scale (with C = 0, C♯ = 1, ..., B = 11), and arithmetic is modulo 12. Collections of pitch classes are written as sets, and are compared under transposition (adding a constant) and inversion (negating). Sets that are equivalent under these operations belong to the same set class; Allen Forte's The Structure of Atonal Music (1973) catalogued all set classes of three to nine notes with "prime forms" and Forte numbers (such as 3-11 for the major/minor triad class).

The pattern of intervals within a set is summarised by its interval vector, which counts the occurrences of each interval class. Related ideas include set complementation, similarity measures, and the Z-relation (sets with identical interval vectors but different content).

History

Antecedents include the twelve-tone theory of Milton Babbitt and the work of David Lewin, whose transformational theory reframed set-theoretic questions in terms of musical "transformations" and networks. Howard Hanson had earlier developed a related classification for tonal harmony. Forte's system became standard in North American music theory pedagogy, though it has been criticised for privileging certain relationships and for its handling of ordering and register.

Applications

Set theory is widely used to analyse the music of Schoenberg, Webern, Bartók, Stravinsky, and other composers whose works are not organised by traditional tonality, and to describe compositional systems such as twelve-tone rows as transformations of sets.
