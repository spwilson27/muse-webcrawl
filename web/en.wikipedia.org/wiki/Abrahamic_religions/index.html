The Abrahamic religions are a group of monotheistic religions that trace their spiritual lineage to the patriarch Abraham. The three largest are Judaism, Christianity and Islam, which together claim roughly four billion adherents — more than half of humanity. Smaller faiths often placed in the family include the Baháʼí Faith, the Druze faith, Rastafari and Samaritanism.

Common features

All Abrahamic faiths worship one God — the God of Abraham — understood as creator and moral lawgiver, and share a body of narrative: the creation, the covenant with Abraham, the Exodus under Moses, and reverence for Jerusalem. They emphasise revealed scripture, prophecy, prayer, charity and a final judgement, though they differ sharply over the nature of God (the Trinity in Christianity versus strict unitarianism in Judaism and Islam), the status of Jesus, and the authority of later scriptures such as the Quran.

History of the term

"Abrahamic" as a family label became common in comparative religion and interfaith dialogue in the later 20th century, in part to frame Jewish–Christian–Muslim relations positively. Scholars have criticised the category as smoothing over deep theological differences, and some Jewish and Muslim thinkers resist inclusion on terms set by Christian theology; nevertheless it remains the standard grouping alongside the "Dharmic" (Indian) and "Taoic" (East Asian) families.
