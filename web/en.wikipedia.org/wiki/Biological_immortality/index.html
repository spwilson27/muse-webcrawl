Biological immortality

Biological immortality
DefinitionA state in which mortality from senescence is absent or negligible
Key examplesTurritopsis dohrnii, Hydra, planarian flatworms, bristlecone pine
Related conceptsNegligible senescence, longevity, life extension

Biological immortality (sometimes referred to as bio-indefinite mortality) is a state in which the rate of mortality from senescence is stable or decreasing, thus decoupling it from chronological age. Various unicellular and multicellular species, including some vertebrates, achieve this state either throughout their existence or after reaching a certain age. A biologically immortal living being can still die from means other than senescence, such as through injury, predation, disease, or lack of available resources.

Cell lines

Biologists chose the word "immortal" to designate cells that are not subject to the Hayflick limit, the point at which cells no longer divide because of DNA damage or shortened telomeres. The first and still most widely used immortal cell line is HeLa, developed from cells taken from the malignant tumor of Henrietta Lacks in 1951. Cancer cells and certain stem cells are effectively immortal at the cellular level, dividing indefinitely under suitable conditions.

Organisms

The jellyfish Turritopsis dohrnii is frequently cited as biologically immortal because it can revert its cells to an earlier developmental stage—the polyp stage—through a process called transdifferentiation, potentially repeating the cycle indefinitely. Freshwater Hydra show no measurable increase in mortality or decline in reproduction with age, owing to continuously renewing stem cells. Planarian flatworms maintain telomere length in their stem cells and can regenerate whole bodies from fragments. Certain plants, including the Great Basin bristlecone pine, include individuals thousands of years old, and clonal colonies such as the quaking aspen stand Pando may be far older still.

Negligible senescence

Related to biological immortality is negligible senescence, a term coined by Caleb Finch for organisms that do not show measurable deterioration, functional decline, or increased mortality with age. Examples include some tortoises, rockfish, and the naked mole-rat, which shows a mortality rate that does not increase with age as other mammals' does.
