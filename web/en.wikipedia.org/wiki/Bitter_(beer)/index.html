Bitter is a British style of pale ale that ranges in colour from gold to dark amber and in strength typically from about 3% to 5.5% alcohol by volume. It is one of the most characteristic beer styles of England and the archetypal draught beer of the traditional English pub.

History

The term came into use in the nineteenth century as drinkers distinguished the new, well-hopped pale ales from older, less bitter mild ales and porters. Brewers themselves generally called the beer "pale ale"; "bitter" was the customers' name for it on draught. Strengths and names settled into a rough hierarchy: ordinary bitter, best bitter and strong or special bitter (also marketed as ESB — extra special bitter — especially in North America).

Characteristics

Bitter is brewed with pale malt, often with a small proportion of crystal malt for colour and body, and English hop varieties such as Fuggles and Goldings. Traditional draught bitter is served as cask ale ("real ale"), conditioned in the cask and dispensed without added carbon dioxide, a tradition defended by the Campaign for Real Ale (CAMRA), founded in 1971. Bottled and kegged versions are also widespread.

Sub-styles

British brewers and style guides generally divide bitter into session or ordinary bitter (up to about 4.1% abv), best bitter (4.2–4.7%) and strong bitter (4.8% and above). Well-known examples include Fuller's London Pride, Timothy Taylor Landlord and Greene King IPA (a low-strength bitter despite its name).
