From Greekὀστέον (osteon, bone) + λόγος

Osteology (from Greek ὀστέον (osteon) 'bones' and λόγος (logos) 'study') is the scientific study of bones, practiced by osteologists. A subdiscipline of anatomy, anthropology, and paleontology, osteology is the detailed study of the structure of bones, skeletal elements, teeth, microbone morphology, function, disease, pathology, the process of ossification (from cartilaginous molds), and the resistance and hardness of bones (biophysics).

Methods

Osteologists examine bones macroscopically (shape, size, muscle markings, joint surfaces) and microscopically (histology of bone microstructure), and use measurements (osteometry) compared against reference collections. In living subjects, imaging techniques such as radiography, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) for bone mineral density, and computed tomography apply the same knowledge clinically.

Applications

In bioarchaeology and forensic anthropology, skeletal analysis estimates the biological profile of the dead — age at death (from epiphyseal fusion, dental eruption, and pubic symphysis morphology), sex (mainly from pelvis and skull), stature (from long-bone lengths), and ancestry — and documents trauma, disease (palaeopathology), diet, and activity patterns. Osteology is also central to zooarchaeology, where animal bones from archaeological sites reconstruct past environments and subsistence, and to vertebrate palaeontology. Clinicians use osteological knowledge in orthopaedics and in diagnosing metabolic bone diseases such as osteoporosis and rickets.
