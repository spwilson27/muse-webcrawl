Semantic theory of truth

A semantic theory of truth is a theory of truth in the philosophy of language which holds that truth is a property of sentences. The approach was pioneered by the Polish logician Alfred Tarski in the 1930s, most famously in his work "The Concept of Truth in Formalized Languages" (1933) and the popular article "The Semantic Conception of Truth" (1944).

Tarski's account

Tarski argued that a satisfactory definition of truth for a language must be materially adequate and formally correct. Material adequacy is captured by Convention T: the definition must entail, for every sentence s of the object language, a "T-sentence" of the form "s is true if and only if p", where p is a translation of s into the metalanguage — for example, "'Snow is white' is true if and only if snow is white." To avoid paradoxes such as the liar paradox, Tarski held that the truth predicate for a language must be defined in a richer metalanguage, producing a hierarchy of languages.

Influence

Tarski's theory strongly influenced philosophy of language and logic. Donald Davidson later adapted the semantic conception as the basis for a theory of meaning, using truth conditions to interpret natural language. Critics debate whether Tarski's formally precise definition captures the ordinary concept of truth or merely a restricted substitute for it.
