The Higgins boat, formally the LCVP (Landing Craft, Vehicle, Personnel), was a landing craft used extensively by the United States and its allies during the Second World War. Designed by Andrew Higgins of Higgins Industries in New Orleans and derived from his shallow-draft "Eureka boat" developed for work in the Louisiana bayous, it became the standard craft for amphibious landings, carrying troops and light vehicles from ship to shore in operations from Normandy to Iwo Jima.

Design and development

The craft was built largely of plywood over a frame, with a shallow draft, a spoon-shaped bow, and a solid bow ramp — its most distinctive innovation — that allowed infantry to disembark quickly and vehicles to drive ashore, rather than climbing over the sides as in earlier designs. A crew of four operated the boat, powered by a diesel or gasoline engine giving about 9 knots. A steel ramp replaced the original wooden one after development work involving a design inspired by Japanese Daihatsu-class landing craft observed in China.

Service

More than 23,000 LCVPs and related variants were built during the war. General Dwight D. Eisenhower later called Higgins "the man who won the war for us," crediting the boat with making the Allies' strategy of open-beach amphibious assault feasible. The type served in every major amphibious operation of the war in both Europe and the Pacific, and post-war derivatives remained in service for decades.
