Ligature (writing)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In writing and typography, a ligature occurs where two or more graphemes or their forms are joined into a single glyph. Ligatures usually replace consecutive characters that share common components, and are part of the rich history of handwriting and typesetting in European and other scripts. The word comes from the Latin ligatura, "a binding".

Letterform ligatures

Some ligatures have become letters in their own right. The ash, æ, began as a ligature of a and e and is a full letter in Danish, Norwegian and Icelandic. The German sharp s, ß, arose from a ligature of long s (ſ) and z or s. The ampersand, &, is a ligature of the letters e and t, forming the Latin word et, "and".

Typographic ligatures

In metal type, ligature sorts such as fi and fl prevented the collision of the overhanging hook of the f with the dot of the i or the top of the l, improving both appearance and spacing. Modern OpenType fonts include standard ligatures (ff, fi, fl, ffi, ffl) that applications can substitute automatically, as well as discretionary and historical ligatures such as the long-s combinations (ſt) used in early printing. The Latin alphabet's ligatures are optional refinements, whereas in some scripts — notably Arabic, and many Indic scripts — joining of letters is an essential feature of the writing system, handled by complex text layout rules rather than by ligature glyphs alone.

Disadvantages

Ligatures can hinder legibility for learners and can complicate searching and text extraction in digital documents, since the ligature is a single character (for example U+FB01, "ﬁ") that does not automatically match the two-letter sequence it replaces.
