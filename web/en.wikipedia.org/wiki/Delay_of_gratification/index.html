Delay of gratification

A child in a marshmallow-test style experiment resists eating the treat to earn a second one

Delayed gratification, or deferred gratification, is the ability to resist the temptation of an immediate reward in favor of a more valuable and long-lasting reward later. It is generally considered a sign of emotional self-regulation and has been studied as a component of self-control in psychology.

The marshmallow test

The most famous research on the topic is the Stanford marshmallow experiment, conducted by psychologist Walter Mischel beginning in the late 1960s. In these studies, a child was offered a choice between one small reward provided immediately (a marshmallow or pretzel) or two small rewards if they waited for a short period. Follow-up studies appeared to find correlations between the ability to wait as a child and later life outcomes such as educational attainment, though a 2018 replication found the effects were smaller than originally reported and were substantially explained by family background and socioeconomic status.

Factors

Delay ability is influenced by trust in the reliability of promised future rewards, attention strategies such as distracting oneself from the tempting stimulus, and cultural and socioeconomic context. Related concepts include impulse control, future orientation, and temporal discounting in behavioral economics.
