Microtubule

Microtubules are polymers of tubulin that form part of the cytoskeleton and provide structure and shape to eukaryotic cells. A microtubule is a hollow tube about 25 nanometres in outer diameter, built from protofilaments of α- and β-tubulin heterodimers. Microtubules are important in a number of cellular processes: they maintain cell shape, enable intracellular transport by the motor proteins kinesin and dynein, form the axonemes of cilia and flagella, and build the mitotic spindle that segregates chromosomes during cell division.

Structure and dynamic instability

Each tubulin heterodimer binds GTP; hydrolysis of GTP in β-tubulin after incorporation weakens the lattice. Microtubules display dynamic instability, stochastic switching between phases of growth and rapid shrinkage ("catastrophe"), rescue and regrowth, driven by the presence or loss of a stabilising "GTP cap" at the plus end. They are nucleated and organised by microtubule organising centres, principally the centrosome in animal cells, where γ-tubulin ring complexes template new polymers. Polarity is intrinsic: the fast-growing plus end exposes β-tubulin, the minus end α-tubulin.

Functions

Intracellular transport

Motor proteins move cargo directionally along microtubules: most kinesins walk toward the plus end (outward to the cell periphery), while cytoplasmic dynein moves toward the minus end (toward the centrosome and nucleus). Vesicles, organelles, mRNA and viruses all hitch rides on this railway system; in neurons, defects in axonal transport contribute to neurodegenerative disease.

Cell division and motility

During mitosis, microtubules form the spindle, attach to chromosomes at kinetochores, and pull sister chromatids apart. In cilia and flagella, a "9+2" array of microtubule doublets, powered by axonemal dynein, produces beating and swimming motions.

Drugs and medical relevance

Microtubule-targeting agents are mainstays of chemotherapy: the vinca alkaloids (vincristine, vinblastine) depolymerise microtubules, while taxanes (paclitaxel/Taxol, docetaxel) stabilise them; both disrupt the spindle and kill dividing cancer cells. Colchicine, used for gout, also binds tubulin, and benzimidazole anthelmintics exploit differences in parasite tubulin.
