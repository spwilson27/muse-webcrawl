Caramelization

Caramelization is the browning of sugar, a process used extensively in cooking that results in a sweet, nutty flavour and a brown colour. It is the pyrolysis of sugar in the absence of proteins, unlike the Maillard reaction, which involves amino acids. As the process occurs, volatile chemicals such as diacetyl and furans (including hydroxymethylfurfural) are released, producing the characteristic caramel flavour. Caramelization begins at relatively high temperatures — generally above 120 °C (250 °F) — and the precise onset depends on the sugar.

Temperatures

SugarCaramelization begins at approximately
Fructose105–110 °C (220–230 °F)
Glucose150 °C (300 °F)
Sucrose160–170 °C (320–340 °F)
Maltose180 °C (355 °F)

Because fructose caramelises at a lower temperature, foods rich in fructose brown more readily. Sucrose first melts and decomposes, inverting partly to glucose and fructose, then forms complex polymeric pigments (caramelans, caramelens, and caramelins) responsible for the deepening colour from pale gold to dark brown.

Chemistry

Caramelization is a complex, incompletely understood set of reactions: sugars dehydrate, isomerise, fragment, and polymerise. Products include difructose anhydrides, organic acids (which slightly lower pH), and hundreds of aroma compounds. The brown colourants are grouped as caramelan (C24H36O18), caramelen (C36H50O25), and caramelin (C125H188O80).

Culinary uses

  • Caramel itself — sugar heated with butter and cream for sauces, confections, and flan.
  • Crème brûlée and crème caramel — a glassy caramel crust or lining.
  • Caramelised onions — slow cooking releases sugars that brown (with some Maillard contribution).
  • Tarte Tatin, roasted vegetables, seared meats (combined with Maillard reactions), and the colouring of cola drinks (caramel colour, E150).
  • Dulce de leche and milk-based sweets, where caramelization combines with Maillard browning.

Caramel colour produced industrially with ammonia or sulfites (classes III and IV) was scrutinised because of the by-product 4-methylimidazole, prompting regulatory labelling in some jurisdictions.
