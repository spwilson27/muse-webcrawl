Limitation period (statute of limitations)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A statute of limitations, or limitation period, is a law that sets the maximum time after an event within which legal proceedings may be initiated. When the period expires, a claim may no longer be filed, or a criminal prosecution begun. In England and Wales the governing statute is the Limitation Act 1980, which generally allows six years for contract and tort claims and twelve years for claims concerning land, with special rules for personal injury and latent damage.

Rationale

Limitation laws rest on several policies: evidence degrades and witnesses' memories fade with time; defendants deserve repose and should not live indefinitely under the threat of suit; and claimants who sleep on their rights (the maxim vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt) should be encouraged to sue promptly. The periods struck by legislatures balance these concerns against the injustice of barring meritorious claims.

Accrual, discovery, and tolling

The clock usually starts when the cause of action accrues — when the injury occurs or the breach is committed. Under a discovery rule, common for latent injuries and fraud, time runs only from when the claimant knew or should have known of the injury. The period may be paused (tolled) during the claimant's minority or incapacity, the defendant's absence from the jurisdiction, or fraudulent concealment. In criminal law, the gravest offences such as murder typically have no limitation period, and several jurisdictions have abolished or extended limits for historic sexual offences. A related but distinct doctrine is laches, an equitable defence based on unreasonable delay.
