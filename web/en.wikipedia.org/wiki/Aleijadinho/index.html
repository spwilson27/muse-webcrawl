Antônio Francisco Lisboa (c. 1730 or 1738 – 18 November 1814), better known as Aleijadinho (Portuguese for "little cripple"), was a sculptor and architect in colonial Brazil, regarded as the greatest artist of the Brazilian Baroque and the foremost sculptor of the colonial Americas.

Life

Lisboa was born in Vila Rica (now Ouro Preto), in the gold-mining captaincy of Minas Gerais, the son of a Portuguese master builder and an enslaved African woman. He learned his craft from his father and in the workshops of the region. From around the age of forty, a progressive disease — the precise nature of which is uncertain, with leprosy, syphilis and other conditions proposed — deformed his hands and feet and cost him his toes and fingers, earning him his nickname. According to tradition, he continued to work with chisels strapped to his stumps, often at night or hidden behind screens, and was carried to work sites by an enslaved assistant.

Works

Aleijadinho's masterpieces stand in the mining towns of Minas Gerais. For the Sanctuary of Bom Jesus de Matosinhos in Congonhas, he carved the celebrated Twelve Prophets (1800–1805) in soapstone, monumental figures of Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel and the minor prophets arranged on the church terrace, and the wooden polychromed figures of the Passos (Stations of the Passion) in the six chapels below. He also contributed sculpture and design to churches including São Francisco de Assis in Ouro Preto and São João del-Rei, whose façades and carved altarpieces exemplify the exuberant Mineiro Rococo.

Legacy

Long neglected after his death, Aleijadinho was rediscovered by Brazilian modernists in the 20th century and elevated as a symbol of national art — a mixed-race, disabled craftsman who transformed European Baroque forms into a distinctive Brazilian style. The Congonhas sanctuary is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
