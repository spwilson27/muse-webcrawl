Pierre Robin sequence

SpecialtyMedical genetics, oral and maxillofacial surgery
SymptomsMicrognathia, glossoptosis, airway obstruction, cleft palate
Named afterPierre Robin (French stomatologist)
The triad of micrognathia, glossoptosis and airway obstruction.

Pierre Robin sequence (PRS), also known as Pierre Robin syndrome or Pierre Robin anomalad, is a congenital condition characterised by an unusually small lower jaw (micrognathia), a tongue that falls back into the throat (glossoptosis), and upper airway obstruction. A cleft palate — typically U-shaped — is present in most cases but is considered part of the sequence rather than a defining criterion in all classifications.

Mechanism

It is called a "sequence" because the abnormalities follow in cascade from a single primary defect: the underdeveloped mandible keeps the tongue high in the oral cavity during fetal development, preventing closure of the palatal shelves and leaving the tongue positioned to obstruct the airway after birth.

Causes and associations

PRS may occur in isolation or as part of another syndrome. It is most commonly associated with Stickler syndrome, and also occurs with Treacher Collins syndrome, velocardiofacial (22q11.2 deletion) syndrome and fetal alcohol syndrome. Genetic studies have implicated regulatory regions near the SOX9 gene in some isolated cases.

Management

Treatment focuses on the airway and feeding. Many infants can be managed by prone positioning, which lets the tongue fall forward; more severe obstruction may require a nasopharyngeal airway, tongue–lip adhesion, mandibular distraction osteogenesis, or tracheostomy. The cleft palate is usually repaired surgically in the first year or two of life. Naming honours the French dental surgeon Pierre Robin, who described the condition in the 1920s and 1930s.
