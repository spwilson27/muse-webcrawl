Barred spiral galaxy

A barred spiral galaxy is a spiral galaxy with a central bar-shaped structure composed of stars. Bars are found in about two-thirds of all spiral galaxies, including the Milky Way. They generally affect the motions of stars, gas, and dust, funnelling material toward the galactic centre and influencing star formation and the growth of central bulges and supermassive black holes.

NGC 1300, a prototypical barred spiral galaxy, imaged by the Hubble Space Telescope.

Classification

In the Hubble sequence, barred spirals are designated SB, followed by a letter (a, b, c, d or m) indicating how tightly wound the spiral arms are and the size of the central bulge. In the de Vaucouleurs system, intermediate forms between barred and unbarred spirals are classified SAB, and the strength of the bar is graded from weak (SBm-like irregulars) to strong.

Formation and evolution

Bars are thought to form through a dynamical instability in a rotating stellar disc: once a slight elongation appears, gravitational feedback amplifies it into a sustained bar of stars on elongated orbits. Bars can be strengthened or weakened by interactions with other galaxies and by the accumulation of gas in the centre. Surveys show bars are more common in the nearby (older) universe than in the distant past, suggesting that discs "settle" into barred configurations as they mature. The Milky Way's bar is estimated to be about 27,000 light-years long, oriented at an angle of roughly 30 degrees to the line from the Sun to the Galactic Centre.
