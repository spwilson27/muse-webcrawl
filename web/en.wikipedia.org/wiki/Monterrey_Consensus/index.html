The Monterrey Consensus is the outcome document of the United Nations International Conference on Financing for Development, held in Monterrey, Mexico, from 18 to 22 March 2002. The conference was notable as the first United Nations summit-level event of its kind to address development finance, and it brought together heads of state and government alongside the leadership of the World Bank, the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the World Trade Organization (WTO), and representatives of business and civil society.

Background

Preparations for the conference began in the late 1990s amid concern that official development assistance (ODA) had declined sharply after the Cold War even as the international community adopted ambitious development targets, including the Millennium Development Goals agreed in 2000. The Monterrey process sought to forge a bargain: developing countries would commit to sound governance, macroeconomic stability, and mobilising domestic resources, while developed countries would increase aid, relieve debt, and open their markets to developing-country exports.

Contents of the consensus

The Monterrey Consensus is organised around six leading actions:

  • Mobilising domestic financial resources for development — good governance, sound economic policies, and the rule of law.
  • Mobilising international resources — foreign direct investment and other private flows.
  • International trade as an engine for development — market access and a successful conclusion of the Doha Development Round.
  • Increasing international financial and technical cooperation — developed countries were urged to make concrete efforts toward the target of 0.7 per cent of gross national product as ODA.
  • External debt — debt relief under the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) initiative and debt sustainability.
  • Addressing systemic issues — coherence of the international monetary, financial and trading systems, and reform of global economic governance.

Outcomes and follow-up

Following the conference, several developed countries announced increases in aid budgets; the United States announced the creation of the Millennium Challenge Account, and the European Union set a timetable for raising its collective ODA. The consensus was reaffirmed and extended at the follow-up conference in Doha, Qatar, in 2008, which produced the Doha Declaration on Financing for Development, and again in the Addis Ababa Action Agenda of 2015, which forms part of the framework for the Sustainable Development Goals.
