The analogy of the sun is an analogy presented by the Greek philosopher Plato in Book VI of his dialogue the Republic (507b–509c). It is presented by the character Socrates in conversation with Plato's brother Glaucon. When pressed to define the Good itself, Socrates declines to give a direct account and instead offers an analogy with the sun, which he calls an "offspring" of the Good.

The analogy

Socrates explains that just as the sun is the cause of sight — illuminating visible objects and giving the eye its power to see — and is also the source of generation, growth and nourishment in the visible world, so the Form of the Good is the cause of knowledge and truth in the intelligible world: it makes objects of knowledge knowable and gives the knower the power to know. Socrates adds that the Good is not itself being (ousia) but is "beyond being, exceeding it in dignity and power" (509b), a much-debated passage.

Context

The analogy is the first of three connected images in Books VI–VII of the Republic, followed by the analogy of the divided line and the allegory of the cave. Together they set out Plato's epistemology and metaphysics and underpin the argument that philosopher-rulers, who have apprehended the Good, should govern the just city.
