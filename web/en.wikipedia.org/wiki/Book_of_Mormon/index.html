The Book of Mormon is a religious text of the Latter Day Saint movement, first published in March 1830 in Palmyra, New York, by Joseph Smith, who said he had translated it from ancient gold plates by divine power. Adherents regard it as a volume of scripture comparable to the Bible — its subtitle in current editions is "Another Testament of Jesus Christ" — while critics regard it as a 19th-century work of Smith and possible collaborators.

Content

The book presents itself as a record, abridged by the prophet-historian Mormon and his son Moroni, of several groups that migrated from the ancient Near East to the Americas, principally the Nephites and Lamanites. It narrates their kings, wars, prophecies and sermons over roughly a thousand years (c. 600 BCE – 421 CE), culminating in a visit of the resurrected Jesus Christ to the Americas (3 Nephi). Passages such as King Benjamin's address and the sermon of Alma are central to Latter-day Saint theology. The name of the book is the origin of the common nickname "Mormon".

Reception and debate

Published just before the founding of the Church of Christ (later the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints) on 6 April 1830, the book is accepted as scripture by that church — the largest Latter Day Saint denomination — and by most other groups in the movement, though Community of Christ has de-emphasised it. Mainstream archaeology, linguistics and genetics have not found evidence supporting its account of pre-Columbian civilisations, and scholars debate its anachronisms; believing scholars, many associated with Brigham Young University, argue for its antiquity. The book has been translated in full or in part into over 100 languages.
