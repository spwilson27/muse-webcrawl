Bloody Sunday (1905)

Bloody Sunday
Date22 January 1905 (O.S. 9 January)
LocationSt Petersburg, Russian Empire
Leader of marchFather Georgy Gapon
OutcomeTroops fire on peaceful petitioners; triggers the 1905 Revolution

Bloody Sunday was a massacre on 22 January 1905 in St Petersburg, Russia, when unarmed demonstrators, led by the priest Father Georgy Gapon, marched to the Winter Palace to present a petition to Tsar Nicholas II and were fired upon by soldiers of the Imperial Guard. Estimates of the dead range from about one hundred to several hundred, with many more wounded.

Background

Amid the hardships of the Russo-Japanese War and industrial unrest, a strike at the Putilov works spread across the capital. Gapon's Assembly of Russian Factory Workers organised a peaceful procession of workers and their families, carrying icons and portraits of the Tsar, to petition for an eight-hour day, fair wages, and democratic reforms.

The massacre

The Tsar was not at the palace. At several points in the city, troops blocked the marchers' routes and opened fire; Cossacks charged crowds near the Narva Gate and on Nevsky Prospekt. The killings shattered the traditional image of the Tsar as the people's protector.

Consequences

Bloody Sunday ignited the Russian Revolution of 1905: strikes, mutinies (including on the battleship Potemkin), peasant unrest and the formation of soviets forced Nicholas II to issue the October Manifesto, promising a parliament (the Duma) and civil liberties. The event is widely seen as a key step toward the revolutions of 1917. Other events share the name Bloody Sunday, including the 1972 shootings in Derry, Northern Ireland.
