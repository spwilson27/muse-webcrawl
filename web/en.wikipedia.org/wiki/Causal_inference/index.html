Causal inference

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Hero image placeholder: a directed acyclic graph encoding causal assumptions]

Causal inference is the process of determining the effect of one variable on another — of drawing conclusions about cause-and-effect relationships from data. It is a central concern of statistics, epidemiology, economics, and the social sciences, and is the scientific response to the warning that correlation does not imply causation.

Frameworks

Two dominant frameworks structure the field. The potential outcomes framework, originating with Jerzy Neyman and developed by Donald Rubin, defines a causal effect by contrasting the outcomes a unit would exhibit under treatment and under control, only one of which can be observed — the "fundamental problem of causal inference". The structural causal model framework of Judea Pearl represents causal assumptions as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) and uses the do-calculus to determine when causal effects are identifiable from observational data.

Methods

Randomised controlled trials remain the benchmark, since randomisation breaks confounding by design. Where experiments are impossible or unethical, researchers use methods such as matching and propensity scores, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, difference in differences, and synthetic controls, each resting on assumptions that must be argued rather than tested from the data alone. Key obstacles include confounding, selection bias, and reverse causation.

Applications

Causal inference underpins the evaluation of medical treatments, public policies, and business decisions such as pricing and marketing interventions, and is increasingly integrated with machine learning in fields such as uplift modelling and causal discovery.
