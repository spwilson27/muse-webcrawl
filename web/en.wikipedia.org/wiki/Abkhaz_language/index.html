Abkhaz language

Abkhaz (Abkhaz: Аԥсуа бызшәа, Apsua byzshwa) is a Northwest Caucasian language spoken by the Abkhaz people, principally in Abkhazia, a region on the eastern Black Sea coast internationally recognised as part of Georgia but controlled since the 1990s by a de facto independent government. It is famous among linguists for its extraordinary consonant inventory and minimal vowel system.

Phonology

Abkhaz has, in its literary Bzyp dialect, around 58 consonant phonemes — including ejective, pharyngealised and labialised series — but only two or three phonemic vowels, /a/ and /ə/ (with [ɨ] and [o] arising contextually or as a third vowel /aː/ vs /a/ analysis). Its relatives Abaza, Ubykh (now extinct, with some 80 consonants) and the Circassian languages form with it the Abkhaz–Adyghe branch. Verbs are strongly polysynthetic and head-marking, indexing subject, direct object and indirect object, while nouns are relatively simple.

History and script

Abkhaz was first written in the 19th century, using scripts devised by Peter von Uslar (Cyrillic-based, 1862) and later Latin (1928, under Soviet Latinisation) and Georgian (1938) alphabets, before returning to an adapted Cyrillic alphabet in 1954 that includes special letters such as ԥ, ҟ, ҕ and ӡ. Speaker numbers are estimated around 100,000–200,000 including a large diaspora in Turkey. UNESCO classifies Abkhaz as "definitely endangered", as Russian and Georgian dominate education and media.
