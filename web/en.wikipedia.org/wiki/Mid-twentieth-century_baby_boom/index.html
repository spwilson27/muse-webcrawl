The mid-twentieth century baby boom was a marked increase in birth rates in many countries following the Second World War, roughly from 1946 to 1964. In the United States the phenomenon produced the baby boomer generation of about 76 million people — the largest generational cohort in American history to that point — and comparable booms occurred in Canada, Australia, New Zealand and much of Western Europe.

Causes

Births had been postponed by the Great Depression and the war; after 1945, demobilised servicemen married in record numbers and started families earlier and in quick succession. Prosperity, low unemployment, the GI Bill's education and housing benefits, suburbanisation and confidence in post-war growth encouraged larger families. Total fertility in the US rose from about 2.1 children per woman in the late 1930s to a peak near 3.7 in the late 1950s. Similar but sometimes briefer peaks occurred in Europe; France and the Netherlands also experienced rebounds, while other countries saw earlier, war-related patterns.

Course and end

Annual US births first exceeded 4 million in 1954 and stayed above that level until 1964. Fertility then fell rapidly — the "baby bust" — driven by the contraceptive pill (approved in 1960), rising female labour-force participation and changing social attitudes, reaching below replacement level by the mid-1970s in most Western countries.

Effects

The boom cohort reshaped societies as it aged: it drove suburban growth and school construction in the 1950s, the youth culture and protest movements of the 1960s, housing and consumer booms in later decades, and, from the 2010s, mounting pressure on pension and health-care systems as boomers reached retirement age.
