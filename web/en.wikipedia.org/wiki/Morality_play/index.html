The morality play is a genre of medieval and early Tudor theatrical entertainment. In their own time, these plays were known simply as "interludes". Morality plays are allegories in which the protagonist is met by personifications of moral attributes — virtues, vices, Death, and so on — who struggle for the character's soul and seek to teach a moral lesson about how to live and die well.

Characteristics

The central character typically represents all of humanity (hence "Everyman", "Mankind", or "Humanum Genus"). Characters are personified abstractions rather than individuals: Good Deeds, Knowledge, Beauty, Strength, the Seven Deadly Sins, and the Seven Virtues. The plays dramatise the Christian journey of life, the temptation to sin, repentance, and the approach of death and judgement. A comic figure, the Vice, often served as a mischievous link between the allegory and the audience and is considered a precursor of later stage villains and clowns.

Notable plays

  • The Castle of Perseverance (c. 1405–1425) — the earliest surviving complete English morality, a vast play requiring a large playing place.
  • Wisdom and Mankind (c. 1465–1470) — among the "Macro plays"; Mankind is notably earthy and comic.
  • Everyman (c. 1510) — the most famous morality play, adapted from the Dutch Elckerlijc: summoned by Death, Everyman seeks a companion for his final journey and finds that only Good Deeds will follow him to the grave.

Development and influence

Morality plays succeeded the mystery plays (biblical cycle drama) as the dominant vernacular form and flourished in the 15th and early 16th centuries, performed by travelling professional troupes. During the Tudor period the form absorbed political and religious controversy, and its allegorical characters and Vice figure influenced Elizabethan drama, including echoes in Shakespeare's history plays and the morality structure of Doctor Faustus.
