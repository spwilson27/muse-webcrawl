Art Blakey's Jazz Messengers, the definitive hard bop band and a finishing school for young jazz talent.

The Jazz Messengers were an American jazz ensemble founded in 1954, associated first with pianist Horace Silver and then, for more than three decades, led by drummer Art Blakey. The band became the archetypal hard bop group and one of the most influential institutions in postwar jazz: Blakey famously used the Messengers as a finishing school, recruiting young musicians and encouraging them to compose, so that the roster of alumni reads like a roll call of modern jazz.

History

The group grew out of a 1954 recording session co-led by Blakey and Silver for Blue Note Records, with Kenny Dorham, Hank Mobley, and Doug Watkins. When Silver left in 1956 to form his own quintet, Blakey took over sole leadership and the name "Art Blakey and the Jazz Messengers." Successive editions featured Benny Golson (who gave the band "Moanin'", "Blues March", and "Along Came Betty"), Lee Morgan, Wayne Shorter — the group's musical director in the early 1960s — Freddie Hubbard, Cedar Walton, and Curtis Fuller.

Later years

Through the 1970s the band's fortunes waned as jazz lost ground commercially, but a late-1970s revival brought a celebrated lineup. In the 1980s the Messengers launched the careers of Wynton and Branford Marsalis, Terence Blanchard, Donald Harrison, Kenny Garrett, and others, fueling the "Young Lions" movement. Blakey continued leading the band until his death in 1990, ending a run of more than 35 years.

Style and legacy

The Messengers' sound — blues- and gospel-inflected melodies, driving swing, Blakey's thunderous press rolls and shuffle beat — defined hard bop. Albums such as Moanin' (1958), A Night in Tunisia (1960), The Big Beat (1960), and Free for All (1964) are canonical. The band's alumni network shaped jazz for half a century, and the "Messengers" concept of a veteran-led training band remains a model.
