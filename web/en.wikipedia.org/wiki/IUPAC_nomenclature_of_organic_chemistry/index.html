IUPAC nomenclature of organic chemistry

The IUPAC nomenclature of organic chemistry is a systematic method of naming organic chemical compounds as recommended by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC). It is published in the Nomenclature of Organic Chemistry (informally called the Blue Book). The system ensures, in principle, that every organic compound has a unique, unambiguous name from which its structure can be reconstructed, and vice versa.

Principles

A systematic name is built from a parent structure (usually the longest carbon chain or a ring system), prefixes identifying substituents, locants (numbers) giving their positions, and a suffix indicating the principal functional group. Thus butane becomes, when substituted, 2-methylbutane; a chain bearing a hydroxyl group as its main function takes the suffix -ol, as in ethanol. Priority rules decide which functional group is the principal one, and stereochemistry is described by descriptors such as (R)-, (S)-, (E)-, and (Z)-.

History

Systematic organic nomenclature originated with the Geneva rules of 1892, developed under the leadership of Charles Friedel and others. IUPAC, founded in 1919, continued this work through successive editions, culminating in the 2013 Blue Book, which introduced the concept of the "preferred IUPAC name" (PIN) for regulatory and general use.

Retained names and common usage

Some traditional names are retained in the system, such as water, ammonia, benzene, toluene, and acetic acid. In everyday practice chemists also use trivial and trade names, but systematic IUPAC names are the standard for publications, patents, and regulation.
