Magic acid

Magic acid is a superacid consisting of a mixture, most commonly in a 1:1 molar ratio, of fluorosulfuric acid (HSO3F) and antimony pentafluoride (SbF5). It is one of the strongest acids known, with a Hammett acidity function around −23, millions of times stronger than concentrated sulfuric acid.

History

The system was developed in the 1960s in the laboratory of George A. Olah, who used it to generate and observe long-lived carbocations in solution — work that earned him the 1994 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The name "magic acid" arose after a Christmas party in Olah's laboratory, when a paraffin candle placed in the acid dissolved, showing that the acid could protonate hydrocarbons, which were assumed inert to acids; the name stuck after the anecdote was published.

Properties and uses

Magic acid protonates very weak bases, including alkanes, and is used in research to stabilize carbocations and other reactive intermediates for spectroscopic study. It is extremely corrosive and reacts violently with water, requiring careful handling.
