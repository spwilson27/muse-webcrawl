Right of way

This article is about the legal right to pass over land. For priority in traffic, see Right-of-way (traffic). For the land strip used by transport infrastructure, see Right of way (transportation).

A right of way is a type of easement granted or reserved over the land of another — landowner's property — for transportation purposes, such as a footpath, road, railway, canal or utility line. The term also denotes the land itself, and, in another branch of usage, the legal right of the public to pass over private land along a defined route, as with the extensive network of public rights of way in England and Wales.

Types

  • Private rights of way — easements benefiting a specific property (e.g. a driveway over a neighbour's land), created by express grant, implication, or prescription through long use.
  • Public rights of way — routes the public may use, classified by permitted users.
  • Utility easements — rights for pipelines, power lines and cables.
  • Transportation corridors — in North American usage, "right-of-way" commonly means the strip of land occupied by a road, rail line or trail, and its width.

Public rights of way in England and Wales

England and Wales have roughly 140,000 miles (225,000 km) of public rights of way recorded on definitive maps maintained by local authorities. Categories include: public footpaths (on foot only), bridleways (foot, horse and bicycle), restricted byways, and byways open to all traffic (BOATs). Historically many routes arose as church paths, drove roads and enclosure roads. The Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 ("CRoW Act") added a right of access on foot to mapped open country ("right to roam"), complementing the path network. Landowners must keep paths unobstructed and maintain stiles and gates; highway authorities maintain the surface.

Creation and extinguishment

Rights of way arise by express grant or reservation, by statute, by dedication by the landowner accepted by the public, or by presumed dedication after a period of open public use — traditionally 20 years under the Rights of Way Act 1932 and the Highways Act 1980. The old maxim "once a highway, always a highway" reflects that extinguishment normally requires a legal order. In the United States, similar results are reached through prescriptive easements, whose period and elements vary by state, and through eminent domain for public corridors.

Related concepts

"Right of way" also denotes traffic priority — the right of a vehicle or vessel to proceed ahead of another (see right-of-way (traffic)), governed by highway codes and the maritime COLREGs. In aviation and maritime law, stand-on and give-way rules allocate the "right of way" between craft.
