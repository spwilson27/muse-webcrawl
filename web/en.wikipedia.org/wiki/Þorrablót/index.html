Þorrablót

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Þorrablót (Icelandic: [ˈθɔrːaˌplouːt]) is an Icelandic midwinter festival that takes place during the month of Þorri, the fourth month of winter in the old Icelandic calendar, beginning on a Friday between 19 and 25 January (Bóndadagur, "Husband's Day" or "Farmer's Day") and continuing through the month. It is celebrated with feasts of traditional preserved foods known collectively as þorramatur.

History

The name derives from blót, a sacrifice or feast in Old Norse religion, associated with Þorri, a legendary figure personifying frost — in one saga tradition the father of the goddess Gói, for whom sacrifices were made when she disappeared. References to þorrablót appear in medieval sources, but the modern festival is largely a 19th- and 20th-century revival: Icelandic students in Copenhagen held a þorrablót in 1873, and celebrations spread through Iceland's local associations (þorrablót committees) during the independence era as an expression of national identity.

Þorramatur

The traditional buffet, served on wooden troughs, consists of foods preserved by the old winter methods of pickling in whey, curing, smoking, and drying:

  • Hákarl — fermented (putrefied) Greenland shark, notorious for its ammonia-rich smell.
  • Súrmatur — whey-pickled items including ram's testicles (hrútspungar), sheep's head jelly (sviðasulta), and blood pudding (blóðmör).
  • Svið — singed and boiled sheep's head.
  • Harðfiskur — wind-dried fish eaten with butter.
  • Hangikjöt — smoked lamb, and rúgbrauð, dense rye bread traditionally baked in geothermal ground.

Meals are commonly accompanied by brennivín, Iceland's caraway schnapps. Modern celebrations add speeches, songs, and satirical annual reviews (annáll).
