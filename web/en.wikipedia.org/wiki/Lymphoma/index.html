Lymphoma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lymphoma is a group of blood cancers that develop from lymphocytes — the white blood cells of the Lymphatic system — forming tumours most often in lymph nodes, the Spleen and other lymphoid tissues. The two broad families are Hodgkin lymphoma and non-Hodgkin lymphomas, the latter comprising dozens of subtypes of very different behaviour.

Features

The hallmark is painless swelling of lymph nodes, often in the neck, armpit or groin. 'B symptoms' — fever, night sweats and weight loss — occur in some patients and guide staging. Hodgkin lymphoma, identified by Reed–Sternberg cells, typically affects young adults and is one of the most curable cancers. Non-Hodgkin lymphomas range from slow-growing (indolent) forms watched without treatment to aggressive forms needing urgent combination chemotherapy.

Treatment

Treatment depends on type and stage: Chemotherapy combinations, the anti-CD20 antibody rituximab (a landmark Immunotherapy), Radiation therapy, and increasingly CAR T-cell therapy for relapsed disease. Some lymphomas are linked to infections — Epstein–Barr virus, HTLV-1, and Helicobacter pylori in gastric MALT lymphoma, which can sometimes be cured with antibiotics alone.
