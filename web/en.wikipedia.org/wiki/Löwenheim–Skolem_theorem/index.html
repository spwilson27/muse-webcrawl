Löwenheim–Skolem theorem

In mathematical logic, the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem states that if a countable first-order theory has an infinite model, then for every infinite cardinal number κ there is a model of size κ. In particular, every countable theory with an infinite model has a countable model. The theorem is named after Leopold Löwenheim and Thoralf Skolem, who proved early forms of it in 1915–1920.

Statement

The theorem has two parts. The downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem says that if a first-order theory in a countable language has an infinite model, it has a countable model; more generally, elementary substructures can be found of any prescribed infinite cardinality not smaller than that of the language. The upward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem says that if a theory in a countable language has an infinite model, it has models of every larger cardinality. The upward part is typically proved using the compactness theorem.

Consequences

A striking consequence is Skolem's paradox: set theory, if consistent, has a countable model, even though it proves that uncountable sets exist. The paradox is resolved by noting that "uncountable" within the model means only that no bijection belonging to the model enumerates the set; from outside, the model is countable. The theorem shows that first-order logic cannot control the infinite cardinalities of its models: no first-order theory with an infinite model can characterise structures such as the real numbers up to isomorphism, and properties like finiteness and countability are not expressible in first-order logic.

History

Löwenheim proved in 1915 that a first-order sentence satisfiable at all is satisfiable in a countable domain. Skolem clarified and generalised the result in the 1920s, and Kurt Gödel's completeness theorem of 1930 supplied another route to the downward part. Anatoly Maltsev extended the results to languages of arbitrary cardinality in 1936.
