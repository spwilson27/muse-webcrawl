Prebiotic (nutrition)

In nutrition, a prebiotic is a substrate that is selectively utilised by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit — the definition adopted by the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) in 2017. Most established prebiotics are non-digestible food carbohydrates, especially fructans such as inulin and fructooligosaccharides (FOS), and galactooligosaccharides (GOS), which pass to the colon and are fermented by beneficial gut bacteria such as bifidobacteria and lactobacilli. Prebiotics are distinct from probiotics, which are live microorganisms themselves; the two are sometimes combined as "synbiotics".

History and definition

The concept was introduced by Glenn Gibson and Marcel Roberfroid in 1995, describing food ingredients that beneficially affect the host by selectively stimulating the growth or activity of a limited number of colonic bacteria. The 2017 ISAPP consensus widened the definition beyond carbohydrates and beyond the gut, while retaining selectivity and a demonstrated health benefit as requirements.

Mechanism

Colonic fermentation of prebiotic fibres yields short-chain fatty acids — acetate, propionate, and butyrate. Butyrate is the principal fuel of colonocytes; short-chain fatty acids also lower colonic pH, support gut barrier function, modulate the immune system, and may improve mineral absorption (notably calcium and magnesium) and metabolic health. Reported benefits include increased stool bulk, and possible improvements in blood lipids and glycaemic control, though clinical evidence varies by compound and outcome.

Sources and safety

Beyond supplements and fortified foods, prebiotic fructans occur naturally in chicory root, onions, garlic, leeks, and wheat; GOS are produced industrially from lactose and are added to infant formula to mimic some functions of human milk oligosaccharides. Excess intake commonly causes gas, bloating, and loose stools — the same fermentable carbohydrates overlap with the FODMAP group restricted in some irritable bowel syndrome diets.
