A Crookes tube (also called a Crookes–Hittorf tube) is an early experimental electrical discharge tube, invented by the English physicist William Crookes around 1875, used to study cathode rays. It is a sealed glass tube containing a partial vacuum (roughly 0.1 Pa or less), with metal electrodes to which a high voltage is applied. Crookes tubes were the workhorse apparatus of late-19th-century atomic physics: with them J. J. Thomson measured the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron (1897), and Wilhelm Röntgen, observing a Crookes-type tube, discovered X-rays (1895).

A Crookes tube with a metal Maltese cross: the cross casts a sharp shadow, showing cathode rays travel in straight lines.

Operation

When a high voltage (several kilovolts, typically supplied by an induction coil) is applied between the cathode and anode, residual gas in the tube ionises; positive ions strike the cathode and liberate electrons, which are accelerated toward the anode. The electrons — "cathode rays" — travel in straight lines, cause the glass beyond the anode to fluoresce green, and cast sharp shadows of objects placed in their path (Crookes's famous Maltese-cross demonstration). Crookes also showed with a small paddle-wheel that the rays carried momentum (the wheel's motion is now attributed largely to radiometric effects).

History and successors

The tube developed from the earlier Geissler tube and the work of Julius Plücker and Johann Hittorf on electrical discharge in rarefied gases. Crookes believed cathode rays were a "fourth state of matter" ("radiant matter"). Successors include the Crookes radiometer experiments, the Braun tube (1897) which became the cathode-ray tube of oscilloscopes and televisions, and the Coolidge hot-cathode X-ray tube, which superseded cold-cathode tubes for radiography. Crookes tubes are still used today in physics teaching to demonstrate cathode rays, their deflection by electric and magnetic fields, and the production of X-rays.
