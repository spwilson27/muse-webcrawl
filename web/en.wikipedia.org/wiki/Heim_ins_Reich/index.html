Heim ins Reich

Heim ins Reich (German for "back home to the Reich") was a propaganda slogan and political policy of Nazi Germany from 1938 to 1944, directed at convincing ethnic Germans (Volksdeutsche) living outside the Reich—principally in territories assigned to Poland, the Baltic states, South Tyrol, and areas of the Soviet Union—to leave those homelands and settle in the German Reich, especially in the territories annexed from Poland at the start of the Second World War. The phrase was first popularised in connection with demands that the Sudetenland, Austria and other German-populated borderlands be incorporated into the Reich, before its primary meaning shifted to the organised population transfers after 1939.

Population transfers

Following the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact and agreements with Italy, the Soviet Union, Estonia, Latvia and Romania, hundreds of thousands of ethnic Germans were resettled under the slogan: Baltic Germans (about 65,000 from Latvia and Estonia in 1939–40), Germans from Volhynia, Galicia and the Narew region, Bessarabian and Bukovinian Germans, and South Tyroleans who opted for Germany under the Option Agreement with Italy. Resettlers were processed by the SS-run Volksdeutsche Mittelstelle, screened for racial and political reliability, and installed on farms and businesses in the annexed Warthegau and Danzig-West Prussia—properties frequently confiscated from Poles and Jews who were expelled or murdered. The programme was administered in the context of Heinrich Himmler's role as Reich Commissioner for the Consolidation of German Nationhood and formed part of the wider Germanisation and Generalplan Ost policies.

Aftermath

Most resettlers were themselves expelled westwards at the end of the war during the flight and expulsion of Germans from Eastern Europe in 1945–50. The slogan has since been studied as an example of irredentist propaganda and demographic engineering.
