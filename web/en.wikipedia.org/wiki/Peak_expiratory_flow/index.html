Peak expiratory flow

The peak expiratory flow (PEF), also called peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR), is a person's maximum speed of expiration, as measured with a peak flow meter — a small, hand-held device used to monitor a person's ability to breathe out air. It measures the airflow through the bronchi and thus the degree of obstruction in the airways. Peak expiratory flow is typically measured in units of litres per minute (L/min).

Function

Peak flow readings are higher when patients are well and lower when the airways are constricted. From changes in recorded values, patients and physicians can determine lung functionality, the severity of asthma symptoms, and treatment needs. Measurement of PEF requires training to correctly use a meter, and the normal expected value depends on a patient's age, sex and height. It is classically reduced in obstructive lung disorders such as asthma, and correlates reasonably with the forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) measured by spirometry.

History

The first instrument for measuring peak expiratory flow was designed by B. M. Wright and introduced in the late 1950s. The original Wright peak flow meter was followed by the smaller, inexpensive Mini-Wright meter, which made home monitoring practical for people with asthma.

Use in asthma management

People with asthma are often advised to keep a diary of morning and evening peak flow readings. A personal best value is established when the asthma is well controlled, and action plans commonly use zones based on percentages of that best: readings in the "green zone" (80–100%) indicate control, the "yellow zone" (50–80%) suggests caution and possible adjustment of medication, and the "red zone" (below 50%) indicates a medical alert. Variability between morning and evening readings — "diurnal variation" — is itself a marker of airway hyperresponsiveness.

Reference values

Normal values in healthy adults are roughly 400–700 L/min in men and 300–500 L/min in women, varying with height and age; charts such as those derived by Nunn and Gregg are used to predict expected values. Because devices and scales differ (the older Wright scale and the newer EU scale give different readings), values should be compared using the same meter and scale.
