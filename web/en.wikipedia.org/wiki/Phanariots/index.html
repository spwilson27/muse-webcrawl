Phanariots

Phanariots, also spelled Phanariotes (Greek: Φαναριώτες), were the influential Greek elite families of the Phanar district of Ottoman Constantinople who, from the later 17th century until 1821, served the Ottoman government as translators and diplomats and governed the tributary principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia as appointed princes (hospodars). This article covers the same families and institutions from the standpoint of their political role within the Ottoman system.

Origins

The Phanariots descended from Byzantine noble houses and from prosperous Greek merchant clans of the Aegean and Constantinople. Their quarter, Phanar — named for a lighthouse (fanari) on the Golden Horn — contained the Ecumenical Patriarchate from 1601, and the families staffed the Patriarchate's administration while trading throughout the Mediterranean. Education in Italian, French, and Turkish, often acquired at the University of Padua, made them the empire's natural intermediaries with Europe.[1]

Offices and rule in the Principalities

As Grand Dragomans of the Porte and Dragomans of the Fleet, Phanariots translated treaties and effectively conducted Ottoman diplomacy; Nicholas Mavrocordatos became the first Phanariot hospodar of Moldavia (1711) and then of Wallachia (1716). Thereafter both thrones rotated among a small circle of families — Mavrocordatos, Ghica, Callimachi, Ypsilantis, Soutzos, Racoviță, and others — who purchased appointments at auction, recouped their investment through taxation, and typically reigned only a few years. The era, called the Phanariot period in Romanian historiography, brought administrative and legal reforms (notably Constantine Mavrocordatos's abolition of serfdom in Wallachia in 1746 and Moldavia in 1749) alongside fiscal exaction and resentment of Greek cultural dominance.

1821 and legacy

When Alexander Ypsilantis — himself a Phanariot — crossed the Prut in 1821 to raise revolt in the Principalities as a prelude to the Greek War of Independence, the Porte destroyed the Phanariot system: the Patriarch was hanged, dragomans were replaced by a Muslim translation office, and native boyars resumed the thrones. Phanariot families nonetheless remained prominent in the new Greek kingdom and in Romanian public life, and their courts had earlier transmitted Enlightenment ideas into southeastern Europe.
