Iodine clock reaction

After a delay, the solution abruptly turns dark blue as iodine forms a complex with starch.

The iodine clock reaction is a classical chemical clock demonstration experiment in which two colourless solutions are mixed and the resulting mixture remains colourless for a period of time before suddenly turning dark blue. It displays the phenomenon of chemical kinetics in action and was discovered by Hans Heinrich Landolt in 1886.

Mechanism

In a common variant, hydrogen peroxide oxidises iodide ions to iodine:

H2O2 + 2 I− + 2 H+ → I2 + 2 H2O

A reducing agent such as thiosulfate immediately converts the iodine back to iodide as fast as it forms:

2 S2O32− + I2 → S4O62− + 2 I−

Only once the thiosulfate is exhausted does free iodine accumulate; it then forms an intensely blue-black complex with starch indicator. The delay before the colour change depends on temperature and concentrations, making the reaction a striking demonstration of reaction rates.

Variations

Several variants exist, including the persulfate–iodide clock and versions using vitamin C (ascorbic acid) as the reducing agent, commonly used in school laboratories. The reaction is related to the Briggs–Rauscher oscillating reaction.
