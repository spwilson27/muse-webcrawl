Light curve

In astronomy, a light curve is a graph of light intensity of a celestial object or region, plotted as a function of time. The light is usually measured in a particular frequency interval or band (for example, visible light or a photometric filter such as V). Light curves may be periodic, as in the case of eclipsing binaries, Cepheid and other variable stars and transiting exoplanets, or aperiodic, as in the light curve of a nova, a cataclysmic variable star, a supernova, a microlensing event or a gamma-ray burst. The study of light curves, together with other observations, can yield considerable information about the physical processes that produce them or constrain physical theories about them.

Variable stars and binaries

For periodic variables, the light curve is often "folded" at the period and plotted against phase. The shape of the curve classifies the variable: Cepheids show a rapid rise and slower decline; RR Lyrae stars have characteristic sawtooth curves; eclipsing binaries show flat maxima with sharp dips at conjunction, whose relative depths reveal the temperature ratio of the two stars, and whose widths give the radii relative to the orbit. Algol-type and β Lyrae-type systems are distinguished by whether the out-of-eclipse light is constant or continuously varying.

Planetary science

When a transiting exoplanet crosses its star, the light curve dips by an amount proportional to the square of the planet-to-star radius ratio; the shape and duration of the transit yield the planet's size, orbital inclination and period, and secondary eclipse (occultation) measurements give the planet's dayside brightness. Space missions Kepler and TESS discovered thousands of planets this way. For asteroids and other small bodies, brightness varies as the object rotates, and the light curve period gives the rotation period while its amplitude and shape constrain the body's shape and albedo distribution; inversion techniques can reconstruct convex shapes from dense light curves.

Transients

Supernova light curves — powered first by shock heating and then by the radioactive decay of nickel-56 to cobalt-56 and iron-56 — classify explosions and, for Type Ia supernovae, underpin their use as standardisable candles for measuring cosmic distances. Active galactic nuclei, tidal disruption events and gamma-ray bursts are likewise characterised largely by their time-varying light.
