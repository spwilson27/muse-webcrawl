Stickler syndrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stickler syndrome (hereditary progressive arthro-ophthalmodystrophy) is a group of hereditary connective tissue disorders characterized by distinctive facial features, eye abnormalities, hearing loss, and joint problems. It is caused by mutations in genes encoding types II, IX, and XI collagen, which are important components of cartilage and the vitreous of the eye. The condition is named after Gunnar B. Stickler, who described it in a 1965 paper with colleagues at the Mayo Clinic. Estimates of prevalence range from about 1 in 7,500 to 1 in 10,000 births.

Signs and symptoms

Affected infants may be born with the Pierre Robin sequence — a small jaw, cleft palate, and a tongue placed unusually far back — which can cause feeding and breathing difficulties. Facial features often include a flat midface with a depressed nasal bridge. Eye problems are prominent: severe nearsightedness (myopia), an abnormally empty-appearing vitreous gel, cataracts, and a high risk of retinal detachment, which can cause blindness if untreated. Hearing loss, affecting both conductive and sensorineural components, is common and varies in severity. Skeletal features include joint hypermobility in childhood, mild spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, and early-onset arthritis that may be the most disabling feature in adults.

Genetics

Most cases are autosomal dominant, caused by mutations in COL2A1 (type 1) or COL11A1 (type 2); rarer autosomal recessive forms involve COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3. The related Marshall syndrome overlaps considerably with Stickler syndrome and is also associated with COL11A1 mutations.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is based on clinical features and confirmed by genetic testing. There is no cure; management is multidisciplinary, including regular ophthalmologic surveillance (with prophylactic retinal treatment sometimes recommended to reduce detachment risk), audiology care and hearing aids, cleft palate repair, and physiotherapy and pain management for joint disease. Genetic counselling is offered to affected families because each child of an affected parent has a 50% chance of inheriting autosomal dominant forms.
