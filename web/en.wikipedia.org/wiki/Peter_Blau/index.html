Peter Blau

Born: February 7, 1918, Vienna, Austria

Died: March 12, 2002, New York City, United States

Known for: Exchange theory, organizational sociology, macrostructural theory

Peter Michael Blau (February 7, 1918 – March 12, 2002) was an Austrian-born American sociologist best known for his work on social exchange theory, the sociology of organizations and bureaucracy, and the macrostructural theory of social structure.

Life and career

Blau was born in Vienna and fled Austria after the Nazi Anschluss in 1938, emigrating to the United States, where he became a citizen in 1943 and served in the U.S. Army during World War II. He studied at Elmhurst College and earned his Ph.D. at Columbia University in 1952, where he was influenced by Robert K. Merton. He taught at the University of Chicago (1953–1970), where he collaborated with Otis Dudley Duncan, and later at Columbia University, where he was a Distinguished Professor.

Major works

His early empirical studies of employment agencies and government bureaus produced The Dynamics of Bureaucracy (1955), a classic field study of how informal practices shape formal organizations. With W. Richard Scott he wrote Formal Organizations (1962). In Exchange and Power in Social Life (1964), Blau developed social exchange theory, analyzing how reciprocal exchanges of rewards generate power differentials and emergent social structures. With Duncan, he co-authored The American Occupational Structure (1967), a landmark study of social mobility whose "Blau–Duncan model" used path analysis to show how education mediates the transmission of occupational status across generations.

In later work, including Inequality and Heterogeneity (1977) and Crossing Social Circles (1994, with Joseph Schwartz), Blau developed a "macrostructural theory": social structure defined by the distribution of people among social positions, with parameters of heterogeneity and inequality determining rates of intergroup association.

Honors

Blau served as president of the American Sociological Association in 1973–74 and was elected to the National Academy of Sciences.
