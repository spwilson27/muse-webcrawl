Strange loop

Strange loop
A self-referential hierarchy that returns to its starting level
FieldPhilosophy, cognitive science, logic
Key proponentDouglas Hofstadter
Related conceptsSelf-reference, tangled hierarchy, paradox

A strange loop is a cyclic structure that goes through several levels in a hierarchical system. It arises when, by moving only upwards or downwards through the hierarchy, one finds oneself back where one started. Strange loops may involve self-reference and paradox.

The concept was developed and popularised by Douglas Hofstadter in his 1979 book Gödel, Escher, Bach: An Eternal Golden Braid, and developed further in his 2007 book I Am a Strange Loop, where he argues that the human self and consciousness are themselves strange loops.

Definition

Hofstadter defines a strange loop as follows:

"The phrase 'strange loop'... refers to an abstract loop in which, in the series of stages that constitute the cycling-around, there is a shift from one level of abstraction (or structure) to another, which feels like going upwards in a hierarchy, and yet somehow the successive 'upward' shifts turn out to give rise to a closed cycle. That is, despite one's sense of departing ever further from one's origin, one winds up, to one's shock, exactly where one had started out."

Strange loops are not just "feedback loops" but "tangled hierarchies": systems of levels in which higher levels act back upon lower ones in ways that violate the intended layering.

Examples

In logic and mathematics

Gödel's incompleteness theorems rest on self-referential statements constructed through Gödel numbering, allowing a formal system to "talk about itself". The liar paradox ("this sentence is false") is a classical elementary example.

In art

M. C. Escher's lithograph Drawing Hands (1948), in which two hands draw each other into existence, and his Print Gallery (1956), in which a viewer looks at a picture of a town that contains the gallery containing himself, are canonical visual strange loops. The Penrose stairs, an impossible staircase that ascends forever in a closed circuit, depict the same structure.

In music

J. S. Bach's canons—particularly the endlessly rising canon in the Musical Offering, which modulates upward repeatedly yet returns to its starting key—realise strange loops in sound; the Shepard tone produces a similar auditory illusion of endlessly rising pitch.

Consciousness

In I Am a Strange Loop, Hofstadter argues that a self—a sense of "I"—emerges when a perceptual system becomes rich enough to represent itself within itself, forming a strange loop in the brain. On this view, consciousness is not an extra substance but the pattern of the loop itself.
