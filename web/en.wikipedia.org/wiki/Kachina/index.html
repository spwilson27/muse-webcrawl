Kachina

A kachina (Hopi: katsina, pl. katsinam) is a spirit being in the religious worldview of the Pueblo peoples of the southwestern United States, most fully developed among the Hopi and Zuni. "Kachina" refers at once to the spirits themselves — beings associated with rain, fertility, ancestors and natural forces — to the masked dancers who personify them in ceremonies, and to the carved figures (tihu, popularly "kachina dolls") that represent them.

Beliefs and ceremonies

According to Hopi tradition, the katsinam dwell for half the year on the San Francisco Peaks (Nuvatukya'ovi), north of present-day Flagstaff, Arizona, and visit the Hopi villages from the winter solstice until midsummer, bringing rain, fertility and instruction. Masked members of kachina societies embody the spirits in plaza dances and ceremonies held in kivas. More than 400 distinct katsinam are recognised; among the best known are the Crow Mother (Angwusnasomtaka), the chief spirit Eototo, and his lieutenant Ahola.

Children are taught that the dancers are the spirits themselves; initiation, usually around the ages of nine to ten, reveals the dancers' human identity and marks entry into deeper religious knowledge. Kachina beliefs are thought to have spread through the Pueblo world after about 1275–1300 CE, possibly arriving from the south, and appear in rock art and kiva murals of the period.

Kachina dolls

Carved from cottonwood root and painted, tihu are traditionally given to Hopi girls and brides at ceremonies, then hung on the walls of the pueblo home — they are teaching objects and representations of the spirits, not idols or toys. Carvings made for the tourist and art market since the late 19th century have developed into a major art form, with master carvers' pieces highly prized by collectors. Zuni and other Pueblo groups have related, though distinct, traditions, such as the Zuni koko.
