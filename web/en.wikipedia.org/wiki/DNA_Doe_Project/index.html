The DNA Doe Project (DDP) is an American nonprofit organisation that uses investigative genetic genealogy to identify unidentified deceased persons — "John Doe" and "Jane Doe" cases. Founded in 2017 by forensic genealogist Colleen Fitzpatrick and writer Margaret Press, it relies on teams of volunteer genealogists working with DNA profiles uploaded to databases such as GEDmatch.

Method

DNA is extracted from the remains — often from bone or hair when samples are degraded — and sequenced to build a SNP profile suitable for genealogy databases. Volunteers then triangulate matches to construct the decedent's family tree and identify candidates, whose identities are confirmed by law enforcement through conventional means. The organisation is funded by donations and crowdfunding for individual cases.

Cases

The project's first identification, announced in 2018, was of a man found dead in a motel room in 2001, previously known by the alias he used, "Joseph Newton Chandler III". Other notable identifications include the "Buckskin Girl", Marcia King, murdered in Ohio in 1981 and identified in 2018, and numerous long-standing cases from across the United States and Canada. The project has announced well over one hundred identifications.

Ethics

The DDP works only on identifying the deceased, not on suspect identification, and observes database terms of service and consent provisions. Its work has raised discussion about privacy in genetic databases, which intensified after forensic genetic genealogy gained prominence in the Golden State Killer case in 2018.
