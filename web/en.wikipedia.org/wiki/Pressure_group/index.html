Pressure group

A pressure group, also called an interest group, advocacy group or lobby group, is an organisation that seeks to influence public policy in favour of a particular interest or cause without seeking to hold governmental office itself. Pressure groups are a central feature of pluralist accounts of democracy, in which power is dispersed among competing organised interests rather than concentrated in the state.

Types

Political scientists commonly distinguish sectional (or interest) groups, which represent a defined segment of society, from promotional (or cause) groups, which campaign for a belief or cause open to anyone. Trade unions, professional associations such as medical bodies, and trade associations are classic sectional groups; environmental organisations, human rights campaigns and anti-poverty charities are promotional groups. A further distinction is between insider groups, which governments routinely consult and which generally work within accepted channels, and outsider groups, which lack regular access and rely on public campaigning or direct action.

Methods

Insider methods include responding to government consultations, giving evidence to legislative committees, informal contact with officials and ministers, and supplying technical information — a resource that can make expert groups indispensable to policy-makers. Outsider methods include petitions, demonstrations, media campaigns, boycotts and, at the extreme, civil disobedience. Groups may also work through the courts, funding litigation, or through political parties, endorsing candidates and donating money where the law allows.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that pressure groups amplify the voices of the wealthy and well-organised: businesses can afford professional lobbying that diffuse interests such as consumers or taxpayers cannot easily match. Mancur Olson's logic of collective action explained why small groups with concentrated interests often defeat larger groups whose members can free-ride on others' efforts. Defenders respond that groups represent citizens between elections, bring expertise into government, protect minorities against majoritarian politics, and give a voice to interests — from patients to rare-disease sufferers — that parties neglect. Many democracies now regulate lobbying through registers of lobbyists and rules on gifts and revolving-door employment.

Relations with parties

Unlike political parties, pressure groups do not field candidates under their own banner or seek to form governments; their aim is to influence those who do. In practice the boundary can blur: some groups are affiliated with parties (as trade unions historically have been with labour parties), and single-issue campaigning organisations sometimes evolve into parties.
