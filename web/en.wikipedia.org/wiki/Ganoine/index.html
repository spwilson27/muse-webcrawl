Ganoine

Ganoine
Type: Enamel-like tissue
Found in: Ganoid scales of gars, bichirs, sturgeons
Underlying layer: Dentine (cosmine) and bone (isopedine)
Historical term: Coined from Greek ganos, "brightness"

Ganoine or ganoin is a hard, shiny, enamel-like tissue that forms the outer layer of ganoid scales in many non-teleost ray-finned fishes, including gars, bichirs, sturgeons and paddlefish, and in some lobe-finned fishes. It gives these scales their characteristic glossy, rhomboidal appearance.


Ganoid scales of a gar

Structure

Ganoine is a multilayered tissue composed of hydroxyapatite crystals arranged in rods, resembling the enamel of tetrapod teeth, though its developmental origin and precise homology with enamel have been debated. Beneath the ganoine layer lies dentine (in the form of cosmine) and a basal layer of lamellar bone called isopedine.

Evolution

Ganoine-covered scales were widespread among Palaeozoic and Mesozoic fishes, including early actinopterygians, and are prominent in the fossil record. Most modern teleosts have lost the ganoine layer, retaining thin elasmoid scales instead.
