Maximum sustained wind

The maximum sustained wind associated with a tropical cyclone is a common indicator of the storm's intensity. "Sustained wind" is the average wind speed over a specified period at a standard height of 10 meters above the surface; agencies differ in the averaging period used.

Measurement conventions

The U.S. National Hurricane Center and Joint Typhoon Warning Center use a 1-minute averaging period, while the World Meteorological Organization recommends, and most other warning centers (such as the Japan Meteorological Agency) use, a 10-minute average. Because of this, 10-minute sustained winds are typically about 0.88 times the 1-minute values for the same storm. The Saffir–Simpson hurricane wind scale classifies Atlantic and eastern Pacific hurricanes by their 1-minute maximum sustained winds.

Estimation

Where direct measurements are unavailable, maximum sustained winds are estimated from reconnaissance aircraft data (flight-level winds and dropsondes), surface observations, and satellite techniques such as the Dvorak technique, which infers intensity from cloud patterns. The strongest tropical cyclones on record, such as Typhoon Tip (1979) in terms of size and pressure and Hurricane Patricia (2015) in terms of wind speed (estimated 1-minute sustained winds of about 345 km/h), illustrate the upper range of observed values.
