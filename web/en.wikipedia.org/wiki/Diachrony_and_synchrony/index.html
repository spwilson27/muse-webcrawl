Diachrony and synchrony

Diachrony and synchrony are concepts in linguistics introduced by the Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure. A synchronic approach studies a language at a particular moment in time, as a complete system of interrelated elements, whereas a diachronic approach studies the development and evolution of a language through history.

Saussure's distinction

In his posthumously published Course in General Linguistics (1916), Saussure argued that linguistics should distinguish between the state of a language at a given time (état de langue) and the succession of such states over time. He illustrated the distinction with the analogy of chess: the state of the board at a given moment can be described without reference to the moves that produced it, while the sequence of moves belongs to the game's history. Saussure prioritised synchronic analysis, in contrast to the 19th-century comparative philology that dominated historical linguistics.

Usage

The distinction became foundational for structural linguistics and influenced other disciplines: Roman Jakobson and the Prague School softened the opposition by showing how change itself could be analysed structurally, and the terms have been adopted in anthropology, literary theory and the social sciences more broadly, where "synchronic" describes analysis of a system at one moment and "diachronic" analysis of its development.
