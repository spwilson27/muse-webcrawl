Leviathan (Hobbes book)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Leviathan; or, The Matter, Forme and Power of a Common-wealth Ecclesiasticall and Civill (1651) is a book by the English philosopher Thomas Hobbes, written during the English Civil War. Taking its name and famous frontispiece — a giant sovereign composed of hundreds of tiny subjects, holding sword and crozier — from the biblical sea monster, it is one of the foundational works of social contract theory and of modern political science.

Argument

Hobbes begins from human nature and reasoning about motion and passion: in the "state of nature" without a common power, equality of ability and scarcity produce a war of all against all, in which life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short". Reason discovers laws of nature — seek peace; lay down rights reciprocally — and rational individuals covenant to authorise a sovereign ("that mortal god") whose absolute, indivisible power guarantees security. Hobbes prefers monarchy but legitimises any effective sovereign form, extends the argument against church claims to independent authority (the Leviathan rules religion too), and leaves subjects only a narrow right to resist direct threats to their own lives. The book scandalised contemporaries (its materialism and Erastianism) and shaped every successor theory of sovereignty and the state, whether in imitation (Spinoza, Pufendorf) or reaction (Locke, and modern critics of absolutism).
