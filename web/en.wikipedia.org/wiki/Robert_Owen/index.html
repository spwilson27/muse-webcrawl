Robert Owen (14 May 1771 – 17 November 1858) was a Welsh textile manufacturer, philanthropist and social reformer, and the founder of utopian socialism and the co-operative movement. He strove to improve factory working conditions, promoted experimental socialistic communities, and sought a more collective approach to social organisation.

Early life

Owen was born in Newtown, Montgomeryshire, Wales, the sixth of seven children. His father was a saddler, ironmonger and local postmaster. Owen received little formal schooling but was an avid reader. At the age of ten he was sent to London to work as a draper's assistant, and he later moved to Manchester, then a centre of the cotton industry, where he rose rapidly from machine-maker to mill manager.

New Lanark

In 1799 Owen married Anne Caroline Dale, daughter of David Dale, owner of the large cotton mills at New Lanark in Scotland. With partners, Owen bought the New Lanark mills and became their manager in 1800. Encouraged by his success in improving conditions at Manchester, he transformed New Lanark into a model community: shorter working hours, no child labour under ten, company housing, free medical care, and schools — including in 1816 the Institute for the Formation of Character, an infant school often cited as the first in Britain.

New Lanark became internationally famous; visitors included the future Tsar Nicholas I of Russia. Owen's essays, collected as A New View of Society (1813), argued that human character is formed by circumstances and environment, and therefore that education and improved living conditions could remake society.

Reform campaigns

Owen campaigned for factory reform, helping to spur the Factory Act 1819, though he considered it far too weak. In 1817 he publicly embraced plans for "villages of unity and cooperation" — self-contained communities of about 1,200 people combining agriculture and industry — as a cure for unemployment and poverty following the Napoleonic Wars.

New Harmony

In 1825 Owen purchased the town of Harmony in Indiana, United States, from the Rappite religious community and renamed it New Harmony. He invested much of his fortune in the experiment, which attracted scientists, educators and reformers, but disputes over property, work and governance caused the community to dissolve by 1827. His sons, including Robert Dale Owen, remained in America.

Co-operatives and trade unions

Returning to Britain, Owen promoted workers' co-operative stores and an equitable labour exchange based on labour notes. In 1834 he helped found the Grand National Consolidated Trades Union, which briefly enrolled hundreds of thousands of members before collapsing. The Rochdale Pioneers, who opened their famous co-operative store in 1844, built on ideas Owen had championed, and he is widely regarded as a founding father of the co-operative movement.

Later life and death

Owen spent his later years lecturing and writing, publishing The Book of the New Moral World and his autobiography The Life of Robert Owen (1857). In old age he embraced spiritualism. He died in Newtown on 17 November 1858 and was buried there in St Mary's churchyard.

Legacy

Owen's ideas influenced labour movements, co-operatives, public education and factory legislation throughout the nineteenth century and beyond. New Lanark is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and New Harmony remains a landmark of American utopian history.
