Logical positivism, later called logical empiricism, is a philosophical movement that grew out of the Vienna Circle in the 1920s. Drawing on Wittgenstein's Tractatus, empiricism and the new mathematical logic, the positivists held that a statement is cognitively meaningful only if it is either analytic (true by definition or logic) or empirically verifiable. Statements that failed this test — much of traditional metaphysics, theology and ethics as factual discourse — were deemed literally meaningless, "nonsense" in the technical sense.

Core doctrines

  • The verification principle: "The meaning of a statement is its method of verification" (Schlick).
  • The analytic–synthetic distinction: mathematics and logic are analytic and a priori; science is synthetic and a posteriori.
  • Unified science: all genuine knowledge can in principle be unified, with a "physicalist" language of observable things and events (Neurath, Carnap).
  • Emotivism in ethics: moral utterances express attitudes rather than stating facts (Ayer, Stevenson).
  • Elimination of metaphysics: exemplified by Carnap's 1932 critique of Heidegger.

History and influence

The Vienna Circle (founded around Moritz Schlick, chair at Vienna from 1922) published its manifesto in 1929; parallel groups met in Berlin under Hans Reichenbach. The movement spread to Britain through A. J. Ayer's Language, Truth and Logic (1936) and to the United States through emigration after the Nazi rise to power — accelerated by Schlick's murder in 1936 and the Anschluss of 1938. Logical empiricism dominated philosophy of science for decades, shaping debates on explanation (the deductive-nomological model), confirmation and theory reduction.

Criticism and decline

The verification principle was attacked as self-refuting (it is itself neither analytic nor verifiable), and strict verifiability proved too strong for universal scientific laws, prompting liberalisations to "confirmability". W. V. O. Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) undermined the analytic–synthetic distinction, while Karl Popper's falsificationism and later work by Kuhn and others displaced the movement's core programme. Nevertheless, its standards of clarity and its analysis of science remain deeply influential in analytic philosophy.
