Encomium

Encomium

Genre: Epideictic rhetoric; speech or text of praise

Origin: Ancient Greece (Greek: ἐγκώμιον)

Notable examples: Isocrates' Evagoras; Gorgias' Encomium of Helen; Erasmus' In Praise of Folly (satirical)

Related: Panegyric, eulogy, epideictic oratory

An encomium (plural: encomia or encomiums) is a speech or piece of writing that praises someone or something highly. The word derives from the ancient Greek enkōmion (ἐγκώμιον), originally a song sung in a festive procession (kōmos) celebrating a victor. In classical rhetoric, the encomium was one of the central forms of epideictic (ceremonial) oratory, alongside its opposite, the invective or psogos (blame).

In classical rhetoric

Rhetoricians such as Aristotle, and the later authors of the progymnasmata school exercises, codified the encomium's structure: a prologue, the subject's origins and upbringing, deeds arranged by the virtues they display (wisdom, justice, courage, temperance), a favourable comparison (synkrisis) with others, and an epilogue. Famous surviving examples include Gorgias' playful Encomium of Helen, which defends Helen of Troy, and Isocrates' Evagoras, the first prose encomium of a contemporary individual, which became a model for later biography and for the Roman laudatio.

Later usage

The genre flourished in Rome (Pliny the Younger's Panegyricus to Trajan), in Byzantine court oratory, and in Renaissance humanism. Writers also exploited it ironically: Lucian wrote mock encomia of a fly and of gout, and Erasmus' In Praise of Folly (1509) made the paradoxical encomium a vehicle of satire. The word is still used today for formal tributes — for instance, of award citations and laudatory reviews — sometimes with a hint of excessive or uncritical praise.
