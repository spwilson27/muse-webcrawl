Dean–Stark apparatus

A Dean–Stark trap fitted between a reaction flask and a reflux condenser

The Dean–Stark apparatus, also called a Dean–Stark trap or distilling receiver, is a piece of laboratory glassware used in synthetic chemistry to collect water (or another liquid) from a reactor. It is used in combination with a reflux condenser and a distillation flask to remove water formed during a chemical reaction by azeotropic distillation, thereby driving an equilibrium reaction towards completion. The apparatus was invented by the American chemists Ernest W. Dean and David D. Stark of the United States Bureau of Mines, who described it in 1920 for determining the water content of petroleum samples.

Design and operation

The apparatus consists of a vertical cylindrical tube with a volumetric graduation down one side and a side arm at the top that connects back to the reaction flask, with a condenser fitted above. The reaction is performed in a solvent that forms an azeotrope with water and is immiscible with it — classically benzene, and more commonly today toluene or xylene. As the mixture refluxes, vapour of the azeotrope condenses and drips into the trap, where the water, being denser and immiscible, sinks to the bottom of the graduated section while the solvent overflows through the side arm back into the flask. The volume of water collected can be read from the graduations, giving both a practical removal of water and a measure of the reaction's progress.

For solvents less dense than water the standard trap is used; for solvents denser than water (such as chloroform), a modified trap allows the heavier solvent to sink and return while water collects on top.

Applications

The Dean–Stark trap is classically used to shift condensation equilibria that produce water, including esterifications, acetal and ketal formation, imine formation, and some aldol and Dean–Stark variants of amidation, exploiting Le Chatelier's principle. Its original analytical purpose — measuring the water content of petroleum and other samples by azeotropic distillation — remains a standard test method (ASTM D95). Similar traps are used in essential-oil distillation and in environmental analysis.
