The Seventeen Provinces were the imperial states of the Habsburg Netherlands in the 15th and 16th centuries. They roughly covered the Low Countries — that is, what is now the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, and parts of French Nord-Pas-de-Calais and western Germany.

The provinces

The seventeen states were the counties of Artois, Flanders, Hainaut, Holland, Namur, and Zeeland; the duchies of Brabant, Guelders, Limburg, and Luxembourg; the lordships of Friesland, Groningen, Mechelen, Overijssel, and Utrecht; the margraviate of Antwerp; and the county of Zutphen. They were first gathered as a single political unit under the Valois dukes of Burgundy and passed to the Habsburgs in 1482.

Union and division

Charles V formally united the provinces through the Pragmatic Sanction of 1549, declaring them an indivisible inheritance within the Holy Roman Empire's Burgundian Circle. Under his son Philip II of Spain, religious persecution and centralisation provoked the Dutch Revolt. The provinces split: the southern provinces formed the Union of Arras (1579) and returned to Spanish allegiance, while the northern provinces formed the Union of Utrecht and, by the Act of Abjuration (1581), renounced Philip — ending the Seventeen Provinces as a unit and giving rise to the Dutch Republic and the Spanish Netherlands.
