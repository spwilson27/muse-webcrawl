Phalaenopsis

Phalaenopsis (/ˌfælɪˈnɒpsɪs/), commonly known as moth orchids, is a genus of about seventy species of flowering plants in the orchid family, Orchidaceae. Native to India, Taiwan, southern China, Southeast Asia, New Guinea, and northern Australia, phalaenopsis are epiphytes or lithophytes with long, coarse roots, short stems, and flat, long-lasting flowers arranged on arching stems. They are among the most popular orchids in the pot-plant trade thanks to their ease of flowering in the home and their blooms that last for months.

Description

Phalaenopsis are monopodial epiphytes: they grow from a single stem bearing two rows of thick, elliptical leaves. Their silvery aerial roots perform photosynthesis and absorb moisture from the air. The genus name, given by Carl Ludwig Blume in 1825, derives from the Greek phalaina (moth) and opsis (appearance), because the flowers of some species resemble moths in flight.[1]

Cultivation

Commercial hybrids — derived largely from P. amabilis, P. schilleriana, and P. stuartiana — are mass-produced in Taiwan, the Netherlands, and elsewhere, frequently cloned by tissue culture. Plants prefer warm temperatures (18–29 °C), bright indirect light, and very open potting media such as bark; a night-time temperature drop induces a flower spike. Because they are CAM plants with low water demands, overwatering and root rot are the most common causes of failure for home growers. The white-flowered Phalaenopsis amabilis is one of Indonesia's national flowers (puspa pesona, "flower of charm").
