Herculaneum

Herculaneum (Italian: Ercolano) was an ancient Roman town located in the modern-day commune of Ercolano, Campania, Italy. It was buried under volcanic ash and pumice in the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in AD 79, the same eruption that destroyed the larger and better-known city of Pompeii.

History

According to legend, the town was named after Hercules. It was settled by Oscans, then influenced by Greeks and Samnites before coming under Roman control in 89 BC. A wealthy resort town of about 4,000–5,000 inhabitants, it was favoured by rich Romans who built luxurious seaside villas there.

Eruption and burial

Unlike Pompeii, which was buried mainly under falling ash, Herculaneum was overwhelmed by pyroclastic surges and flows that carbonised organic material and buried the town under roughly 20 metres of volcanic material that hardened into a rock-like layer. Because of this, wooden objects, textiles, foodstuffs, and even upper storeys of buildings survived in remarkable condition, and the remains of some 300 victims were found in the boathouses along the ancient shoreline.

Excavations

The site was rediscovered by chance in 1709 during the digging of a well, and tunnel excavations followed under Bourbon patronage. Open-air excavations began in earnest in the 20th century under Amedeo Maiuri. Only part of the ancient town has been excavated, as much lies beneath modern Ercolano.

Villa of the Papyri

The most famous building is the Villa of the Papyri, a luxurious villa attributed to Lucius Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus, father-in-law of Julius Caesar. Its library yielded some 1,800 carbonised papyrus scrolls, mainly Epicurean philosophical texts associated with Philodemus of Gadara. Modern imaging techniques, including X-ray phase-contrast tomography and machine-learning-based "virtual unwrapping," have begun to recover legible text from the scrolls without physically opening them.
