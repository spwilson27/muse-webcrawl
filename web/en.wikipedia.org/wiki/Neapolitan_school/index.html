Neapolitan school

The Neapolitan school was a school of operatic composers who worked in Naples, Italy, from roughly the 1650s to the early 19th century. Associated with the city's four conservatories, the school played a foundational role in the development of opera seria and in disseminating Italian operatic style throughout Europe.

Background

Naples, then one of Europe's largest cities, maintained four famed conservatories — Santa Maria di Loreto, Pietà dei Turchini, Sant'Onofrio a Capuana and Santa Maria della Pietà — which trained generations of composers and singers, including many castrati. The conservatories made Naples a centre of musical education whose graduates spread across the courts and theatres of Europe.

Style

The Neapolitan style codified conventions of Baroque and early Classical opera: the alternation of recitative (often secco, accompanied only by continuo) with da capo arias, the exit-aria convention, a clear hierarchy of singers by rank, and a sinfonia (overture) in a fast–slow–fast pattern that influenced the development of the Classical symphony. Alessandro Scarlatti is usually regarded as the school's founder; Pergolesi's intermezzo La serva padrona (1733) became a landmark of opera buffa.

Legacy

Composers trained in or influenced by the school — including Leonardo Vinci, Nicola Porpora, Leonardo Leo, Francesco Durante, Paisiello and Cimarosa — shaped operatic practice across Europe, from London to St Petersburg. The school's dominance waned in the early 19th century as tastes changed, though Naples remained an important operatic centre in the age of Rossini and Donizetti.
