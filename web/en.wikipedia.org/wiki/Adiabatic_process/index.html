An adiabatic process is a thermodynamic process in which no heat is transferred between a system and its surroundings. The term derives from the Greek adiabatos ("impassable"), referring to a boundary heat cannot cross. Adiabatic processes occur in perfectly insulated systems, or so rapidly that there is no time for significant heat flow. Compression of a gas adiabatically raises its temperature; adiabatic expansion cools it — effects that underlie diesel engines, refrigeration and much of atmospheric physics.

Reversible adiabatic processes

For a reversible (quasi-static) adiabatic change of an ideal gas, pressure and volume obey PVγ = constant, where γ = CP/CV is the heat capacity ratio (≈ 1.4 for air). Such a process is also isentropic — entropy stays constant — and curves of constant PVγ on a pressure–volume diagram (adiabats) are steeper than isotherms. For free expansion into a vacuum, by contrast, no work is done and (for an ideal gas) the temperature does not change, though entropy increases.

Applications

  • Atmosphere: rising air parcels cool at the dry adiabatic lapse rate (~9.8 °C per kilometre), governing cloud formation and stability; descending air warms, producing foehn and chinook winds.
  • Engines: the rapid compression stroke of a diesel engine heats air enough to ignite fuel without a spark.
  • Refrigeration and gas liquefaction: adiabatic expansion cools gases in refrigerators and in the Joule–Thomson and turbine expansion stages of liquefiers.
  • Sound: pressure oscillations in sound waves are approximately adiabatic, a fact used in deriving the speed of sound.
