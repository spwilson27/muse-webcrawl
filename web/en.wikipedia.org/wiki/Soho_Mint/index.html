Soho Mint

The Soho Mint was a private mint established by the manufacturer Matthew Boulton at his Soho works in Handsworth, near Birmingham, England, in 1788. It was the first mint to use steam-powered coin presses, and it revolutionised coinage by producing coins of consistent weight, size, and quality that were difficult to counterfeit.

History

Britain in the 1780s suffered an acute shortage of small change and a flood of counterfeit copper coins. Boulton, whose partner James Watt supplied the steam engines, set up presses that could strike up to 120 coins per minute, each with raised rims and lettering that made clipping and forgery easy to detect. Initially producing trade tokens and coinage for the East India Company and foreign governments, the mint won government contracts, most famously for the 1797 "cartwheel" pennies and twopences — enormous copper coins containing their face value in metal.

Boulton's mint also supplied the machinery and expertise for Britain's recoinage and later equipped the rebuilt Royal Mint in London. The Soho Mint closed in 1848, a few decades after Boulton's death, and its presses were sold; some survive in museum collections, and one press was later used by the Birmingham Mint's successors.

Legacy

The Soho Mint pioneered modern mechanised coin production, exporting complete mints abroad, including to Russia, Spain, and Denmark, and helped establish Birmingham as the world's centre of private minting in the 19th century.
