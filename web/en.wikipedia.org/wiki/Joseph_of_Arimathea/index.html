Joseph of Arimathea

Saint Joseph of Arimathea
Venerated inRoman Catholic Church, Eastern Orthodox Church, Oriental Orthodox Churches, Anglican Communion, Lutheran Church
Feast31 August (Catholic); 31 July (Orthodox traditions vary)
Known forProviding his own tomb for the burial of Jesus

Joseph of Arimathea (Ancient Greek: Ἰωσὴφ ὁ ἀπὸ Ἀριμαθαίας) is a figure in the New Testament who, according to the Gospels, was a wealthy and respected member of the Sanhedrin and a secret disciple of Jesus. After the crucifixion he asked Pontius Pilate for Jesus' body, wrapped it in linen and laid it in his own new rock-cut tomb — the burial place from which Christians believe the resurrection occurred. He is venerated as a saint in many Christian traditions.

Gospel accounts

All four canonical Gospels describe Joseph's role in the burial of Jesus. Matthew calls him a rich man and a disciple; Mark describes him as a respected member of the council "who was also himself looking for the kingdom of God"; Luke adds that he had not consented to the Sanhedrin's condemnation of Jesus; John presents him as a secret disciple who, with Nicodemus, prepared the body with a large quantity of myrrh and aloes. In the Gospel accounts he rolls a great stone against the tomb entrance, and the tomb is found empty on the third day.

Later traditions

Apocryphal writings such as the Gospel of Nicodemus expanded Joseph's story, portraying his imprisonment by the authorities and miraculous release. Medieval legend transformed him far beyond the biblical account: in the romance of Robert de Boron (c. 1200) Joseph receives the Holy Grail and uses it to collect Christ's blood, a story developed further in the Vulgate Cycle and Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur. British legend, attached to Glastonbury, held that Joseph came to Britain — in one version accompanied by the young Jesus — founded the first church there, and planted his staff, which flowered as the Glastonbury Thorn.

Veneration

Joseph is commemorated as a saint across Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican traditions. He became the patron of funeral directors and gravediggers, and his image — an elderly, dignified councillor bearing the body of Christ — is a frequent subject of deposition and entombment scenes in Western art, from Rogier van der Weyden to Caravaggio.
