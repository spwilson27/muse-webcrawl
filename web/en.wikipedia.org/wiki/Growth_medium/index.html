Growth medium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Bacterial colonies on an agar plate

A growth medium or culture medium is a solid, liquid, or semi-solid designed to support the growth of a population of microorganisms or cells via the process of cell proliferation or to support plants via tissue culture. Growth media contain the nutrients, growth factors, and other components necessary for the organisms' metabolism and division.

Types

Defined (synthetic) media have precisely known compositions, whereas complex media contain ingredients of uncertain composition, such as yeast extract or casein hydrolysate. Selective media contain agents (for example antibiotics, bile salts, or dyes) that favour the growth of particular organisms and suppress others, while differential media contain indicators that distinguish organisms by visible characteristics, as in MacConkey agar, which is both selective and differential. Enriched media are supplemented with blood, serum, or other nutrients to support fastidious organisms.

Agar and liquid media

Solid media are typically gelled with agar, a polysaccharide derived from red algae introduced to microbiology in the early 1880s by Fanny Hesse and adopted by Robert Koch's laboratory. Liquid media (broths) are used to grow large volumes of cells or to assay growth turbidimetrically. Plant tissue culture uses media such as Murashige and Skoog medium, and animal cell culture relies on formulations like DMEM and RPMI 1640, often supplemented with fetal bovine serum.

Use

Growth media are fundamental to clinical diagnostics, food and water safety testing, industrial fermentation, and research. Because no single medium supports all organisms, many environmental and clinical samples contain organisms that have never been cultured in the laboratory — the so-called "great plate count anomaly."
