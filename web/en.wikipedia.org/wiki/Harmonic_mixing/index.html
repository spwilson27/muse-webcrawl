Harmonic mixing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Harmonic mixing matches tracks by musical key, often using the circle of fifths or the Camelot system.

Harmonic mixing (also called key mixing or mixing in key) is a technique used by DJs in which successive tracks are mixed not only at matching tempos (beatmatching) but in harmonically compatible musical keys, so that the overlapping passages of two songs sound consonant rather than clashing. The practice draws on the classical rules of modulation and the circle of fifths.

Method

A DJ determines the key of each track — traditionally by ear at a piano, now usually by key-detection software (such as Mixed In Key, or the analysis built into Rekordbox, Serato and Traktor) — and selects transitions between tracks in the same key, in the relative major or minor, or in adjacent keys on the circle of fifths. Mixing up or down a perfect fifth, or shifting between relative major/minor, changes energy smoothly; bolder modulations (for example raising a semitone at a drop) are used deliberately to lift a set's intensity. Because changing playback speed alters pitch, classic vinyl mixing restricted key compatibility by tempo, a constraint removed by modern "key lock"/master-tempo processing that holds pitch constant while tempo changes.

The Camelot system

The widely used Camelot wheel, popularised by the software Mixed In Key, recodes the circle of fifths with alphanumeric labels (1A–12A for minor keys, 1B–12B for major), letting DJs mix between adjacent numbers or matching A/B pairs without music-theory knowledge. It is algorithmically equivalent to the circle of fifths; the rival Open Key notation serves the same purpose.

History and criticism

Although disco and early house DJs mixed harmonically by ear, harmonic mixing was systematised in the 1990s–2000s and popularised by trance and progressive house DJs who used long, blended transitions. Critics argue that over-reliance on key charts can make sets predictable and discourage adventurous selections; proponents counter that most great recorded DJ sets were harmonically coherent whether or not the DJ used the vocabulary.
