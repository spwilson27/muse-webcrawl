Laffer curve


The Laffer curve: revenue peaks at an intermediate rate

The Laffer curve illustrates a theoretical relationship between rates of taxation and the resulting levels of government revenue. It posits that at a tax rate of 0% the government collects nothing, and at 100% it also collects nothing, because no one would work for no return; somewhere between the two extremes lies a revenue-maximising rate, so that beyond a certain point raising rates actually reduces revenue by discouraging economic activity.

History

The concept is associated with the American economist Arthur Laffer, who, according to a famous anecdote, sketched the curve on a napkin during a 1974 dinner in Washington attended by Dick Cheney and Donald Rumsfeld. The idea itself is much older: it appears in the writings of the fourteenth-century scholar Ibn Khaldun and was discussed by John Maynard Keynes and Andrew Mellon. Popularised by Jude Wanniski and embraced by the Reagan administration, the curve became an emblem of supply-side economics and the case for the tax cuts of 1981.

Analysis and criticism

Economists broadly accept the curve's basic shape but dispute where real economies sit on it. Empirical estimates of the revenue-maximising top income-tax rate are typically far above recent rates in developed countries, and studies of the 1980s cuts found that they did not pay for themselves. The curve thus functions more as a caution against extreme rates than as a guide to policy.
