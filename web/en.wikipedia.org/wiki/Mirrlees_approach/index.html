Mirrlees approach

The Mirrlees approach is a method in mathematical economics, introduced by James Mirrlees in his 1971 paper "An Exploration in the Theory of Optimum Income Taxation", for solving optimal taxation and principal–agent problems in which individuals have private information about their characteristics (such as ability or effort). Instead of directly optimising over tax schedules subject to many incentive-compatibility constraints, the approach reformulates the problem so that the constraints can be handled with the tools of optimal control and calculus of variations.

The problem

In Mirrlees's model, individuals differ in skill (productivity) n, which the government cannot observe. Each person earns income y = n·l by supplying labour l, consumes c = y − T(y), and has utility u(c) − v(l). A redistributive government wants to design the income tax schedule T(·) to maximise social welfare, but any schedule must be incentive compatible: each person must prefer the income–consumption bundle intended for their type to the bundle intended for any other type. A high-ability person must not gain by "mimicking" a low-ability person and earning less.

The approach

Applying the revelation principle, the planner can be seen as choosing, for each type, an allocation (consumption and income) directly, subject to incentive-compatibility and resource constraints. Mirrlees showed that (under regularity conditions) it suffices to impose the local incentive-compatibility condition — an envelope (first-order) condition stating that a type's equilibrium utility must satisfy a differential equation driven by the labour allocation — together with a monotonicity condition on income. The design problem then becomes an optimal control problem: utility is the state variable, the allocation is the control, and the optimal tax schedule is recovered from the optimised allocation.

Results and applications

The approach yields the structure of optimal non-linear income taxes — notably that marginal tax rates should generally be positive, and (in the original bounded-skill model) zero at the very top — and it founded the modern "mechanism design" treatment of taxation, insurance, regulation, and contracts. Mirrlees shared the 1996 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with William Vickrey "for their fundamental contributions to the economic theory of incentives under asymmetric information". Later literature has examined where the first-order approach can fail, for example when monotonicity or concavity conditions are violated.
