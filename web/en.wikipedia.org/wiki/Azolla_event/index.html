Azolla event

The Azolla event is a paleoclimatological scenario hypothesized to have occurred in the middle Eocene epoch, around 49 million years ago, when blooms of the freshwater fern Azolla are thought to have covered the Arctic Ocean. As the ferns sank to the stagnant seabed they were incorporated into sediment, potentially sequestering large amounts of carbon dioxide and contributing to global cooling.

The floating fern Azolla, whose Eocene blooms may have drawn down atmospheric CO2

The event

During the early Eocene the Arctic Ocean was partly enclosed and, owing to heavy rainfall and river inflow, its surface layer became brackish to fresh — conditions in which Azolla, nourished by its nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterial symbiont Anabaena azollae, could multiply rapidly. Fossil Azolla remains and episodic organic-rich layers in Arctic Coring Expedition (ACEX) sediment cores indicate the blooms persisted, in episodes, for roughly 800,000 years.

Climatic significance

Some researchers estimate the event could have drawn down enough carbon dioxide to help end the early Eocene greenhouse, shifting Earth toward the "icehouse" climate of the later Cenozoic. The size of the contribution remains debated: critics note the Arctic basin's restricted size and question how much carbon was permanently buried rather than recycled.
