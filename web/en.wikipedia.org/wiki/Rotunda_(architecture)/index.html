Rotunda (architecture)

In architecture, a rotunda (from Latin rotundus, "round") is any building with a circular ground plan, and sometimes covered by a dome. It may also refer to a round room within a building, sometimes surmounted by a dome. The form has been used since antiquity for temples, tombs, churches, libraries, capitols and civic buildings.

History

The most famous ancient rotunda is the Pantheon in Rome (c. 125 CE), a rotunda of unsurpassed engineering whose concrete dome, pierced by a central oculus, remained the world's largest for many centuries. Roman tholos temples and imperial mausolea also used the form. Renaissance architects revived it — Bramante's Tempietto (1502) and Palladio's Villa Rotonda (1567–) are landmarks — and it became central to Neoclassical design.

Notable examples

  • Pantheon, Rome — ancient temple, now a church.
  • The Rotunda at the University of Virginia, designed by Thomas Jefferson (1826).
  • The United States Capitol rotunda in Washington, D.C., beneath the Capitol dome.
  • The Round Reading Room of the British Museum, London (1857).
  • The Rotunda of Mosta, Malta, with one of the world's largest unsupported domes.

Use

Rotundas often serve ceremonial or commemorative purposes: state funerals and lying-in-state ceremonies are held in capitol rotundas, and the form is common for mausolea, baptisteries and exhibition halls because of its symbolic completeness and acoustic properties.
