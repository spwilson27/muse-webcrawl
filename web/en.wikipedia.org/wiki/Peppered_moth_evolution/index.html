Peppered moth evolution

Peppered moth

Species: Biston betularia

Forms: typica (light), carbonaria (melanic)

Key researcher: Bernard Kettlewell

Gene identified: cortex (2016)

The evolution of the peppered moth (Biston betularia) is one of the most famous and frequently taught examples of natural selection in action. It describes the rise and fall of dark (melanic) forms of the moth in response to industrial air pollution in Britain.

Background

Before the Industrial Revolution, the light-coloured form of the moth, typica, predominated; its speckled wings camouflaged it against lichen-covered tree bark. A black form, carbonaria, was first recorded near Manchester in 1848. By the end of the 19th century, carbonaria made up about 98% of peppered moths in industrial areas, a change attributed to soot darkening trees and lichens dying off, so that birds preyed disproportionately on the now-conspicuous light moths.

Evidence and controversy

In the 1950s Bernard Kettlewell's release-and-recapture experiments in polluted and unpolluted woods supported the bird-predation explanation. Later critics questioned details of his methods, and the case was popularised — and sometimes misrepresented — in debates over evolution. Subsequent fieldwork by Michael Majerus, published posthumously in 2012, confirmed differential bird predation as the main driver. After clean-air legislation, carbonaria declined sharply, mirroring its rise.

Genetics

The melanic trait is controlled by a single gene. In 2016 researchers identified the responsible locus as the cortex gene, with a transposable element insertion dated to around 1819, matching the historical timeline.
