Star atlas

A star atlas is a collection of charts of the night sky, showing the positions of stars, constellations, and often deep-sky objects such as nebulae, star clusters, and galaxies. Star atlases have served astronomers for centuries as finding tools at the telescope and as records of the celestial sphere; amateur astronomers frequently use them together with the technique of star hopping — moving step by step from a bright, known star to a faint target.

History

Among the earliest printed star atlases was Johann Gabriel Doppelmayr's Atlas Coelestis (c. 1742), built on earlier charts. Johann Elert Bode's Uranometria (1801) pictured all stars visible to the naked eye. The photographic era produced the great survey atlases: the National Geographic Society – Palomar Observatory Sky Survey (POSS) plates of the 1950s, which for decades were the standard reference for faint objects, and its southern counterpart from the UK Schmidt Telescope.

Modern atlases

Popular printed atlases for observers include Norton's Star Atlas (first published 1910), Wil Tirion's Sky Atlas 2000.0, and the detailed Uranometria 2000.0, which plots over 280,000 stars. Computer planetarium programs and digital sky surveys — the Digitized Sky Survey, Hipparcos and Gaia catalogues — have largely superseded paper charts for professional work, but printed atlases remain valued at the telescope for their simplicity, scale, and freedom from screens and batteries.

Use in star hopping

In star hopping, the observer centers a bright star in a low-power finder, then moves the telescope through a sequence of recognizable star patterns shown on the atlas charts until reaching the target. The atlas's limiting magnitude and field scale are chosen to match the finder's field of view, making a well-matched atlas essential equipment for visual observers without computerized mounts.
