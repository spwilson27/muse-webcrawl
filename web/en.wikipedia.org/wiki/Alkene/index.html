Alkene

In organic chemistry, an alkene, also called an olefin, is a hydrocarbon that contains a carbon–carbon double bond. The term is often used more specifically to describe any unsaturated hydrocarbon that contains one or more double bonds. The simplest alkene is ethene (ethylene), C2H4. Acyclic alkenes with one double bond have the general formula CnH2n.

The presence of the double bond gives alkenes distinctive reactivity and geometry. The double bond consists of one sigma bond and one pi bond; rotation about it is restricted, which gives rise to cis–trans (E/Z) isomerism. The carbon atoms of the double bond are sp2-hybridised and trigonal planar, with bond angles of about 120°.

Reactions

Alkenes are relatively stable but more reactive than alkanes because of the pi bond. Characteristic reactions include electrophilic addition (of halogens, hydrogen halides, and water), hydrogenation to alkanes, oxidation, and polymerisation. Addition reactions often follow Markovnikov's rule. Industrially, polymerisation of ethene and propene yields polyethylene and polypropylene, among the world's most produced plastics.

Nomenclature

Alkenes are named using the suffix -ene in the IUPAC system, with a locant indicating the position of the double bond, e.g. but-1-ene and but-2-ene. Older common names such as ethylene and propylene are retained for the simplest members.

Occurrence and uses

Ethene is produced by plants as a hormone that promotes ripening of fruit. Alkenes are obtained industrially by steam cracking of petroleum fractions and are feedstocks for polymers, alcohols, and many other chemicals.
