Cayley's theorem

In group theory, Cayley's theorem, named in honour of Arthur Cayley, states that every group G is isomorphic to a subgroup of a symmetric group. In particular, a finite group of order n is isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group Sn of permutations of n elements.

Proof

The group G acts on itself by left multiplication: for each element g of G, the map sending each x in G to gx is a permutation of G. The assignment of these permutations to the elements of G defines an injective homomorphism from G into the symmetric group on G, showing that G is isomorphic to the subgroup formed by these "translation" permutations. The representation obtained in this way is called the regular representation of G.

Significance

Cayley's theorem shows that to understand finite groups, it suffices in principle to understand subgroups of symmetric groups, and it justifies studying abstract groups through their actions by permutation. Although the embedding is rarely the most efficient way to study a particular group, the theorem is a foundational result in the development of modern abstract algebra.

History

Cayley first stated the result in his 1854 paper "On the theory of groups, as depending on the symbolic equation θⁿ = 1", one of the earliest works of abstract group theory.
