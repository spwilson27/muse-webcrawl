RNA polymerase II (RNAP II or Pol II) is the enzyme complex in eukaryotic cells that transcribes DNA into precursors of messenger RNA and most small nuclear RNAs and microRNAs. Work on its structure and mechanism, notably by Roger Kornberg, was recognised by the 2006 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Structure

Pol II contains twelve subunits (named RPB1–RPB12 in yeast) forming a crab-claw shape with a central cleft that holds the DNA template, a funnel for incoming nucleotides and pores for the exiting RNA. The largest subunit, RPB1, carries a long C-terminal domain (CTD) made of repeats of the heptapeptide Tyr-Ser-Pro-Thr-Ser-Pro-Ser (26 repeats in yeast, 52 in humans), whose phosphorylation pattern — the "CTD code" — coordinates the stages of transcription and RNA processing.

Transcription cycle

Pol II cannot find promoters alone: general transcription factors (TFIID, TFIIB, TFIIF, TFIIE, TFIIH and others) assemble with it into a pre-initiation complex at core promoters, often marked by a TATA box. TFIIH unwinds the DNA and phosphorylates the CTD at Ser5 to trigger promoter escape and recruit capping enzymes. During elongation, Ser2 phosphorylation predominates and recruits splicing and 3′-end processing factors, so the mRNA is capped, spliced and polyadenylated as it emerges. After passing a polyadenylation signal, the polymerase is released and the transcript cleaved.

Regulation

At many metazoan genes Pol II pauses 20–60 nucleotides downstream of the start site and is released into productive elongation by the kinase P-TEFb — a major control point in development and responses to stimuli. Pol II is specifically inhibited by α-amanitin, the toxin of the death cap mushroom, which is why that poisoning destroys liver cells' ability to make new mRNA.
