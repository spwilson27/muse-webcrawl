Exorcism

Exorcism is the religious or spiritual practice of evicting demons or other malevolent spiritual entities from a person, place, or object believed to be possessed. The practitioner is called an exorcist, often a member of a religious order, a priest, or an individual believed to be endowed with special powers or skills.

In Christianity

The New Testament describes Jesus casting out demons, and exorcism remained part of Christian practice from antiquity. In the Roman Catholic Church, solemn ("major") exorcism may be performed only by a priest with the explicit permission of the local bishop, and only after medical and psychiatric evaluation has excluded natural causes. The rite was codified in the Rituale Romanum of 1614 and revised in 1999. Many Protestant and Pentecostal traditions practise related "deliverance ministry", typically with fewer formal restrictions.

In other religions

In Islam, ruqyah — recitation of Qur'anic verses and prayers — is used against jinn possession. Judaism has the tradition of expelling a dybbuk, a possessing spirit of the dead. Hindu and Buddhist traditions employ mantras, rituals and specialist practitioners against afflicting spirits, and shamanic cultures worldwide practise extraction of intrusive spirits by healers.

Medical and scholarly views

Medicine and psychology generally interpret possession states in terms of conditions such as dissociative disorders, psychosis, epilepsy or sleep paralysis, and regard exorcism's reported effects as arising from suggestion, ritual expectation and the placebo effect. Cases in which exorcisms have led to injury or death have resulted in criminal prosecutions in several countries.

In popular culture

William Friedkin's 1973 film The Exorcist, adapted from William Peter Blatty's novel, fixed the modern popular image of the rite and prompted a measurable rise in requests for exorcisms.
