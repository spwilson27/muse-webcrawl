Scottish banknotes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Scottish banknotes are banknotes of the pound sterling issued in Scotland by three authorised commercial banks: the Bank of Scotland, the Royal Bank of Scotland, and the Clydesdale Bank. Scotland is unusual in retaining a system of competitive private note issue dating back to the seventeenth century; the Bank of Scotland, founded in 1695, was among the first banks in Europe to issue notes redeemable for cash, and the Royal Bank introduced the world's first overdraft and the first fully printed notes.

Legal status

Contrary to popular belief, Scottish banknotes are not legal tender anywhere in the United Kingdom — not even in Scotland, where no banknote (including Bank of England notes) has legal tender status for most purposes, although they are universally accepted in Scotland and are legal currency approved by Parliament. Their issue is regulated under the Banking Act 2009, which requires the issuing banks to hold backing assets — a proportion in Bank of England notes and deposits — at least equal to the value of their notes in circulation. The Bank of England backs much of this with giant-denomination notes (£1 million "Giants" and £100 million "Titans") held in vaults.

Denominations and designs

The three banks issue £5, £10, £20, and £50 notes, and the Royal Bank also issues a £1 note, discontinued for general circulation in some series but still printed on special occasions; Clydesdale issued Scotland's first polymer £5 note in 2015 and also prints a £100 note. Designs celebrate Scottish people, places, and wildlife: Bank of Scotland notes feature Sir Walter Scott and bridges of Scotland; Royal Bank notes portray Lord Ilay and Scottish castles; Clydesdale notes have featured Robert Burns, Mary Slessor, and, on its polymer series, the scientist Mary Somerville and the poet Nan Shepherd — the first women to appear on Scottish notes other than the Queen.

Acceptance

Scottish notes are occasionally refused by shops and drivers in England and Wales unfamiliar with them, which prompts periodic political discussion, though they must in principle be accepted wherever sterling is taken and can always be paid into banks. Commemorative notes, such as the Royal Bank's issues for the Ryder Cup and the Commonwealth Games, are also produced.
