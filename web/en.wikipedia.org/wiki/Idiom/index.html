FieldLinguistics, lexicography
DefinitionA fixed expression whose meaning is not predictable from its parts
Examples"Kick the bucket" (die), "spill the beans" (reveal a secret)
Related termsProverb, cliché, euphemism, colloquialism

In language, an idiom is a group of words whose meaning is established by usage as having a definition not deducible from the meanings of the individual words. To "kick the bucket" has nothing to do with kicking or buckets: it means "to die". Idioms occur in every natural language; estimates for English run to tens of thousands. They are a subset of the broader class of multi-word expressions that includes phrasal verbs, collocations, and proverbs.

Characteristics

Idioms are conventionally fixed: their wording resists modification ("spill the chickpeas" does not work), and they are non-compositional: the meaning cannot be built from the parts. Many are metaphorical or metonymic in origin — "let the cat out of the bag" probably derives from market fraud involving substituted animals — though the origin is often opaque to speakers, as with "the whole nine yards". Some idioms are only partly frozen and admit variation ("take a rain check / give someone a rain check").

Types

  • Opaque idioms: meaning entirely unpredictable from the parts ("beat around the bush").
  • Transparent idioms: some connection is recoverable ("lay one's cards on the table").
  • Phrasal idioms built on phrasal verbs ("put up with", "run into").
  • Similes and binomials: "as cool as a cucumber", "part and parcel".
  • Proverbs, which overlap with idioms in being fixed and formulaic ("a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush").

In linguistics and processing

Idioms pose a problem for compositional theories of semantics: a purely compositional grammar would predict "kick the bucket" to mean striking a pail. Psycholinguistic research suggests that familiar idioms are stored and retrieved as units, and that idiomatic meanings are accessed directly rather than assembled word by word, though decomposition does play a role for less fixed expressions. In translation and second-language learning, idioms are notoriously difficult: they rarely translate literally, and idiomatic mastery is a mark of advanced proficiency.
