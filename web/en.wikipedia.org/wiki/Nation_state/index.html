Nation state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nation state is a political unit in which the boundaries of the state (the governing apparatus) and the nation (a cultural and ethnic community) coincide — "a state for a nation". It is the reigning ideal-type of modern political geography, legitimised by the principle that each nation deserves its own sovereign state (self-determination), and the model to which the world's map was progressively redrawn after 1918, 1945 and 1989.

Ideal and reality

The concept crystallised with the French Revolution (the nation as the sovereign people) and 19th-century nationalism (Herder, Fichte, Mazzini): language, history and culture define the nation; the nation demands the state. Germany and Italy's unifications and Greece's independence were its first triumphs; the settlement after the First World War applied it across Eastern Europe — imperfectly, since populations were hopelessly intermixed, producing the minority treaties and, ultimately, the catastrophic "solutions" of population transfer and genocide. In truth, few states are pure nation states: Iceland and the Koreas approach homogeneity; Japan is often cited but hosts Ainu, Okinawan and immigrant communities; most states are multinational (the United Kingdom contains four nations; India, dozens) and most nations stateless or divided (Kurds, Catalans — see Catalonia's 2017 crisis). Scholars therefore treat the nation state less as a description than as a claim — states "nationalise" themselves through schools, armies, language laws and censuses — and globalisation, migration and supranational bodies like the EU are said both to erode it and to provoke its nationalist defence. The form remains, however imperfect, the default container of political life on Earth, from the largest democracies to the smallest microstate.
