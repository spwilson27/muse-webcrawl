Safety case

Safety case
DefinitionStructured argument, supported by evidence, that a system is acceptably safe
SectorsRail, nuclear, offshore oil & gas, defence, aviation, medical devices
Common notationsGoal Structuring Notation (GSN), Claims-Argument-Evidence (CAE)
OriginsUK safety regulation following inquiries such as Piper Alpha (1988)

A safety case is a structured argument, supported by a body of evidence, that provides a compelling, comprehensible and valid case that a system is safe for a given application in a given environment. Rather than merely complying with prescriptive rules, the duty holder must identify the hazards of the system, assess the risks, and demonstrate that controls reduce those risks to an acceptable or "as low as reasonably practicable" (ALARP) level, with the whole argument documented for a regulator or assessor to scrutinise.

History and regulatory use

Safety case regimes were introduced prominently in the United Kingdom after disasters such as the Piper Alpha oil platform explosion of 1988, whose public inquiry under Lord Cullen recommended that offshore operators produce a formal safety case accepted by the regulator. Similar regimes were adopted for the onshore chemical industry, railways (following the Railways (Safety Case) Regulations), nuclear installations and defence equipment. Comparable documentation is required in many countries and in standards-based sectors such as automotive (ISO 26262) and medical software.

Structure

A safety case typically states top-level safety claims, decomposes them through an argument strategy into sub-claims, and links each claim to evidence such as hazard analyses (HAZOP, FMEA), test results, simulations, inspection records and operational experience. Goal Structuring Notation (GSN) diagrams are widely used to present the claim–argument–evidence structure. Because a safety case embodies assumptions about operation and maintenance, it must be maintained and updated throughout the system's life as the design and its use evolve.
