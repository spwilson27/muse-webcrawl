An aria is a self-contained piece for one voice in an opera, oratorio or cantata — an expressive, set-piece song, usually with orchestral accompaniment, in which a character pauses the action to dwell on an emotion. The word is Italian for "air", meaning a tune or melody. In Baroque opera the aria contrasted with recitative, the speech-like singing that carried the plot; in ensemble scenes, arias may be joined into duets, trios and larger numbers.

Forms

The dominant Baroque and early Classical form was the da capo aria (ABA), in which the first section is repeated after a contrasting middle, the repeat ornamented by the singer. Other types include the strophic aria, the two-tempo cavatina-and-cabaletta of 19th-century Italian opera, the prayer, drinking song and mad scene, and the accompanied monologue of Wagner and Verdi, where the strict division between aria and recitative dissolves. Famous showpiece arias display bravura coloratura, as in the Queen of the Night's "Der Hölle Rache" in The Magic Flute.

History

Arias developed from the solo songs of early opera around 1600, grew to spectacular length in opera seria, were reformed toward dramatic truth by Gluck, and reached new psychological depth in Mozart, Rossini, Bellini, Verdi and Puccini. In the age of recording, individual arias such as Puccini's "Nessun dorma" (popularised by Luciano Pavarotti) became cultural currency far beyond the opera house. The term is also borrowed for instrumental pieces of songlike character.
