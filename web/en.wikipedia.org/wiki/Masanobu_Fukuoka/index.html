Masanobu Fukuoka (Japanese: 福岡 正信; 2 February 1913 – 16 August 2008) was a Japanese farmer and philosopher celebrated for his natural farming and re-vegetation of desertified lands. He was a proponent of no-till, herbicide-free grain cultivation and the author of The One-Straw Revolution (1975), one of the most influential books in the organic farming and permaculture movements.

Life

Trained as a microbiologist and plant pathologist, Fukuoka worked as a customs inspector in Yokohama. At twenty-five, after a near-fatal illness and a sudden insight into the vanity of human knowledge, he resigned and returned to his family's farm on the island of Shikoku to put his conviction — that nature, left alone, is perfectly balanced — into practice.

Natural farming

Fukuoka called his method "do-nothing farming": no plowing, no chemical fertilisers or prepared compost, no weeding by tillage or herbicides, and no dependence on chemicals. He sowed rice and winter grains in balls of clay ("seed balls") among a living mulch of white clover, returned straw to the fields, and relied on ducks, frogs, and natural predation for pest control. His yields rivalled those of chemical agriculture in Japan.

Later in life he travelled widely — to India, Africa, Europe, and the United States — promoting seed balls for greening deserts and receiving awards including the Ramon Magsaysay Award in 1988.
