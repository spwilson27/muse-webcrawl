Free cash flow

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In corporate finance, free cash flow (FCF) is the cash a company generates from its operations after accounting for the capital expenditures needed to maintain or expand its asset base. It is the cash available to all investors — both debt and equity holders — or, in the variant called free cash flow to equity, to shareholders alone. Analysts regard free cash flow as a fundamental measure of a company's financial performance and health because it is harder to manipulate than accounting earnings and shows the money available for dividends, share buybacks, debt repayment, and growth.

Calculation

The most common definition of free cash flow to the firm (FCFF) is:

FCF = Operating cash flow − Capital expenditures

Starting from accounting figures, it is often expressed as net income plus non-cash charges (such as depreciation and amortisation), minus changes in working capital, minus capital expenditures. Free cash flow to equity (FCFE) additionally subtracts net borrowing effects, giving the cash available to common shareholders after all expenses, reinvestment, and debt payments.

Uses

Free cash flow is the basis of the discounted cash flow (DCF) method of valuation, in which a company's value is the present value of its projected future free cash flows. Investors also use the FCF yield — free cash flow per share divided by share price — as a valuation ratio comparable to the earnings yield. Persistently negative free cash flow may signal that a company depends on external financing, though for young growth companies it can reflect heavy investment.

Free cash flow hypothesis

In a widely cited 1986 paper, Michael Jensen proposed the free cash flow hypothesis: managers of firms with large free cash flow and few profitable investment opportunities tend to waste the surplus on empire-building acquisitions and perquisites rather than returning it to shareholders — an agency cost of free cash flow. Jensen argued that debt can discipline managers by committing cash to interest payments, a thesis used to explain the leveraged buyout wave of the 1980s.

Limitations

Free cash flow can be lumpy because capital expenditures are discretionary in timing; a company can flatter a single year's figure by deferring investment. It also says nothing directly about profitability, and definitions vary between data providers, so comparisons require care.
