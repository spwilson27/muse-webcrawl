Claw-free graph

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In graph theory, a claw-free graph is a graph that does not have a claw as an induced subgraph. The claw is the complete bipartite graph K1,3: a single central vertex joined to three leaves that are pairwise non-adjacent. Equivalently, a graph is claw-free if no vertex has three pairwise non-adjacent neighbours, that is, if the independence number of every neighbourhood is at most two.

Examples

Many familiar graph families are claw-free. Line graphs are claw-free: in a line graph, the edges incident to any one vertex of the original graph form a clique, so three mutually non-adjacent neighbours cannot occur around a single vertex. Other examples include the Petersen graph, which despite its many small cycles contains no claw, and all graphs of maximum degree two (disjoint unions of paths and cycles). The complement of a triangle-free graph is also claw-free.

Properties and results

Claw-free graphs generalise line graphs, and several theorems proved first for line graphs extend to them. A classical result of this type concerns Hamiltonian cycles: while deciding Hamiltonicity is NP-complete in general, it is polynomial for claw-free graphs in several important subclasses. Chvátal and Szekeres showed that various properties of line graphs are shared by claw-free graphs, motivating a long research programme.

A major achievement is the structure theory developed by Maria Chudnovsky and Paul Seymour, which gives a decomposition describing how all claw-free graphs can be built from basic pieces. Their work also yielded a polynomial-time recognition algorithm and progress on colouring: the chromatic number of a claw-free graph can be bounded in terms of its clique number in ways that are impossible for general graphs.

Related concepts

Every line graph is claw-free, but the converse is false; the Petersen graph is a standard counterexample. Beineke's characterisation of line graphs by nine forbidden induced subgraphs begins with the claw and adds eight further obstructions. Claw-free graphs also arise in the study of matching theory, because a graph has a perfect matching related structure when its line graph — always claw-free — has corresponding properties.
