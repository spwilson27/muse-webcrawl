Algonquian peoples

The Algonquian peoples are one of the most populous and widespread North American native language groups. Today, thousands of individuals identify with various Algonquian peoples, whose languages belong to the Algonquian language family. Historically, Algonquian-speaking peoples stretched from the Atlantic coast of Newfoundland and New England westward across the Great Lakes and the northern plains to the Rocky Mountains.

Peoples and distribution

Prominent Algonquian peoples include the Anishinaabe (Ojibwe, Odawa and Potawatomi), the Cree, the Mi'kmaq, the Abenaki, the Shawnee, the Cheyenne, the Arapaho, the Blackfoot Confederacy, the Powhatan of Virginia, the Lenape (Delaware) and the Illinois Confederation.

History

Algonquian peoples of the Eastern Woodlands farmed maize, beans and squash in the south of their range while relying more heavily on hunting, fishing and wild rice in the north. They were among the first peoples encountered by French, Dutch and English colonists; the fur trade drew many nations into alliances and wars, including the Beaver Wars against the Haudenosaunee. Alliances shifted across the colonial wars, Pontiac's War and the American Revolution, and through the nineteenth century most Algonquian nations were displaced westward or confined to reservations and reserves in the United States and Canada.

Culture

Shared cultural features traditionally included patrilineal or bilateral clan systems, wigwam dwellings, birchbark canoes, and the Midewiwin religious society among the Anishinaabe. Many communities maintain vibrant governments, language-revitalisation programmes and cultural institutions today, and Powhatan, Cree, Ojibwe and other Algonquian communities number among the largest Indigenous nations in North America.

Languages

The Algonquian languages form a well-defined family with some thirty attested members, conventionally grouped into Plains, Central and Eastern branches, though these groupings are partly geographic rather than strictly genetic. Cree and Ojibwe remain among the most widely spoken Indigenous languages in Canada.
