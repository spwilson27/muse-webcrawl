Film projector

A film projector is an opto-mechanical device for displaying motion picture film by projecting it onto a screen. Most of the optical and mechanical elements, except for the illumination and sound devices, are present in movie cameras.

Principle

The projector advances the film intermittently: each frame is pulled into the film gate, held stationary for a fraction of a second while a shutter opens to let light from the lamp pass through it and the projection lens onto the screen, then advanced to the next frame. Films are usually projected at 24 frames per second. A rotating shutter produces multiple flashes per frame (typically two or three), raising the flicker rate to 48 or 72 per second so the image appears steady. Persistence of vision and the phi phenomenon make the sequence of still images appear as continuous motion.

Components

Sprockets engage the perforations along the film edges to move the film; a claw or Geneva mechanism (Maltese cross) provides the intermittent motion. The lamp house historically used carbon arc lamps and later xenon arc lamps. Sound is read by an optical soundtrack reader (or, in older systems, by separate magnetic or disc systems). Common gauges include 35mm, 16mm, 70mm, 8mm and Super 8.

Digital projection

From the 2000s onward, most cinemas converted to digital projection, in which a Digital Cinema Package file is played by a server and displayed by DLP or LCoS projectors, largely replacing film projectors in commercial exhibition.
