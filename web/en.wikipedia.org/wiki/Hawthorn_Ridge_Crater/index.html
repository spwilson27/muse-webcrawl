Hawthorn Ridge Crater

The Hawthorn Ridge Crater is a mine crater from the First World War located near Beaumont-Hamel in the Somme department of northern France. It was formed by the explosion of a large mine beneath a German strongpoint known as the Hawthorn Redoubt on 1 July 1916, the first day of the Battle of the Somme.

The mine

The mine was dug by the 252nd Tunnelling Company, Royal Engineers, beneath the German front-line position on Hawthorn Ridge. It was packed with about 40,000 pounds (18,000 kg) of explosives. Controversially, the mine was blown at 7:20 a.m., ten minutes before the infantry assault began at 7:30 a.m. — a decision taken to allow the debris to settle, but one that warned the German defenders that the attack was imminent and allowed them to re-man the crater's far lip and their trenches.

Filming

The explosion was famously filmed by the official cinematographer Geoffrey Malins for the documentary The Battle of the Somme (1916). The footage of the white plume of earth erupting from the ridge became one of the best-known images of the war. Malins recalled that the ground where he stood "rocked and swayed" as the mine went up.

The battle

The crater was briefly occupied by men of the Royal Fusiliers, but German troops consolidated around the far lip and the position was not held. Hawthorn Ridge remained in German hands until 13 November 1916, during the Battle of the Ancre, when a second, larger mine was blown and the ridge was finally captured by the 51st (Highland) Division.

Present day

The crater, roughly 40 metres across, survives today preserved under the care of the Hawthorn Ridge Crater Association, which acquired the site in 2018 and has carried out archaeological and historical research, including non-invasive surveys of the tunnels and a second crater from the November 1916 explosion. It lies close to the Beaumont-Hamel Newfoundland Memorial and Sunken Lane, associated with the Lancashire Fusiliers.
