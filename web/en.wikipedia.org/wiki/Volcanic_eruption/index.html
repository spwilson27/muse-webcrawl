Volcanic eruption

A volcanic eruption occurs when material is expelled from a volcanic vent or fissure. Several types of volcanic eruptions have been distinguished by volcanologists, named after famous volcanoes where that type of behaviour has been observed: Hawaiian, Strombolian, Vulcanian, Peléan, Plinian and others. Eruption magnitude is commonly measured on the Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI), a logarithmic scale from 0 to 8 based on the volume of ejected material.

A sustained eruption column of the Plinian type, the most powerful class of eruption.

Mechanism

Eruptions are driven by the buoyancy of magma and the pressure of exsolved gases. As magma rises, dissolved volatiles such as water vapour and carbon dioxide form bubbles; if the magma is viscous, the bubbles cannot escape and pressure builds until the magma fragments explosively.

Hazards

Eruption products include lava flows, pyroclastic flows, lahars, tephra falls and volcanic gases. The eruption of Mount Tambora in 1815 (VEI 7) caused the "Year Without a Summer" in 1816, while the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo measurably cooled the global climate for several years.
