Nim

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Nim position with heaps of matches

Nim is a mathematical game of strategy in which two players take turns removing (or "nimming") objects from distinct heaps or piles. On each turn, a player must remove at least one object, and may remove any number of objects provided they all come from the same heap or pile. Depending on the version being played, the goal of the game is either to avoid taking the last object or to take the last object.

History

Variants of Nim have been played since ancient times; the game is thought to originate in China, where it resembles the game of Tsyanshidzi ("picking stones"), and the earliest European references date to the beginning of the 16th century. Nim was given its name and its complete mathematical theory by Harvard mathematician Charles Bouton in 1901; the name is sometimes said to derive from the German word nimm, "take". The game featured prominently in Alain Resnais's 1961 film Last Year at Marienbad, and an electromechanical machine called the Nimrod played Nim at the 1951 Festival of Britain.

Mathematical theory

Nim is fundamental to combinatorial game theory. The Sprague–Grundy theorem states that every impartial game is equivalent to a Nim heap of a certain size. For the normal-play version, the winning strategy is computed with the nim-sum: the bitwise XOR of the heap sizes. If the nim-sum is zero, the position is losing for the player to move (with perfect play); otherwise, the player should make a move that makes the nim-sum zero. In the misère version, where taking the last object loses, the strategy is identical until the endgame, when the player instead aims to leave an odd number of heaps of size one.

For example, heaps of sizes 3, 4, and 5 have nim-sum 3 ⊕ 4 ⊕ 5 = 2, so the position is winning for the first player, who can move to a nim-sum of zero by reducing the heap of 5 to 3.

Variants

Many variants exist, including the subtraction games (removing from a single heap with a restricted set of moves), Wythoff's game (two heaps, with removal allowed from both at once), Grundy's game, and turning-turtles puzzles. Nim also inspired the programming language namesake: Nim, a statically typed compiled systems language, though the language is unrelated to the game's strategy.
