Subdominant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In music theory, the subdominant is the fourth degree of a diatonic scale, and the chord built upon it. The term derives from the subdominant's position "under the dominant": just as the dominant lies a fifth above the tonic, the subdominant lies a fifth below it. In the key of C major, the subdominant note is F and the subdominant triad is F major (F–A–C); in Roman numeral analysis it is labelled IV in major keys and iv in minor keys.

Function

In functional harmony, the subdominant chord has a pre-dominant (or dominant-preparation) role: it typically moves away from the tonic and toward the dominant, as in the ubiquitous progression I–IV–V. Subdominant-function chords also include the supertonic (ii) and the Neapolitan sixth. The plagal cadence — IV–I, the "Amen" cadence of hymn singing — is the principal cadence in which the subdominant resolves directly to the tonic.

In popular music, the subdominant is one of the three chords of three-chord songs and of the twelve-bar blues (I–IV–V). The famous four-chord loop I–V–vi–IV and its rotations rely on the subdominant for their sense of continuous motion.

Terminology

The term was popularised in the 19th century, notably through Hugo Riemann's theory of harmonic functions, in which tonic, dominant, and subdominant are the three fundamental functions of tonal harmony. In scale-degree nomenclature, the fourth degree is also sometimes called the subdominant because it is the mirror of the dominant around the tonic.
