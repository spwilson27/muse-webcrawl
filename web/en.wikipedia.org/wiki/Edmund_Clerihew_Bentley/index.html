Edmund Clerihew Bentley (10 July 1875 – 30 March 1956), usually known as E. C. Bentley, was a British novelist and humorist. He invented the clerihew, an irregular four-line biographical verse form named after his middle name, and wrote Trent's Last Case (1913), a landmark in the development of the detective novel.

The clerihew

Bentley devised the clerihew as a schoolboy at St Paul's School in London, where G. K. Chesterton was a close friend. A clerihew consists of the name of a famous person as its first line, followed by three rhyming lines of uneven length in an AABB scheme, offering a whimsical pseudo-biographical "fact". The classic first example is:

Sir Humphry Davy / Abominated gravy. / He lived in the odium / Of having discovered sodium.

His collection Biography for Beginners (1905), illustrated by Chesterton, was followed by More Biography (1929) and Baseless Biography (1939), and the form remains a staple of light verse.

Detective fiction

Trent's Last Case (1913), dedicated to Chesterton, introduced Philip Trent, an artist and amateur detective. Unusually for its time, the novel is built around a brilliant but fallible detective whose confident solution proves wrong — a twist that influenced later writers and helped move detective fiction toward psychological realism. Agatha Christie and Dorothy L. Sayers admired it, and Bentley returned to Trent in Trent's Own Case (1936) and the story collection Trent Intervenes (1938).

Bentley worked as a journalist on several London papers including the Daily News and the Daily Telegraph, wrote humorous verse and reviews, and was a founder member — later president — of the Detection Club. His son was the artist and stage designer Nicolas Bentley.
