Pashtunistan

Pashtunistan (Pashto: پښتونستان, "land of the Pashtuns") is a historical name for the Pashtun-inhabited region that straddles present-day northwestern Pakistan and southern and eastern Afghanistan. The term is also bound up with a twentieth-century political movement that sought an independent or autonomous Pashtun state — a cause championed at various times by Afghan governments and by Pashtun nationalist movements, and a recurring source of tension between Afghanistan and Pakistan.

Background

The Pashtun homeland was divided by the Durand Line, drawn in 1893 between British India and the Amir of Afghanistan. When British India was partitioned in 1947, the Pashtun areas east of the line — the North-West Frontier Province and the tribal agencies — joined Pakistan after a referendum, a result Afghanistan refused to recognise; Afghanistan was the only country to vote against Pakistan's admission to the United Nations.

The Pashtunistan question

Key facts
RegionPashtun homeland: NW Pakistan and S/E Afghanistan
Dividing lineDurand Line (1893)
ProponentsAfghan governments; Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan's Khudai Khidmatgar movement

In the years around 1947, the Khudai Khidmatgar ("Red Shirts") movement of Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan demanded an independent Pashtunistan, boycotting the referendum. Successive Afghan governments pressed the cause diplomatically and through propaganda and tribal subsidies; in the 1950s and early 1960s the dispute twice led to the rupture of diplomatic relations and border clashes, most seriously in 1961. Pakistan regarded the demand as irredentist and answered with integration measures, eventually merging the tribal areas (FATA) into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province in 2018. Afghan governments after the 1970s gradually let the claim lapse in practice, though the Durand Line's legitimacy remains politically sensitive, and no Afghan government — including the Taliban — has formally recognised it as an international border.
