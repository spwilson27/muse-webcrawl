Dutch Golden Age

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dutch Golden Age
Period: Roughly 1588–1672 (or the seventeenth century broadly)
Centre: Dutch Republic (esp. Holland)
Hallmarks: Global trade, religious tolerance, painting, science

The Dutch Golden Age was a period in the history of the Netherlands, roughly spanning the seventeenth century, in which Dutch trade, science, art and military power were among the most acclaimed in the world. The young Dutch Republic — seven provinces rebelling against Spanish rule — became, for a time, Europe's commercial and financial centre.

Trade and society

The Dutch East India Company (VOC, founded 1602), often called the first multinational corporation, and its West India counterpart built a global trading network; Amsterdam's stock exchange, bank and warehouses made the city the warehouse of the world. Relative religious tolerance drew immigrants and capital. Tulip mania (1636–37), history's most famous speculative bubble, bloomed and burst within this prosperity.

Art and science

An art market of unprecedented breadth supported Rembrandt, Vermeer, Frans Hals and thousands of lesser masters, producing an estimated millions of paintings — including The Night Watch (1642) and the Girl with a Pearl Earring. In science, Leeuwenhoek's microscopes revealed the microbial world (feeding, much later, the germ theory), Huygens advanced optics and astronomy, and Grotius founded international law. The era's conventional end, the disaster year 1672, brought invasion and the murder of the De Witt brothers; Dutch primacy passed gradually to England.
