Social ecology

Social ecology holds that ecological problems arise from social problems, particularly hierarchy and domination.

Social ecology is a philosophical theory and political philosophy developed principally by the American thinker Murray Bookchin (1921–2006). It argues that nearly all contemporary ecological problems are rooted in deep-seated social problems — above all, systems of hierarchy and domination among human beings, which are projected onto the natural world as the domination of nature. Social ecology therefore proposes a radical restructuring of society along ecological, democratic, and non-hierarchical lines.

Principles

Drawing on ecology, anthropology, and dialectical philosophy, Bookchin contended that the idea of dominating nature emerged from the domination of human by human — in families, cities, economies, and states. Capitalism, with its imperative of endless growth ("grow or die"), is for social ecology uniquely destructive, but the theory criticizes hierarchy as such, including in nominally socialist states. Its ethical outlook, which Bookchin called dialectical naturalism, views humanity as nature rendered self-conscious, capable of acting as a steward rather than a master of the evolutionary process.

Libertarian municipalism

The political program associated with social ecology is libertarian municipalism: the creation of directly democratic citizens' assemblies in municipalities, confederated from the local to the regional level, which would gradually displace the nation-state and administer a municipalized, commons-based economy. Bookchin's ideas influenced the eco-anarchist movement, the founding of the Institute for Social Ecology in Vermont in 1974, and, decades later, the "democratic confederalism" implemented in the autonomous Kurdish-led administration of northeastern Syria, whose theorist Abdullah Öcalan read Bookchin in prison.

Criticism and related schools

Social ecology is distinguished from deep ecology, which Bookchin attacked as misanthropic and politically evasive, and from ecofeminism and environmental sociology, which share some concerns but differ in analysis. The term is also used more generally, including in public health, for the study of how social organization interacts with environments — a usage that predates and is independent of Bookchin.
