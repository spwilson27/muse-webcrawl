Schaumburg-Lippe was a small state in northwestern Germany, created as a county in 1647 and raised to a principality in 1807. Centred on the town of Bückeburg between Hanover and Minden, it was one of the smallest member states of the German Confederation, the North German Confederation and the German Empire, and existed as a free state within the Weimar Republic until it was absorbed into Lower Saxony in 1946.

History

The territory arose from the division of the County of Schaumburg after the death of Count Otto V in 1640: the southern portion, combined with part of the County of Lippe, passed to Count Philipp of Lippe-Alverdissen, founder of the Schaumburg-Lippe line of the House of Lippe. His grandson joined the Confederation of the Rhine in 1807 and took the title of Prince. The tiny principality, never more than about 45,000 inhabitants, maintained a modest court at Bückeburg — where the young Johann Gottfried Herder served as court preacher in the 1770s — and a famous court orchestra tradition.

After 1918

Prince Adolf II abdicated in November 1918, and Schaumburg-Lippe became a free state with democratic governments; its citizens voted against merging with Prussia in a 1926 referendum. After the Second World War the British occupation authorities incorporated it into the new Land of Lower Saxony. The district of Schaumburg perpetuates the name, and the princely House of Schaumburg-Lippe, headed from Bückeburg Palace, still exists.
