Egeria (mythology)

ConsortNuma Pompilius (legendary)
DomainWater nymph; divine counsellor; childbirth and prophecy
Cult sitesGrove of the Camenae and Porta Capena, Rome; Lake Nemi at Aricia

Egeria (Latin: Ēgeria) was a water nymph in Roman mythology, one of the Camenae, who according to legend became the wife and counsellor of Numa Pompilius, the second king of Rome. Numa was said to meet Egeria by night in a sacred grove near the Porta Capena, where she dictated the religious laws and institutions — the priesthoods, festivals, and calendar — that he established for the Roman state.

Legend

Ancient authors including Livy, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Plutarch, and Ovid tell how Numa, reluctant to accept the kingship, devoted himself to divine law. Egeria's counsel legitimised his reforms: the king claimed that Rome's sacred rites — the offices of the flamines and pontifices, the worship of Fides and Terminus, the division of the year — came from his nocturnal meetings with the goddess. Livy notes dryly that Numa "pretended" these meetings as useful fiction to impress a rough populace, while Plutarch treats the story as a genuine divine romance comparable to the loves of other goddesses for mortal men.

After Numa's death, the grieving Egeria wandered to Aricia on the shore of Lake Nemi, where her weeping disturbed the rites of Diana; the goddess Hippolytus-Virbius consoled her, and Diana transformed her into a cool spring — the spring of Egeria at Nemi — melting her body away in tears. Ovid tells this version in the Metamorphoses (Book 15) and the Fasti.

Cult and associations

Egeria was associated with springs, prophecy, and childbirth: pregnant women prayed to her for an easy delivery, connecting her name in folk etymology with egerere, "to bring forth." Her grove outside the Porta Capena, with its spring and cave, remained a favoured retreat; the satirist Juvenal complained that it had been rented out and marred in his own day. The name "Egeria" later became a byword for a female adviser behind a statesman, and the fourth-largest asteroid, 13 Egeria, is named after her.
