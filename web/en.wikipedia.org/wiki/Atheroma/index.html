Atheroma

An atheroma, or atheromatous plaque, is an abnormal accumulation of material in the inner layer of the wall of an artery. The material consists largely of macrophage cells, lipids (cholesterol and fatty acids), calcium and fibrous connective tissue. Atheromas are the defining lesion of atherosclerosis, the underlying cause of most heart attacks and many strokes.

Development

Plaques begin as fatty streaks in the intima of large and medium arteries, developing where endothelial injury and retained LDL cholesterol provoke inflammation. Smooth muscle cells form a fibrous cap over a lipid-rich necrotic core. Over decades the plaque can narrow the vessel (stenosis), or rupture and trigger thrombosis that suddenly blocks blood flow.

Clinical significance

Rupture of a coronary plaque causes most myocardial infarctions; carotid and cerebral plaques cause ischemic stroke; peripheral plaques cause claudication and limb ischemia. Risk factors include hyperlipidemia, hypertension, smoking, diabetes and chronic inflammation. Treatment centres on lipid lowering (notably with statins), antiplatelet therapy, risk-factor control, and, where needed, angioplasty or bypass surgery.
