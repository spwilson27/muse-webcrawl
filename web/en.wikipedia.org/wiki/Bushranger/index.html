Bushranger

Bushrangers were originally escaped convicts in the early years of the British settlement of Australia who used the bush as a refuge from the authorities. By the 1820s the term had evolved to refer to those who took up "robbery under arms" as a way of life, holding up travellers, inns, banks, and gold escorts; bushranging flourished during the gold rush decades of the 1850s–1870s and became a central subject of Australian folklore.

History

The first bushrangers were convict escapees — "bolters" — from penal settlements such as Sydney Cove and Van Diemen's Land, some of whom, like Matthew Brady and Martin Cash, became folk figures. A second, deadlier wave accompanied the gold rushes, when bushrangers such as Ben Hall, Frank Gardiner, and John Gilbert robbed gold transports and towns; the 1862 Lambing Flat and Forbes-area raids and the Eugowra Rocks robbery, which netted a large haul of gold, were among the biggest crimes of the era.

Colonial governments responded with the Felons Apprehension Acts, which effectively outlawed named bushrangers and allowed them to be shot on sight. The last major outbreak was the Kelly gang in Victoria: Ned Kelly and his associates murdered police at Stringybark Creek in 1878, robbed banks at Euroa and Jerilderie, and made a final stand at Glenrowan in 1880 wearing homemade plate armour; Kelly was hanged in Melbourne that year.

Legacy

Bushrangers occupy an ambivalent place in Australian national identity, variously portrayed as brutal criminals and as anti-authoritarian rebels against a harsh convict system. They feature prominently in ballads, art (such as Sidney Nolan's Kelly series), and film, beginning with The Story of the Kelly Gang (1906), often cited as the world's first feature-length narrative film.
