A hazzan (Hebrew: חַזָּן, plural hazzanim; in English, cantor) is a Jewish musician trained to lead the congregation in sung prayer in the synagogue. The hazzan serves as the shaliach tzibbur, the emissary of the congregation, reciting prayers on its behalf with the traditional melodic chant (nusach) appropriate to each service and festival.

History

The office developed as synagogue prayer became more elaborate in late antiquity and the medieval period; by the geonic era the hazzan was a recognised communal functionary who also composed liturgical poetry (piyyutim). In early modern Europe the role expanded to include virtuoso vocal display, and by the 19th and early 20th centuries a "golden age of cantorial music" produced celebrated hazzanim such as Yossele Rosenblatt and Gershon Sirota, whose recordings spread cantorial art widely.

Role today

In traditional services the hazzan chants the liturgy, leads responsive readings, and may read from the Torah and teach bar and bat mitzvah students. In Reform, Conservative, and Reconstructionist Judaism, cantors are ordained clergy — equals of rabbis — trained in seminaries such as Hebrew Union College's Debbie Friedman School of Sacred Music and the Jewish Theological Seminary's H. L. Miller Cantorial School; women have been ordained as cantors since 1975 in the Reform movement. Cantorial duties also include pastoral care, officiating at weddings and funerals, and directing choirs.

Qualifications

Traditionally a hazzan was expected to be upright in character, learned in Jewish law, married, and possessed of a pleasing voice. The Shulchan Aruch codifies requirements for the prayer leader, stressing that he should be acceptable to the congregation he represents.
