Endothermic process

An endothermic process is any thermodynamic process in which the system absorbs energy from its surroundings, usually in the form of heat. The term, coined by Marcellin Berthelot from the Greek endon ("within") and thermē ("heat"), contrasts with an exothermic process, which releases energy. In an endothermic change the enthalpy of the products is greater than that of the reactants (ΔH > 0).

Examples

Common endothermic processes include melting ice, evaporation of water, and the dissociation of molecules. Photosynthesis is endothermic: plants absorb sunlight to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen. In chemistry, reactions such as the decomposition of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂) require sustained heating. The mixing of barium hydroxide octahydrate with ammonium chloride is a dramatic demonstration, becoming cold enough to freeze water beneath the flask.

Everyday uses

Instant cold packs exploit endothermic dissolution, typically of ammonium nitrate or urea in water, absorbing heat from the surroundings and cooling an injury. Sweating cools the body because evaporation of water from the skin is endothermic. Endothermic steps also occur in industrial processes such as steam reforming of natural gas.

Thermodynamics

An endothermic reaction may still be spontaneous if the increase in entropy is large enough that ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is negative, as in the evaporation of water at room temperature. Otherwise, energy must be supplied continuously to drive the process.
