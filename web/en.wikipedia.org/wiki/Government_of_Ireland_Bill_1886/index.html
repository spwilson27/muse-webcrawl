Government of Ireland Bill 1886

The Government of Ireland Bill 1886, commonly known as the First Home Rule Bill, was the first major attempt made by a British government to enact a law creating home rule for part of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland. It was introduced by Prime Minister William Ewart Gladstone on 8 April 1886 and would have created a devolved Irish legislature responsible for most domestic affairs. The bill was defeated in the House of Commons on 8 June 1886 by 341 votes to 311, splitting the Liberal Party and leading to the creation of the Liberal Unionist Party and a generation of Unionist-dominated politics.

Background

The demand for Irish self-government — "Home Rule" — had been organized politically since Isaac Butt founded the Home Government Association in 1870. Under Charles Stewart Parnell, the Irish Parliamentary Party became a disciplined force, and in the general election of 1885 it won 85 of Ireland's 103 seats, holding the balance of power at Westminster between the Liberals and Conservatives. Gladstone, whose son Herbert revealed his conversion to Home Rule in the "Hawarden Kite" incident of December 1885, formed his third government in February 1886 with the support of the Irish party and committed it to a Home Rule bill.

Provisions

The bill proposed:

  • A unicameral Irish legislature (the "Legislative Body") sitting in Dublin, composed of two "orders" that would normally vote together but could vote separately on demand: the first order of 28 Irish representative peers and 75 members elected on a high property franchise, and the second order of 204 members elected on the existing parliamentary franchise. The two orders held a mutual veto.
  • An Irish executive under the Lord Lieutenant, in practice responsible to the Irish legislature — though the text avoided the full cabinet system.
  • Westminster's retention of the Crown, war and peace, foreign affairs, trade and customs, coinage, and other imperial matters; customs and excise revenues would remain under imperial control, with Ireland contributing a fixed share (roughly one-fifteenth) of imperial expenditure.
  • The withdrawal of Irish MPs from Westminster entirely — a provision that provoked the famous objection of "no taxation without representation."
  • Continued authority of the imperial parliament, preserving the legal supremacy of Westminster.

A companion Land Purchase Bill, offering state credit to Irish tenants to buy out their landlords, was introduced alongside it and fell with the main bill.

Debate and defeat

The bill's second reading debate was one of the great parliamentary occasions of the Victorian era. Gladstone closed the debate with a celebrated peroration urging members to stand by "the golden threads of history" and not to reject the hopes of Ireland. Opposition came from the Conservatives under Lord Salisbury and, decisively, from within Gladstone's own party: Joseph Chamberlain and the radical wing objected to the exclusion of Irish MPs and to the bill's centralizing financial scheme, while the Whig leader Lord Hartington led aristocratic Liberal opposition. On 8 June 1886 the bill was defeated at second reading, 341 votes to 311, with 93 Liberals voting against their government.

Consequences

Gladstone dissolved Parliament and fought the ensuing general election on Home Rule; the Conservatives and the breakaway Liberal Unionists won a commanding majority, beginning twenty years of mostly Unionist government (1886–1905, interrupted by Gladstone's and Rosebery's 1892–1895 ministry). The Liberal split of 1886 was one of the great realignments of British party politics: figures such as Chamberlain and Hartington permanently left the Liberal Party, and the Liberal Unionists eventually merged with the Conservatives in 1912. Gladstone introduced a second Home Rule Bill in 1893, which passed the Commons but was crushed in the Lords. Home Rule was finally enacted (though suspended and then transformed by partition) with the Government of Ireland Act 1914.
