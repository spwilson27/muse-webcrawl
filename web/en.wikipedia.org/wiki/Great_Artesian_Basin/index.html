The Great Artesian Basin (GAB) is the largest and deepest artesian basin in the world, stretching over 1.7 million square kilometres (660,000 sq mi) and underlying about 22 per cent of the Australian continent, including parts of Queensland, New South Wales, South Australia, and the Northern Territory. It stores an estimated 64,900 cubic kilometres of groundwater in Jurassic–Cretaceous sandstone aquifers laid down between about 100 and 250 million years ago, and in places reaches depths of 3,000 metres, with water temperatures ranging from 30 °C to over 100 °C.

Importance

The basin is the only reliable source of fresh water through much of arid inland Australia. Thousands of bores supply towns, pastoral stations, and mining operations; naturally flowing mound springs, fed by basin discharge, support unique ecosystems and were vital to Aboriginal peoples and early European exploration. Water in the deepest parts of the basin may be up to two million years old, having travelled slowly from recharge areas along the Great Dividing Range.

Management and decline

Uncontrolled free-flowing bores drilled from the late 19th century caused large falls in artesian pressure and the extinction of some mound springs. Since 1999 the Great Artesian Basin Sustainability Initiative (GABSI) has funded bore capping and piping programmes, rehabilitating hundreds of bores and saving hundreds of thousands of megalitres per year. Water extraction for coal seam gas and mining, notably around the Surat Basin, remains a subject of environmental controversy.
