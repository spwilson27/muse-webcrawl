Lenticular cloud

Lenticular clouds (Latin: Altocumulus standing lenticularis) are stationary, lens-shaped clouds that typically form in the troposphere when moist, stable air flows over a mountain or mountain range. Their smooth, saucer-like appearance has frequently led to them being mistaken for unidentified flying objects.

Formation

When wind strikes a topographic barrier, it can be deflected upward and set off a series of standing waves on the lee side. Where the air rises and cools to its dew point at a wave crest, moisture condenses into a cloud shaped like the wave — rounded, elongated, and layered. Although the wind streams continuously through the cloud, the cloud itself remains stationary: droplets form at the windward edge and evaporate at the leeward edge. Lenticulars may stack in layers ("pile d'assiettes") and can appear far downwind of the terrain that generated the wave.

Occurrence and hazards

Lenticular clouds are common downwind of ranges such as the Sierra Nevada, the Rockies, the Andes, and the Southern Alps, and over isolated peaks such as Mount Fuji and Mount Rainier. Pilots of powered aircraft avoid the violent rotor turbulence associated with the wave system that produces them, while glider pilots prize the smooth, powerful lift found in the rising branches of mountain waves, which can carry sailplanes to extreme altitudes.
