Pumpernickel


Westphalian pumpernickel, a dense, dark steamed rye bread.

Pumpernickel is a typically dense, slightly sweet rye bread traditionally made with sourdough starter and coarsely ground rye meal. It is sometimes made with a combination of rye flour and whole rye grains ("berries"). The bread originates in the Westphalia region of Germany, where the earliest known bakery was founded in 1570.

Production

Traditional Westphalian pumpernickel contains no colouring agents; its deep brown, almost black colour comes from the Maillard reaction during an extremely long steaming process—loaves are baked in sealed tins in a steam-filled oven for 16 to 24 hours at low temperature (around 120 °C / 250 °F). The long, slow cooking caramelizes the sugars in the dough, producing the characteristic dark colour and sweet, earthy flavour. The bread is leavened with sourdough rather than baker's yeast and keeps for months when sealed.

Etymology

The name is often explained by folk etymology as deriving from the German words pumpern ("to break wind") and Nickel (a goblin or devil), presumably a reference to the bread's reputation for being hard to digest—"the devil's flatulence." The word is recorded in German from the 17th century.

North American version

The pumpernickel commonly sold in North America is a different style: a yeasted loaf coloured dark with molasses, cocoa, coffee, or caramel colour and often made with wheat flour as well as rye. It is popular in delicatessens and is frequently baked as a marbled rye swirl loaf.
