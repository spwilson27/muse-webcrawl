Spinal cord injury

Spinal cord injury
Specialty: Neurology, neurosurgery
Causes: trauma, tumours, infection, vascular events

A spinal cord injury (SCI) is damage to the spinal cord that causes temporary or permanent changes in its function. Symptoms may include loss of muscle function, sensation, or autonomic function in parts of the body supplied by the cord below the level of injury. Injuries at cervical levels often cause tetraplegia (impairment of all four limbs), while thoracic and lumbar injuries cause paraplegia.

Causes

Most spinal cord injuries result from trauma, including road traffic collisions, falls, violence and sports injuries. Non-traumatic causes include tumours, infections, inflammation such as transverse myelitis, degeneration and loss of blood supply. The cord may be injured by fracture or dislocation of vertebrae, disc herniation, or direct penetrating injury, followed by secondary damage from swelling, bleeding and inflammation.

Classification

Injuries are described by the neurological level and by completeness. The American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale grades injuries from A (complete, no motor or sensory function in the lowest sacral segments) to E (normal). Syndromes such as central cord syndrome and anterior cord syndrome describe characteristic patterns of deficit.

Treatment and prognosis

Acute care focuses on immobilisation, maintaining blood pressure and oxygenation, and surgery to stabilise the spine and decompress the cord where indicated. Long-term management is built around rehabilitation, prevention of complications such as pressure injuries and urinary infections, spasticity management, and assistive technology. Recovery depends on the severity and level of injury; complete injuries have a limited prospect of regaining function below the lesion, while incomplete injuries often improve.
