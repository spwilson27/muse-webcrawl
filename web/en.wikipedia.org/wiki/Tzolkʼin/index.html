Tzolkʼin

The Tzolkʼin (also spelled Tzolkin; in modern Kʼicheʼ orthography Chol Qʼij, "count of days") is the 260-day sacred calendar that forms the core of the Maya calendar system and of Mesoamerican divination. Used since at least the first millennium BC in various forms across the region — the Zapotecs and Aztecs had equivalents, the Aztec version being the tonalpohualli — it remains in daily use among traditional Maya communities of the Guatemalan highlands, where day-keepers (ajqʼijabʼ) count the days and divine by them.

The 260 days arise from interlocking cycles of 20 day names and 13 numbers.

Structure

The count combines two interlocking cycles: a cycle of thirteen numbers (1–13) and a cycle of twenty named days — in Yucatec Maya, Imix, Ikʼ, Akbal, Kʼan, Chikchan, Kimi, Manikʼ, Lamat, Muluk, Ok, Chuwen, Eb, Ben, Ix, Men, Kib, Kaban, Etzʼnab, Kawak, Ajaw. Because 13 and 20 are coprime, every pairing occurs exactly once before the count repeats, giving 13 × 20 = 260 unique days.

Combined with the 365-day solar calendar (the Haabʼ), the Tzolkʼin produces the 52-year Calendar Round: a Tzolkʼin date plus a Haabʼ date repeats only after 18,980 days. Dates in inscriptions of the Classic Maya were further anchored in absolute time by the Long Count.

Origin and purpose

The origin of the 260-day period is debated. Proposed explanations include the interval between zenith passages of the sun at the latitude of early Mesoamerican centres such as Izapa (about 260 days between the August and April zeniths), the human gestation period (approximately nine lunar months, or about 260 days), the synodic cycle of Venus as morning star variants, and agricultural or eclipse-cycle considerations. Functionally, each day-sign and number carried character and prognostications: the calendar was a divinatory almanac used to choose days for rituals, planting, warfare, marriage, and the naming of children, whose birth day-sign was held to shape their destiny.

Modern practice

Remarkably, the count has been maintained unbroken by highland Maya day-keepers through the colonial period to the present, despite persecution; communities in places such as Momostenango and Chichicastenango still consult ajqʼijabʼ. In recent decades the Tzolkʼin has also been adopted, in adapted form, by New Age movements, notably José Argüelles's "Dreamspell" calendar, which scholars and many Maya practitioners distinguish from the traditional count.
