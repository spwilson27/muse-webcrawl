Computer chess includes both hardware (dedicated computers) and software capable of playing chess. Computer chess provides opportunities for players to practice even in the absence of human opponents, and also provides opportunities for analysis, entertainment and training. Computer chess applications that play at the level of a chess grandmaster or better are available for hardware from supercomputers to smart phones. Standalone chess-playing machines are also available for purchase.

History

The idea of creating a chess-playing machine dates to the eighteenth century, when Wolfgang von Kempelen built the Mechanical Turk, a celebrated hoax that concealed a human player. Genuine theoretical work began with Claude Shannon's 1950 paper "Programming a Computer for Playing Chess" and Alan Turing's hand-simulated program Turochamp (1951). Shannon proposed two search strategies, "Type A" (brute force) and "Type B" (selective), and outlined the minimax approach with an evaluation function.

Programs improved steadily through the 1960s and 1970s (Mac Hack VI, Chess 4.x, Belle) as hardware accelerated. In 1996 IBM's Deep Blue lost a match to world champion Garry Kasparov, but won the rematch in May 1997 by 3½–2½, the first time a computer defeated a reigning world champion under classical time controls. Deep Blue evaluated about 200 million positions per second using massively parallel custom hardware.

Modern engines

Modern engines such as Stockfish, Komodo, and Houdini far exceed the best human players, with ratings estimated above 3500 Elo. A major shift came in 2017 with DeepMind's AlphaZero, which learned chess by self-play using deep neural networks and Monte Carlo tree search, defeating Stockfish in a widely publicised match. Open-source projects such as Leela Chess Zero applied similar methods. Stockfish subsequently adopted efficiently updatable neural network (NNUE) evaluation, combining classical alpha–beta search with learned evaluation.

Methods

Engines typically represent the board with bitboards, search the game tree with alpha–beta pruning augmented by techniques such as transposition tables, iterative deepening, null-move pruning, and quiescence search, and evaluate leaf positions using material, piece-square tables, and features of pawn structure, king safety, and mobility.
