Hymen

The hymen is a thin piece of mucosal tissue that surrounds or partially covers the vaginal opening in females. It forms part of the vulva and develops during embryogenesis from the sinovaginal bulb. Its shape and extent vary greatly among individuals: it may be annular (ring-like), crescentic, fimbriated, or, rarely, imperforate, cribriform, or septate.

Development and anatomy

Before puberty the hymen is thin and inelastic; under the influence of estrogen at puberty it becomes thicker, more elastic, and often redundant. The hymen typically has one or more openings that allow menstrual blood to pass; an imperforate hymen, occurring in roughly 0.1% of newborn girls, blocks this flow and may require minor surgical correction after puberty. The hymen can be stretched or torn by many ordinary activities, by tampon use, or by medical examinations, and it may stretch rather than tear during intercourse; consequently its condition is not a reliable indicator of sexual history, a point emphasized by the World Health Organization, which has condemned "virginity testing" as unscientific and a violation of human rights.

Cultural significance

In many cultures the hymen has historically been (incorrectly) treated as proof of virginity, a belief that has had significant social consequences for women. The word derives from Hymen, the Greek god of marriage ceremonies.
