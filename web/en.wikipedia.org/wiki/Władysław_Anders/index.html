Władysław Anders

Władysław Albert Anders (11 August 1892 – 12 May 1970) was a general in the Polish Army, and later in life a politician and prominent member of the Polish government-in-exile in London. He is best known as commander of the Anders Army, the Polish II Corps in the West, which fought at the Battle of Monte Cassino.

Early life and career

Anders was born in the village of Błonie near Kutno, then part of Congress Poland in the Russian Empire, into a family of Baltic German descent that had settled in Poland. He studied at the Riga Polytechnic and, during the First World War, served as a cavalry officer in the Imperial Russian Army, where he was wounded and decorated several times.

After the rebirth of Poland in 1918, Anders joined the Polish Army and fought in the Polish–Soviet War (1919–1921), commanding the 15th Poznań Uhlan Regiment. In the interwar years he rose steadily through the cavalry arm, studied in France, and by 1939 commanded the Nowogródek Cavalry Brigade.

Second World War

During the German and Soviet invasions of Poland in September 1939, Anders' brigade fought in the north; he was wounded and captured by the Red Army. He was imprisoned by the NKVD in Moscow, including in the Lubyanka, and was repeatedly interrogated.

Following the German invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941 and the Sikorski–Mayski Agreement, Anders was released and appointed commander of the Polish Armed Forces in the USSR, being formed from Polish POWs and deportees. Disagreements with the Soviets over supplies, the fate of missing Polish officers (later known to have been murdered in the Katyn massacre), and the political future of Poland led Anders to evacuate his army — over 100,000 soldiers and civilians — to Iran in 1942.

In the Middle East his force was reorganised as the Polish II Corps under British command. It fought in the Italian campaign, famously capturing Monte Cassino in May 1944 after months of Allied assaults, and later took part in the battles for Ancona and Bologna.

Post-war life

After the war, Anders settled in London. The communist government in Warsaw stripped him of his Polish citizenship in 1946 (restored posthumously in 1989). He became a leading figure of the Polish emigration, serving on the Council of Three, which acted as a collective head of state of the government-in-exile after 1954. He died in London on 12 May 1970, the 26th anniversary of the assault on Monte Cassino, and was buried among his soldiers at the Polish War Cemetery at Monte Cassino.
