The Leduc 0.22 was a French experimental aircraft built in the 1950s to explore the use of the ramjet as a means of propulsion for a supersonic interceptor. It was the largest and most ambitious of the series of aircraft developed by engineer René Leduc, following the smaller Leduc 0.10 and Leduc 0.21 research aircraft, and was intended to lead to an operational Mach 2 interceptor.

Like its predecessors, the 0.22 was powered by a large ramjet in which the entire fuselage acted as the intake and combustion chamber. Because a ramjet cannot produce thrust at rest, the aircraft also carried a conventional turbojet engine housed inside the ramjet duct, which provided power for takeoff, landing, and acceleration to the speed at which the ramjet could take over.

Design and development

The 0.22 was a single-seat mid-wing monoplane of all-metal construction, with a long cylindrical fuselage forming the ramjet. The pilot sat in a cockpit in the nose cone, which also contained the central body of the intake. The annular intake admitted air around the nose cone into the ramjet burner, where fuel was injected and burned in a series of concentric rings. The supplementary turbojet, a SNECMA Atar 101D, was mounted coaxially within the duct.

Unlike the earlier Leduc aircraft, which had been carried aloft and released from a mother ship such as the Sud-Est Languedoc, the 0.22 was designed to take off under its own power from a conventional runway using the turbojet, the first of the Leduc designs able to do so.

Operational history

The 0.22 made its first flight on 26 December 1956, powered by the turbojet alone, from Istres in southern France. The ramjet was first lit in flight on 18 May 1957. A total of some thirty flights were made, and the aircraft reached speeds of around Mach 0.9; ramjet-powered flight was achieved but transonic and supersonic performance remained limited because the Atar produced insufficient thrust to push the aircraft to the speeds at which the ramjet delivered its full performance.

Budgetary pressures and the greater promise of conventional turbojet-powered interceptors such as the Dassault Mirage III led the French government to cancel further development of the Leduc interceptor in 1957, and flight testing ended. The sole prototype was preserved and is displayed at the Musée de l'Air et de l'Espace at Le Bourget Airport near Paris.

Specifications

Crew: 1; length 18.21 m (59 ft 9 in); wingspan 9.95 m (32 ft 8 in); wing area 21.7 m². Powerplant: 1 × SNECMA Atar 101D turbojet plus the Leduc ramjet. Maximum speed was intended to exceed Mach 2 at altitude in the definitive interceptor, though this was never approached in testing.
