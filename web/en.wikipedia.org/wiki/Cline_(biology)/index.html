Cline (biology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A trait varying gradually along a geographic gradient

In biology, a cline is a measurable gradient in a single characteristic (or biological trait) of a species across its geographical range. The term was coined by Julian Huxley in 1938. Clines arise when natural selection acts differently across an environmental gradient (such as altitude, temperature, or moisture) while gene flow between neighbouring populations keeps the species genetically connected.

Types of clines

  • Ecocline: a gradient correlated with an environmental variable, e.g. body size increasing with latitude (Bergmann's rule).
  • Geocline: variation along a geographic transect, such as a mountain slope.
  • Chromocline: variation in colour, as in the plumage gradients of some bird species.

Examples

Classic examples include the ring species complex of herring gulls around the Arctic, latitudinal gradients in Drosophila body size and inversion frequencies, and skin pigmentation clines in humans correlated with ultraviolet radiation intensity.

Distinction from subspecies

Clines describe continuous variation and contrast with the discrete categories of subspecies; populations at the extremes of a cline may differ markedly yet remain interfertile where they meet.
