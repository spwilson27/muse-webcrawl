Cyclotomic polynomial

FieldAlgebra, number theory
NotationΦn(x)
RootsThe primitive n-th roots of unity
Degreeφ(n), Euler's totient function

In mathematics, the n-th cyclotomic polynomial, denoted Φn(x), is the monic polynomial whose roots are exactly the primitive n-th roots of unity—that is, the complex numbers ζ with ζn = 1 that are not roots of unity of any smaller order. It is defined by Φn(x) = ∏ (x − ζk) over all k coprime to n with 1 ≤ k ≤ n, where ζk = e2πik/n.

Properties

Cyclotomic polynomials have integer coefficients and are irreducible over the rational numbers—a fact proved using reduction modulo a prime. Their degrees are given by Euler's totient function: deg Φn = φ(n). The fundamental identity xn − 1 = ∏d|n Φd(x) allows Φn to be computed recursively via Möbius inversion. Examples include Φ1(x) = x − 1, Φ2(x) = x + 1, Φ3(x) = x2 + x + 1, Φ4(x) = x2 + 1, and Φ6(x) = x2 − x + 1.

Significance

Cyclotomic polynomials are central to algebraic number theory: the cyclotomic fields ℚ(ζn) are the archetypal abelian extensions of the rationals, and by the Kronecker–Weber theorem every abelian extension arises from them. They also play a role in the theory of constructible polygons—Gauss's construction of the regular 17-gon depends on the structure of Φ17—and in Gauss's proof that Fermat primes characterise constructible prime polygons. An interesting subtlety is that although Φn for n with at most two distinct odd prime factors has coefficients only in {−1, 0, 1}, larger coefficients occur, first at n = 105.
