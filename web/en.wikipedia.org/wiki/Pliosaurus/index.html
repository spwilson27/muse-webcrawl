Pliosaurus

Pliosaurus (meaning "more lizard") is a genus of extinct marine reptiles of the family Pliosauridae, a group of short-necked plesiosaurs known as pliosaurs. It lived during the Late Jurassic, roughly 155 to 147 million years ago (Kimmeridgian to Tithonian stages), in the seas that covered Europe and, possibly, South America. The genus was named by Richard Owen in 1841, and its species were among the largest predators of Jurassic oceans.

Description

Pliosaurus species were large pliosaurs, with body lengths commonly estimated between about 9 and 13 metres for the biggest individuals. The skull was long and robust — in the largest specimens approaching or exceeding 2 metres — with a triangular profile, a short symphysis at the front of the lower jaw, and large conical teeth that are subtriangular (three-edged) in cross-section, a hallmark of the genus. The neck was short, and the four limbs were transformed into powerful paddles, the rear pair larger than the front. Estimates suggest the bite of the largest species was among the most powerful of any known animal.

Species

The taxonomy of Pliosaurus has been repeatedly revised, and many species named in the nineteenth century are now considered doubtful. Species generally recognised in modern revisions include:

  • Pliosaurus brachydeirus — the type species, from the Kimmeridge Clay of England.
  • Pliosaurus brachyspondylus and Pliosaurus macromerus — also from the Jurassic of England, of debated validity.
  • Pliosaurus funkei — a very large species from the Middle Volgian (Tithonian) of Svalbard, originally publicised as "Predator X", with an estimated length of roughly 10–13 metres.
  • Pliosaurus kevani — named in 2013 from a large skull found on the Dorset coast of England.
  • Pliosaurus rossicus and Pliosaurus irgisensis — from Russia.
  • Pliosaurus patagonicus and Pliosaurus almanzaensis — from Argentina, among the few records from the Southern Hemisphere.

Discovery and history

The first Pliosaurus material was described in the early nineteenth century from the Kimmeridge Clay Formation of England; Owen erected the genus in 1841 for the species P. brachydeirus, based largely on a tooth and associated vertebrae. Over the following century and a half, pliosaurid remains from Oxford Clay and Kimmeridge Clay localities, as well as from Russia, Norway's Svalbard archipelago, and Patagonia, were assigned to the genus. A spectacular, nearly complete skull more than 2 metres long, collected from the cliffs near Weymouth, Dorset, was prepared and displayed in 2023–2024; it is regarded as possibly the most complete Pliosaurus skull ever found.

Palaeobiology

Pliosaurus was an apex predator of Late Jurassic seas, feeding on fish, cephalopods, ichthyosaurs, and other plesiosaurs, as suggested by bite marks on bones and gut-content associations. Its large eyes and well-developed olfactory regions imply reliance on both sight and smell in hunting. Like other plesiosaurs it swam by flapping all four paddles and almost certainly gave birth to live young at sea.
