Robot ethics

Robot ethics, sometimes known as "roboethics", is an area of study concerned with the moral behaviour of humans as they design, construct, use and treat robots, and with the moral behaviour of the robots themselves, whether human-like artificial moral agents or not. Robot ethics is a sub-field of the ethics of artificial intelligence, and is related to machine ethics and information ethics.

Background

The term was prominent from the First International Symposium on Roboethics in 2004. Earlier imaginative treatments, such as Isaac Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics, framed the problem of constraining machine behaviour by built-in rules. Modern scholarship instead addresses liability for robot actions, the use of military robots and autonomous weapons, robots in healthcare and elder care, privacy and data collection by domestic robots, and the social effects of humanoid appearance.

Key issues

Responsibility

A central question is who is accountable when an autonomous system causes harm: the manufacturer, the programmer, the owner, or the operator. As systems become more autonomous, traditional liability frameworks are strained, leading to proposals for new standards of certification and insurance.

Military robots

Autonomous and semi-autonomous weapons raise questions about whether machines should make life-or-death targeting decisions, prompting calls for "meaningful human control" over lethal systems.

Robots as moral agents and patients

Some researchers examine whether robots could eventually warrant moral consideration themselves, and how humans' treatment of lifelike robots affects human behaviour and empathy.
