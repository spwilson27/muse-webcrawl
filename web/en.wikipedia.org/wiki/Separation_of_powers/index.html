Separation of powers

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The separation of powers is a model for the governance of a state, under which the state is divided into branches, each with separate and independent powers and areas of responsibility, so that the powers of one branch are not in conflict with those of the others. The typical division is into three branches: a Legislature, an executive and a Judiciary — sometimes called the trias politica model.

History

The concept has ancient roots: Aristotle described a deliberative, a magisterial and a judicial element in constitutions, and Rome mixed consuls, Senate and assemblies. John Locke's Second Treatise of Government (1689) distinguished legislative, executive and federative powers. The model is most famously associated with Montesquieu, whose The Spirit of Law (1748) argued that liberty is protected when the power of making laws, executing them and judging disputes are not united in the same hands. Montesquieu's account, influenced by his (partly idealised) reading of the British constitution, shaped the framers of the United States Constitution.

Checks and balances

In many systems the branches are not wholly sealed off but restrain one another — a system of checks and balances. In the United States, the President may veto legislation, Congress may override the veto and impeach officers, and courts may declare laws unconstitutional. Appointments, budgets and treaty ratification similarly require cooperation across branches. The degree of separation varies: the United States has a comparatively strict separation, while parliamentary systems exhibit a fusion of powers, because the executive is drawn from and accountable to the legislature.

Models and variations

Some constitutions recognise additional branches: Taiwan's constitution provides five branches (including examination and control yuans), and several modern constitutions establish independent electoral commissions, audit institutions and central banks as a "fourth branch" of integrity institutions. Critics of the doctrine argue that strict separation can produce gridlock, while defenders see it as a structural safeguard against tyranny and a complement to the Rule of law.
