Synoptic problem

The synoptic problem concerns the many similarities and differences in wording, order and content among the first three canonical gospels — Matthew, Mark and Luke, the Synoptic Gospels (so called because they can be viewed "together", in parallel columns, in a synopsis). The three share a great deal: hundreds of verses are nearly identical, and much material is common to all three ("triple tradition"), while a body of sayings material common to Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark is called the "double tradition". Explaining this pattern of agreement and divergence is the synoptic problem, a central question of New Testament scholarship since the 18th century.

The two-source hypothesis: Matthew and Luke independently used Mark and a lost sayings source, "Q".

Proposed solutions

The dominant solution in modern scholarship is the two-source hypothesis: Mark was written first (Marcan priority) and used as a source by both Matthew and Luke, who independently supplemented it with a hypothetical lost collection of Jesus's sayings, called Q (from German Quelle, "source"). Competing proposals include the Farrer hypothesis (Mark first; Luke used Matthew, dispensing with Q), the two-gospel hypothesis (Matthew first, Luke used it, Mark conflated both), the Augustinian hypothesis (Matthew first, Mark abbreviated Matthew, Luke used both), and various multi-source and oral-tradition models. Arguments turn on the pattern of agreements, editorial fatigue (traces left when an editor imperfectly alters a source), the order of episodes, and the primitive character of Mark's Greek style and theology.

Significance

Solving the synoptic problem underlies redaction criticism (studying how each evangelist edited sources to express his theology), the historical quest for Jesus, and reconstructions of Q and of earliest Christian communities. Although Marcan priority commands broad assent, the existence and content of Q remain debated.
