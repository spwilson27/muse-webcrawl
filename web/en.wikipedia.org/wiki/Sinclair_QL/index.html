The Sinclair QL (for "Quantum Leap") is a personal computer launched by Sinclair Research in 1984, intended as an advanced successor to the ZX Spectrum for professional and business users. It was the first British home computer built around a Motorola 68000-family processor — in the event the cost-reduced 68008, which has a 32-bit internal architecture but an 8-bit external data bus.

Hardware and software

The QL shipped with 128 KB of RAM, a full-travel keyboard in a slim black case, and two built-in ZX Microdrive tape-loop cartridges for storage instead of floppy disks. Its operating system, QDOS, written largely by Tony Tebby, offered multitasking — unusual in a machine of its price — and the SuperBASIC interpreter was a structured, extensible dialect of BASIC. The machine was bundled with the Psion Xchange office suite (word processor, spreadsheet, database and business graphics), giving it genuine productivity credentials.

Launch troubles and reception

The QL was famously announced in January 1984 before development was complete — it was rushed out ahead of Apple's Macintosh — and early production machines shipped late and sometimes with an external "kludge" ROM dongle to accommodate unfinished firmware. Combined with reliability concerns over the Microdrives and the 68008's constrained performance, this damaged the machine's reputation in the business market it targeted. Sales were disappointing, and after Amstrad bought Sinclair Research's computer business in 1986, QL production ended.

Legacy

Despite commercial failure, the QL developed a loyal following. Its operating system concepts influenced later designs, and as a young employee at Psion's QL software efforts, Finnish programmer Linus Torvalds owned a QL — experience with QDOS and the desire to improve on it fed into the background from which Linux later emerged. Enthusiast hardware and emulators keep the platform alive.
