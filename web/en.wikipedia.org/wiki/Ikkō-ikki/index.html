The Ikkō-ikki (一向一揆, "single-minded leagues") were confederations of peasants, merchants, monks, and local samurai in 15th- and 16th-century Japan who united under the banner of the Jōdo Shinshū (True Pure Land) school of Buddhism. Inspired by the teachings of the Hongan-ji temple network and energised by the priest Rennyo, the Ikkō-ikki resisted the authority of the shogunate and the daimyo, at times governing whole provinces as religious republics.

History

The movement grew out of the congregational organisation of Jōdo Shinshū, whose doctrine of salvation through faith in Amida Buddha appealed strongly to commoners. Rennyo (1415–1499) revitalised the Hongan-ji and spread the faith through pastoral letters (ofumi), creating a network of devout local congregations (monto) bound by mutual aid.

Kaga province

In 1488 the Ikkō-ikki of Kaga overthrew the provincial governor Togashi Masachika in what is often called the first successful peasant uprising in Japanese history. Kaga was thereafter ruled by the league for roughly a century — a period sometimes described as "the peasants' kingdom" — until it was crushed by Oda Nobunaga in 1580.

Wars with Nobunaga

The Ikkō-ikki strongholds at Ishiyama Hongan-ji in Osaka and Nagashima resisted Nobunaga for a decade. The Ishiyama Hongan-ji War (1570–1580) ended with the temple's negotiated surrender, while the Nagashima sieges ended in massacre. Remnants of the movement later served under Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and the Hongan-ji survived by submitting to secular authority.
