Ether

In organic chemistry, an ether is a compound that contains an oxygen atom connected to two alkyl or aryl groups. They have the general formula R−O−R′, where R and R′ represent the alkyl or aryl groups. Ethers can be classified into two varieties: if the alkyl or aryl groups are the same on both sides of the oxygen atom, then it is a simple or symmetrical ether, whereas if they are different, the ethers are called mixed or unsymmetrical ethers.

A typical example of the first group is the solvent and anaesthetic diethyl ether, commonly referred to simply as "ether" (CH3−CH2−O−CH2−CH3). Ethers are common in organic chemistry and even more prevalent in biochemistry, as they are common linkages in carbohydrates and lignin.

Structure and bonding

The C–O–C bond angle in ethers is about 110°, close to tetrahedral, because the oxygen atom is sp3-hybridised. Ethers are relatively unreactive: the C–O bonds resist hydrolysis under neutral and basic conditions, though strong acids such as hydrobromic or hydroiodic acid can cleave them. This stability makes ethers useful as inert solvents for reactions such as the Grignard reaction, whose reagents cannot be formed in protic solvents.

Synthesis

Symmetrical ethers are prepared industrially and in the laboratory by the acid-catalysed dehydration of alcohols. Unsymmetrical ethers are most often made by the Williamson ether synthesis, in which an alkoxide ion displaces a halide from a primary alkyl halide in an SN2 reaction.

Notable ethers

Diethyl ether was the first widely used general anaesthetic, publicly demonstrated in 1846. Cyclic ethers include the solvents tetrahydrofuran (THF) and dioxane, and the epoxides, whose strained three-membered rings are far more reactive. Crown ethers are cyclic polyethers that bind metal cations selectively and act as phase-transfer catalysts. On standing in air, many ethers slowly form explosive peroxides and must be stored with care.
