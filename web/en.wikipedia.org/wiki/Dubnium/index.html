Dubnium is a synthetic chemical element with the symbol Db and atomic number 105. It is extremely radioactive: its most stable known isotope, dubnium-268, has a half-life on the order of a day, and only minute quantities—individual atoms—have ever been made. Dubnium is named after the town of Dubna, Russia, home of the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR), where it was first produced. Within the periodic table it sits in group 5 below vanadium, niobium, and tantalum, and is expected to behave as a heavy homologue of tantalum.

Discovery and naming controversy

Teams at Dubna reported synthesizing element 105 in 1968–1970 by bombarding americium-243 with neon-22 ions, while a Berkeley team led by Albert Ghiorso claimed synthesis in 1970 by bombarding californium-249 with nitrogen-15. The priority dispute, part of the "transfermium wars," lasted decades: the Dubna group favored the name nielsbohrium and the Americans hahnium (which had also been used for the element in the United States for years). In 1997 the IUPAC settled on dubnium, crediting the Dubna laboratory's contributions.

Properties

Because only a few atoms have been created, dubnium's physical properties are largely predicted: it should be a dense refractory metal, similar to tantalum. Single-atom gas-phase chemistry experiments have confirmed group-5 behavior—dubnium forms volatile chlorides and oxychlorides (DbCl5, DbOCl3)—though relativistic effects cause measurable deviations from tantalum's behavior, such as a reversal in the volatility order of its halides relative to niobium and tantalum. In aqueous solution it shows a stable +5 oxidation state, like its lighter congeners.

Isotopes

About thirteen isotopes are known, from 255Db to 270Db, decaying mainly by alpha emission and spontaneous fission. Dubnium-268, discovered in the decay chains of moscovium and nihonium produced at Dubna, is unusually long-lived for such a heavy nucleus and anchors the alpha-decay chains that confirm the synthesis of elements 115 and 113.
