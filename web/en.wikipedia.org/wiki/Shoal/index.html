Shoal

In oceanography, geomorphology and earth sciences, a shoal is a natural submerged ridge, bank or bar that consists of, or is covered by, sand or other unconsolidated material and rises from the bed of a body of water to near the surface. Shoals are characteristically elongated features formed by the deposition of sediment transported by currents, waves or tides.

Formation

Shoals develop where flowing water loses energy and deposits its sediment load: at river mouths, along tidal channels, across harbour entrances, and on shallow continental shelves. Related landforms include sandbars, spits, barrier islands and the navigation hazards generally called banks (e.g., the Grand Banks of Newfoundland, famous fishing grounds formed on submerged plateaus).

Navigation

Shoals are a classic hazard to shipping because water over them may be too shallow for a vessel's draft, and because they shift over time as storms and currents move sediment. "Shoaling" in seamanship means entering progressively shallower water. Navigational charts mark shoal areas with soundings, and channels through shoal regions are maintained by dredging in busy ports.

Wave shoaling

In wave mechanics, shoaling describes the change in wave height as waves enter shallow water: as depth decreases, wave speed and wavelength fall while wave height grows, until the wave breaks — the process behind surf on beaches.
