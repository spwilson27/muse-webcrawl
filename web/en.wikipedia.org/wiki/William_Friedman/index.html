William Frederick Friedman (September 24, 1891 – November 12, 1969) was an American cryptologist who is considered the dean of modern American cryptography. Born Wolfe Friedman in Chișinău (then in the Russian Empire), he emigrated to the United States as an infant and studied genetics at Cornell before being drawn into code-breaking at the Riverbank Laboratories in Illinois during the First World War.

Career

At Riverbank, Friedman and his wife Elizebeth Smith Friedman trained the first generation of American military cryptanalysts. In the 1920s he introduced rigorous statistical methods to cryptanalysis, devising the index of coincidence and other techniques still taught today. He joined the War Department in 1921 and in 1930 took charge of the Army's new Signal Intelligence Service.

Under Friedman's direction, his team — including Frank Rowlett — reconstructed and broke the Japanese diplomatic cipher machine known to the Americans as PURPLE in 1940, without ever seeing the machine itself. He also directed the development of the SIGABA cipher machine, the most secure American system of the Second World War. After the war he served with the Armed Forces Security Agency and then the National Security Agency, retiring in 1956.

Legacy

Friedman wrote the foundational American training texts on military cryptanalysis and coined terms such as "cryptanalysis" in its modern usage. The NSA's Friedman Auditorium and the joint NSA/CSS Friedman Conference Center are named in honour of William and Elizebeth Friedman.
