Islamic calendar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A printed Hijri calendar page showing Arabic month names beside a crescent moon]
A printed Hijri calendar page showing Arabic month names beside a crescent moon

The Islamic calendar, also called the Hijri calendar, is a lunar calendar consisting of 12 months in a year of 354 or 355 days. It is used by Muslims to determine religious observances — the beginning of Ramadan, the dates of Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha, and the Hajj — and as the civil calendar in Saudi Arabia (alongside the Gregorian). Its epoch is the Hijrah of Muhammad from Mecca to Medina in AD 622, designated 1 AH.

Structure

The months — Muharram, Safar, Rabi' al-Awwal, Rabi' al-Thani, Jumada al-Ula, Jumada al-Akhirah, Rajab, Sha'ban, Ramadan, Shawwal, Dhu al-Qa'dah, Dhu al-Hijjah — begin with the sighting (or, increasingly, the calculation) of the crescent moon. Because the lunar year is about 11 days shorter than the solar year, Islamic dates drift through the seasons over a 33-year cycle — Ramadan falls in every season across a generation.

History and variants

The calendar was formalised under Caliph Umar (c. 638), backdating the era to the Hijrah. A tabular version with a 30-year leap cycle serves most printed calendars; the Umm al-Qura calendar of Saudi Arabia is astronomically computed. Regional variants (the Bengali and Persian solar calendars, Ottoman fiscal calendars) complicate the historical record, and converting dates between Hijri and Gregorian systems is a staple task of Islamic historiography.
