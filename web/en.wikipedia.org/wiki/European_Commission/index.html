European Commission

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The European Commission is the executive branch of the European Union, responsible for proposing legislation, implementing decisions, upholding the EU treaties and managing the day-to-day business of the Union. It operates as the EU's civil service and executive combined — a cabinet government of (currently) one commissioner per member state, each holding a policy portfolio, led by a President who is proposed by the European Council and elected by the European Parliament.

Functions

The Commission holds the near-exclusive right of legislative initiative in most EU policy areas, drafts the budget, enforces competition law (with powers to fine companies and veto mergers that make it a global regulator), negotiates trade agreements on the Council's mandate, represents the EU externally in its competences, and acts as "guardian of the Treaties", bringing infringement proceedings against member states before the Court of Justice. Commissioners swear independence from their home governments — a claim tested by the College's political composition and the Spitzenkandidaten experiment linking the presidency to European Parliament elections (applied in 2014, contested since). The Commission's combination of technocratic administration and political executive ambition is the central constitutional ambiguity of the EU's design.
