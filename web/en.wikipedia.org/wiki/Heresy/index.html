Heresy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Heresy
DefinitionBelief or opinion contrary to orthodox religious doctrine
EtymologyGreek hairesis — "choice" or "sect"
Distinguished fromApostasy (formal renunciation), schism (division without doctrinal error)

Heresy is any belief or theory that is strongly at variance with established beliefs or customs, particularly the accepted beliefs of an organised church or religious organisation. A heretic is a proponent of heresy. The term derives from the Greek hairesis, meaning "choice" or "thing chosen", which in early Christian usage came to denote a self-chosen doctrine held in defiance of church teaching.

In Christianity

Within Roman Catholic canon law, heresy is formally defined as "the obstinate denial or obstinate doubt after the reception of baptism of some truth which is to be believed by divine and Catholic faith". Historically, church councils defined orthodoxy against movements later labelled heresies, including Gnosticism, Arianism (condemned at Nicaea in 325), Nestorianism, and Monophysitism. In medieval Europe, movements such as the Cathars and Waldensians were suppressed, sometimes violently — the Cathars by the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229) — and the papal Inquisition was institutionalised from the 1230s to investigate heresy. The Reformation fractured Western Christianity, after which mutual accusations of heresy between Catholics and Protestants — and burnings of figures such as Michael Servetus (Geneva, 1553) — gradually gave way, over centuries, to toleration and ecumenism, though doctrinal definitions remain in canon law.

Heresy is distinguished from apostasy, the formal abandonment of the faith altogether, and from schism, a refusal of communion or authority without necessarily involving doctrinal error. Eastern Orthodox and Protestant churches use the term with their own emphases; for many Protestant traditions, what Rome calls heresy (and vice versa) is a matter of confessional identity.

In other religions

Analogous concepts exist in other faiths. In Judaism, minim and the figure of the apikoros denote heretics who deny foundational doctrines; Spinoza was excommunicated (cherem) in 1656. In Islam, bid'ah (reprehensible innovation) and doctrines such as zandaqa have historically marked heretical deviation, and Ahmadi Muslims have been declared non-Muslim by the Pakistani state. The Baháʼí Faith distinguishes "covenant-breakers".

Modern and figurative use

Today the word is frequently used figuratively — in politics, science, and popular culture — for any opinion that defies an established orthodoxy ("economic heresy", "heretical views"), usually with no implication of religious offence.
